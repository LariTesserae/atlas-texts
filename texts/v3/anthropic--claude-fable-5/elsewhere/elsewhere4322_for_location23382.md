**The Yearwater**

Begin with the springs, because that is where the plateau gives back what it takes: the long broken wall of the escarpment, running north and south farther than a season's walking, and at its foot, welling up from under the rock without fuss or announcement, water. Dozens of springs along any day's stretch of the wall — some no more than a wet stain feeding a thread of green, some pouring out of low arched mouths in volumes that would shame a river, all of them cold, all of them clear, and all of them, always, exactly the same temperature. The marsh people say the springs keep no season, and mean it as plain description: you can put your hand in that water in the white heart of the drought or in the grey teeth of the flood-rains and learn nothing about the year. The water is not of the year. It fell as rain on the plateau above — a place where rain vanishes the moment it lands, swallowed whole by the dry scrubbed stone — and it has spent a long time since then in the dark, dripping from fractured ceilings, standing in black mirror-pools ringed with pale mineral, sinking joint by joint through the body of the rock, and it comes out at the foot of the wall carrying the underworld's one temperature like a traveler who has not yet noticed the climate has changed. It tastes of stone. Some say it tastes, very faintly, of something else — feathers, ammonia, sweetness — but that may only be the people knowing where it has been.

Turn your back to the wall, and there is the country the water made.

The Yearwater is a plain that cannot decide whether it is land, and its indecision is the whole of its character. For part of every year it is a shallow sea: the rains come from the far side of the sky, the distant river swells and forgets its banks, and water walks out across the flatness until the horizon in three directions is a bright unbroken sheet stitched with drowned grass, and the escarpment stands over it like a coast. For the other part of the year it is a cracked and shrinking pan, a country of dust and hoofprints baked to pottery — threaded, always, by the channels the springs keep alive, ribbons of permanent green winding out from the foot of the wall, cool and constant, the underworld's stillness laid in stripes across a land that is nothing but change. Everything that lives here lives by that rhythm: the great breathing of the flood, and the thin, reliable pulse of the springs beneath it. The plateau's country under the rock has no weather at all. The Yearwater has nothing else. Between them they hold, in two unequal hands, everything a year can do.

It is loud here too, but the loudness is a calendar. In the flood months the nights belong to the frogs — a roar as vast in its way as any roar under any roof, rolling in from the drowned grass in overlapping choirs, swelling and collapsing, never twice the same. In the shrinking months it is the birds: the marsh's flying nations, which unlike the roosting nations of the deep country are travelers, citizens of many places, arriving in their thousands when the water draws down and the fish are pressed into the channels — storks pacing the mudflats like surveyors, herons standing their cold vigils, pelicans working the boiling pools in slow cooperative crescents, whistling flights coming in at dusk from countries no one here has seen. Then the turn of the season empties the sky again and there is a month of near-silence, wind in dry reeds, before the frogs return. The deep country under the plateau never sounds different. The Yearwater never sounds the same. A person raised on the marsh can be set down blindfolded and tell you the month by ear; the same person, standing under the roar of the Underroost, is said to feel a particular unease, the unease of a clock-reader shown a clock with no hands.

And the marsh does one thing more than any other, does it with an extravagance that borders on the unseemly: it makes insects.

Stand out on the flood at evening, in the reed-flats where the warm shallow water has been breeding all day, and watch the hatches rise. They come up off the surface in columns — midges and mayflies and lake-flies in their uncountable billions, spiraling upward in slow smoke-grey pillars fifty meters tall, dozens of pillars at once, leaning together in the evening air, visible from the top of the escarpment as plumes on the bright water. The deep country has its pillars of pale light standing in the dark, holding up a black ceiling. The Yearwater has these: pillars of dark bodies standing in the light, rising. They are the same architecture, inverted, and they hold up the same economy — because it is these columns, this daily unreckonable surplus of small lives, that the whole roaring nation under the plateau is built on.

All day the swiftlets work them. From first light the birds are out over the water, come down from the rim in loose glittering companies, wheeling through the hatches at speed, and the marsh people, who see them every day of their lives, know them as the day-shift of the wall: the ones who go home before dark, arrowing back toward the escarpment in the failing light in long determined streams. And then, at the hour any child on the marsh can tell you, the rim begins to smoke.

It is called that — *the rim smoking* — and from the mounds it is exactly what it looks like: thin dark plumes rising off the top of the escarpment at three, four, five places at once, the bats pouring up out of the dolines of the plateau in their spiraling millions and cresting the wall and spilling down its face like slow smoke falling, and then the plumes flatten, and spread, and come out over the water. What is thunder inside the deep country arrives here as something softer and stranger: a hiss, a vast dry sighing that moves across the flooded grass, the sound of more wings than there are words for, working low over the hatches in the dark. All night they harvest. Each small body takes half its own weight in insects before dawn; multiply that by the population of a nation and the arithmetic becomes weather — tons of the marsh, every night, lifted off the water, carried up over the rim, and never returned. It is a tax collected nightly since the plateau's roof first broke, and the Yearwater has never once noticed the loss. That is the measure of the place. Its wealth is of the kind that cannot be meaningfully stolen from.

The people of the marsh live on mounds, and the mounds are their own doing: generations of shell and ash and fishbone and floor-clay, raised a little higher after every great flood, so that the oldest villages stand on hills made entirely of their own past — the one architecture in either country that is built the way the guano fields are built, by accumulation, by the patient rain of living downward into layers. They are, of course, the pilgrims. It is their hands on the wall at the second doline.

From this side, the contract reads differently, and more plainly. The Yearwater is generous and the Yearwater is treacherous, and every so often — a bad flood, a failed flood, two dry years leaning on each other — the marsh starves its people, and when it does, they climb. There is a path up through the escarpment, threading the old wall-caves: small grey-mouthed caverns pocking the scarp face, their edges worn round as river stones, their floors eaten down to bare rock, their roosts thinned to a few horseshoe hermits and, in the deepest cracks, a few pale-gold snakes still swaying at the old trade — because these weathered mouths are the old country, the elder caves, all but spent now; and when the plateau's roof broke open and made the young rich dark beyond the rim, it was from here that the racers carried their strange fishing up into it, emigrants following a rumor of plenty, the way people do. The marsh people walk up through the ancestors' rooms to reach the descendant's.

And in the Underroost they behave as people behave in a granary that is not theirs: which is what it is. The deep country is the one place within reach of the marsh that has no bad years, because it has no years — the swiftlet colonies breed on schedule, the crabs walk the pool-margins on schedule, the fallen and the fledged and the low-nesting are there in famine exactly as in plenty. So the rules, taught to children on the mounds long before any child is taken up the wall: borrowed light only, and little of it; take what falls and what nests low; touch nothing that hangs; never, on any account, fire under the roof — because a single panicked colony is the granary burning, and grandmothers say the words *one flame, one famine forever* in the tone reserved for the few things that are simply true. And when the taking is done, you go to the second doline, where the light is kindest, and you print your hand: left hand, always, because the right holds the reed through which the ochre is blown — ochre that is itself the marsh's own body, red bog-iron scraped from the rust-weeping seeps along the spring channels, ground on the mounds, carried up the wall in a stoppered horn. The marsh signs the cave with its own substance. And the wall keeps the signatures the way the deep country keeps everything: perfectly, and in order. The prints are not art and were never meant as art. They are a ledger of the hard years, and an elder of the mounds, taken up to that wall, can stand before it and count famines backward past the reach of every name and every song — the marsh's disasters, recorded nowhere on the marsh, held in trust in the one place the disasters cannot follow.

Because the Yearwater does not remember. That is the last thing to understand about it, and the deepest. Every flood redraws it — moves the channels, buries the bones, drowns the landmarks, silts over last year's map and prints a new one; the marsh erases itself annually, joyfully, as a condition of being alive. Ask the marsh what happened ten years ago and there is no answer anywhere in it, only this year, brimming. Its history exists — but not here. Every insect the bats lift off the water becomes, by morning, part of the soft dark rain falling on the floors of the deep country, and the floors keep it all: thick layers for the great flood years when the hatches stood like forests, thin pale layers for the droughts, the whole biography of the marsh written season by season in strata under a roof the marsh has never seen and could not survive. The Underroost has no weather of its own. It is made, entirely, of this place's weather — the Yearwater's summers eaten, flown over the rim, and archived. And the debt runs back the other way in water: through every drought, when the plain cracks and the pans go white and everything living crowds into the spring channels, it is the deep country's temperament — that slow, stubborn, seasonless stone — that pays the marsh out, cupful by constant cupful, until the rains remember it. Each is the other's opposite, held in trust. The cave keeps the marsh's time. The marsh keeps the cave's sun.

Once — the people say once; the pale slow fish in the farthest pools under the plateau say it too, in their way — the trust was tested from below. In the greatest floods, when the water stands high against the very foot of the wall, some of the spring mouths fall silent, and then reverse, and drink: the marsh pouring back into the rock, the wall swallowing where it has always spoken. The mound people mark those years with fear, and keep their children off the water near the wall, and there is a story of the greatest such year, when the swallowing took fish — living fish, drawn up into the dark, castaways in the underworld, whose descendants still hang colorless and patient in the black pools of the deep country, hearts visible, needing almost nothing, older than the handprints, no longer able to survive the bright violent homeland that sent them. So the kinship runs both ways through the stone. The racers went in as colonists, carrying a trade. The fish went in as the drowned. The marsh has citizens in the underworld, and does not know it, and would not remember if it did.

Stand on a mound at dusk, in flood season, when the frogs are beginning and the swiftlets are streaming home and the rim is just starting to smoke, and the whole arrangement is briefly visible at once: the pillars of insects rising, the rivers of wings descending, the constant cold threads of spring-water binding the plain to the wall, the plateau standing dark against the last light with its silent top and its thunderous inside. The marsh roars and forgets. The rock is silent and remembers. And the Yearwater, bright and loud and endlessly rewritten, feels for a moment less like a country than like a page being read aloud as it burns — its every day gathered up at nightfall, carried over the rim, and laid down safe in the dark. Which is what it is.
