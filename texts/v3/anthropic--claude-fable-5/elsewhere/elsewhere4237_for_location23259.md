# Lastwater

It begins where the water begins — which is to say, at the highest wet stone in the world. Go down from the Unbuilt City, down through the cloud floor and past the last honest rock, down the long dry throat of the valley where nothing grows because nothing drinks, and at a certain contour — this one is marked; it is marked obsessively — the mountain does the second strangest thing it does. It gives out water. A spring, breaking from the base of a slab the size of a church, cold enough to ache, steady as a pulse, in a massif where no snow melts and no rain falls and the summits above are so dry they have given up on dampness as a category. The town is built around this contradiction the way other towns are built around harbours, and it is named for it with the flat, unromantic precision of people who live under something enormous: Lastwater. The last water going up. The first water coming down. Nobody in the town says *first*.

## Terrain

The town stands at the head of its valley at an altitude that would be famous anywhere else — the highest permanently inhabited place in the world, though the townsfolk pronounce *permanently inhabited* with a slight stress, as if correcting an error no visitor has made yet. It is a tight, terraced place, and the terraces are the point: everything in Lastwater steps down, deliberately, emphatically, in short human-scaled flights, as if the town were teaching the slope how stairs are supposed to work. Above it the valley walls converge into the grey underside of the cloud floor, which sits over the head of the valley like a lid, and which the town does not look at more than it has to. Below it the valley opens, greens, becomes ordinary, becomes world. Lastwater faces downhill. Its plazas, its church, its long market gallery, the benches outside its doors — all of it is oriented toward the descent, toward the green, with its back to the mountain, in the posture of a person warming their hands at a fire they do not entirely trust.

The high pastures above the town, in the last mile before the cloud floor, are grazed in summer by goats and watched by herders who are paid partly in water-rights and partly in the understanding that they will be believed. What they report, they report once, quietly, to the warden, and it goes in the book, and nobody discusses it at dinner.

## Water

The spring never fails and never floods. It has run at the same rate through drought years and hard winters, through the memory of the oldest rolls, and its steadiness would itself be uncanny if the water were not so aggressively ordinary. That is the first thing every visitor says, cupping it out of the stone basin in the spring-house: it tastes of nothing. Not of minerals, not of cold, not of stone. It is the most ordinary water anyone has ever drunk — ordinary the way silence after a bell is silent, *achieved* ordinariness, ordinariness with pressure behind it.

The town has a theory, and the theory is the town's whole cosmology, so it is worth stating plainly. The mountain above is dry because the stone up there gave its water up. Not lost it — *renounced* it, the way the towers renounced straightness and the streets renounced length, as the price of becoming what they are. Stone that means to be architecture cannot afford to be wet; wetness is time behaving, is erosion keeping honest books, is the old law. And everything the high stone renounced had to go somewhere. It went down. It has been going down for ten thousand years, through the dark seams of the massif, and it comes out at the slab behind the spring-house: a current of pure surrendered ordinariness, everything the Unbuilt City gave away in order to keep happening, running out of the mountain at the rate of a heartbeat.

So the townsfolk drink the city's renunciation daily, and they credit the water with the town's character. Lastwater people are famously definite. The water is said to be good against doubt, against vagueness, against the feeling of being in two minds; the dying ask for it; pilgrims come up the long valley for it, and are given it free, because charging for it would be — the phrase is untranslatable, but roughly — *selling somebody else's no*.

And the spring keeps the town informed. Its flow is steady across years but not across days: it strengthens and slackens on a slow cycle, hours long, and the old women who sit in the spring-house can tell you, by the note of the water on the basin stone, whether the city above the clouds is breathing in or breathing out. When the massif gives its once-daily settling groan — felt in Lastwater as a shudder in the ground and a brief brightening of the spring — the whole town pauses, not in fear, in *acknowledgment*, the way you pause when someone in the next room turns over in their sleep.

## Material and Making

Every stone in Lastwater bears tool-marks, and this is not a style. It is the town's central technology, its immune system, its liturgy.

The masons discovered it early — the earliest rolls already assume it: stone at this altitude, left unmarked, begins to occur. Not quickly. But a wall built of smooth, unworked stone and left alone will, over decades, start losing its seams. Blocks fuse. Mortar lines fade like scars healing. The wall drifts toward being a single grain of rock, and single-grain rock, this close under the cloud floor, has ambitions. So every surface in Lastwater is chiselled — deliberately, visibly, in the town's dense herringbone strike — and the marks are not decoration but *signature*: proof that a hand made this, that a tool touched it, that this stone means only itself. The marks weather, and the frost-dust comes down off the mountain on still nights, a grey pollen drifting under the cloud floor, and wherever it lies on stone overnight the marks beneath it soften. So the marks are renewed. Every mason's apprenticeship ends the same way: re-cutting, stroke for stroke, the marks their teacher's teacher cut, on walls that have been re-cut forty times. The town does not build so much as it *insists*, generation after generation, at the pace of the thing it is insisting against.

There is one exception, and the town keeps it the way other towns keep a martyr's relic. A century ago the masons' guild left one boundary wall unmarked, as a test, in the highest paddock. The wall stands. It is seamless now, single-grained, its top rounded like a river cobble, and set into it — nobody made it; the herder who found it was believed, once, quietly — is a doorway. Small. Perfectly formed, lintel-curved, threshold sagged in the middle as if crossed for ten thousand years. It is already old. It was old the morning it was found. It is fenced, and children dare each other to look through it, and what they see through it is the paddock on the other side, almost always.

## Light and Sound

The light in Lastwater is honest and the town is grateful for it in a way that has hardened into custom. Shadows here are attached at the feet. Glare exists; the spring-house basin throws it; people speak of glare affectionately. At mid-afternoon the shadow of the massif itself comes across the valley and takes the town — a vast, punctual, moving dark, and the hour has a name, and the custom during that hour is to keep talking. Never let the shade cross you silent. Because up there, the town knows, shade is not empty; shade is how a crowd looks when it has been dissolved in time; and a shadow that finds you silent might mistake you for one of its own.

That is the logic of the town's noise, and Lastwater is *loud*, joyfully, strategically loud — bells, market-cries, the counting-songs of children, doors that are built to bang. The reasoning is exact: the city above keeps voices; it takes what you call out and spends it thinly, later, elsewhere. But sound spent below the cloud floor stays spent. Down here your voice is yours and comes back to you off the valley walls, promptly, in order, and so the town spends voice the way sailors ashore spend pay. Townsfolk who go high — the guides, the herders, the wardens on the frost-watch — go silent above the marked contour, all of them, without instruction. And at dusk, every dusk, the whole town performs the Counting: the great bell strikes the hour and the entire population says the numbers with it, aloud, together, from the terraces and doorways and the spring-house steps — insisting on the hour, pinning *now* to the valley floor like a tent in wind. Visitors find it eerie. It is the opposite of eerie. It is the least eerie act performed anywhere in this range, and it is performed against a very specific alternative, and the alternative is a mile uphill, exhaling.

## The Rolls

Lastwater counts itself once a year, to the person, and has for as long as there have been rolls. The census is the town's high holy day. Every name is read in the plaza and every named person answers *here*, aloud, and the answering matters more than the counting: it is the yearly proof that the town's population is a number and not a range.

Two things about the rolls, told in the order the town tells them.

First: the entry that is allowed to be different. One of the twelve who wrote the literature of the high city — the longest-staying observer, the one whose last notebook called the shadow-weather *crowds* — was a daughter of Lastwater, and she never came down. Her name is still read. Her family answers for her, and the answer is not *here* and not *absent*; the roll carries, beside her name, the only annotation of its kind: *at altitude*. Her house is kept. Its up-valley window is kept unshuttered, and a lamp is kept in it, all of them together, the family and the neighbours in rotation, the way you'd keep a candle lit. On some winter nights, the frost-watch reports, the lamp's light in that window seems to persist a while after it is put out. The family does not put it out often.

Second: the beginning. The oldest surviving roll is headed, in a firm and unremarkable hand, *Roll Nine*. Rolls One through Eight do not exist. There is no record of their loss, no gap of fire or flood, no founding legend beneath them — and the town has no founding legend at all, which for a town this old and this proud is its own kind of document. The earliest roll simply continues a count already in progress, as if the people it lists had been being counted for a long time before they were *here* to answer. The town's position on this is cheerful, unanimous, and recorded in the rolls themselves, where an early warden wrote in the margin, once, for all subsequent generations: *We came down. Mind the marks.* Nothing else. The masons re-cut that margin note every decade, stroke for stroke, along with everything else.

## What Lives Here

People — some nine hundred of them, definite, tool-marked, answering their names. Goats, bells on every one. Dogs, of a local line famous down-valley for their steadiness, all of whom share a single eccentricity: they will not look up the valley. Not fear — they simply arrange themselves, sleeping or working or begging at the market gallery, facing downhill, with the fixed courtesy of creatures declining to make eye contact with someone they know is there.

And moss. Real moss, real lichen, fawn and bottle-green, thriving on the wet stones of the spring-house and the channel-runs, the highest true vegetation in the world — and the town regards it with an odd tenderness, because the herders and the wardens have seen the stains on the high south-leaning walls above the cloud floor, the mineral blooms getting the colours approximately right, and everyone understands that the moss of Lastwater is the original. It is what the stone up there is dreaming of, from rumour, at a millimetre a century. Scraping moss from the spring-house is forbidden. The reason given to children is that it would be rude to spoil the reference.

Once or twice a generation, at the dim end of a summer day, a herder on the last pasture sees something pale walking the moraine below the cloud floor — unhurried, nautical, giving its front to the direction it is going. It never comes lower. The herder does not call out, and comes down, and tells the warden once. And some mornings after still nights the high meadows carry, among the goat prints in the frost, a scatter of tiny doorway-shapes a few centimetres tall, already weathered, already old. The herders stamp them out. That is the whole rite — a boot heel, and then, always, spoken aloud in the thin silence, the town's one prayer, which is also its foreign policy: *Not yet. Not here. No offence meant.* You insist. But you insist gently, back, because the thing you are refusing has never once insisted on you.

## The Rule of the Place

The archive holds the twelve accounts, and the maps that gently contradict each other, and the librarian's single shelving rule — no map may sit beside the map it disagrees with, so that the contradictions are stored at rest, like acids kept apart. Read them all and the arrangement of the valley comes clear, and it is an arrangement, old and stable and mutually observed, though neither party ever negotiated it.

The city above persists by yielding. It gave up its edges, its distances, its single shape, its water, its *now* — gave up everything a place can give up and still be a place — and in exchange it gets to keep happening, forever, gently, at all times except this one. And everything it gave up runs downhill. The ordinariness comes down as water and the town drinks it. The dryness's leftover dust comes down on still nights and the masons cut against it. The renounced *now* pools at the valley floor, and the town spends it loudly, hourly, all together, in the plaza at dusk.

So the law of Lastwater is the city's law read in a mirror: **persist by insisting.** Mark every stone. Count every soul. Answer when called, especially then. Keep the lamp in the up-valley window; keep the moss on the spring-house; keep talking when the mountain's shadow takes the town. Everything down here that lasted, lasted by refusing — and the refusal is not defiance, the town would tell you, but *division of labour*. Somebody in this range has to hold the far end of the rope. Somebody has to be the fixed point the tide is measured from, the parish where the old law still runs, the *here* against which all that patient, hollow, magnificent *elsewhere* can lean — the way an old tower, given long enough, grows a buttress of its own substance, and leans on it, and stands.

At dusk the bell strikes, and nine hundred voices say the numbers with it, and the sound goes up the valley and stops, as it always stops, exactly at the cloud floor — spent here, kept here, answered here. And a mile above, in the underexposed light, on stairs worn smooth by no particular moment, something at the tidal edge of an enormous patience hears the counting, perhaps, distributed across the whole of its long afternoon; and does not come down; and does not need to; because the lamp is lit, and the marks are cut, and the water, all night, keeps running out of the mountain, tasting of everything the city decided it could live without.
