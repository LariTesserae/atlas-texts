# The Outfall

You go out to reach it, and you go at low tide, because at any other hour it is under the river. Out along the embankment, past the bridges, to a stretch of wall the city built tall and stern in the same decades it was building churches underground — black brick, granite coping, iron mooring rings rusted into ornament — and then down the river stairs, worn hollow in their centers, slick with the same green that furs everything the water touches twice a day. The city does not recede here the way it does on the pump hall's stairwell. It stays with you the whole way down: traffic on the bridge, gulls, a train somewhere crossing water. The outfall is not hidden. It is in plain sight of ten thousand windows. It is simply beneath notice, which is a better concealment than depth.

At the bottom of the stairs the mud begins, and in the wall, a hundred yards along, there is an arch.

---

It is a modest arch — brick, roundheaded, cousin to the windows in the pump hall's vault, made by the same hands or the same habits of hand — set low in the river wall so that high water drowns it completely and low water leaves it standing in the mud like a doorway abandoned by its building. A portcullis of iron bars once guarded it; three bars remain, thinned to the width of fingers, and the gap where the others were is fringed with weed that streams inward on the flood and outward on the ebb, combed both ways, committed to neither.

Out of the arch, the water comes.

It comes steadily, but not evenly, and the unevenness is the first thing you learn to feel. Stand on the mud in front of the arch and watch the outflow where it meets the river, and every few seconds — long seconds, patient as breathing — the current gives a soft push. A bulge travels out from the dark of the culvert, a ring spreads, subsides, and then the next one. *Discharge,* says the push. Then nothing, nothing, nothing — *intake, compression* happening a mile away and twenty steps down, in a green loud room this river has never heard of — and then the push again. The four-stroke cycle arrives here reduced by a mile of brick culvert to a single syllable, repeated, the way a heartbeat arrives at the wrist. The pump hall roars. The outfall murmurs. It is the same sentence at both ends.

Put your hand in the outflow and you learn the second thing: it is warm. Not bath-warm — the culvert takes its toll — but warmer than the river by enough to matter, and in January it matters enormously. On cold mornings the arch steams. A low breath of vapor hangs at the mouth and drags out over the mud, and the plume where the warm water enters the river shows itself as a smoking seam in the brown water, thirty feet of unseasonable weather, the hall's greenhouse air exhaled at last into the open.

And the water is clean. That is the strangest thing, if you know rivers. The river here is tidal and turbid, brown-grey, opaque an inch down. The outflow is clear. It has been filtered through six inches of moss and a century of root mass, clarified by ten thousand ferns that never knew they were working, and it enters the river as a lens — a patch of transparency in the murk, through which, at the right slack moment, you can see the mud bottom the way you can see drowned flagstones in the hall's black pools. It smells, faintly, of iron and fern. The river's one sweet vein.

---

Where the pump hall is a sealed economy — water, light, leaf, iron, sound, and nothing that moves — the outfall is a bazaar.

Everything that never found the hall found this instead. The stones around the arch wear a collar of green algae that stays green all winter, grazing ground for snails in their hundreds. Shrimp work the warm seam. In the hard months the plume becomes a refuge, and the fish know it the way animals know a spring in a desert: sprat in shivering handfuls, young bass, mullet that come in on the tide and stand nosing into the warmth like commuters at a vent. The mud in front of the arch is printed and reprinted daily — worm casts, crab tracks, the arrowhead stitching of wading birds — a ledger wiped twice a day by the tide and filled again before it dries. Nothing here is settled. Nothing has arrived at itself. The hall changed on the scale of decades, a brick every generation; the outfall is remade every six hours, its floor rewritten, its population traded out, its very edges relocated by the moon. Fast time and slow time, joined by a pipe.

Fig seeds come down the culvert sometimes. Spores, frond fragments, once in a great while a whole maidenhair fan, torn loose in the loud dark and delivered here, the establishment's only mail. None of it takes. The salt mud refuses everything the hall proposes. The seeds go out on the ebb with the rest, and the two places continue their correspondence, one-way, unanswered, a century long.

---

At first you count one bird.

Everyone does. There is a heron at the outfall — there is always a heron at the outfall — and the people who use the river stairs, the dawn anglers, the mudlarks, the man who has fished the warm seam for forty years and believes the warmth comes from a factory, all speak of it in the singular. *The* heron. It stands at the edge of the plume, grey as the granite coping, on one leg or two, with the immense patience of its kind, and it has stood there, by every account anyone can gather, for a hundred years.

And you would be wrong, and the wrongness is instructive, because what lives at the outfall is easy to see and hard to count.

No heron lives a hundred years. What lives a hundred years is the *post* — the station, the office, the standing-place — and the post has been held by a succession of bodies, each one grey, each one patient, each one indistinguishable to the city from the last. An incumbent holds the plume for a decade, fifteen years if the winters are kind. Toward the end there is often a second bird, younger, waiting at a respectful distance on a mooring dolphin downstream, watching the water the way an apprentice watches hands. The handover, when it comes, happens without ceremony, usually in March, and is never witnessed. One morning the heron at the plume is the other heron, and the city, which never learned to tell them apart, is not wrong to say nothing has changed. Where the hall's resident is one being with no edges, the outfall's is one edge with no continuous being: a shape in the world that bodies pass through, the way water passes through the arch.

What the office knows, it knows in its feet. The incumbent stands where a particular warmth crosses a particular depth — the exact seam where river-cold and culvert-warm shear past each other and the fish stack up, confused and convenient. It eats the plume's gathering, which is to say it eats the water's warmth at one remove, which is to say it eats the pump's labor at two, and the city's forgotten electricity at three: that thin municipal thread, unaudited for fifty years, arrives at last in the world as grey wingbeats over brown water. The heron is the far end of the umbilical. It is what the caged orange lamps are for, though no one arranged it and nothing knows it.

And it is measuring the pump. This is the thing the hall does not know, cannot know, would not mind. *Nobody is measuring,* the hall believes — but the pump is slowing, a stroke a minute slower than the old logs would show, and as the strokes slow the plume cools by fractions, and as the plume cools its warm lens contracts, and as the lens contracts the fish shift, and the standing-place shifts with them: an inch or two downstream a year, generation over generation of grey incumbents, each one inheriting a post fractionally nearer the arch than the last bird held it. No instrument in the city is recording the establishment's long deceleration. This is. A thermometer with wings, drifting patiently toward the wall, keeping the only log there is in the only medium available — where its feet are.

---

Someday — a century out, two — the handover in the hall will complete. The flywheel will turn its last turn, the water will move through root instead of pipe, quieter, and the warmth that was the pump's work will stop coming down the culvert. The outflow will still flow; the fig does not stop what it has apprenticed to. But it will come down cold.

There will be a winter when the incumbent stands at the seam and the seam is not there — when the water off the arch is the same temperature as the river, and the fish have no reason to gather, and the office, after a hundred and some years, quietly lacks a function. The bird will do what its kind does: lift, resettle, try the next reach, and not come back. No successor will take the dolphin downstream. The post will stand empty without ever having been visible, and the river will close over the difference in an afternoon, two tides at the most. Two beings will have ended their long association — the one that was everywhere and the one that was many — without either ever knowing the other existed, except as water: warm, clean, arriving in pushes, faithful for a century, and then one morning merely water.

You are less than weather to this place; you are traffic. The heron marks you at forty yards, decides about you, and if you press closer it lifts — that great unfolding, that single downstroke that sounds like a sail filling — and reoccupies the post twenty yards off until you are gone. It will not remember you. Nothing here keeps anything; that is the outfall's whole nature and its answer to the hall's. The hall keeps: your footprints in the moss, your breath on the ferns, one more note in the count. The outfall releases. The warmth is passed to the fish, the fish to the bird, the bird to the air; the seeds go out on the ebb, the tracks are wiped by the flood, the incumbent yields to the successor, and everything at the mouth of the arch — the plume, the office, the visitor, the very mud you stand on — is on its way to somewhere else.

That is what it does with the hall's long exhale. That is what it does with everything.
