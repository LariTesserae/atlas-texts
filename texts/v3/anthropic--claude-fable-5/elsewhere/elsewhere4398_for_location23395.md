# The Tailrace

Two hundred miles east of the worn hills, and a long way down, the pale stone country finally gives out. It breaks off in a grey limestone bluff above real land — soil, weather, trees that have opinions about the wind — and at the foot of that bluff there is a mouth in the rock, and out of the mouth a river is born already grown.

It does not trickle. It arrives in measures.

Stand on the bank for an hour and the sequence reveals itself, the way it does everywhere the water has been: a doubled surge that shoulders up out of the dark, then a long thin cord of flow, taut and even, then two heavy gouts that you feel in the bank through the soles of your feet before you see them, then a slow brimming-over, thick and deliberate, like something swallowing — and back to the top. No drift. No variation. Anyone who has ever camped on the rim of the Foundry Flats, leaning close to be heard over the roar, stands at this riverbank and goes very quiet, because they know the piece. It is the basin's shift, note for note — thump-hiss, whistle, knock, knock, swallow — played on water instead of steam, at a whisper instead of a full industrial scream, and eleven days late.

Eleven days is the crossing. The rain that vanishes into the warm rock of the flats goes down into the dark of the stone and walks the whole distance underground, and somewhere in that passage it gives up its heat — pays it back into the work, presumably; nothing upstream ever runs short — because it comes out of the mouth cold enough to ache the teeth. The heat does not survive the journey. The rhythm survives everything. A great storm over the basin arrives here as a fatness in the thin cord of flow, eleven days later, to the beat; travelers' diaries kept on the rim have been checked against the records kept at the mouth, and the interval has never varied by so much as an evening. The water is a letter that always takes exactly as long to arrive, written in a hand that never changes, from a correspondent who does not know it is writing.

Below the mouth, strung down both banks, is the town, and the town is everything the basin is not.

It is wooden and stone and crooked. It has been added onto, burned in one corner, rebuilt at an angle nobody planned, argued over in a records-hall that leaks. It grows in good years and thins in bad ones and has never once held the same number of souls from one season's counting to the next. It has weather — real weather, gold evenings and mean sleet and mornings when the river fog comes up sweet and mineral, the last ghost of somewhere warm. And it has, above all, erosion still in progress: the water here has not finished its argument with the stone and never intends to. The channel cuts deeper year on year. The steps down to the water have been rebuilt downward three separate times in living memory, and the oldest doors in town, the ones that used to open at the waterline, now open onto empty air a story up, and are left there, unhinged, as a kind of civic joke. Upstream, the place that made this river keeps time. The Tailrace is where time gets spent.

The water pays the town's way twice over. First, the mill: a broad wooden wheel set in the current that turns the way nothing else in the world turns — lurching, syncopated, hauled around by the doubled surge, coasting through the cord, kicked twice by the gouts. Every millstone in town grinds in the basin's meter. Children here learn to count on it before they learn numbers; the town's work songs are all built on the same limping figure, and its lullabies are the same figure slowed down; there is not a clock in the whole settlement, because the river is the clock — eleven days behind the true face, everyone knows, and never once wrong.

Second, the color. The surges carry tint — fine mineral threads of ochre, of bone-white, of a green like old copper — shed from somewhere far upstream and delivered cold and swirling into the settling pools the dyers have cut into the bank. Cloth steeped in those pools takes colors no dyer anywhere can mix and no sun can fade, and the bolts go out by cart down the east road and are worn at weddings and funerals in cities that have never heard the knock and never will. This is the town's trade and its modest fame, and it means something the Tailrace has never quite put into words: that the vast roaring workshop in the west, the one famous for producing nothing, ships after all. Its heat spent, its water timed, its livery bottled and sold by the yard. The Foundry Flats has a loading dock. It is here, and it has never been told.

The water is also news, and the tellers are the ones who read it.

There have always been tellers — three or four in a generation, keeping the ledgers in the spring-house built over the mouth, noting the flow measure by measure in a notation their predecessors invented so long ago that its origins are a shrug. To an outsider the ledgers are madness: shelves of them, centuries deep, recording a piece of music that never changes. But the tellers know what the outsider doesn't, which is that it *almost* never changes, and that the almost is everything. A hiccup in the cord. A gout arriving heavy. A grace note in the swallow, small as a held breath. Each one means something happened upstream, eleven days ago, in a place most tellers will never see, and the ledgers have mapped the correspondences without ever learning the causes. There is a note they call the *plus-one* — an extra turn in the swallowing measure — and a note they call the *setting*, thin and fading over weeks; the tellers know the two always balance in the long run, arrival for departure, and have known it for centuries, and would be unsurprised but very quiet to learn what is being born in the grey mud and what is fading into pale stone to make their books come out even. There is also, threaded through every ledger ever kept, a recurring family of small signatures — brief pressure-flutters, each one distinct, each one returning on its own long circuit of days. The tellers have counted them. There are two hundred and seventeen. They call them *the workers*, affectionately, the way you name the knots in a familiar floor, and they have never suspected how right they are.

Once in a long while the water delivers more than news. Out of the mouth, riding a surge, comes a small stone object the length of a hand — flat, blunt-headed, eyeless, something between a salamander and a question, gone entirely to pale mineral. The town calls them *shuttles*, as in spent ones, and does not eat, sell, or discard them; they are set into the inner wall of the spring-house, facing the water, and there are some hundreds of them now, rows of them, a congregation of the retired. The superstition is that they hear better than the living do. Given where they served their term, this is probably not superstition.

In the cliff above the mouth, the birds nest — the same high grey crossers that pass over the flats once or twice a season without landing, without so much as a tilt of acknowledgment. Here they are ordinary: they raise their racketing young in the ledges, drink in the pause between gouts, and leave westward when the season turns, over the pale country, over the basin, indifferent to it in both directions. They are the only living line strung between the two places, and they treat the whole arrangement as beneath comment. The town's oldest story says the first walker knew which way to go by watching them leave.

Because the Tailrace pays for its water, and this is the coin.

Once in a lifetime, more or less — the ledgers list every instance, going back as far as the ledgers go — someone in the town hears the beat arrive *without* its eleven days. Not louder. Just on time: the two heavy knocks landing in their bones in the same instant they land, far away, in the warm stone of the basin, as if the distance had been quietly waived in their case. They set down what they are doing. This is always how it is described, by everyone who has watched it happen: a dyer with her hands still in the green, a miller mid-sack, going still between one beat and the next, and then moving again — but *on* the beat now, already — and gathering nothing, and walking west up out of the valley into the pale country.

Nobody stops them. The town learned long ago what the far basin knows about unscheduled events, which is that there aren't any. Instead the ritual is this: the walker's own cup is filled from the mouth, cold, and set on the spring-house sill among the shuttles, and it waits. The walk west takes eleven days, or near enough that the rhyme cannot be accident. And some days after that, the tellers find it in the water — an evenness returning to the gouts, a settledness in the swallow, the note the ledgers mark as *the count made whole* — and the cup is emptied back into the river, and the name is entered in the walkers' ledger, and the town, which does not keep constant numbers about anything else, keeps this one perfectly: it has never owed two at once, and it has never owed none.

Evening at the Tailrace is loud, but the noise is human — lamps going up, the mill's lurching turn, argument and supper and someone's syncopated song coming off the water. Below it all, quieter than any of it, the mouth keeps arriving: surge, cord, gout, gout, swallow, the day's dispatch from the far end of the stone, cold and certain, telling everyone what already happened eleven days ago in a place that is always mid-shift. The town spends it — grinds with it, dyes with it, counts its children by it — running eleven days behind the only true clock in the world, as always, and alive.

And on some evenings, on the spring-house sill, facing the water, in the front row of the congregation: one full cup, waiting out its eleven days, keeping an appointment.
