The sink.

You find it by the trails. The flats are written all over with them — thin pale lines worn into the crust by generations of feet and hooves and bellies, and every one of them, followed far enough in the right direction, bends toward the same point, so that seen from any height the land looks like a shattered pane with the sink at the impact. Days east of the basin, across ground that carries no water on its surface and never has, the trails converge, and the crust darkens, and a stripe of true green appears — reeds, sedge, a single crooked tree of great age — and then the ground opens, and there it is.

It is a pool, lens-shaped, perhaps twenty meters at its longest, held in a fissure of old grey limestone — old the way the basin's rock is young, rounded, patient, licked smooth at the waterline by ten thousand years of mouths. The water is dark from a distance and transparent up close, so clear that depth becomes a rumor: you can see the fissure walls converging below you, grey going to blue going to a blue that is only politeness on the way to black, and at the bottom of the visible world, the throat. An opening in the rock, smooth-lipped, wide enough to take a cart. The water goes down it. All of it, always, forever.

You would not know. That is the first thing about the sink, and the thing visitors mention quietly, afterward: the taking is invisible. The surface lies like set glass. There is no gurgle, no swirl you could point to, no sound of drainage at all — the water enters the rock full-bore and laminar, without turbulence, the way a well-made thing runs. The only evidence is a dimple at the deep end, a depression in the surface the size of a fingertip, where the water stands fractionally lower than itself. That, and a rotation: the whole pool turns, once every several minutes, a movement you detect only by watching a floating leaf and doubting your own eyes. The sink is drinking at a rate that would empty it in an afternoon, and refilling from seeps in the fissure walls at exactly that rate, and the balance is so perfect that the pool appears to be doing nothing at all. It is doing everything. It is simply doing it without commentary.

And where the basin is the loudest place on the flats, the sink is the quietest — not silent, but quiet the way a concert hall is quiet, quiet as a made condition. The fissure shelters it from the wind. The water swallows echo. Sounds here arrive singly and complete: one beetle crossing gravel, one drop falling from the reed-shade, the click of a bird's foot on stone. People who have sat here for an hour report that their hearing seems to improve — that it does not improve, rather, but is finally being paid what it is owed. The basin erases the acoustic world. The sink is where the acoustic world is kept.

The water is cold. Not cool — cold, a few degrees above freezing, in high summer, at noon, unchanging, the mirror of the basin's unchanging heat. And it is empty. Taste it and you will spend a moment confused, because it tastes of nothing so completely that the nothing acquires a flavor — a mineral silence, water with all its cargo holds swept clean. This is not purity in the ornamental sense. It is capacity. The water goes down the throat unfreighted because it is on its way to be loaded; it will do its dissolving below, in the dark, in the heat, and it will surface again — days east and years later — roaring out of an iron collar in a white column that never varies, carrying the silica and sulfur and iron it picked up en route, and it will spend that freight building terraces, shelf on shelf, a millimeter a season. The sink and the basin are the two ends of one breath. Here the inhale; there the exhale; between them, under the flats, the throat and the lungs and the engine, and whatever it is the engine does to the water in passing — that is the task. The two places are its lips. Nobody has seen its face.

At the pool's edge, cut into a natural shelf of the limestone, there is a mark: a horizontal groove, finger-deep, dressed smooth, very old. The water's surface kisses it. Not near it — kisses it, to the width of the meniscus. Rain comes to the flats, rarely, torrentially, and the mark and the water kiss. Drought comes, years of it, and the mark and the water kiss. The intake adjusts. The sink holds its level with the same painted-flame fixity as the basin holds its pressure, and for the same unexplained reason, and someone — long before the drillers, long before iron — noticed this, and thought it worth a mark, and worth watching.

It is still watched.

Begin with the truce, because it is the first thing you will see and the last thing you will understand.

Everything on the flats that lives by listening comes here, because everything must drink, and the sink is the only water. So the trails converge, and at the waterline the whole anxious economy of the flats suspends itself: the dog-shaped things and the hare-shaped things drink within a body's length of each other, ears working, eyes averted, observing the old law of the waterhole, which is not mercy but bookkeeping — nothing can afford a fight at the only well. Doves land in fleets at dusk. Swifts nest in the upper fissure walls and stitch the air above the pool all day, drinking on the wing, touching the glass surface with a tick you can hear from thirty paces, because here you can hear everything. The basin, deaf and roaring, was left to lineages older than ears. The sink, quiet and listening, is the capital of the hearing world — its parliament, its truce-ground, the one room on the flats where attention is safe to relax because attention works so well. Every creature that routes around the rising vapor of the basin routes toward this water instead. The two places sort the world between them and have never been introduced.

In the water itself, at the threshold of the throat, live the sink's own tenants: small pale crustaceans, translucent, eyeless, that drift up from the dark on no schedule anyone has found and hang in the blue like punctuation. They are blind the way the basin's mats are deaf — not deprived but prior, descended from stock that went below before eyes were worth the expense, kin to whatever lives further down the plumbing than anything surfaces to report. They are the only beings known to travel any distance into the throat and return. What they do down there, how far they go, whether they go all the way — whether some ancestral cousin of theirs grazes the hot end of the circuit among the mustard nations of the basin, making the round trip a matter not of individuals but of ten million generations — is not known. They come up. They hang in the light they cannot see. They go down. They are the sink's rainbow: punctual by their own clock, resident in the way a thought is resident.

And there is the keeper.

She lives in the dry-stone house above the fissure's south lip, one room, a hearth, a wall of ledgers. There has been a keeper at the sink for as long as there have been ledgers, and the ledgers go back further than any other continuous record on the flats — further than the gauge-house dial, further than iron. The office descends by teaching, not blood, though it has often been blood too. The work is this: each morning she goes down the worn stair to the shelf, kneels at the mark, and looks. Then she climbs back and writes the date, and the word that means at the mark, and the weather, and — in a final column headed with a word that means otherwise — almost always nothing.

Almost always. The ledgers, read end to end, are thousands of identical entries, and this is not futility; it is the entire point. The record's sameness is the record. It is the longest instrument reading in the world, and what the instrument reads is the constancy of the thing below, from the intake side, in a hand that changes every few decades as one keeper's script gives way to the next. She knows what she is: the surface's answer to the engine. A process running at fixed pitch, provisioned by habit, partway through a task measured in spans she will not see the end of, and untroubled by that, the way the engine is untroubled. She and it keep the same kind of time from opposite sides of the rock. She would not put it that way. She would say she checks the mark.

The otherwise column has been written in living memory exactly once, and it concerns the basin. Her grandmother's hand: an entry recording that surveyors had come from the west, that they had asked to read the ledgers, that they had been lent the ledgers and had sat up three nights with them doing arithmetic. The arithmetic was simple and enormous: this much water, down this throat, for at least this many centuries, must go somewhere, and must come up changed. The surveyors drew their line east across the flats, found the heat shadow at the end of it, and drilled. The borehole, the collar, the roar — all of it descends from those three nights and this pool and the sameness of these books. The sink is the basin's memory in the most literal way available: the basin exists as a made place, with its iron mouth, because the sink was watched, and written down, and believed.

Her grandmother's entry ends with the only judgment the keepers have ever passed on the affair. In the days after the drilling — after a bit chewed down somewhere far to the west and punctured whatever envelope holds the thing below — the water at the mark did not move. Not a meniscus. The pinprick that visitors to the basin wonder about was measured here, at the intake, by the finest instrument on the flats, and the measurement was: nothing. Beneath notice, or noticed and found to change nothing that mattered. The ledger declines to speculate. At the mark, says the next entry, and the next, and all of them since.

One custom remains to be mentioned, because it is the sink's answer to the bootprint. People come here — have always come here — to give the water something small. A coin. A smooth stone with a name scratched on it. The oldest keepers' ledgers record the practice without explaining it, which means it predates the ledgers. You hold the thing, and you say or do not say whatever you came to say, and you let it go over the deep end, and it falls through the blue, shrinking, and enters the throat, and is gone. There is no ripple. Nothing given to the sink has ever come back or been found below, and this is understood to be the point. The basin keeps by entombing: it sheathes what stays in white stone and holds it in place forever, a fossil record of everything that ever touched it. The sink keeps by removing: it takes what it is given out of the world entirely, without haste, without receipt. Two ways of remembering, or one way of remembering and one way of being relieved. The flats' people have always known which errand they were on, and walked accordingly — west with what they wanted kept, east with what they wanted carried away.

Whether the names and the coins arrive anywhere — whether they fetch up in some mineral eddy of the plumbing, or feed the freight, or pass through the engine's attention the way the eyeless shrimp presumably do, unremarked — is not a question the keeper entertains. She has held the instrument's other reading in her hands: a recording, brought once by a traveler, of the basin's roar, its waveform of near-mechanical regularity. She set it beside the intake measurements a curious predecessor once took with a float and a clock. The two traces, laid side by side, are one trace. The same fixed period, the same composure, the inhale and the exhale of a single unhurried breather, days of flats apart at the lips and no distance apart at all in the chest. She keeps the traveler's recording in the wall with the ledgers. In the otherwise column, for that day, there is nothing. It changed nothing at the mark.

So: what lives there. The whole hearing world, on loan, twice a day at dawn and dusk, under truce. Swifts in the walls, keeping hours. Blind pale commuters at the mouth of the throat, keeping depths. One human lineage, keeping the count — the only people on the flats whose vocation, like the engine's, has never once been interrupted and sees no reason to expect interruption now. And below all of it the same landlord as the basin's, felt here not as roar but as appetite: patient, provisioned, partway through, drinking the cold empty water at a rate tuned to the meniscus, and paying it back, days east, as heat and stone and that one sustained note.

You will want to drink before you leave. Everyone does; the animals have made it seem so reasonable. The cold of it lands in your teeth and stands there, a clear ringing absence, the exact inverse of the note the basin leaves in your sternum — and if you have stood in both places, you will notice, on the walk out, that you are carrying one of each. The exhale in your chest, the inhale in your jaw, and the flats going by under your feet like the long held breath between them. Whatever you sweat or spill out here will sink into the crust and find its way, in a season or a century, down some seep to the fissure, to the pool, to the mark, to the throat. You drank from the current; the current will collect. The sink does not host visitors either. It waters them, and writes nothing in the otherwise column, and lets them go — for now — the way a lender lets you go.
