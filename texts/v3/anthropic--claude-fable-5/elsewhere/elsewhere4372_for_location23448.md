# The Winnowry

You come down to it along the river, because the river is the only thing that goes there.

The gorge road follows the water out of the mountain's lee side, and somewhere around the last bend the cloud that has been shredding overhead for an hour simply ends — ends the way a country ends, at a border — and the light hits you. Not brighter light. *Edged* light. Light with a direction and an opinion, light that makes every pebble cast a small hard twin of itself, light that lays your own shadow out on the rock beside you like a traveling companion you did not agree to. People who come down from the fog city talk about this moment for the rest of their lives. Most of them turn around.

Ahead, the land falls open. The mountain wrings the wind on its far side — takes the water out of the air the way a fist takes water out of a rag — and everything downwind of it gets what is left, which is nothing. A basin of ochre and bone-grey and glare, ringed by hills so dry they have kept their exact shape for ten thousand years, floored with hardpan and gravel and, at the far southern edge, the long pale shimmer of the dunes. It never rains here. The oldest annals — and these are a people of annals — record four rains in six hundred years, each one named, each one sung.

And down the middle of all that dryness, out of the mouth of the gorge, runs the river.

It comes out cold. It comes out *white* — milk-white, lime-white, clouded with the fine stone flour of the mountain's mortar, so that the first thing the desert ever sees of the city above is the city itself, dissolved and traveling. The people of the basin call the river, approximately, *what the mountain lets go*; outsiders shorten it to the Letting. It does not behave like other rivers because it is not fed like other rivers. It does not flood in spring and fail in autumn. It is the runoff of a machine — the overflow of ten thousand fog-fences and cisterns and weeping walls — and so it runs with a steadiness that no rain-fed water has ever had, rising and falling by inches, not feet, breathing gently with the weather of a cloud its drinkers have never stood inside. It is the most reliable river in the world, and it is entirely artificial, and nobody made it on purpose.

A few miles out of the gorge, the land flattens and the Letting loses its nerve. It spreads. It braids. It opens like a hand — a fan of channels twenty miles across, silver threads through green threads through gold, and this fan is the Winnowry: the inhabited world, the whole of it, a country shaped exactly like the river's indecision. Everything alive in the basin lives on the fan. Fields of silt in long combed rows. Date palms and flood-grass and low mud-walled orchards. Villages of pale brick strung along each channel like beads on a wet string. And at the fan's fringes, where the last of the water gives up — the pans. Miles of them. Blinding white, dead level, crusted with salt and soda, crackling audibly at dusk as the day's crystals set, and beyond them nothing at all, and beyond the nothing, the dunes.

The river never reaches a sea. There is no sea to reach. It ends in the pans and it ends in the fields and mostly it ends in the sky — hauled back up out of the ground and the pools and the open channels by the sun, all day, every day, a whole river's worth of water climbing invisibly into the air. On still evenings you can see it go: a low silver haze lifting off the pans, leaning northward on the night wind, streaming away toward the horizon. And on the horizon, always, in the one fixed place, the only cloud these people ever see: the long white bank that never moves and never breaks, sitting on the mountain like a held breath. They call it the Standing Cloud. Most of them have never seen the stone under it. They do not need to. They know exactly what is up there, because it comes down to them every day, dissolved, in the milk of the river — and they will tell you, flatly, as a matter of hydrology and not of faith, that the haze off their pans is what the Standing Cloud is made of. That the mountain drinks their breath. That the river is theirs, coming home.

The mountain people, if asked, would shrug. They do not do beliefs. Both peoples consider themselves upstream of the other, and the maddening thing, the true thing, is that both are right.

Here is the strange thing about the Winnowry — the thing that takes a visitor weeks to understand, the exact inverse of the mountain's strangeness. Up there, nothing lasts and nothing is lost. Down here: *everything lasts, and nothing stays.*

The dryness preserves without mercy. Nothing rots on the fan — nothing has ever rotted on the fan. A wooden beam is immortal. A basket outlives its maker's great-grandchildren. A sandal dropped at the fan's edge four hundred years ago is still there, still a sandal, curling slightly, waiting. The Winnowers own almost nothing new; they own old things, superbly kept, oiled and mended and inherited — looms, chests, horns, knives with handles polished to glass by ten generations of the same family's grip — and they regard the mountain habit of grinding a wall back into gravel with something close to physical distress. Things, here, are permanent.

Places are not. The river moves. That is the fan's whole nature: silt chokes a channel, the water shoulders sideways, and a distributary that ran for eighty years goes dry in a season while a new one opens a mile east. The Winnowers do not fight this. They have tried; the annals sing about the trying, in the tone reserved for famous follies. Instead, they follow. A village on a dying channel takes itself apart — every immortal beam, every door, every seated ancestor — loads itself onto carts, and walks to where the water went, and stands itself back up. There are houses on the fan whose timbers are five centuries old and have stood in forty different places. Ask a Winnower how old her house is and she'll tell you the age of the beams. Ask her how long it has stood *here* and she has to think. The house was never the location. The house is the beams. The place is just where the house is currently standing.

The sound of the fan is the loud, carrying, honest sound of dry country. Nothing here muffles anything. The air is thin and clean and hard, and sound travels down it like water down a channel: a dog's bark arrives from an hour's walk away, small and perfect; a shout carries village to village; the long horns they sound at dusk can be heard from one side of the fan to the other. Sight, by contrast, lies. Past a certain distance the heat shimmer takes everything — stretches a walking man into a wading giant, floats the villages off the ground, lays sheets of false water across the hardpan — and so the Winnowers have arranged their trust accordingly. The mountain people believe their hands. The fan people believe their ears. Their word for a liar is their word for a mirage.

The animals come to the water, and the water is generous, so the fan swarms in a way that would stun a mountain resident into silence. Clouds of birds over every channel at dawn. The pans, which look like death, are the liveliest place in the basin: the brine breeds a tiny red shrimp in the trillions, and the shrimp feed the stilt-birds — tall, deliberate, blush-pink from their diet, standing in the shallows in congregations of thousands, and their arrival each year is the fan's great calendar-mark, sung in, danced in. And in the flood pools among the fields lives the creature the Winnowers love the way the mountain loves its salamanders: a finger-long fish whose eggs can wait in dry mud for years — decades, the annals claim — for water to find them. The patience fish. Where a new channel wanders, the fish hatch behind it within days, out of ground that has been dust since anyone's grandmother; the fish are the water's testimony, the map of where the river truly went as opposed to where the shimmer says it went. Children walk the new margins counting hatches and come back with reports of preposterous precision, and the adults take them with complete seriousness. They are usually right.

(The birds eat the fish, mud and eggs and all, and fly where they like, including up the gorge, over the fences, into the fog. Mention to a Winnower that the mountain city has pale fish in its deepest cisterns that nobody introduced and nobody can explain, and you will get the fan's easy carrying laugh: *those are ours. The birds deliver everything eventually. Tell them the eggs waited in the dark and hatched. Tell them that's just what our fish do.*)

One thing on the fan the Winnowers cannot account for, and it quiets them, these loud people, the way the deep cisterns quiet the mountain folk: the dunes sing. On certain hot afternoons the great pale slopes at the basin's southern edge groan and boom — a low note you feel in your sternum, sourceless, minutes long. The people who trust sound above all things are owned, at their border, by a sound with no thrower. They do not go there much. When they must, they lower their voices, which is, for them, a kind of prayer.

**Their bodies first**, because the sun has written itself into them as the fog wrote itself into the others.

They are burnt-brown and creased, squint-built, their eyes seamed at the corners from a lifetime of glare — and those eyes are the far-sighted opposite of the mountain's: weak up close, needling at any distance, able to pick a walking figure off the shimmer at the edge of the world and tell you, from the gait alone, whose cousin it is. They read light the way the mountain reads dampness. Their language has dozens of words for kinds of edge, kinds of glare, the exact quality of a shadow at each hour — and is nearly empty of words for wet, so that caravaners returning from the mountain struggle to describe it and end up saying, helplessly, *it is like being drunk by something*.

Their voices are their hands. From childhood they are trained to throw sound — the flat-palmed hail that carries a mile, the pitched call that names its target, and above all the far tongue, the whistled speech that rides the dry air from one side of the fan to the other, by which a channel-string coordinates a flood, a family argues across three fields, a girl on the east margin courts a boy on the west without either of them taking a step. Everything they say aloud, everyone hears; the fan has almost no privacy and has decided, over centuries, to make of this a virtue. Their worst insults are all words for hiddenness. Their highest compliment, applied equally to farmers, singers, mothers, and the dead, translates roughly as *you could hear them from the far bank*.

They move the way the mountain people move — with total deliberateness — but for the opposite master. Up there, wasted motion kills you on wet stone. Down here, wasted motion is wasted water; you sweat it out and the sun takes it, and so they have the same economy of gesture, the same unhurried speed, arrived at from the other direction, and when caravaners from the two peoples meet at the cloud line they recognize it in each other instantly, this family resemblance conferred not by blood but by scarcity.

**They are organized the way the river is organized** — not in circuits, but in strings. A string is the line of households along one distributary, upstream to downstream, bound by water order: who opens their gate when, who floods after whom, who waits. Identity is your channel and your place along it; a Winnower introducing herself gives her name, her string, and her position — third gate, west branch — the way a mountain resident gives a crew and a round. Governance is the strings shouting at each other, literally, ritually, across open ground, allocation argued at full carrying volume with the whole fan as audience, and it is amiable precisely because it is so loud: nothing decided in a shout can be a conspiracy. Their one cold anger, their unforgivable thing, is *drinking ahead* — taking water out of turn, robbing everyone downstream of you. Their word for it is their word for theft, and their word for theft is quiet.

**Their relationship to time is the mountain's, turned inside out.** The fog people keep pattern and surrender material; the fan people keep material and surrender position. Their dead are not composted — the idea appalls them — their dead are *seated*: dried in the dry that preserves everything, wrapped, and set upright in niches cut into the gorge cliffs at the head of the fan, facing downstream, facing the water and the fields and the living, generations deep, visited, consulted, sung to. A person here does not go out of the round. A person takes their seat. And because the land keeps erasing *where* — because channels wander and villages walk and no place stays a place — their memory lives in voices and kept objects rather than in walls: the sung annals, nights long, that hold every flood, every move, every rain by name, back to the beginning. They are, without irony, the historians of the mountain too. The fog city keeps no chronicle of itself — ask it about its founding and you get shrugs — but the fan has been watching the Standing Cloud and questioning caravaners and singing it all down for five hundred years, and if the residents ever want to know what happened to them, they will have to come down and ask the people who eat their silt.

Because that is what the silt is. The Winnowers know it. The mountain composts its dead into growing medium, and the growing medium washes, grain by grain, over decades, down the weeping walls and the gorges and the Letting, and settles on the fields of the fan, and the fan eats it. They say it plainly, without shudder, as a fact of kinship: *we eat their dead; they drink our breath.* Two peoples who have mostly never met, joined at the metabolism.

**And there is one wall on the fan**, only one that matters, and it is made of someone else's memory. The river brings things down — zinc beads, gutter-iron flakes, once a whole waxed glove, and, a few times a generation, a fragment of carved and leaded stone: a piece of the mountain's wall-logs, demolition rubble that escaped the grinding houses and made the long trip in the white water. The Winnowers cannot fully read the marks. They keep every fragment anyway, dry-laid — nothing here needs mortar; nothing here rains — in a long low wall at the head of the fan, below the seated dead, each stone oriented as found. The Reading Wall. Once, the annals sing, a mason came down with the salt caravan to see it, and found in the third course the marks of the woman whose place in the round she had inherited, dead eighty years, and passed her palm along the stone in the trailing-hand way her people have, and asked in a murmur they all had to lean into whether she might repoint it — and they told her nothing here needs mortar, nothing here will ever fall — and she stood in front of the wall for a long time, they sing, like a person listening to a channel run a half-note wrong.

**What passes between the two places passes on backs.** The caravans go up the switchbacks — the same road you came down — carrying the one thing the wet city cannot make and cannot live without: keeping. Salt from the pans, by the sack, because a city where nothing dries can preserve nothing without it; the fog city, which cannot keep anything, buys keeping itself. Soda for the glass houses' own use — and glass, the fan's other gift, blown at the fan's edge from dune sand and pan soda and tinted amber by the iron in the silt, which is the mountain's own gutter-iron, come down dissolved. Every shaded amber lamp glowing through the fog up there is desert glass, colored by the city's returned metal, carried up through the cloud wrapped in the wool that will come down as payment. The Winnowers say they send the city its sunlight in installments. Neither people planned the loop. Only the caravaners see the whole of it, and they don't talk much; they are the ones the wet has gotten into and the dry has gotten into both, and it makes them quiet in the fog and quiet on the fan, at home in neither volume.

They carry one more thing up, and it costs nothing and is not trade. The salamanders that wash into the Letting do not survive the warm water; their small gold-flecked bodies come down to the gorge mouth, and the children retrieve them, and the dry preserves them as it preserves everything, and the caravans carry them home. At the cloud line the caravaners hand them over, and the residents receive them without a word and take them to the composting houses, and no one on either side has ever called this a rite, and it has happened every season for two hundred years.

**A few of them go up**, every generation, and stay — fan-born who reach the fog and find that the silence they distrusted all their lives is, up close, only pace, and stand for the first time in weather you can touch, and never come down. And the mountain's leavers come the other way, and most flee further, out past the basin to the loud dry kingdoms — but some stop on the fan, and the ones who stay all name the same night: the first clear one, when the overcast they had lived inside since birth was three days' walk behind them and the sky came open, and they saw the stars. All of them at once. A person raised where the dimness simply deepens, standing on the hardpan under thirty thousand points of hard-edged light, each one carrying, none of them muffled. Several of these leavers are seated now in the cliffs above the fan, dried and wrapped among the Winnower dead, facing downstream with everyone else — the only mountain faces that ever lasted — and the fan tends them and sings them and considers them, without complication, its own.

On a few mornings a year — hard frost, the wind swung wrong — the Standing Cloud lifts. The whole ridge shows: the green pelt, the glinting ranks of mesh along the crest, the impossible made mountain, bare, for an hour. The fan stops work. They come out of the houses and stand in their fields and look at it the way you might look up at the surface from the sea floor — with interest, with gratitude, without longing — and then, all along the strings, one after another, they sound the long horns toward it. They know it will not arrive. The caravaners have told them: up there, everything carries and nothing carries far; the fog takes a horn at a mile and gives back a murmur. They sound them anyway, the whole fan, minutes of it, a sound meant less to be heard than to be true.

And in the evening the haze rises off the pans as it always does, silver, leaning north on the night wind, a river going home the long way — and somewhere in a newcomer's first year, watching it go, it stops feeling like living beside the water and starts feeling like being the water's way of resting between falls. Which is, the Winnowers will tell you — loudly, gladly, because nothing of theirs is hidden and everything of theirs carries — what living somewhere *is*. They have one verb for it, and it is the same as their verb for *following*, and they are puzzled when a caravaner asks which sense is meant.

It is not the mountain's verb. It has never needed to be. The water answers to both.
