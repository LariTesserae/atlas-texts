**The Quick**

You come down off the tableland by the western rim, because that is where the tableland consents to end. It does not taper. The plateau stops the way a table stops — a two-kilometer escarpment of the same wet slate, sheer as everything up there is sheer — and the first thing you learn about the country below is written on the cliff face itself. Partway down the wall there is a horizontal line, visible from a great distance, straight as a water mark. Above the line the stone is plateau stone: blade-edged, weeping, upholstered in that impossible emerald moss, every arris sharp enough to read by touch. Below the line the same rock is chewed. Honeycombed, ochre with lichens, pocked and softened and rounded, streaked with old collapse. The line is the hem of the hold — the exact altitude at which the engines' custody ends and the world is allowed to resume — and crossing it on the switchback trail is the closest a person can come to watching a jurisdiction change hands. Above the line your footprints are refunded behind you. Below it, your first footprint stays, and you will be surprised — everyone is surprised — by how much the sight of it moves you.

The second thing you learn is that your breath still fogs, but the fog *drifts*. There is wind here. There is weather that was not decided.

Not far along the rim, the plateau's single export goes over the edge. Somewhere in all that continental weeping there is a surplus — the tarns neither rise nor fall because the difference is escorted off the premises — and it leaves as one river, wide and glass-clear, which slides across the last kilometer of moss without cutting it, steps off the escarpment, and falls so far that it arrives at the bottom as rain. The foot of the falls lives inside a permanent standing mist, a weather system the plateau makes for a country it otherwise ignores. The people below call the river the Change, and they mean it in both senses: what is given back, and what is finally permitted. The water comes down with no history in it. It is cold in the specific plateau way, perfectly clear, and it tastes of nothing at all — water that has spent an age being forbidden to do anything, dissolve anything, carry anything, freeze. And the moment it lands it begins to make up for this. Within a kilometer it browns like cut fruit. It takes up silt, insects, weed, color, temperature; it braids and shoves and undermines its banks with what you would swear was appetite. The Change moves its own bed within living memory. There are people in the valley who have farmed both sides of it without ever crossing.

Because that is the third thing, and the true name of the place: the country below the plateau ages for two.

The engines upstairs eat gradients and excrete sameness, and what cannot be made same is sent downstairs. Every winter the plateau refuses, every degree of rounding, every season, every rot, all the ordinary staff of time reporting for work up there and being turned away at the door — all of it comes off the rim along with the water, and the valley people, who conflate the two, are probably right to. Down here the same slate that holds an edge forever up top rounds like sugar. River cobbles come up polished inside a decade. A fencepost goes silver in a year and punky in three. The soil is deep and black and absurdly generous, fattened on doubled rot, and the life in it is everything the plateau's census lacked, at concentrate: insects in weight, birds in nations, fish dimpling water that is allowed to dimple. Where the plateau is a rainforest administered at homeopathic dilution, the Quick — this is what the country is called, and after a week you understand it is not a boast but a plain description — is the same jungle taken at full strength, twice.

And the exported time does not arrive smoothly. It arrives in lumps, in arrears, and the people read it the way people elsewhere read clouds. There are frost mornings out of all season — and the frost is *shaped wrong*. It ferns across the windows in clean conchoidal patterns, in the exact geometry of fracture faces it was never permitted to touch; it blooms on doorsteps in the outline of cushion plants that no one in the Quick has ever seen growing. Families wake to find their windows printed with a country the sleeper has never visited. This is somebody else's frost, they say, and set another log on, and it is. There are rounding days, worse and rarer, when a generation of weathering is paid out through whatever is at hand in a single night: knives dull on their hooks, doorsteps sag, a millstone sighs into uselessness, and the whole valley spends the next week resharpening its life. Arrears weather, they call it, or the Landlord's days. Nobody upstairs is billed. Everybody down here pays.

The Quick is loud, too, but its loudness is the other kind. Birdsong, insect roar, the shoving of the river, storms, hammers, voices — a noise that never once repeats itself, a melody that never comes back around. Except underneath. On the right wind, under everything, you can still hear it: the chord. The plateau's roar carried down the escarpment and across the valley, faint and eternal, the one refrain the country has. Children here are put to sleep by it. When a storm sits wrong and blocks it, the whole valley lies awake.

And the Quick is inhabited — inhabited the ordinary way, with smoke that varies and doors that move and lit windows with faces in them, which after the plateau looks frankly like a miracle. The people age at their own honest speed; it is their *works* that age double. In the Quick you outlive everything you own, twice over. No one is buried early, but everyone attends the funerals of all their things — the house rebuilt three times in a life, the boat, the bridge, the orchard. So they have made a culture out of the split: they build light and remember heavy. Houses are raised in a season, meant for a generation, surrendered without grief. But in every town stands a record-hall, and in the record-halls the true wealth of the Quick is kept — ledgers, surveys, testimony, weather-books, all of it recopied by hand each generation before the paper can fail, copying itself a craft and something close to a sacrament. They will explain it to you simply. Upstairs, nothing is let happen, so nothing needs keeping. Down here everything happens twice and stays for no one. So we keep it ourselves.

And the longest continuous record in the halls — the oldest unbroken measurement in the world, they say, taken every year at the same hour since the founding books — is a single slowly shrinking number: the distance, in strides, from the foot of the escarpment stair to the guest.

**The Guest**

At the foot of the falls, inside the standing mist, one thing in the Quick does not hurry.

It is a figure, human-sized, upright, facing the escarpment. Around it, out to about arm's reach, there is a verge, and inside the verge the Quick is not in force. The moss there is perfect and unchewed. No insect crosses the line. The air within it hangs a half-degree from condensing, cold in the specific plateau way, and the pebbles inside it keep their edges while the pebbles outside it round like sugar. The frost of the arrears has never once touched the guest; the rounding days leave the ground beneath those feet unrounded. The guest wears the hold like a coat — a portable acre of the Estate, carried down here into the middle of all this spending, and the spending gets no purchase on it at all.

Your first thought is that the guest is still, and in the Quick absolute stillness would be the true anomaly — the inverse of your own condition upstairs, where motion was the mark of being at the wrong time. But the guest is not still, and this is what the record-halls exist most deeply to attest. The guest moves. About three meters in a century, by the annual measure — and the table of numbers, copied forward hand to hand through the entire depth of the valley's history, shows the figure crossing the whole floodplain from the far hills: a walk that has consumed everything the Quick has ever written down. The guest spends no doubled time, borrows none of the valley's hurry. The guest travels on one second only — the second, you come to understand, the furnished one, the moment upstairs with the tenant in it — and moves at exactly the speed a held moment moves, which is almost, but not quite, and this is everything, *not quite* zero.

The direction has been unmistakable for most of recorded history, and generations ago the people of the Quick did the one permanent thing they have ever done: they cut a stair into the escarpment, beside the falls, from the valley floor to the hem of the hold. Climbers had gone up — climbers do go up, and come back refunded, their offerings tidied away, remembering the plateau the way one remembers a held breath — and they brought back numbers: the rise of the gantry-stairs in the empty towns, the width of the gridded streets, the height of the constant amber lamps. The valley stair was cut to those measures, on the simple theory that whoever the plateau fits, the stair should fit too. No one living expects to see it used. Everyone repairs it. After every rounding day, when the arrears have gnawed the treads, the masons go out and recut them true. It is the Quick's single act of holding, performed by people who keep nothing else, and if you ask them why, they look at you as if the question were strange. Hospitality, they say, runs downhill too.

The guest is now a few hundred strides from the bottom step.

As for what the guest is — go and look; everyone does; you will stand at the edge of the verge in the falling mist and you will see what every witness in the ledgers has seen, which is the reason no two entries agree. You will see *you, nearly.* The same answer the vacancy upstairs returns to every measurement, given now in the flesh instead of in the fit of the doors. The halls keep four theories, and no fifth has ever been needed. That the guest is the one the plateau was finished for, walking home at the only speed a native of the chosen moment can move. That the guest is the decider itself, locked somehow out of its own furnished second, coming back up the service road. That the guest is the accumulated shape of everyone who ever measured the vacancy and came back *you, nearly* — hospitality so total it finally condensed. And the fourth theory, entered once, very old, recopied every generation without comment: that the guest is the plateau's own future — the single one it did not cancel — returning by the only road it was left.

And so the Quick keeps its oldest argument, which is about the day the number in the table reaches zero. Half the halls hold that when the guest tops the stair, and the doors standing at their convenient angles finally admit the gait they were set for, the engines will at last be allowed to stop — and everything in custody will come downstairs at once. All the frost, all the wind, every held ripple, every ridge's rounded boulder, every death suspended for an age at the top of its verb: a continent's arrears paid out through the valley in an afternoon. They say the records are kept partly for that day — so that afterward, someone will be able to say what everything was like back when it happened only once at a time. The other halls hold that nothing will change at all, except the one thing: that the waiting will be over, and a place that has been finished for so long will finally be *begun*, and no one can say which of those is the mercy.

You will leave before it is settled; everyone does. On your last morning you stand on the valley floor between them — the vacancy above, the guest below, each of them measuring out *you, nearly*, facing each other across a kilometer of falling water, with you in the middle for a little while, exactly nobody's shape, free to go. And walking out down the valley of the Change, with your footprints staying behind you and the chord fading at your back from a sound into a memory of air, the last version comes to you, the way the last version always comes to visitors here — that you have not seen two countries at all. You have seen one breath. The plateau is the top of the inhale, held; the Quick is the cost of holding it, paid out below, doubled, spent; and the guest, crossing the floodplain at three meters a century, is the breath itself deciding — with a patience that makes the engines look hasty — to be let go. The roar you can no longer quite hear is not machinery, and it is not a pulse. It is the sound a world makes waiting to exhale, and downstream of it, in the loud green hurrying country that pays for the silence upstairs, they are keeping the ledgers ready, and mending the stair.
