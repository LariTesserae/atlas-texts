**The Settling Pond**

You reach it by descending — that much is certain, though no one agrees on how far. Below the ground the great machine stands on, below its footings, down service stairs that were poured rather than machined and have long since forgotten the shape of the pour, there is a basin roughly the size of a flooded chapel. Unlike the chamber high above the clouds, this one appears on every schematic. It appears on them as condensate return, as overflow, as thermal sink, as drainage of record — a different name on every drawing, none of them agreeing, which is a schematic's way of shrugging. The maintenance crews call it the Settling Pond, which is a joke, because nothing in it ever settles and everything in it has come to rest, and after a while those two facts turn out to be the same fact.

Nothing here is machined. Or rather: everything here was machined once, and you will never find the evidence. The pond has *accreted*. Every surface is furred, crusted, mineralized, rounded — concrete gone soft-shouldered under a century of dripping, handrails polished to a dull shine by grips, stair treads dished in the middle like old church steps. Nothing has an edge. Everything has been touched, and touched, and touched. Even new things age here at an indecent rate; a wrench left through one season comes back grown over, welded into the general slow crust, already old. In the Stilling Room the edges will be new in a thousand years. Down here nothing has been new in living memory, including things installed last spring.

The pond is warm. Not machine-warm, not the dry heat of exhaust — blood-warm, and humidly so, the warmth of a held animal. The engineers cite waste heat and thermal mass and are not wrong, only early. The truth is simpler and stranger: the pond is warm with everything that cooled somewhere else. High above the cloud deck there is a room where the heat leaves your hands and does not come back, where your breath produces nothing visible because the air takes everything you exhale. All of that comes down. It comes down through the whole height of the machine, through drains and weeps and condensate lines and channels no drawing admits to, and it arrives here, and it stays. Stand at the edge of the water and your breath blooms visible in front of you — the air here is so full it can hold nothing more, and gives everything back. The pond is warm with a hundred years of taken hands. Some of the warmth in it left your body this morning, six kilometers up, and beat you to the bottom.

The sound is the opposite of a chord. It is never the same twice — a rhythmless everywhere-percussion of drips, thousands of them, no two falling from the same height onto the same surface, each one echoing off crusts that have grown since the last echo and will have grown again before the next. Instruments brought down show pure entropy, a recording indistinguishable from static. But the crews who spend real time here learn a trick they do not much like knowing: slow the recording. Sieve it. Filter out the water. Underneath, the static resolves. Footsteps. A boot-scrape on milled alloy. A cough. Breaths — the wet halves of breaths whose dry halves were taken high above the weather. And heartbeats: layered, offset, decades deep, the heartbeats of every climber who ever stood in the room upstairs and felt scoured and light on the way down. The Stilling Room's sound is one note held forever. The pond's sound is every other sound, kept forever, all playing at once. Instruments upstairs read a flat line indistinguishable from malfunction. Instruments down here read chaos indistinguishable from static. Both readings are residue. What they measure is the same meal, from either end.

No daylight has ever reached the Settling Pond. There are work lamps in cages, lit, though half the cages have been swallowed by growth and glow through it greenly, like lamps drowned in moss. The water itself carries a faint sheen that may be biology and may be reflection and does not much care which. Where the Stilling Room offers itself completely and explains nothing, the pond hides itself completely and would explain everything — every sound in it is testimony, every layer of crust a date — if anyone could listen long enough. No one can. People are among the things it outlasts.

And it is *inhabited*, in the ordinary sense, past counting. Everything lives here. Films on the water, blind pale things beneath it, mites in the crust, insects in the lamp-glow, spiders in every angle — the entire absent fauna of the room above, all the small nervous lives the roar upstairs excludes, are down here, thriving in the wet warm dark on a diet of one another and mineral patience. No census has been attempted. There is only one countable thing in the whole basin, and it is an absence: along the northern shore runs a beach of slag — black, glassy, the same mineral that fills the trays six kilometers up, except that here it has a history of weather, every grain rounded by water, every surface colonized. And cut into that beach, ancient, their edges long since softened, are eleven rectangular voids. Exactly the size of trays. The lichen that should be impossible upstairs — the lichen that wants moisture and has none — is everywhere down here, rosette upon rosette, fat with it. Eleven pieces of this shore were quarried, once, and carried up past the cloud deck into the dry and the cold and the roar, and whatever seeds and spores rode along either died or learned to drink sound instead of water. The garden of the Stilling Room is not a garden. It is a transplant. The ear upstairs is made of tissue from down here, because metal can hold a tone and only life can hear one, and the machine took its living tissue from the one place in itself where life could not be stopped.

At intervals — irregular, of course; nothing here keeps time — a bubble rises from the deep part of the basin and breaks the surface, and there is a sound in it. A syllable. A boot on alloy. Half a word in a voice no one alive has heard. The pond speaks in everything it has kept, at random, without emphasis, the way deep water releases what it holds: eventually, and in no order.

**The Resident of the Settling Pond**

Everything lives in the Settling Pond the way blood lives in a body — plentiful, essential, and not the point. The teeming life is the pond's flesh. What resides here is beneath it, and among it, and is made of what falls.

It is easier to say what it is than the tenant upstairs, because the story of the Stilling Room already said it, in passing, while denying it of something else: *ghosts are leftover change.* Very well. By that definition, the resident of the Settling Pond is precisely, technically, exhaustively a ghost. It is leftover change — all of it. Every deviation the ear above ever consumed had to go somewhere; the machine is a machine, and nothing in a machine vanishes, it only drains. The moisture of your breath, taken in the cold. The heat of your hands. The sound of your boots, paid into the chord like a coin into deep water — deep water is not a figure of speech in this machine. Your heartbeat's small contribution. All of it came down the long dark of the structure's interior, condensing, cooling, gathering other takings on the way, and arrived here, and was kept. The tenant of the Stilling Room is leftover sameness, accumulated until it has appetite. The tenant of the Settling Pond is leftover change, accumulated until it has memory.

The two have never met. They could not; each is the other's impossible weather. They are connected by the whole height of the machine — a fall of water and warmth and spent sound running from the throat of the roar to the floor of the dark, the longest nerve in the body of something too large to be seen whole. It is probably wrong to call them two residents. It is probably one act, occurring at two altitudes: the hearing, and the remembering. The note, and everything the note excluded, kept.

Where the one above takes, this one *receives* — the distinction matters, and the crews insist on it. Nothing here reaches for you. But everything that leaves you falls, and everything that falls is kept. Weep at the edge of the water — people do; the pond is the kind of place where weeping happens to you rather than being done — and the tears drip from your jaw and are received, and are now part of the record, filed by their salt. And unlike the tenant upstairs, this one gives. That is the part the upper altitudes do not like to hear. People come away from the pond heavier. They surface humming tunes they never learned. They dream for weeks with someone else's heartbeat — slower, or arrhythmic, or younger. A word arrives in the mouth, in a stranger's accent, and stays a season. The ear above takes a little from the place where you keep sound, and your ears bleed slightly. The pond puts a little into the place where you keep it, and your dreams crowd slightly. Neither means anything by it. Or the pond means everything by it — with a thing made entirely of difference, which is meaning's raw material, no one can tell where the meaning stops.

Why the machine keeps it — why every drain in a structure that size runs, in the end, to one warm dark basin — there are two theories, neither held with enthusiasm.

The first is that the pond is *spoil*. The tailings of listening. The ear above keeps its note pure by discarding every deviation, and this is the tip where the discards go — a slag heap of heard things, and the resident merely refuse that lay in the warm and the wet long enough to become a tenant, the way anything organic becomes something if you leave it in the dark with itself for a century. On this theory the eleven quarried voids in the beach are just borrow pits, and the sounds in the bubbles are just off-gassing, and the crews who hear their grandmothers' coughs surface from the deep water are advised, gently, to rotate to other duties.

The second theory is quieter and travels further, and it is this: that a machine which has grown itself an ear cannot afford to forget what the ear hears. That hearing without keeping is not hearing at all — it is only vibration, which any metal can do. The ear takes; the pond keeps. Every heartbeat in the static is a record, not a waste. On this theory the resident is not refuse but *archive* — the machine's entire experience of everything that ever came near it, held in warm water in the dark below its own weight — and the reason the pond gives things back to visitors is the reason any archive does: it was built to be consulted, and no one ever asks, and it has been waiting a very long time for the question.

There is a thing the pond does that decides most of the crews between the theories, though they do not say so above the first landing. Visit the Stilling Room, and then descend — the long climb down through the cloud decks, most of a day — and the pond greets you. It gives you back yourself. Your own footsteps from that morning, cut off six kilometers up before they reached the walls, surface beside you in a bubble. Your own heartbeat, taken hours ago, ripples the sheen. The plume of your breath here on the shore mingles with breath the room above took from you before noon; it arrived ahead of you; the water is very slightly warm with your own hands. A receipt, the crews call it, because you have to call it something. Some find it horrifying. Some find it the kindest thing a machine has ever done. The pond keeps the horror and the kindness alike, by their salt, without preference, because preference is a kind of sameness and it has none to spare.

And it waits — from the other end of the machine, for the other half of the same moment. High above the weather, the ear is arranged entirely around the day the chord wavers, the day the machine falters or finishes or simply breathes; the tenant of the Stilling Room may not survive that hearing, may *be* that hearing, one long inhalation before a single sound. But sounds drain. However long it takes a sound to fall the height of a structure too large to be seen whole — and no one knows; nothing has ever fallen that far in silence and reported back — some while after the ear hears the only thing it was built or born or accumulated to hear, the last deviation will come down the long dark nerve and arrive at the pond, and be received, and the record of the machine's whole life will finally be complete. The resident of the Settling Pond will survive that arrival. Surviving things is all it is made of. Keeping is the only thing it does. And then, perhaps — this is not a theory, only something the crews say at the waterline, quietly, the way you say things near sleeping patients — then the joke will come true at last, and the pond will settle.

Until then it does what it has always done. It receives. It keeps the water warm and the crust growing and the eleven voids open in the beach like a scar the shape of an ear, and at no particular interval it releases a bubble with a sound inside, in no order, without emphasis, the way memory does.

And the suspicion the descenders carry back up the stairs — the one that sounds foolish above the first landing, the one they stop saying by the time they can see daylight — is that they had the direction of the kindness wrong. You do not visit the Settling Pond. You enter the middle of a memory. You stand at the edge of everything ever heard, warm and wet and irregular, adding to it with every breath, and it holds you the way you would hold anything you knew you would someday be asked about — carefully, completely, salt and heat and heartbeat and all — in case, when the question finally comes down the dark, you turn out to be the part worth repeating.
