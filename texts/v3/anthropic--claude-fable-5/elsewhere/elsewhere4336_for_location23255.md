The Rootfall

The Rootfall is the same plant. This is the first thing to understand, and the one thing the people of the plateau, two and a half miles overhead, have never quite had to believe. Up there, the plant is a country. Down here, it is a wall.

The Wall is what they call it — no one in the Rootfall says *the plateau*, because from below there is no tabletop, no flat patience, no province-sized instrument played by the wind. There is only the flank: two and a half vertical miles of bark rising out of the plain and going up until it stops being a thing in the landscape and becomes the landscape's edge, the place where the world folds and becomes sky. It is not sheer. It is buttressed — vast root-limbs, some of them a mile through at the base, come down the face like the knuckles of a braced hand and run out across the lowland for eighty, ninety, a hundred miles, dwindling as they go, sinking gradually into the ground until at last they are only long low swellings under the dust, and then not even that. The people of the Rootfall live between these fingers, in the wedge-shaped countries the buttresses divide the lowland into — the *vales*, each one with its own weather of shadow, its own hour when the sun clears the rim.

Because there is sun here. That is the first shock for anyone who comes down. The spore-haze that roofs the plateau in its dim paper-colored overcast hangs close to the crown; it spills over the rim in slow silent cataracts of mist, but it thins as it falls, and by the time it reaches the lowland the sky above the Rootfall is mostly open — hard, high, and blue in the day, and at night filled with stars. Travelers who make the descent through the Throat, six days walking down inside the flank, step out of the gallery mouth at the bottom, look up, and stop. There are plateau-born who have wept at it. There are Rootfall children who have grown up on the story of the crying visitors and still do not really understand what there is, in a clear night sky, to grieve.

The trade of skies runs the other way too. A Rootfall traveler who climbs the Throat comes out on top under the glowing haze, into the green sourceless dusk-light, and feels the roof close over them, and most of them describe the same thing: not oppression but *interiority* — the sudden understanding that the people up there live indoors, all of them, all the time, inside the plant's own exhaled breath. The two peoples have a shared saying about it, one of the few sentences that survives in both tongues nearly unchanged: *they got the light, we got the dark, and the plant kept the weather.*

---

The Wall makes the day. Its shadow is the Rootfall's great tide — in the eastern vales the sun rises out of the open plain and the shadow of the Wall retreats toward it all morning, a hard-edged darkness a hundred miles long drawing back like water; in the western vales the sun does not clear the rim until midday, and morning is a long blue occasion, cool, the buttresses standing up out of the dimness while the far plain already burns with light. No one in the Rootfall gets lost. The Wall is visible from everywhere; there is no *lost* here, only *far*. Distance is not measured in volume, as it is on the crown — the Rootfall is quiet, and this is the second shock. It is measured in shadow: a neighboring village is *two hours of shade* away, meaning you can make the walk before the Wall's darkness catches you.

Quiet, but not silent. The plateau's famous roar — the organ-drone that the towns up top hide from in their acoustic dead spots — comes down here too, but it falls two and a half miles to arrive, and the falling smears it. What reaches the lowland is a high, thin, continuous murmur out of the sky, directionless, like a storm in the next country over, and the Rootfall people call it, with the flat affection of long cohabitation, *the upstairs*. Children fall asleep to it. When the wind up top shifts and the upstairs changes key, everyone below notices without looking up, the way one notices a change in a sleeping person's breathing from another room.

The Rootfall's own sound does not come through the air at all. It comes through the ground.

The plant is still eating. That is what the buttress roots are — not anchors, or not only anchors, but the working ends of the organism, run out through the corpses of the mountain ranges it long ago overtopped, grinding what is left of them into the talc-fine mineral dust that the circulation hauls, grain by charged grain, up through the body to feed the crown. The mountains of the old world still stand in the Rootfall, after a fashion: the *eaten hills*, whole ranges reduced to porous grey husks, hollowed and honeycombed by feeder roots, so light in places that a strong wind makes them boom. And beneath everything, day and night, runs the grind — the deep milling of stone in root, far below the reach of hearing, pure infrasound, present only as a texture in the bones. Up on the crown they speak into each other's sternums to beat the roar. Down here the sternum is for listening. The Rootfall people lie down to consult the world: full length on the bare ground, cheek and palms and chest against it, reading the grind the way sailors once read swells. It is how they weather-cast, how they track the herds, how the old settle arguments about whether the milling in the third vale has moved. There is no ceremony to it. A person lying flat in the middle of a road is not in distress; they are checking something, and it is rude to interrupt.

When the grind pauses — it does, rarely, regionally, for reasons no one has found — the Rootfall suffers the exact inverse of the plateau's affliction. The stillness comes up through the feet like cold. Herds bunch. Villages sit up all night, not speaking, waiting for the floor of the world to resume, and when it does — always so far, in eleven thousand years, it always has — the relief is physical, communal, and followed by an unscheduled holiday.

---

At intervals along the Wall, the plant excretes.

The circulation is not a closed loop; what the crown takes from the stone it does not wholly keep, and the spent dust — discharged, inert, its charge given up somewhere in the vast internal ledger — comes back down. It comes down in the *falls*: cataracts of grey powder pouring from vents in the flank, some of them beginning more than two miles up, falling so far that the columns lose themselves, dissolving into slow standing towers of haze long before they reach the ground. A dustfall makes almost no sound. Up close there is only an enormous soft descending *shh*, the plateau's sealed interior hiss finally unsealed, and beneath each fall the land has built itself into banks — deltas of settled talc, pale grey, miles across, older than the people, still growing. The banks migrate as the vents shift over centuries, and there are villages in the Rootfall that have moved four times in their remembered history, walking out from under the slow advance of a grey country falling out of the sky, carrying their doorframes with them.

The banks are where the Rootfall settled, long ago, one of the great open questions of the crown. The plateau's quietwrights press their ears to the bark and hear knots of turbulence swimming upstream in the circulation, and their scholars decline to commit, and their children say *dust-swimmers*, and no one topside has ever caught one. Below, everyone has seen one. They come out with the spent dust — flushed, or spent themselves, or dying, no one is sure — glassy eel-bodied things a few feet long, made, as near as anyone can tell, of the dust itself, fused and briefly animate. They emerge at the falls, thrash down the banks in long looping furrows, and either find a living root-channel and pour themselves back into the plant, or don't, and stop, and within a day are indistinguishable from the bank. A plateau scholar once came down the Throat carrying forty years of careful agnosticism on this subject, was walked to the nearest fall by a polite twelve-year-old, watched three swimmers come down the bank in the first hour, and sat in the dust for the rest of the afternoon without saying anything. The Rootfall found this deeply satisfying and has never stopped telling the story.

Water comes from the roots. Where a working root meets certain stone, the grinding sweats — a slow chemical weeping, beading along seams in the bark — and every village in the Rootfall sits at a seep, the way every town on the crown sits in a dead spot with its grown well at the center. The water tastes of stone and, underneath, of something green, and the child's first ceremony here is the same drop on the tongue, with the introduction turned by a quarter: on the crown they say *this is the landlord*, and below they say *this is the hand*. Because that is the Rootfall's whole theology, if it can be called that, delivered in one sentence to every visitor who asks how the two peoples can be tenants of the same organism and live such different lives: *they live on the body. We live in the hand.*

---

The towns are built, not grown, and the Rootfall is quietly proud of this.

There is no grafting here — or rather, there is no petitioning. The field-language, the optative engineering, the whole architecture of courteous request that raises the crown's glowing vesicles over decades: none of it ever took root below, and the Rootfall's account of why is characteristically blunt. *You don't ask the hand for things. The hand gives you what falls from it.* And it does. The plant sheds — dead outer root-layers sloughing off the buttresses in slabs and beams, root-tips dying back at the far frontier and lying on the plain like the timbers of a fleet — and this is the only wood in the world, iron-hard, close-grained, and workable, and the Rootfall built a civilization of carpenters on it. Their houses are joined and pegged, raised on the ground rather than out of it, roofed against sporefall, floored — always — with a single wide plank of root-wood laid directly on the earth, so that the grind comes through. A Rootfall house is judged by its floor before its walls. Children are put to sleep on the bare board, and their lullaby is the mountain being eaten, forty miles down, forever.

Names are given at the Wall. The crown names its children in silence, in the dead center of the dead spot, on the principle that a name first spoken where nothing else sounds will carry through any noise. The Rootfall's principle is the mirror of it, and the older of the two, they claim: a name is not yours until the plant has said it back. So the family carries the infant to the foot of the Wall — a journey, for the far villages; there are naming-roads a hundred miles long, walked in stages, the whole vale contributing food — and the name is called once, loudly, at the flank, and then everyone waits through the long seconds while it climbs and returns. The echo off two and a half miles of living bark comes back changed — thinned, deepened, worn round like everything the plant handles — and *that* is the name, the returned version, and a Rootfall person will tell you, if pressed, that what they carry is not what their parents shouted but what the Wall answered.

---

They are the ones who stayed. That is the kinship, and the Rootfall keeps it the way the crown's grafters keep their registries — completely, and a little grimly.

The plateau's records are coy about whether its people arrived or arose. The Rootfall's records are not coy about anything. They say: there was one people, down here, in the last age of the old world, when the sea still stood in its bed at the edge of the plain and rain still came off it; and when the crown closed over the last of the free mountains and the plant settled into its final form, some of the people went up — climbed, or were lifted; on this one point even the Rootfall admits the ledger is smudged — to live in the new country the plant had made of its own back. And some stayed. The reasons recorded are various and human: the graves were here; the sea was still here, then, and someone believed it would come back; someone's mother refused. Eleven thousand years have pulled the two tongues apart until they meet only in old words, but the Rootfall still calls the plateau people, without rancor, *the ones upstairs*, and the plateau — which has half-forgotten there is a downstairs — calls the Rootfall, when it remembers, *the keepers*. Of what, the crown's records do not say. The Rootfall knows exactly what.

They keep the words. The Rootfall tongue is the old tongue's direct heir, slow and heavy, and it is full of vocabulary with nothing left to attach to — *river*, *forest*, *tide* — words maintained like tools oiled against a use that will not come. Some found new work: *snow*, unemployed for millennia, was given to the sporefall, and fits it — the fine grey-green fall that comes down off the rim on the evening wind, when the crown cools and exhales and its breath descends the Wall, and settles overnight on the fields. Spent spore is the only soil in the world. Everything the Rootfall farms grows in the fallen breath of the plant, in beds of it built up over generations, and the harvest words all survived intact because they never stopped being needed. But *rain* found nothing. *Rain* is kept empty. It is taught to every child — the sound of it, the grammar of it, the old expressions it lives in — as an heirloom is kept, polished and unused, and there is a Rootfall idiom for hopeless longing that translates, literally, as *waiting for the word's work to come back*.

And they keep the poles. At the far edge of the Rootfall, past the last living root, past the *still line* where the frontier of the plant's appetite halted and the dead root-tips lie bleaching on the plain, the land runs out onto the bed of the old sea — the Bright, they call it, a floor of salt going flat and blinding to the horizon, the one place in the world where the grind cannot be felt, because nothing of the plant reaches there. Whether the plant drank the sea, or merely outlived it, or took the rain into itself until the sea starved slowly, the Rootfall scholars keep all three positions, in that order of bitterness. What is not in dispute is the record: certain families held the shore as it left, and each generation drove a pole at the waterline, and the line of poles now runs forty-one miles out onto the salt — one lifetime, one pole — ending at the last pole, standing alone in open salt, driven by a woman whose name is known, who watched the water leave it. Walking the poles is the Rootfall's great pilgrimage. It is done in silence, which out there is not a discipline but the local condition: the Bright is the true silence, the only zero in a world built on the plant's sound, and it does to some people what the roar does to some people on the crown. The Rootfall has its *still-gone*, out on the salt, as the plateau has its loud-gone at the vent-chimneys — the same handful per generation, gone over into the opposite absolute, coming back slow and strange, if they come back, and consulted, when they do, on matters no one else can be asked about. The two peoples do not know they share this. It is exactly the kind of thing they share.

---

The fliers come down.

They are not supposed to — a failing flier rides its last thermal to a distant pore and folds itself against the opening and is drawn in, letters returned to sender, and this is the crown's one absolute law and its oldest tenderness. But the rim winds are jealous, and some of the dying are caught and carried over the edge, and then there is nothing for it but the long descent: a kite-bodied speck dropping out of the spilling haze, gliding down the face of the Wall for the better part of an hour, visible from twenty miles out in the vales as a slow pale spark against the dark bark. The Rootfall watches for them. It is called *watching the Wall*, and it is not an assignment; it is a thing eyes do here, the way crown eyes track the herds. When a flier lands — and they land badly, these creatures born aloft, folding wrong on the one surface they were never made for — whoever reaches it first takes up the body, which weighs almost nothing, and carries it to the nearest living root-mouth, and presses it there, and the root draws it in. Sometimes the nearest living root is weeks away. The carrier goes anyway. There is no law below either; the Rootfall simply completes the mail, and has for eleven thousand years, and calls the office — when it calls it anything — *the last leg*. The crown does not know this happens. The plant, the Rootfall assumes, does. Somewhere upstairs, they figure, a vent opens on time that would have opened late, and no one up there will ever know why, and this thought appears to be all the payment the carriers have ever wanted.

The knocking reaches down here too — the deep tenants' patient knuckles, heard in the buttresses at night, sometimes descending, floor by floor, over hours. The Rootfall listens and does not answer. Their children, told of the crown's rule — *once is a game, twice is a conversation, and you do not knock three times* — are reliably appalled at knock number one, and the Rootfall's entire recorded position on the subject is a single dry proverb: *you don't knock from inside the hand.*

---

When the Turning comes, the Rootfall is the only place that sees it from outside.

The crown goes indoors, into its glowing vesicles, while the plateau thunders through its vast internal work. Below, the falls run heavy and change color — ochres, dim reds, once in the records a season of blue — and the grind rises until it can be felt standing up, and the sporefall comes down in colors and doubles, and the Rootfall goes *out*: the Turning is harvest, the great one, and the vales work through the green-lit nights under a snow of tinted breath while the Wall shudders faintly at their backs. Two festivals, then, at the same hour, one sealed in and one turned out, neither people thinking of the other — the same weather, worn differently — which is, the few who have lived in both places say, the truest single fact about the world.

And it is during Turnings that the Rootfall's scholars take their measurements, and the measurements say what they have said now for three hundred years: the still line has not moved. The frontier roots are done. The grinding is drawing inward, vale by vale, as if the plant were finishing its sums — and the seaward falls, the ones on the flank that faces the Bright, run a little heavier every Turning, and the banks beneath them build. The crown's scholars say the plant has settled into its final form. The Rootfall says: *packing*. The belief below — unproven, unrefuted, held the way that people hold beliefs at the feet of a sleeping god — is that the last chapter ends with a seed; that the plant emptied the sea, all those ages ago, not out of thirst but the way one clears a bed for planting; and that when the time comes, something will come down the Wall — down the seaward falls, or out of a seam no one petitioned for — and it will need carrying, and the carrying will fall to the people who have spent eleven thousand years practicing the errand, one flier at a time.

Nobody knows. Nobody has been asked yet. But every Rootfall child is taught the way to the salt before they are taught their letters, and the naming-roads and the pole-road are kept swept, all hundred-some miles of them, against a delivery no ledger has scheduled. The crown lies down at night in the glowing body and listens to it breathe. The Rootfall lies down on bare root-wood floors and feels it work — the mountains going grain by grain up into the dark of the great flank, the sums being done — and they have decided, on the whole, in the absence of proof and after eleven thousand years of errands, that they were not left below. They were kept below — the way you keep a hand free.
