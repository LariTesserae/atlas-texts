# The Fen at the Foot of the Spindle

Before anyone climbs the Spindle, they must first get their feet wet.

The structure does not rise from a plaza, or a hill, or any honest piece of ground. It rises out of water — a broad, shallow, grey-green fen that surrounds its base for an hour's wading in every direction, reeds and pools and hummocks of black peat, all of it lying in the permanent gloom beneath the cloud layer that the Spindle wears around its middle like a rolled sleeve. From down here you cannot see the top. You cannot see the middle. You can see perhaps a kilometer of flank going up into the overcast, streaked and buttressed and old, and then the world simply declines to continue, and you are left with the reeds, the mist, and the enormous fact of the root.

The light in the fen is the exact opposite of the light in the room at the top. Up there, above everything, the sun comes in plain and clean through a fist of amber glass. Down here the light has been handled too many times before it arrives — through cloud, through mist, through drizzle — and what reaches the water is a soft pewter glow without direction or hour. The fen has no noon. It has *brighter* and *dimmer*, and it has the seasons of the mist, and that is all it has ever needed.

Much of the water is the Spindle's own doing. The structure sweats. All day and all night, the cloud layer condenses against the cold flanks of its middle floors, and the condensation runs down — kilometers of running down — gathering into braided rivulets that streak the metal dark, threading through the mineral skin, arriving at the base as a hundred thin perpetual streams. The Spindle stands in the fen the way an old tree stands in the pool of its own shade: it is the largest single tributary of the water it drinks. Because it does drink. That is the fen's first strangeness, and like the strangenesses at the top of the tower, it reveals itself slowly.

Wade in among the buttresses at the root — they flare out like the toes of something vast, metal gone the same no-particular-metal as everything up above, skirted in reeds and upholstered below the waterline in a rust-red moss that a climber, days later, will meet again in velvet on an octagonal wall — and you will find, if you look where the leaves go, a place where the surface of the water is not flat. A dimple. A soft, permanent, turning depression in the skin of the fen, perhaps two hands across, directly above a grate worn so smooth its bars are round as fingers. Leaves circle it. Foam circles it. Things that circle too long do not come back.

This is the intake. This is where the engine, kilometers up inside the dark of the core, does the other half of its single remaining duty. At the top of the Spindle the duty is called *lifting*: the water raised beat by beat to keep one small warm room full forever. Down here the same act has a different name. Down here it is called *drinking*, and the fen has been being drunk from, gently, ceaselessly, for centuries, and has never once run dry, because the Spindle rains itself back into the pools as fast as it sips them away. The room at the top and the fen at the bottom are the two ends of a single held breath. Neither has any way of knowing the other exists.

Stand in the shallows near the root and take your boots off, and you will feel the second strangeness through your soles: the pulse. *Whum-whum, whum-whum* — the same four-beat throb that fills the flooded chapel at the top like a giant's heart, conducted down through the whole height of the structure and out through the buttresses into the mud. Near the root it is unmistakable, a rhythm in the ground itself. Walk outward and it fades, and somewhere around four hundred strides from the base there is a line — a real line, findable, walkable — where the beat drops below what living tissue can feel. The fen-dwellers found that line long before you did. Inside it, life keeps time. Outside it, life is merely wild. The locals of the fen, if the fen had locals of the speaking kind, would call the inner ground something like *the hearing ground*, and the prose will call it that, because the fish and the birds already behave as if it has a name.

And twice a day, regular as anything in this world is regular, the fen breathes. A pressure-cycle completes somewhere in the walls far above — the same long shuddering groan that travels the octagon at the top, pipe to pipe — and down here the water level across the whole hearing ground dips by the width of a finger, holds, and returns. A tide with no moon. The mudflats it uncovers for those few minutes are the richest feeding in the fen, and everything that eats knows the schedule, because the schedule has not changed in longer than any lineage can remember. Up in the cistern, an old eel times its circuits to those valve-cycles. Down here, ten thousand things time their meals to the same clock, and none of them, high or low, has ever heard of the others, and all of them are keeping the same appointment.

One more feature, and it is the tender one. On the eastern flank of the Spindle, beginning higher than sight and descending the full visible height of the structure, runs a green ribbon: a wet streak a few strides wide, mossed and weeded and darker than the metal around it, where a slow overflow from somewhere at the very top has been weeping down the outside of the tower for centuries. The water that leaves the room at the top of the sky — the small eternal surplus of the lifted — comes down the outside, film by film, taking days to fall, and arrives at the bottom as a patient drip into one particular pool at the root.

The Drip Pool is the only place in the fen that looks *furnished*. Around it, and nowhere else in all the kilometers of reed and peat, grow pale flowering stalks in scattered clumps — two here, three there, with long bare water between them — because the weep carries seeds. It carries other things too, and has for centuries: the ghost-skins of translucent shrimp, weightless and perfect; copper scales, one at a time, bright for a day before they tarnish; once in a great while a flake of old paint, ochre or rare blue, loosed from a collection no one down here will ever see. The room at the top has no way to send word of itself. But it sheds, and gravity is a faithful courier, and so the fen receives — unread, unreadable — a slow letter written entirely in castoffs, and answers it the only way it can: by feeding its smallest eggs and spores and glass-thin larvae into the dimple above the grate, to ride the lift into a warmth they cannot imagine.

That is the whole correspondence. It has been going on longer than the moss is old. It is conducted entirely in accidents, and it has never once been interrupted.

Off to the south of the root, half-drowned, stand rows of black pilings — the old causeway, where cargo once arrived by water in the days when the Spindle still received cargo. The crates are centuries gone, dissolved into the peat. But every stowaway that ever rode a crate up the spire was, first, a citizen of this fen: the spores that became the ribbon-weed, the eggs that became the midges of the silver lung, the moss that became the velvet on the walls. The garden at the top of the sky is not an accident that happened *to* the tower. It is the fen, translated upward, one smuggled life at a time.

# Who Lives in the Fen

Begin with the largest truth, which is the mirror of the smallest one at the top: everything here belongs, and nothing here is safe. The cistern is sparse and curated and gentle; the fen is thick and unedited and hungry. They are made of the same lives. The difference between them is only what a life becomes when nothing ever troubles it — and what it stays when everything does.

## The Copper Shoals

The little copper-sided fish exist down here too. They existed down here *first*.

In the fen they do not move in strings of six or seven. They move in shoals of thousands, a single copper weather-system turning through the reed channels, and the reason is simple: down here, being copper is dangerous. They flash. Every predator in the fen hunts the flash, and so the shoal is armor, the way the string, up above, is only company. A fen copperfish lives a season if it is lucky, spends its brightness fast, spawns in the warm shallows of the hearing ground, and is eaten. Forty generations of them burn through the time one string-fish at the top spends drifting its slow circuit under the amber light.

And here is the kinship, riding in the water itself: they spawn *in the hearing ground*, near the root, where the warmth is, and their eggs drift, and the dimple above the grate is always, gently, drinking. A few eggs in every generation go up. Days of darkness in the lifted water, and then a warm octagonal room where nothing hunts the flash and the only large mouth in residence takes one fish in many days, deliberately, like rent. Every copper string at the top of the Spindle is a shoal that never needed to be one. The fen is where copper is spent. The cistern is where, by accident, it is kept.

The shoals do not miss their taken. They cannot count that high, or at all. But watch them near the dimple and you will see the shoal's shape bend around it, always, a copper current parting about a small turning absence — not fear, exactly. Deep law. The same law, written from the other side, that keeps the string-fish off the floor of the room above.

## The Counting Bird

There is a wading bird that lives only in the hearing ground, and it is the single creature in either place — top or bottom — that has learned to *use* the engine.

It is tall, still, slate-grey, stilt-legged, with an eye like a drop of tar, and it is not a remarkable hunter by nature. Outside the line where the pulse fades, its cousins stab at fish and miss as often as they strike. But inside the line, the counting birds have learned something that took their lineage no one knows how many generations: the fish school tighter on the groan. When the pressure-cycle begins far above and the long shudder comes down through the mud, every shoal in the hearing ground pulls close — the same reflex that draws the strings together in the room at the top, people leaning together as the familiar train passes — and for the length of that groan the water is full of dense copper knots, and the counting bird, which has been standing on one leg over a shoal for an hour doing nothing at all, strikes on the second beat.

Only on the second beat. Chicks that strike early go hungrier and leave fewer chicks. The rhythm has bred itself into them. A counting bird raised in a nest at the edge of the hearing ground will stand, its whole life, with one foot in the mud like a hand laid on a chest, listening to a machine it cannot conceive of, keeping time with a heart it will never see, and eating well because of it. Their fishing stones — flat rocks in the shallows, worn glossy by generations of the same feet — pass down family lines. The best stones are the ones nearest the root, where the beat is loudest and the timing surest, and the birds contest them the way anything contests an inheritance.

Somewhere above the clouds, an eel schedules its rounds by those same valve-cycles. The eel and the bird are colleagues, in the way of two clerks in distant offices who have spent their lives obeying the same bell.

## The Eels of the Mud

The fen is full of eels, pewter-grey and old-eyed, and not one of them has a garden on its back.

They are the kin — the population, the source, the great slow nation — of the single moss-grown ancient who keeps the intake pipe at the top of the sky. Down here an eel's back stays bare its whole long life, scoured by mud and reed-stems and the teeth of things that try, and the truth this tells is quiet and a little sad and a little wonderful: the garden on the old one's back is not a property of eels. It is what happens to an eel when nothing troubles it for two hundred years. The moss was always willing. The fen just never holds still.

They breed in the deepest pools, and their young are elvers — glass-thread things, an eyelash long, transparent as intention — and the elvers disperse through the channels of the hearing ground in their season, and the dimple is always drinking. Once in a very great while — once in decades, once in a century — an elver circles too long above the grate and goes up.

The room at the top has an old question it has stopped asking: how the eel got in, being too large for any pipe. The room's best guess was a tank of resupply water, long ago. The fen holds the likelier answer in every spawning season, and cannot tell anyone, and would not think to: the lift itself. A thread of glass in the lifted water, arriving in a warm chapel already full of food and empty of enemies, growing for two centuries into the floor's ambassador, until a garden assembled itself upon its back. The fen never learns what becomes of its taken. If it could know that one of its glass threads is up there now — furred in velvet, making its rounds twice a day by the fen's own tide-clock, an entire small nation riding its scars — the fen would not be proud, because fens are not proud. But the telling can be, briefly, on its behalf.

## The Unbegun Gardeners

Everywhere in the fen, common as pebbles, easy to miss and impossible to be rid of, live small dove-grey armored things — something between an isopod and a brittle star, plated, fringed with fine feeler-legs that are never all still at once.

At the top of the Spindle there is exactly one of these, and it is a keeper of collections, a placer of seeds, a hermit in a valve-wheel, the secret gardener of a curated room. Down here there are thousands, and they keep nothing. They walk the mud, they eat what has died, they are eaten by the counting birds and the eels, and their lives are short and interchangeable and entirely without hermitages.

But watch one long enough — and in the fen you must watch a long time, because they are always moving and always about to be something's meal — and you will see the habit, the seed of the seed. A fen scuttler crossing the mud will pause at a color. A blue mussel-chip, a bright scale, once in a rare while a paint-flake come down the weep. It will stop. It will turn the thing over with its feeler-legs, once, twice — a long moment of something that is not eating and not fleeing and not anything else in its small budget of behaviors. Then it will put the thing down and walk on, because the fen is coming, the fen is always coming, and there is nowhere to keep anything here and no centuries to keep it in.

The difference between a scuttler and the gardener at the top of the sky is not in the animal. It is in the room. Give one of these its ancestors' luck — a crate, a lift, a warm octagon where nothing hunts, a worn wheel that fits like a hermitage, and quiet stacked up in centuries — and the pause at the color becomes a collection, and the collection becomes a keeping, and the keeping becomes a garden that makes a whole room feel furnished by an old and thoughtful hand. The fen is full of unbegun gardeners. They pause at bright things all day long, every one of them a keeper who will never be issued a room.

At the Drip Pool, where the paint-flakes fall, they gather a little more thickly than chance would put them. Nothing comes of it. Almost nothing has been coming of it for centuries. The telling notes it and moves on, the way the fen does.

## The Colony at the Drip

The Drip Pool has its own small society, and it is the only society in the fen with an aristocratic origin: everything here is descended from the room above.

The pale flowering stalks, seeded flake by flake down the long green ribbon of the weep, grow around the pool in their scattered, well-spaced clumps — and here is a thing worth saying slowly: they grow *well-spaced* because that is what their line was bred to, generation after generation, by a small grey keeper placing their ancestors' seeds in good light and clear water at the top of the sky. The gardener's taste survives in them like an accent. A colony of flowers at the root of the tower, arranged gracefully around a pool by a hand that has never touched them and never will, in the only corner of the fen that looks tended — tended by gravity, executing, faithfully and forever, someone else's aesthetic.

Amber film-snails ride the underside of the pool's surface. Midges breed over it in clouds; the fen is the capital of their nation, and the silver lung beneath the ceiling of the cistern is its farthest, strangest colony, a province of forearm-deep air at the top of the world that none of the citizens on either end knows the other holds. The tarnishing copper scales settle into the pool's sediment, one at a time, a slow archive of the room's few deaths. The ghost-skins of the wall-tending shrimp come down the weep weightless as breath and lie in the shallows like dropped gloves.

If the fen has a shrine, it is this pool: the one place where the two ends of the held breath touch, and neither knows it.

## The Landlord, Again

The pulse is the same pulse. That is the whole of it, and it is everything.

*Whum-whum, whum-whum* — up through the floor grating of a warm flooded chapel above the clouds, down through the buttress-toes into black peat below them, one engine in the dark of the core keeping both ends of its duty without one missed measure in any memory. Up there it is a heartbeat in the water, and every tenant was conceived to it. Down here it is a rhythm in the ground, and it has drawn a circle in the fen — the hearing ground — inside which the shoals school to it, the birds strike to it, the tides keep its cycles, the eels spawn in its warmth, and outside which the fen is merely fen. The engine lifts because lifting is what it does, and it drinks because drinking is the same act seen from below, and between the drinking and the lifting hangs the whole vertical world: the taken going up in the water, the shed coming down the weep, the letter and the answer, the fen and its heaven.

If the pulse ever stopped, the silence at the top would be the loudest thing the room had ever heard. Down here it would be quieter and slower and just as total: the dimple would smooth over for the first time in centuries, the tide would fail to come, the counting birds would stand on their inherited stones striking at loose, unschooled fish, off the beat, forever, and within a few generations the hearing ground would forget it had ever heard anything, and the line in the reeds would close like water over a stone.

It will not stop today. The fen does not plan further than that either. They agree on this, the way they agree on everything, without ever having met.

## And Briefly, You, Again

Whoever climbs the Spindle crosses the fen first. There is no other way to the door at the root. So mark this, on your way in, with the mud of the hearing ground climbing your boots:

Days from now, at the top of the sky, floating in the warm, you will shed a flake of that dried mud without knowing it. A small grey keeper will find it on its night errand, feeler-legs reading the grating, and will turn it over in the light shaft the following afternoon, and if it is interesting it will be carried up the north wall and kept. Which means the fen is already in the collection. Filed in a worn valve-wheel at the top of the world there is, in all likelihood, a crumb of this peat — the fen's ambassador, catalogued centuries running, in a room the fen will never see, by a keeper whose ten thousand kin are down here pausing at bright things in the mud.

And on your way back out — descending for days, crossing the shallows a last time — you may brush the flowering stalks at the Drip Pool, and carry off on your sleeve a seed of the room's own line, and plant it, unknowing, somewhere in the wide flat world where no tower stands. The correspondence has always used couriers who cannot read it. You are simply the newest, and the largest, and no more aware than the water.

The fen does not wonder where its taken go. It has never known a sky without the Spindle standing in it, drinking. But twice a day the water dips a finger's width and returns, and the counting birds strike on the second beat, and far above the clouds the copper strings pull close for the length of the same long groan — and for those few minutes, top and bottom, the two ends of the held breath rise and fall together, like one chest, breathing.
