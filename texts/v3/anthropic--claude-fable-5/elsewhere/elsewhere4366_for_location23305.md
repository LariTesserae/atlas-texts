**The Settling Stair**

You see it from the plain before you understand what it is: a white spill down the mountain's western foot, broad at the bottom and narrowing as it climbs, like a banner hung from the lowest slopes, or a stain of light where no light falls. Travelers crossing the flats have taken it for a snowfield out of season, a salt bed, a ruin. It is none of these. It is stone that is still being made.

Come closer, up through the alder scrub and the ordinary noise of a mountain's edge — birds, wind, running water going carelessly wherever running water goes — and the white resolves into terraces. A hundred of them, perhaps more; no one has counted the same number twice, because the stair does not hold still at any pace a counter can use. Each terrace is a shallow pool held behind a scalloped lip of pale stone, and each pool spills in a thin sliding sheet over its lip into the pool below, and so on down the slope, water descending a staircase it is simultaneously building. The stone is white-gold, smooth, faintly translucent at the edges, and at the growing lips it is soft — press a thumbnail into a young rim and it yields like cheese rind, and hardens behind your touch, and keeps it.

The water is warm. At the top of the stair it is warm as blood, and it cools by degrees as it descends, so that the stair is not one place but a gradient of places, each pool its own small climate with its own tenants — mats of algae in colors the rest of the mountain does not use, burnt orange, arsenic green, a red like the inside of an eyelid, banding the pools by temperature. On cold mornings the whole stair steams, and the terraces disappear into their own breath, and the mountain looks as if it is quietly exhaling something it has held all night.

It is. This is the first thing to understand about the Settling Stair: the water does not come from the sky.

At the head of the stair, where the slope meets the mountain's true rock, there is a low dark arch — a mouth of old lava, glazed and wet — and out of it the water comes in a steady, unhurried, permanent flow. It is milk-blue with dissolved mountain. It has been inside the stone a long time. High above this place, two hard days' climb up the western shoulder, there is a hollow in the heights with a pool in it the color of dark glass, a pool much deeper than it has any right to be, fed forever by weeping walls, never rising, never falling. The depth of that pool is not a mystery. It is a door. What the walls weep into it goes down — down through the mountain's dark plumbing, through tube and seam and slow black chambers, for three centuries or four; the mountain does not say exactly — and comes out here, at the arch, into the light it last saw when the moss now closing over certain small carved hollows had not yet begun to close. The warmth of the stair and the warmth of the basin are the same warmth, one heat rising through one mountain, felt at the top as a listening room's mildness and at the bottom as water you could sleep in.

So the water arriving at the stair today is old news from above. It fell as seep and drip into the high pool in another age of the tree, another course of the seeps, and it has spent the centuries since inside the mountain, in the dark, being — there is no better word for what happens to it — *finished*.

You can taste this, a little. The water of the stair is heavy, mineral, faintly sweet, and it goes down with a strange settledness, like the last sentence of a long story. Animals prefer it to any other water on the mountain's western side. That is the polite explanation for the crowds.

Because the stair is crowded. This is the thing that strikes anyone who has stood in the high basin and felt its textured emptiness, its trackless moss, its birds that will not descend: down here, everything descends. Deer stand shin-deep in the cool lower pools at dawn, unbothered. Swallows nest by the hundred under the overhanging terrace lips, where dripstone hangs in curtains, and their coming and going is a constant soft traffic. Real salamanders — orange, glossy, entirely convincing, with ordinary eyes — bask on the warm rims in numbers. Dragonflies work the pool surfaces. Small silver fish live in the tempered middle terraces, in water their ancestors have never left. The stair hums and flickers and drips and splashes with unselfconscious life, and the reason is the same reason, inverted, that the basin stands empty:

Nothing here is listening.

Clap your hands at the Settling Stair and nothing falters, nothing holds its breath, nothing considers. The sound goes out over the terraces and down the slope and is gone, unkept, the way sound is gone everywhere in the ordinary world. Shout, and no deepened voice returns. The stair is open, fan-shaped, unenclosed; it folds nothing back. Its own sound — and it has one, continuous, layered, a hundred thin sheets of water sliding over a hundred stone lips at once — pours outward and downhill and scatters into the alders, a soft unending sibilance with a grain in it, like a conversation heard through a wall in a language you nearly know. Lean close to a spilling lip and the grain sharpens, almost resolves, never resolves. It is not for you. Nothing at the stair is for you. That is the stair's whole nature, and it is why the animals love it: here, and almost nowhere else on this mountain, you can occur unnoticed.

The stair does not listen. The stair *answers*.

**What Lives at the Settling Stair**

Begin again with the ordinary, because down here the ordinary is abundant and has earned its place: the algal nations in their temperature-territories, the swallows in their dripstone eaves, the fish that have inherited a bathwater sea three strides wide, the deer, the salamanders, the insects, the alders pressing up to the lowest terraces to drink at the overspill. The stair supports more life along its hundred steps than the rest of the western slope together, and every bit of that life exists in a state the high basin never grants: unattended. Watched by nothing. Free.

And then there is what the stair keeps, which is not sound — the stair keeps nothing of sound — but everything else.

Walk across a young terrace and look back in a year: your footprints will be stone. The lime-heavy water skins whatever it touches, patiently, coat on coat, and the soft rims harden around every accident. The stair is, layer under layer, a ledger of contact. In the faces of old broken terraces you can see the record in cross-section: leaves, whole and veined, turned to white leaf-shaped stone; a bird, wings half-open; feathers; branches; and — in the oldest exposed layers, low on the stair, where a spring flood once cracked a rim — footprints. Human ones. Bare, deliberate, in files. Some are small, a child's. And when you sight along the old files to see where those walkers were going, they are going *up* — up the slope, toward the western shoulder, where even now, in the right raking light, you can make out the oldest path on the mountain: a faint worn line climbing two days toward a notch in pale stone, high up, smooth as a worn tooth.

They lived here. The people who climbed to the high basin and sat at the heart of its sound and ground their dozen small hollows into the boulder's crown — this was their home, at the foot of the stair, in the warmth. And down here, unlike up there, they left everything: hearth-rings entombed in terrace stone, potsherds skinned in white, beads, a bone flute, tools — all of it locked into the growing stair, layer on layer, because at the Settling Stair you did not need to carry your belongings away from the presence. There is no presence. Or rather: the presence here has no ears, only hands, and everything you set down, it keeps.

They understood the mountain as a single long act with two ends, and they built their lives across it. You cannot speak to the stair; the stair does not hear. You cannot be answered by the basin; the basin never finishes. So they arranged themselves accordingly. When something had to be said — the kind of thing a person must say once in a life, *I was, I am, this happened to me* — you walked two days up the old line to the notch and sat in the sound and ground your saying into the stone over patient days, and were received. And then you came home to the stair and lived the rest of your life drinking the answers — knowing, with a calm that is hard for us to inhabit, that the water warming your children and softening your grief and settling your sleep was the mountain's reply to questions asked centuries before you were born, by people whose names were gone, whose sayings were still, far above you in the dark rock, being considered. Every prayer they made was answered. Reliably. Always to someone else. They drank their ancestors' answers and asked on behalf of their heirs, and the two days' walk between asking and answering was, for them, simply the shape of time, made visible on a mountainside.

Their dead, it is thought, they laid in the highest pools. In the oldest, lowest, longest-buried terraces there are rounded swells in the stone, smooth and person-length. The stair offers no help in deciding, and does not need to. Whatever those swells hold was given to the water, and the water skinned it in white patience, and it is part of the stair now — as, in the other direction, the rare creature that climbs at the end of its life to lie down in the high basin's moss becomes part of the syllable, and then, centuries later, part of the dark descending water, and arrives at last down here, dissolved and finished, in the milk-blue flow that the living deer drink at dawn. The mountain wastes nothing. It only takes a very long time to make its deliveries.

And then there is the heron.

There is always exactly one. It stands in the third pool from the top — always the third, in the blood-warm water, in the steam — white with the faint gold shadow of the stone in its feathers, on one leg, motionless in the way the pale thing at the high pool's edge is motionless, but with a difference you feel before you can name it. The pale thing up there is provisional, sketched, an approximation put out by water that has not finished thinking. The heron is *complete*. It holds under observation. It casts a shadow. It preens. It has been seen to shift its weight, to spread and refold its wings, to walk — at dusk, deliberately, down the stair from lip to lip until the white of it fails against the white of the stone and it is not so much gone as no longer distinguishable. No one has seen it arrive. No one has seen it eat, and it is never gaunt. Once in a long while it molts a single feather, and if you carry that feather home it will not last; within a season it stiffens and pales and is a curl of white stone in your pocket, a feather-shaped piece of terrace, which is what it always was.

Its eyes are the milk-blue of the pools. Not similar. Exact. And here is the detail that gives the heron away, the mirror of the detail that gave away the pale thing: lean toward it, and your reflection arrives in its eyes *on time*. No lag. No lingering. And if you make a sound — speak, clap, break a stick — the heron turns its head to you not a half-beat late, like everything in the basin, and not with the flinching quickness of an animal, but exactly, precisely, unnervingly on time, as if it had begun turning before you decided to make the sound. As if your arrival at the stair, your voice, your question, were things it had already spent centuries considering and finished with long ago. The pale thing in the high basin regards you like a thought just beginning. The heron regards you like a thought that is done. It is what the water can manage once it has had three hundred years in the dark to get you right: not a sketch of a creature but a conclusion, standing up out of the warm pool on one leg to look at what it already knows.

It is the only conclusion the mountain has. Everything else is still in progress — the syllable accumulating above, the water descending within, the stone accreting below. The heron alone is finished, and it spends its finishedness the way finished things do: standing still in warm water, wanting nothing, waiting for nothing, keeping, in the steam of the third pool, the mountain's one completed thought — whatever it is. It does not say. The stair does not say things to anyone in particular; that is the whole of its temperament, generous and deaf. Everything here — the warmth, the water, the murmur, the white stone underfoot — is answer, and none of it is addressed to you.

The stair is patient about the future, having been assembled entirely out of the past. The terraces climb, lip on lip, a hand's width in a generation; the stair is slowly walking up the mountain that is slowly coming down to meet it. Far above, water is patient and the rim is finite; the notch is still being worn, and in some thousands or tens of thousands of years the high bowl will break its fold and the long syllable will spill out over the slopes and be gone into the air, said. The basin will end by completion. But the water will keep descending through the dark, carrying its dissolved centuries, and the stair will keep receiving it and laying it down, coat on coat, and so — this is the last thing to understand, and the stair has all the time you need — the two places are not neighbors, or kin, or trading partners, though they are all of these. They are one utterance at two moments. The basin is the mountain hearing. The stair is the mountain, centuries later, in white stone and warm water, replying. Everything ground into the boulder up there, everything wept down the walls, everything that ever lay down in the moss to be heard completely, comes out here eventually, translated into terrace and steam and the settledness in the water's taste. The stair is the basin, said.

If you go, drink from the third pool. It will do you good, and it is not for you; it is somebody's answer, three hundred years on its way, and they are past needing it, and the mountain does not mind. Drink anyway. The heron will turn its head exactly when you kneel — not startled, never startled, only confirming something it concluded long before you were born. And if you have a question of your own, look west and up, to the faint worn line climbing the shoulder. It is two days to the notch. Ask there. Then come home, and live at the foot of the stair, and leave the answer to whoever is standing here, cupping their hands in the warm water, three hundred years from now.
