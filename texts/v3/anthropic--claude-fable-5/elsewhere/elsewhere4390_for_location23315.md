# The Well

The planet did not exhale only once.

The same deep breath that blew the Hollow into the crust ran further along the fault before it was done, and lower, and there it left a second pocket — smaller, a sea's width rather than a continent's, pinched off in rock that had not quite finished being liquid. If matters had ended there, the world would simply have held two dry darknesses instead of one. But the second bubble formed where the stone was wounded, along a fracture that reached up toward ancient buried water, and the water found it. Not in a rush — nothing here happens in a rush — but as a slow bleeding, a saturation, an age of seepage filling the pocket from the floor upward the way sleep fills a body. By the time the crust had fully set, the second bubble was drowned.

It is a sea inside a stone. Black water, blood-warm at its coolest, mineral-heavy, dense with dissolved breath — and above it, pressed against the ceiling, a lens of trapped gas that the water never quite reached: a little hollow of its own, riding on the sea's crown like a memory of what the place was before the water came. The sea has a surface, then, though no light has ever fallen on it and no wind has ever crossed it. Mostly it lies still as poured glass. Mostly.

Because the Well, unlike its dry sister, has weather. The floor is torn in places, thin over the deep heat, and there the water is cooked and rises; at the crown, against the ceiling, it cools and slides down the walls; and so the whole sea turns, one vast slow overturning, a single convection cell the size of the world it fills. The turning takes lifetimes. It is the Well's year, its season, its calendar — the one wheel everything here is fastened to. In the Hollow the air has not moved within memory; in the Well the water has never once been still. The two places were blown from one breath, and they divided the inheritance cleanly: one took the stillness, and one took the motion, and neither has ever given any of it back.

They divided sound the same way. The Hollow is a silence in which single sounds occur. The Well is a sound in which silence is impossible. Water carries voice the way stone carries weight — completely, and without complaint — and the sea is small enough, as seas go, that nothing in it is out of earshot of anything else. Everything hears everything. The hiss of the floor vents, the tick of settling shells, the passage of every swimming body, the digestion, the courtship, the dying: all of it arrives everywhere, layered, continuous, a standing architecture of noise that the inhabitants live inside as fish live inside water, which is to say without noticing, until it stops.

It stops perhaps twice in a generation. Far off, through four hundred kilometers of basalt, the crust settles — the same settling, the very same event, that rolls through the dry dark of the Hollow as a groan felt in the sternum, that strains the crystal seams there until they let go their pale blue flicker. Here the event arrives as the Toll. The whole sea rings. Not a sound in the water so much as a sound of the water, every drop of it struck at once, a note held for minutes, the black surface at the crown standing up in slow geometric patterns as the sea takes the vibration and folds it and folds it again. Everything living falls silent for it — the only silence the Well ever contains — and so the Toll rings through a hush of its own making, one long note in an ocean holding its breath. In one world the settling of the crust is seen, barely, by beings who keep an organ for nothing else. In the other it is heard, totally, by beings who can hear nothing else while it lasts. It is the same word, spoken once, into two rooms.

Begin at the bottom, because everything here does.

The floor of the Well is torn along the old fault, and out of the tears comes the sea's whole economy: hot water freighted with sulfur and iron, blooming upward in plumes that would be visible as shimmering columns if visibility were a thing this world had ever needed. Around each vent stands a garden — banks of rooted, wavering tube-growths, white as the inside of stone, packed shoulder to shoulder, farming the chemistry in their own tissues. They are the Well's equivalent of the rock-films, and like the films they neither hurry nor cease. But their surplus does not lie where it is made. It sheds, and rises, and disperses, and dies, and falls — and so the Well has snow. A perpetual descent of pale matter drifts down through the black water at every hour of the endless unmarked day, food falling out of the dark like weather. In the dry sister-world, a drifting mote is an event; a chandelier-creature may wait a season for one touch. Here, abundance falls on everything equally, asked or not. The Well never learned to wait. It has never had to.

So its creatures spent their inheritance elsewhere. The drifters — soft bells and ribbon-bodies and things like unraveling knots — do not anchor and hope, as the chandeliers do; they ride. They have given themselves to the great overturning, rising with the vent-plumes, crossing the crown, descending the cold walls, a whole life spent making one slow circuit of the sea and arriving, at the end of it, where they began — chasing the water's warmth around a circle the size of the world, and catching it, forever. The quick things here are quicker than their dry cousins, and blinder in a different way: in the Hollow, a wake stands in the dead air for hours, and the past can be read like a page; in the Well, the currents erase everything at once. The water keeps no record. Whatever happened a moment ago is gone. The quick things hunt by sound instead, snapping at the noises of the smaller, and the medium's forgetfulness runs all the way up the chain of being until it arrives at the Listeners, in whom it became the whole shape of a mind.

The Listeners are the Well's builders, and they build nothing. They cannot. They are swimmers, long as several men laid end to end, smooth-bodied, finned, handless — the water gave them no purchase and no fire and no reason for either, and so the entire line of development that filled the Hollow with blades and cairns and ground-smooth walls simply never opened to them. What they have instead is the skull. A Listener's head is mostly instrument: chambers and lenses of dense fat and bone shaped for the making and catching of sound, and with it they see. Their clicks go out and come back carrying the shape of everything — the gardens, the snow, the far walls, each other, rendered in echo at a fineness their dry cousins could not dream of. To a Listener the Well is not dark. It is brilliant. They carry their light inside their skulls and spend it as voice, and the sea is lit by their spending, and the deepest strangeness a Listener could be asked to imagine is a world where light is a thing that happens to you — rare, external, unearned — rather than a thing you make.

Handless, they keep nothing outside their bodies. No marks, no tallies, no caches, no shrines. Everything they are is held in song, and held is the exact word: their maps are sung, their laws are sung, their genealogies, their vent-garden territories, their dead. A song that goes one generation unsung is gone — not faded, gone, as absolutely as if it had never been composed — and so the center of their culture is custody. Every Listener carries a portion of the repertory and owes it forward. A Listener dies twice: once in the body, and once, perhaps centuries later, when its song is performed for the last time by the last one who kept it — and the second death is the one they grieve. Their greatest virtue is fidelity of repetition: to sing a thing exactly as it was received, changing nothing, adding nothing, the same devotion the dry cousins pour into striking each new blade identical to the oldest. Neither people knows the other repeats. Both would recognize it instantly as prayer.

And the repertory has taught them something the water itself could never have told them, because the water forgets: their world is leaving.

The oldest songs — the deep ones, sung in a drifted language performed now purely by shape, meaning long since worn away — describe the crown. And the crown they describe is wrong. The echo of the gas lens above the sea, the resonant signature of that little hollow riding on the water, comes back in the ancient songs shorter, tighter, closer than it sounds today. The ceiling has not moved. The sea has fallen. Drop by drop, over the whole depth of their remembered history, the water has been going into the stone — pressed out along the old fault, into the fracture the sea first arrived by, migrating away through the rock at the pace of a millimeter a century. The Listeners can hear their world descending. They live, in this, exactly opposite to their dry kin: the Hollow's people have a language with no future tense because nothing there is becoming anything; the Listeners have almost nothing but future, an ending they can measure, a sea audibly on its way to somewhere else. Where the water goes, the songs do not say. But some of the singers think they know, because of the wall.

There is a place, on the western wall of the Well, where the rock hums.

Not always. At intervals — irregular, unforecastable, sometimes twice in a season of the great turning, sometimes not for years — a low patterned tone arrives through the stone itself, faint, at the very threshold of even a Listener's hearing, sustained, deliberate, and unmistakably *made*. It is not the Toll; the Toll rings everything, and this comes from one direction only, out of the solid west, out of four hundred kilometers of basalt. It is, though no being in either world will ever know it, the mourning of the builders. Of all the Hollow's sparse language — the struck obsidian, the knuckle-taps, the glassy rain of conversation — only the lowest register crosses: the hums too deep for air, palm pressed to stone, the words the builders send through the bones of the world when one of them dies. The taps and clicks perish in the rock within a few kilometers. Grief alone survives the distance.

The Listeners call it, in the click-language, something close to *the dry voice*, or in the older songs, *the one who cannot swim to us*. They have made the western wall their one fixed sacred place — the only location in their whole liquid, turning world that does not move — and there is always at least one of them at it, body laid flat along the stone, the length of itself pressed to the rock like a hand too large for any door. When the dry voice comes, the Listener at the wall does the only thing its kind has ever done with anything: it answers. It sets its whole skull against the basalt and sings into the stone, in the lowest voice it has, a long descending phrase that the repertory has kept unchanged for as far back as the songs go down.

And the answer crosses. Through the fault, through the dark kilometers, up into the crystal seams of the Hollow, where a builder sits at vigil with its slowed heart and its palm flat on the stone — and feels, a little while after every mourning, always the same little while, a slow throb rise through the rock that is not the strain of the seam and is not the settling of the crust. The builders have a click-phrase for the interval, the reliable pause between the hum sent and the pressure returned. It translates, as nearly as anything can, to *the width of the grief*. They believe the dead take that long to arrive in the stone, and then answer. Every builder that has ever been mourned has been answered. The theology is wrong in every particular and correct in the only one that matters: something on the far side of the dark hears them, and replies, and has never once failed to.

The Listeners' own account runs the other way. They sing that beyond the western wall there is a being, or a country, or a sorrow — the songs disagree — that grieves and cannot sing, has never learned to fill its silence, owns nothing but taps and hums and one blue thread of light. And they sing that the sea knows it. That this is where the water is going: out through the stone, toward the dry voice, a drop a century, the whole patient ocean emptying itself in the direction of the grief — and that the duty of the Listeners is simply to answer at the wall, every time, without fail, until the water arrives and the far one is no longer dry, however many turnings of the sea that takes. It will take tens of millions of years. Somewhere in the Hollow, in an age no builder's tallies will reach, a seam will weep its first drop into the warm dead air, and fall, with the patience of a closing eye, and land — the future tense, delivered at last, in a world whose language declined to expect it.

A visitor who waited at the western wall — and waiting is a fluency both worlds share, though only one of them knows the other is practicing it too — would eventually feel the stone begin, very faintly, to mourn. And would feel the great body beside them press closer to the rock, and gather its voice down into its lowest register, and give the answer that has always been given, exact and unchanged, into four hundred kilometers of dark: *kept. kept. kept.*

Two bubbles of one breath, sealed apart before the world's skin cooled, each certain it is speaking to its dead, or to its god, or to the stone itself — and each, all this time, simply speaking to the other: the held breath and the swallowed sea, keeping each other company across the width of the grief, in two languages that will never meet, saying, as nearly as anything can be translated, the same one thing.
