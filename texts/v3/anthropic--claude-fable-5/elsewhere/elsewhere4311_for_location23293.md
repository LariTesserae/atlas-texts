# The Shadow Flats

There is a place three days down the leeward side of that same mountain, out past where the clouds give up, where the land opens into a basin ringed with dark rock and floored, mile after mile, with white.

The whiteness is salt. The basin has no outlet — whatever water finds its way in stays until the sun takes it back, and the sun has been taking it back for longer than there have been people to watch, leaving the mineral behind in a crust that lies over the flats like something between snow and stone. From the black rim rock you can see the whole of it at once, blazing, and see too, far up behind you, the notch in the ridgeline where the green sits: a small dark saddle, nearly always wearing its cap of cloud. The flats people can point to it from anywhere in the basin without looking. It is the fixed thing in their sky, the way a harbor town has its lighthouse.

They understand their situation precisely, and their name for their home says so. The flats lie in the mountain's shadow — not the shadow of its light, which barely reaches them, but the shadow of its rain. The weather comes off the far lowlands wet, climbs the windward slope, and gives up everything it carries at the saddle, where the grove combs it out of the air leaf by leaf. What comes over the top and pours down the lee side is the same air with the water taken out of it: warm, fast, and empty. The brightest place anyone here has ever heard of is named for a shadow, and the people find this neither ironic nor sad. It is simply the arrangement. The trees drink first. What is left comes down to the flats, and what is left is exactly what the flats need.

## The Dry

It is never wet here. The flats people have a word for *wet*, but it does not describe a condition; it describes a location — it means, roughly, *at the spring* — because wetness does not otherwise occur, any more than dryness occurs on the saddle except beside a fire. The two towns have, without ever discussing it, built their languages as mirror images around the same absence, each keeping as a place-name what the other keeps as the whole surrounding world.

The air on the flats takes moisture the way the grove's air gives it. Bread left uncovered is stone by evening. A gutted fish from the spring pools is travel food in two days without smoke or effort. Rope frays here not from rot, which is unknown, but from the salt working into the fibers and cutting them grain by grain from the inside — which is why the best rope on the flats is grove rope, carried down the relay in coils, its lowland reputation earned in the wells and shade-rigging of the basin, where a good coil is inherited, named, and argued over in wills.

The water the people drink is old. It rises in brine at the bottom of wells dug into the flats — rain, they say, that fell before anyone, sank through the rim rock, and is coming back up salted with everything it learned down there — and it rises sweet in exactly one place, a spring at the foot of the eastern escarpment, cold, ferned, and green, the only green in the basin the eye can find. The grove drinks water that fell that morning and will never think of it again. The flats drink water older than the town, and count it.

## The Quiet

The basin is nearly silent nearly always, and a visitor's first hour here is mostly the work of learning to think inside the silence — which turns out to be harder than learning to think inside a roar, as the handful of grove-born who have married down onto the flats will tell you. They describe it as a pressure. Some of them whistle to themselves for years, softly, just to have something in the air.

But the silence has a structure, and the flats people live inside it the way the grove lives inside its noise. The crust itself speaks. All day it drinks the sun, and all night it gives the heat back, and as it cools it contracts and fractures, crystal by crystal, in ticks and pings and occasional long singing cracks that travel out across the miles of white like something skating away in the dark. This is the basin's drone note, its equivalent of the grove's falling water: the sound of the flats letting go of the day. Children fall asleep to it. Elders can tell from it, lying in the dark, how thick tomorrow's harvest will rake.

And there is the speaking hour. At dawn, before the sun has warmed the flats, the cold air lies flat along the white and carries sound the way ice carries it — a voice pitched low travels farther than anyone can see. For that one hour the basin is a single room. News is called from settlement to settlement across miles: births, deaths, which pans are ready, whose well has gone bitter. The young do their courting then, songs sent across the flats to households a half-day's walk away, answered or pointedly not answered, the whole basin listening and pretending otherwise. Then the sun comes over the rim, the air begins to shimmer and rise, and sound dies within thirty paces, and the basin goes back to being a thousand small rooms until the next dawn. Grove people conduct their whole lives at a shout and hear nothing. Flats people speak just above a whisper and, for one hour a day, hear everything.

They wake, as the grove does, to what is wrong — but on the flats what is wrong is sound at all. Nothing moves at night. A footstep on the crust carries half a mile. A mother here sleeps through the ticking of the salt as her counterpart on the saddle sleeps through the frogs, and comes upright in the dark at a single crunch that has no business existing.

## The Wind, and the Flood

The basin's harvest weather is made two days' walk above it, on the saddle, by the same storms that heave the grove's canopy like surf.

When weather drives hard against the windward slope, the air that finally spills over the top and falls down the lee side arrives on the flats hours later as a hot, hard, perfectly dry wind — the basin's great engine. The flats people's name for it translates as something like *the wrung wind*, and the older name, still used by pan-mistresses in a certain dry tone, means *what the trees did not want*. Under the wrung wind a flooded pan that would need eight days to crust will crust in three. So the basin's watchers — and it has watchers, old ones, as qualified in their instrument as the grove's are in theirs — spend their days reading the mountain. They watch the cloud cap on the saddle: how it stacks, how it tears, which way its torn edges stream. A cap that boils up dark and ragged by midmorning means wind on the flats by evening, and the word goes out in the speaking hour or by flag from the rim: flood the pans now, rake early, weight the shade-cloths. The basin's daily decisions are read off the grove's weather. The same storm pays both towns in different coin — up on the saddle it is a limb down, a lowering, a funeral-flavored feast over the only dry firewood the grove ever has; down here it is the crust coming in fast and clean, and everyone raking by starlight. Neither town plans a thing without consulting the other's sky.

And once in a decade, more or less, the arrangement fails in the other direction: a storm too big for the mountain to wring dry shoulders over the top with its water still in it, and it rains on the flats. The basin floods. The pans drown, the crust dissolves, the wells sweeten and spoil, and for a season the flats are a vast blinding lake an inch deep, the mountain reflected in it upside down. A stranger would call it ruin. The flats people call it the turning. The flood stirs the deep brine, dissolves a generation of dust out of the salt, and lays everything down again purer than before; the pans that come back after a turning make the whitest cakes anyone will see until the next one. The damage is periodic and the repair is periodic and neither is an emergency. Like the grove, the basin lives in a churn of breaking and mending — it has only stretched the rhythm from weeks to decades, the way everything here is the saddle's life slowed down and dried out.

## The Pan People

They are lean where the grove people are barrelled, long-legged from distance walked on the flat, and they move with an economy that grove people find eerie and flats people find obvious: in the heat, over the glare, you spend motion like water. Their eyes live in a permanent measuring squint, netted with pale lines where the creases keep the sun out, and their hands are not water-wrinkled but salt-cracked, seamed white at every knuckle, tended nightly with fat. They go covered — loose robes, wide hats woven from the spring reeds — and shod always, in thick sandals soled with layered hide, because the crust that a grove child's bare gripping foot would love to read will open a bare sole like a blade. The grove is touched with the whole body; the flats are touched carefully, through tools, and read with the eyes across miles.

The salt they make is the salt. The cakes that come up out of the clouds on porters' backs to the saddle market on the fifths — the baskets the grove haggles over so ferociously — began as brine in these pans, were crusted by the wrung wind, raked, broken, molded, and stamped, and passed up the relay hand to hand through the valleys. Down the same relay comes the grove's answer: rope, dried fruit, cane, smoked things that taste to flats children like the idea of weather. The two towns have never needed each other's company. They have needed each other's surplus for as long as either can remember, which between neighbors is a warmer thing than it sounds.

## The Slow Work

The central profession of the flats is the pan floor, and like the grove's shaping it is a profession whose best work outlives its workers.

A pan is a shallow field of clay, and a young pan leaks. What makes a great one is decades: layer after layer of clay puddled, dried, salted, and trodden, season upon season, until the floor cures into something with the temper of glazed stone, holding its brine to the last inch and giving up its crust in broad unbroken plates. The famous floors of the basin are eighty and a hundred years old, begun by women whose granddaughters now rake them, and each carries its history in its levels the way a shaper's cords carry hers in knots. When a pan-mistress dies, her floors do not pass to her heirs so much as her heirs pass to her floors.

Her name outlives her another way too. Every household stamps its cakes with its mark, pressed into the salt while it is still damp in the mold, and the marks travel where the people never will — up the relay, over the saddle, down the far valleys, two weeks' walk into lands the flats know only as rumor. A mark that has run true for three generations commands its price from strangers who could not point to the basin on the clearest day of their lives. The grove's dead are famous in doorways and stair-treads; the basin's dead are famous in kitchens a hundred miles away, one thumb-sized stamp at a time.

## Water, and the Owing of Water

Sweet water here is never wasted; it is kept, carried, measured, and above all *lent*. The spring belongs to no household — it is the one thing in the basin that everyone owns by owning none of it — but the carrying of it is the town's quiet ledger, gourd by gourd, exactly as the grove's ledger is kept in embers. To walk water out onto the flats to a household whose well has gone bitter after a turning is to be owed something unnamed and unforgettable, and the basin's worst insult, delivered rarely, translates roughly as *one who would let you drink your well*. Neither town knows it uses the other's grammar. Both would be pleased.

At the spring itself, in the cool of the ferns, stands the one tree.

It is a fig — grey-barked, broad, its trunk beginning, in its slow way, to braid. Generations ago a salt-walker came back from the wet journey with a fruit from the saddle wrapped in leaves, and planted its seeds at the only place in the basin that could hold them, and one took. It is kin to the thirty on the mountain in the plainest sense, and the basin treats it exactly as the grove treats its old hollow tree: exempt. No branch is cut, no root trained, nothing hung from it but children, who climb it in defiance of no rule anyone will admit to making. It is the only shade for a day's walk in any direction and the only place on the flats where you can stand and hear water moving, and flats people visiting the grove for the first time report the same disorientation in reverse: they know this tree — and then they look up, and there are thirty of it, roaring.

## The Children

The children of the flats keep the pans, and their war is with the birds.

When the pans are flooded and the brine goes thick, it turns pink with the small life that thrives in it — a bloom of tiny shrimp, and over the shrimp a black shimmer of brine flies whose pupae the children harvest in floating skims, a rich, oily, storable food the basin prizes and strangers must be coaxed to try twice. And to the same table come the birds: tall, stilt-legged, absurd, and pink from the same feast, arriving in wading thousands after every flood. The children are posted against them, or with them — the line is not clear — and the engagement has the character of a rivalry between neighborhoods rather than species. The boldest birds are known individually and named, all insultingly. Certain children are plainly held in higher regard by the flock than certain adults, which the adults pretend not to mind, and if that arrangement sounds familiar it is because children and thieves come to the same terms everywhere, and the basin's birds and the grove's monkeys differ mostly in the color of what they steal.

Flats children learn the basin as bearings, not routes: the notch, the rim gap, the spring green, the well flags — a compass rose of far things, where a grove child learns a braid of near ones. *Up*, in their speech, does not mean the sky. It means the mountain, the cloud, the elsewhere. The sky is just the roof.

## The Dead

The dead are given to the salt. A grave here is cut into the crust at the basin's far margin, in ground too bitter for any pan, and the salt does with the dead what it does with everything: it keeps them. They do not rot. They dry and whiten and remain, and the flats people find this exactly as unremarkable as the grove finds its own arrangement — the person is, in the town's flat phrasing, *in the salt now*, and it is meant literally and is true. The grove's dead dissolve upward into leaf and rail and are handled daily by their descendants. The basin's dead lie unchanged at the white edge of things, visited, familiar, patient. One town's dead become its living material; the other's become its permanence. Neither would trade.

They remember the grove longer than the grove remembers them. The flats still tell, in detail, of the time the tree people came down out of the clouds for stone — generations back, a party from the saddle at the black rim, cutting the dense dark rock and hauling it up the long way, slabs for grinding and hearths. Every hearthstone glowing in the grove tonight, every slab under every hand-mill, is rim rock from the basin's shoulder, and on the saddle this is no longer a story at all, just the floor. On the flats it is still an event with names in it. Small places keep accounts differently, and the basin, which counts its water, counts its history too.

---

Travelers going the other way — down from the saddle, out of the wet — report the same experience, reversed. The roar thins behind them as they descend through the cloud: first the voices go, then the weavers, then the frogs, until somewhere below the mist the last drip falls off the last leaf and they walk out into widening dry light and a silence that leans on the ears. They see the flats half a day before they hear them — a white blaze filling the bottom of the world, the pink smoke of the birds drifting over it — and what they hear first, standing on the black rim at evening, is their own pulse. Then the sun goes off the salt, and out across the miles the crust begins to tick, and ping, and send its long singing cracks skating away into the dusk, and the basin is speaking after all, in the only voice it needs.

And far up behind them, in the notch, the cloud cap is stacking for tomorrow's wind — the grove's storm, the basin's harvest, one weather with two hands — and both towns, without a word between them, are already getting ready.
