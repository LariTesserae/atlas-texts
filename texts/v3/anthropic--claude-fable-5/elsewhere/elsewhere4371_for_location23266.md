**Underfall**

You come down to it through cloud, and the cloud is the last made thing you will touch for a long time.

Below the cloud line there is a country — a real one, of the old kind, rock and soil and weather that answers to the sun. It sits at the foot of the mountain the Works stands on, in the permanent shade of the overcast, because the cloud the deck floats above is Underfall's ceiling and never lifts. No one in Underfall has ever seen the Works. No one in Underfall has ever seen the sky. Between the two lies the cloud, a grey unbroken roof a few thousand feet up, glowing faintly all day with the light it is holding back, and the light that gets through comes from above, weakly, in one direction, the way light is supposed to. Everything in Underfall casts exactly one shadow. The shadow is soft and honest and lies where you'd expect. People here do not know that this is remarkable. Their cousins uphill do.

The descent tells you everything about the relation between the two places before you reach the bottom. It begins as a stair of bright bolted steel clamped to the mountain's flank — deck-made, mill-finished, sharp enough at the edges to cut a careless palm. A thousand feet down, the treads are worn. Another thousand, and the steel gives out and the stair continues as steps cut into living rock, their corners rounded. Lower still it is only a path, and then a hollow way trodden shoulder-deep into the hillside, and then mud. You descend through the whole history of surfaces, from the just-made to the long-used, and when you step off the last worn stone onto actual dirt — dirt, the thing the deck has none of, not one spoonful in a region the size of a river valley — you are in Underfall, and the first thing you understand about it is the opposite of the first thing you understand up there.

Underfall is *old*. Door sills are hollowed by boots. Coins are worn smooth of their faces. Well ropes have grooved the well stones. The names of families have been shortened by generations of saying them. Nothing here has an edge, because everything here has finished happening and has been being handled ever since. The crews at altitude break the deck's edges by hand, deliberately, as civic labor. Underfall got its edges broken by time, for free, and has forgotten it was ever sharp.

The walk down the mountain takes a day. The walk back up takes a day and a third, always, for everyone. The Underfall traders shrug at this the way they shrug at the overcast. Uphill is uphill twice, they say. Everything about that place costs extra.

**The Fall**

The town is named for what arrives.

Kilometers up, at the deck's rim, the drainage channels stamped into all that plating reach the edge and let go. What leaves the rim as runnels arrives at Underfall, hours later, as a fine perpetual drizzle along the mountain's western skirts — rain that was never weather, water that never fell from a sky, cloud-harvest and condensate, mineral-flat, tasting of nothing at all. It falls in a broad soft band you can walk into and out of, and the band never moves, and under it the country is green in a deep, drenched, moss-heavy way, and beyond it the country is ordinary. Underfall's farms crowd the edge of the fall the way farms elsewhere crowd a river.

And the water does not come down alone. It carries the grey.

Swarf — the machining dust of a works that is still, slowly, machining itself — washes off the deck with every afternoon's condensation, goes over the rim in suspension, and sifts down through the cloud. On wet days it arrives in the drizzle and silvers the puddles. On the rare still, dry days it comes down on its own, a glittering descent, the deck's snow finally finding a country that knows what snow is for. It works into the soil. Turn a spade of Underfall earth and it sparkles. The bread here has a faint shine to its crust that visitors remark on and residents can no longer see. The oldest fields, closest under the fall, have taken too much grey over the generations and gone sterile — *salted*, the farmers say — and the only thing that grows on them is a pale wiry plant that came downhill at some point and needs nothing but metal dust and moisture, and which the farmers cut for goat fodder and regard with the suspicion due to an immigrant that thrives where nothing local can.

Through the middle of the town runs the river, and the river is called the Filings, and it is grey-white, milk-colored with metal. Panning it is Underfall's oldest trade. Sievers work the gravel bars in lines, sluicing out the deck-dust, and the smiths melt it down into small bright ingots that never tarnish — not in a season, not in a lifetime, not in the flooded strongbox of a house that burned eighty years ago, from which the ingots came out shining. Underfall metal is famous three valleys in every direction. But the smiths are careful with it. They alloy it thin, one part in ten — *enough to remember, not enough to refuse* is the smiths' rule — because a tool of pure deck-metal never dulls, never rusts, and never wears in. It stays exactly as made. A knife of it is called a *stranger*: it will not take the shape of your grip, it will not darken with your years, it is permanently new, and no one in Underfall keeps a stranger long. They are sold downriver to people who think an edge that never softens is a virtue. Underfall knows better. Underfall is a town founded entirely on the belief that softening is the point.

**The Weather of Sound**

The second thing, and the defining thing: Underfall is loud, and nothing in it makes the noise.

The Works at altitude runs in near-silence — the crews walk among laboring machines and hear only water. Underfall could have told them where the sound went. It goes downhill. All of it. Every clang of every hatch, the wing-clatter of the one pigeon stated ten thousand times, the screaming of the swifts, the deep hum, the strokes of the great pistons, every unguarded sentence ever littered into that untrustworthy air — the deck defers it, and the mountain shrugs it downward, and it arrives at Underfall late, sorted loosely into fronts, and falls on the town like weather. The town's founders, whoever they were, understood what they were living under and named the region for it in their oldest records, which do not say *Underfall* but *the Delivery*.

The sound-weather has its climate. There are *hatch days*, all iron percussion, doors closing in their thousands, doors that closed last month. There are *wing days*, when the air is a soft continuous thunder of pigeon-clatter and people speak up without noticing. There are *hum days*, when the deep frequency of the whole Works arrives at once and stands in the valley like humidity, and the dogs lie flat, and old people say their bones ring. And there are *voice days*.

On voice days the littered speech of the crews comes down. Fragments, mostly — half-sentences spoken in open aisles by people who knew better, weeks or years or decades uphill of now. They arrive at conversational volume, from no direction, in the middle of the market or over a supper table: a woman counting goats, a man cursing a bearing, a child reciting a knock-name aloud to learn it, someone saying *wait, wait for me* to somebody long since not waited for. The etiquette above is to hear such deliveries out and never answer. The etiquette below is stronger: you *write them down*. Every household keeps a slate by the door for it. A delivered voice unrecorded is considered a letter thrown away, and Underfall does not throw away mail, because Underfall long ago understood what it is.

It is the memory of the Works. All of it. The deck keeps nothing — it is too young, too busy becoming; its sound leaves, its dead are absorbed into the plating, its geometry revises itself and forgets its own previous opinions. But the sound comes *here*. Underfall's archive — a long low building of river stone, roofed in unrusting deck-metal, called the Letterhouse — holds shelf upon shelf of transcribed deliveries going back eleven generations: the acoustic diary of a place that cannot hear itself. The Listeners who staff it work in shifts at the Ear, a horn-shaped coomb in the mountainside above the town, faced with hammered bright metal, where the sound-fronts pool and can be taken down clean. They date each fragment by internal evidence, cross-file the voices, learn to know the dead of the deck by timbre. It is said, and it is true, that Underfall knows the history of the Works far better than the Works does. The crews know it too. It is half of why they come down.

The trade, on the face of it, is simple: salt and grain go up the stair, bright steel and glassy goat-hide come down. But at the Letterhouse a second trade happens, quieter. A crew trader, spotted at once in the market by the faint fan of plural shadows he casts even in this single light, will finish his salt business and then walk to the archive and ask, in the flat voice of a man pretending the question is administrative, whether anything has been delivered in a particular voice. And the Listeners will check the ledgers, and sometimes there is something — a dead mother counting stitches, a dead friend laughing at a joke whose first half never arrived — and the crews pay for their own past in grain, and carry it back up the mountain folded in oilcloth, and Underfall considers this its most honorable export: selling a forgetful country its dead.

The crews have made a custom of it, at the far end. It is called *posting yourself*. A crew elder who feels the end near will walk out into an open aisle — the one place where speech is otherwise littering — and stand in the bright sourceless light and say everything. Everything unsaid, settled, confessed, forgiven, named. It is addressed, by convention, *downhill*. The air takes it, and defers it, and some year or decade later it falls on Underfall in the drizzle, and a Listener on shift at the Ear takes it down word by word, weeping sometimes over the griefs of strangers, and it is shelved, and it waits. Above the cloud, the dead persist a while as luminous sketches walking their lines. Below the cloud, they persist as pages. Each place finds the other's afterlife slightly horrifying, and each is quietly glad the other exists.

**Census of the Loud**

Where the deck's creatures are few and iterated, Underfall's are many and emphatically single. The crows especially — huge, glossy, deafening, mobbing the sound-fronts as if the noise were edible, screaming back at deliveries in a way the townsfolk consider vulgar but enjoy. The pigeons of Underfall are ordinary pigeons, plural, individually unremarkable, audibly cooing, and the town takes an odd civic pride in this. The one pigeon of the Works has never been seen below the cloud line. There is a standing debate in the Letterhouse about what would happen if it came down — whether it would scatter into a true flock, or the town's flocks would collapse into it — and the debate stays theoretical, and everyone prefers it that way.

The goats are the kinship, and the grievance. Eleven generations ago — the ledger survives; the Letterhouse keeps it — a herder took a flock of thirty up the stair, following the rumor of high grazing, and never came down. The deck's goats, the borrowed ones, the ones that stand iterated on the housings and survive their own slaughter, are held in Underfall to be *that flock*, still owed. The debt is re-recorded each generation in a small ceremony that has long since stopped being angry and become something more like a family keeping a place set at table. And once — once, ninety years ago, attested by four households — a goat came down the stair alone, chipped-horned, deck-grey, and walked into an Underfall paddock and stayed. From that day it was in one place at a time. It grew old. It died, singularly and permanently, and was buried, and the grave is kept, because it is understood in Underfall to be the grave of a creature that chose an ending over a habit, and the town honors that above almost everything.

Which brings us to the Wrung.

The plural shadows of the crews do not survive down here. Below the cloud, in honest light, the extra selves dry out — the pale second and third shadows fade over months, then years, until only the first remains, dark and single. The crews call the process *being wrung*, and mostly they avoid it, cutting their trading trips short, hurrying back up before the light can simplify them. But some come down on purpose. Every year a few crews descend the stair with no salt-money and no goods, only a bundle, and take rooms, and stay, and let it happen. They are the ones who have felt the deck taking an interest — who have been drafted once too often, seen themselves tending a line across a trench while verifiably elsewhere, and understood that at their death the Works would keep their habits and wear them, luminously, until it had learned them all the way into the plating. Some crews find that a comfort. Some find it a theft. The ones who find it a theft come to Underfall to be wrung, and to die *single*, and to be buried in soil — soil, the one thing the deck cannot offer at any price — under a stone that will weather, in a grave that will soften, in a town where the dead are permitted to stop.

Underfall's own restless ones go the other way, of course. Every generation a few of its children look at the worn door sills and the finished hills and want edges, want the bright wrong noon, want to matter to a place that is still deciding what it is — and they go up the stair and join the crews and grow their second shadows, and their families speak of them the way coastal towns speak of those who went to sea. The two places are each other's escape. It is the oldest current between them, older than the salt.

And there is one channel that is neither late nor uphill-costly, one honest wire through all the deferral: the stone. The knock-language of the crews travels through the deck's plating, into the mountain's bones, and down — and rock, unlike air, is faithful. Scattered through Underfall are the talking stones, outcrops of the mountain's living root, and a hand or an ear laid on them reads the pipe-traffic of the Works in real time, on time, every time. Underfall children learn the knock-language alongside their letters. Cousins converse through four thousand feet of mountain, one knuckle on a stanchion in the bright silence, one ear on a boulder in the drizzle. It is the only conversation between the two countries that happens in the present tense, and both of them treat it, without ever saying so, as sacred.

**The Long Quiet**

One page in the Letterhouse is empty, and it is the most visited page in the archive.

Three generations back, the sound-weather stopped. For nine days no front arrived — no hatches, no wings, no hum, no voices. The town stood in a silence so total that people report their ancestors whispered in it, though there was no one to disturb. Then the weather resumed, mid-clang, as if nothing had happened, and the backlog never arrived; those nine days of the Works' sound are simply missing, undelivered, owed.

The Listeners have two doctrines about it and no way to choose. The first holds that it was a lapse — a gap in delivery, the sound of nine days lost in transit, still falling somewhere else or nowhere. The second holds what the crews' elders, consulted by knock through the talking stones, are said to have answered with a single long unhappy rhythm: that on the deck, work is sometimes posted *early*. That the Long Quiet was a delivery from ahead. That what fell on Underfall for nine days was the sound of the day the Works finishes — every flywheel stopping, every piston settling, the whole self-manufacturing country arriving at last at its final cubic meter and going still — and that the town has already heard the end of the world uphill, out of order, filed forward, waiting for its date.

The empty page is kept for it. The Listeners dust it. No one writes on it. Underfall, a town built on receiving everything late, holds in its archive exactly one thing that may have arrived too soon, and treats it with the caution due to a letter you are not yet the person addressed by.

**Evening, Under the Fall**

So: a siever comes up from the gravel bars at dusk with a day's grey in her pans, through the drizzle that was never weather, past fields that glitter where the plough turned them. The overcast is dimming from within, evenly, the cloud giving up its borrowed light. Her shadow walks with her, one shadow, dark and certain, and she has never once thought about it.

A sound-front is arriving. She hears it come up the valley like rain moving across water: hatch-iron first, then the soft thunder of a single bird's ten thousand wings, then, threading through it, a voice — a man's, elderly, patient, saying something about a bearing and the right grease and the right amount, a shift-instruction from some year uphill of now. She stops at her door slate and writes it down, all of it, dated tonight, delivered from whenever. In the morning she will carry the slate to the Letterhouse, where it will be filed by timbre, and perhaps matched, and perhaps, someday, sold back up the mountain to whoever finds they cannot live without it.

Inside, her son has his ear against the hearthstone, which is a talking stone, which is why the house was built here. He is laughing at something. Two districts above the cloud and four thousand feet of mountain away, his cousin — second shadow just budding, their mother says, pale as milk — is knocking the day's news into a stanchion, and it is arriving now, in the present, the one current between the two countries that runs on time. Around the house, the stale voices of the Works fall gently on the roofs of Underfall like the grey falls on the fields, and are gathered, and are kept.

The boy knocks back: *goodnight, goodnight.* The stone carries it up. Somewhere in the bright unmoving noon of the deck, a knuckle on a pipe receives it.

The drizzle continues. The Filings runs milk-pale in the dark. The archive holds its one blank page, and the graves soften, and the ingots in the strongboxes shine on regardless — and over it all, unseen, unheard except belatedly, the Works labors at becoming permanent, while Underfall does what it has always done beneath it, which is the other half of the same long work: it receives, it records, it wears things smooth, it lets things end.

The deck is where the world is written.

Underfall is where it is read — late, in the rain, by lamplight, out loud.
