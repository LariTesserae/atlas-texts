## The Answering Wood

The land here has never once finished happening.

You come to it from the open country by walking in under the sky. That is the honest way to say it: the cloud sea the plateau looks down upon has, from beneath, an underside — a ceiling of grey vapor two thousand meters up, fixed as architecture, going on for days in every direction — and at some point on the road toward the mountains you pass beneath its eave, and the light changes, and you are indoors. The brightness of the outer world does not follow you in. What replaces it is a pearl dimness that comes from everywhere and nowhere at once; the whole ceiling glows faintly and evenly, and so here, too, shadows barely exist. But where the high country abolished them with too much light, the wood abolishes them with too little. Stacked one atop the other, these are the two shadowless countries of the world, and they arrived at shadowlessness from opposite directions, like a couple agreeing on something for entirely incompatible reasons.

The hum finds you before you understand it. Somewhere in the first hour under the ceiling you notice that your sternum has been informed of something your ears have not — a pressure at the very bottom of hearing, below weather, below distance, a floor under all other sound. It does not rise and it does not fall. Cup water in your hands and stand still, and the surface shivers into fine standing rings. This is the plateau, of course, though almost no one down here would say it that way. Two kilometers up and a country away, the rim of the world is roaring through its stone flutes exactly as it always has, and by the time that shout has come down through the cloud and the miles it has been worn to a single vast underlying tone, the fundamental of the region, the note beneath the wood. Children born here have never heard silence and would not recognize it; what they call silence is the hum alone, with nothing on top of it. Travelers who walk out from under the eave into the bright outer lands report that the quiet there feels wrong — thin, unfloored, like a room with the ground taken out — and that for weeks they keep listening backward over their shoulders for the missing note. The people of the wood have a word for this ailment. It translates, roughly, as *unstrung*.

The wood itself is everything the high country is not, which is to say: unfinished. The soil is black and deep and busy, made of everything that has ever died into it and not yet done being used. The trees are enormous, soft-barked, sleeved to the crown in moss; deadfalls rot with visible enthusiasm; fungus proceeds through the understory like slow weather. Nothing has reached its final shape. Nothing intends to. On the plateau you walk across a surface that finished becoming before anything had a name; down here you walk through a place that is being revised hourly, and your footprints are erased — grown over, washed out, eaten — sometimes before you have finished making them. The plateau remembers everything and contains nothing. The wood contains everything and remembers it differently.

At the wood's inner edge stands the wall. The fluted cliffs, seen from below, are a darkness that goes up into the roof of the world and does not stop: you can never see the top, not on the clearest day — and *clear*, here, means only that the ceiling has lifted a little and the drizzle has paused. Down the wall's face come the threads. The rim far above weeps after every cloud, and that weeping gathers into falls that drop two thousand meters and mostly never arrive as falls at all; the wind up the face atomizes them, and they come down instead as a permanent fine rain along the cliff-foot, and as streams — white ones. Cliff-milk, the people call it, water gone pale with stone-dust and the guano of a hundred thousand birds, and the trees that drink from those streams are the giants of the wood, visibly richer, visibly darker, a green wall matching the grey one. All the water near the cliffs tastes faintly, unmistakably, like the inside of a bell — diluted, softened, but the same taste that waits in the drinking bowls on the roof of the world. The wood has been drinking the plateau's water since before either had a name.

And it has been giving the plateau its breath. This is the wood's own wind, and the second thing you learn to feel here: a soft, ceaseless drift of air, everywhere, always in one direction — toward the wall. It is gentle. It would not turn a leaf. But it never stops and it never turns, because the standing river of air that pours over the rim above must come from somewhere, and it comes from here: the whole atmosphere of the lower country is being drawn, patiently, eternally, up the face of the cliffs and through the flutes. Smoke in the wood leans toward the mountain, every fire, every hearth, every season. Half the trees make plumed seeds that fly only upward, surrendered to the intake, and some of those seeds ride the wall all the way over the rim and land on the high stone and die there in the brightness — though not, perhaps, all of them; there are botanists' heresies about where the cushion plants of the plateau originally came from. The people of the wood put it more simply. *The mountain speaks with our breath*, they say. Every exhalation down here — every sigh, every song, every last word at every deathbed — drifts wallward, is drawn up the dark face, and is spent, eventually, through the flutes, as some infinitesimal fraction of the shout. Nothing said in the wood is ever entirely finished being said. It is merely still in transit.

The strangest thing about the wall, stranger than its size, is that it does not echo. Every child of the wood discovers this and never quite stops being unsettled by it. Stand at the cliff-foot and shout, and nothing comes back — not a slap, not a whisper. The intake takes it. The sound goes up the face with the air and is gone, folded into the note. Cliffs everywhere else in the world answer; this one keeps. The children have a dare about it, standing at the wall and shouting their names into the stone and waiting through the silence after, and the dare has an old formal ending that the children say without knowing its weight: *the mountain heard you*. Which is, of course, the same verb the climbers use — the one that means dying, and confessing, and standing at the rim with your mouth open. The wood is where that verb was made. It was made at the bottom of the wall, by people listening to their own voices not come back.

You leave the wood, if you leave, by walking out from under the eave, and the hum thins behind you step by step until the world goes unfloored. Most people who were born under the ceiling turn around.

Begin with what falls, because what falls is where the two countries actually touch.

It rains birds here. Not often — the wood is vast and the falls are scattered — but steadily, forever, at the fixed rate of everything connected to the mountain. When a rimwing dies in the standing wind above, it folds and goes down into the cloud, and some minutes later, somewhere in the wood, a white shape comes through the canopy with a sound the whole forest recognizes: a long tearing hush of leaves and then the single soft conclusion. Feathers follow for days, sifting down out of the ceiling, so that every fall has an afterlife of slow white drift. The wood is fertilized from the sky. It always has been. The great trees of the interior, away from the cliff-milk streams, are great because of what has landed near their roots over ten thousand years; the whole forest is, looked at coldly, the plateau's graveyard, and a well-kept one.

The keeping is done first by the fungus. There is a decomposer here, thread-fine and pale, that specializes in what falls, and it glows — faintly, greenishly, for decades after its work is done. Walk the wood at night and the floor is scattered with dim constellations: this smudge a bird that fell before your grandmother; that ring, wide and soft, something large from long ago. The forest floor is a star map of deaths from the sky, and it is legible. The people can read it. Their word for the glow translates as *where it landed*, and their old routes bend respectfully around the brightest patches. High above, the living colony converses in glints of sunlight; down here, its dead persist as glints in the dark. The two vocabularies have never been introduced.

The keeping is finished by the sextons, and the sextons are the wood's great secret, though they walk about in the open. They are birds — heavy, dark, flightless, standing thigh-high, striding the understory on thick legs — and they are rimwings. Cousins, anyway: the descendants of birds that came down through the cloud alive, long ago, landed, ate well, and stayed. Down here there was no roar to erase the voice, and so the sextons' line kept what their sky-kin lost: the syrinx, whole and working. The silent people of the wind have singing relatives on the ground, and the relatives sing *to the falls*. A sexton hears the tearing hush in the canopy from a great distance and goes to it at a run, and stands over what has landed, and before it takes the first bite it delivers a long, low, carrying phrase — the same phrase, every sexton, every fall, an inheritance as fixed as the foxes' roads on the roof of the world. The people call them the answerers. It is not sentiment; it is a job description. Something falls out of the silence, and something in the wood says so, aloud. The wood is named for them, though the name has since collected other debts.

The streams have their own diaspora. When the cloud-weep runs heavy on the plateau, the drinking bowls overflow, and their water goes over the rim carrying, now and then, the eggs of the pale provincial shrimp — down the wall, into the cliff-milk, into the pools of the wood. Their descendants live here still: larger, tinted amber by richer water, citizens of streams instead of nations of stone. They will never meet their ancestors thirty meters apart in the bowls above, and the bowls will never know they founded anything. But the wood-shrimp have kept one habit intact through every generation of the descent — they orient. Each one holds itself at a fixed angle to the tremble the hum puts into the water, the same angle their frozen forebears hold to the roar. Two populations, two kilometers of cliff and cloud between them, all facing the same way. None of them knows this either.

The people, then. They are the same people — the ones who climb; the ones with the forehead-language for the roof of the world — but down here, at home, they are rich in the one currency the mountain confiscates: sound. Their villages are the most melodious places imaginable, and every note of that melody is in one key. It has to be; the hum is the drone under all their music, under all music in the wood, including the music no person makes — the frogs, the night insects, the sextons' phrase, all of it settled over ages into consonance with the fundamental, until the entire biophony of the region plays in the key of the mountain. A stranger with an instrument tuned elsewhere is winced at, politely, and retuned by supper.

Their black glass comes from a seam at the cliff-foot, exposed where an old fall-stream cut the ground, and every ascent takes a core of it up the long routes to the rim, where it is knapped and the flakes are left — paying the mountain in edges, they say. The cores come back down as heirlooms, called spoken stones, because they have been inside the roar, and are believed to keep a little of it: hold one to your ear and you hear a thin, seashell hiss. Every child in the wood has done this. It is most of them's first idea of what the top of the wall is like, and it is not far wrong. The red ochre comes from a bank above the river, the same red for as long as red has meant anything here, and it was carried up the wall once, at least once, by someone who pressed a hand to the underside of a perched boulder in the brightness and left the only insisted-upon mark in that whole finished country. The wood does not claim that hand. When pressed, its people say only that the paint was theirs, and the meaning was between the hand and the noise.

Those who go up to be heard come down changed, voiceless for weeks, and the wood has made an office of it: the returned are the only people permitted to sit judgment in disputes, precisely because for a while they cannot talk over anyone. And those who do not come down — there are houses in the wood kept swept for them, hearths laid and never lit, on the standing chance. And there is the other custom, the one the wood does not explain to visitors: that in the weeks after someone has climbed and shouted their worst thing into the roar and returned, their family watches the canopy. Because sometimes, near the house, something falls — a feather-drift, a bird, once in a great while a small knapped flake that can only have come off the rim — and the verb for this is the wood's own, the mirror of the mountain's: not *to be heard*, but *to be answered*. Whether the mountain answers, or gravity does, is a distinction the wood has examined and declined.

Between the two countries lies the third, where nothing lives: the cloud itself, two thousand meters of grey, the wall between the rooms. The rimwings sleep in its upper margins, half a brain at a time. The dead pass through it in seconds. The weep-water spends an hour in it becoming rain. It is the only part of the world that belongs to both places and neither, and both peoples — the fox-road keepers of the top, the singers of the bottom — regard it the same way: as a floor, and as a roof, and as none of their business. The note passes through it without asking. It is, in the end, one household. The plateau is the room where the word is spoken; the wood is the room where it lands; and everything alive in either — bird, fox, shrimp, midge, sexton, singer — is furniture the syllable has arranged around itself over a very long time, in two matched sets, each ignorant of the other, each pacing its heartbeats to the same pressure from opposite sides of a cloud.

So it is perhaps worth recording that within living memory the hum has learned to waver. On certain nights — a few a year now, and more than there were — the floor of all sound develops a slow beat, a swelling and settling, as if the held note were breathing; the standing rings on the hush-pools blur and re-form; the eldest listeners sit up in the dark with their hands flat on the ground. And on those same nights, though no one in the wood has ever counted a fall to explain it, the sextons can be heard far off among the glowing constellations of the forest floor, delivering their long low phrase over and over into the empty dark — answering, as is their office and their inheritance, something that has not landed yet.
