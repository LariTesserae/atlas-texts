**The Gathering Ground**

It is a high place, and high places receive. You come up to it the way weather does — from a long way off, with the whole sky behind you, and no particular intention of staying.

The moor climbs for miles, thinning as it goes — heather giving way to bent grass, bent grass to moss, moss to nothing — until the ground simply runs out of soil and the stone comes up to meet the sky directly. What lies at the top is a pavement. There is no better word and no worse one: an expanse of bare grey limestone, acres of it, laid out in great flat blocks — clints, the old workers called them — separated by a network of deep slots, the grikes, some a finger wide, some wide enough to lose a sheep in, all of them running down into the dark of the stone like the seams of an enormous floor. It looks exactly like the floor of a building whose walls were an idea nobody followed through on. The sky stands on it. There is nothing else up here tall enough to argue.

The light does not arrive tired, the way it does in low places. It arrives first. Whatever the day is doing, the pavement gets the original — the unbroken wind, the unfiltered sun, the rain before anything else has had a chance at it. There is a great deal of rain. The hill is inside a cloud one day in three, a grey soft grazing presence that moves over the clints slowly, beading everything, and passes on. And it is here that you meet the first strange thing about the place, which is that all this water comes to nothing. There are no pools. There are no streams. An hour after the hardest rain the pavement is dry to the touch, pale, faintly warm if there's sun, as if the storm were a rumor. Stand out in a downpour and you can watch a puddle form in a dish of stone and lose its argument in real time — shrinking at the edges, taken from below, gone. The rock here is thirsty. It drinks everything the sky can carry, without hurry and without exception, and gives back nothing at all.

Or rather — nothing *here*. Stand at the southern lip of the pavement, where the ground breaks and falls away down-country, and two miles off and a long way below you can make out a small grey socket in the shoulder of the hill: a round basin, walled in stone, holding a dull pewter light. Twice a day, when the temperature shifts, it fills with mist, quietly, like a cup being topped up by someone considerate. That is where the drinking goes. Every drop the pavement swallows sets out on a journey down through the body of the hill — not rushing, not even flowing in any way a stream would recognize, but seeping, threading, filtering through the packed dark of the stone — and emerges, at last, as the weeping walls of the Sump. The hill drinks at the top and weeps at the bottom, and between the swallow and the tear lies something close to a human lifetime. The rain falling on your shoulders this morning will arrive in the basin as seep when everyone currently alive up here — and nearly everyone down there — has been replaced. The Gathering Ground is the Sump's weather, running a hundred years late. The two places have never been out of touch and have never once been in the same century.

The second strange thing is the sound, or the underside of it. The wind is constant and does most of the talking, but walk out across the clints and the floor itself joins in: the blocks are not all seated, and some of them clink underfoot, a dry crockery note, block against block — a floor that comments when you cross it. And the pavement is *hollow*. Not everywhere, and never dramatically, but kneel at the mouth of a grike, put your ear to the slot, and under the wind you will hear it: water, thin and continuous, trickling downward somewhere inside the stone. Not arriving anywhere. Leaving. It is the sound of the day's weather being forwarded. The Sump, at its end of the hill, is a resonator — a bowl that holds each falling drop like a struck glass. The Gathering Ground is the opposite instrument: a lid, a sounding-board over a departure, and everything you hear through it is already on its way somewhere else.

The third thing is the works.

At the pavement's western edge, where soil begins again, stands the shell of an engine house — proper masonry, two storeys once, roofless now, its window arches full of sky. Beside it a chimney, still whole, forty feet of tapering stone that the wind plays like a flue. And between them, set flush in a collar of worn coping stones, a circle of riveted iron plate, bolted down: the capped mouth of the winding shaft. The coping stones are cut to the same pattern as the ones ringing the flooded circle in the Sump's floor — the same masons dressed both collars, top door and bottom door of the same descending house. From here the old pipe route runs down the slope toward the basin, marked now only by a stitch-line of tumbled supports in the grass; the pipe itself was carted off for scrap generations ago, all but the last riveted length, which still climbs the Sump's inner wall and ends in a sheared flange, reaching up toward a connection that no longer exists. Up here was the head of the works. Down there was its drain. The men walked the two miles between them daily, in all weathers, keeping both ends of the same long breath.

The engine that stood in this shell was the twin of the one below — bigger, prouder, the one that got its picture in the county paper. It was stopped, stripped, sold, and melted within a decade of the closure. Its sibling in the pump house, knee-deep and unreachable and beneath anyone's economic notice, was left turning. There is a story the shepherds still hand around, worn smooth as a coping stone: that the last engineman, on the last day, banked what needed banking, oiled what needed oiling, and walked down to the basin and stood a while, and did not stop the low engine — that he said, or is said to have said, that a place like that shouldn't be left without a heartbeat. The story is unverifiable and the basin has never contradicted it. There is a slab near the chimney with his initials and his dates. The rain that falls on it joins the seep like all the rest, and by the pace of the hill's swallow, the water of his own lifetime — the storms he stood pumping through, the winters he kept the fires against — is what fills the Sump's channels *now*. The old white olm that leans its full length against the engine block down there, feeling the thump come up through the iron, is resting in the rain of the years when the man was alive. Whether that constitutes company is not a question either of them is equipped to raise.

One more thing the works left behind, and this one arrived after they were gone. The enginemen, to settle a wager about where the hill's water went, once tipped a keg of green dye down the largest of the eastern swallow-holes. Nothing came of it. The wager was argued, adjusted, inherited, and eventually forgotten, and the men died in their turns. Some sixty years after the closure, deep in the layered felts of the Sump — those wet stacked pages that record everything and report to no one — there is a single thin band of faint viridian. One season, one page. The question arrived a lifetime late; the answer was filed on time; there was no one left to inform except everyone who lives there. The hill keeps its correspondence. It simply does not keep it fast.

**Who Lives in the Gathering Ground**

The honest answer is: almost no one, and the almost is doing a great deal of work.

Start with the lichens, because they are the only residents with tenure. They lie on the clints in slow continents — grey-green, rust, a sulphurous yellow-map species that grows a hand-span in a century — and they are the pavement's gentry the way the felts are the Sump's: the founding population, the keepers of the oldest arrangements. But where the felts eat the water's minerals in the dark, the lichens live on the two things the hill does not swallow — light, and the rain *in passing*. They tax the weather a thin percentage on its way through, and that is all they ask, and it has been enough for longer than the works, the shepherds, and possibly the pavement's current shape. The felts keep minutes no one will read. The lichens keep, if anything, a ledger of exposure — every year of sun and scour entered as a millimeter of spread — and the two archives, top and bottom, describe the same centuries without once consulting each other.

Then the grikes, which are the strange soft heart of the place. Lower your face to one of the wider slots — hand-span across, six feet deep — and you find, packed into that shelter, a garden. Hart's-tongue fern, glossy and improbable. Wood sorrel. Herb robert, red-stemmed, flowering in the dimness. It is a forest's understory with the forest omitted: plants that belong beneath old trees, living beneath old *air* instead, in slots where the wind cannot reach and the light arrives softened and the humidity holds steady off the trickling dark below. Every grike, in other words, is a small rehearsal of the Sump — dim, damp, sheltered, quiet, occupied — and the pavement carries hundreds of them, narrow prototypes of the basin scored across the summit like first drafts. The Sump itself is only the oldest and most successful grike the hill ever opened: a slot that kept widening, kept deepening, until it could hold a population and a heartbeat. The Gathering Ground has been practicing the Sump for a very long time. It is still practicing.

In the grike gardens live the snails, and here the kinship stops being a figure of speech. They are banded, bright-shelled, eyed, and quick by the standards of snails — and they are family. Long ago, in floods or by the carelessness of swallow-holes or down the shafts of the working years, some of their line went below and stayed: gave up the eyes as unnecessary luggage, gave up the colors as an expense without an audience, took up the mineral crust and the year-long circuits of the mats, and became the herds of the Sump. Two branches of one house — the one that stayed in the light and the one that emigrated downward and stopped writing. Though "stopped writing" is not quite fair. Everything up here forwards itself down eventually; the branches correspond constantly, in the only currency the hill accepts, which is water, a lifetime in transit.

The birds are lodgers, not residents, and know it. Wheatears nest in the engine-house walls, flashing white as they go, and scold anything that crosses the pavement. A pair of ravens uses the chimney top as a watchtower and the updraft along the southern lip as a private amusement. Jackdaws own the chimney's interior and have for generations, their nest-sticks accumulating down the flue in strata a felt would respect. In summer there are skylarks, hung invisibly in the light, and in the wet months the golden plover crosses over with its single far call, which is the loneliest sound the hill produces and possibly the loneliest it has heard. All of them leave when the weather says so. The Gathering Ground holds this against no one. Leaving is the local custom; the birds are simply fluent in it.

The wind is the landlord. It is the one resident the Sump has never met — the basin's air is still as a held breath, and always has been — and it lives up here permanently, sits on everything, edits everything, decides what may stand (the chimney, the ravens, the lichens' slow continents) and what must keep its head below the clint-line (everything else). The grike gardens exist entirely in its blind spots. In a sense the whole population does.

The capped shaft is the resident the census approaches carefully. Nothing lives *in* it that anyone can name — but the mine beneath the hill breathes with the barometer, as old workings do, and the shaft breathes through its bolted lid: a thin exhale at the rim when the pressure falls, a long sipping inhale when it rises, a faint whistle at the bolt-holes in the worst weather. Put your palm on the iron on a dropping glass and you can feel the house below letting its breath out. It is the same house whose lower door stands open and drowned in the Sump's floor — the same darkness the basin's shy clerks serve, carrying finished things down to it, rent paid, no questions pressed. Up here the front door is bolted and the tenant uses it only to breathe. Deliveries, as ever, go round the back.

And the rain. If the Gathering Ground has a congregation, it is this: the endlessly arriving, never-staying population of the water itself. Each shower is a crowd that gathers on the pavement, mills briefly, and files down through the grikes and the swallow-holes into the dark — and each crowd, unlike every other crowd in the world, is *expected somewhere*. The Sump downstream is not a possibility for this water; it is an appointment. The seep that beads the basin's walls, the channels the shrimp keep time in, the films the amphipods live pressed against, the pools that ring like struck glass — all of it is up here first, falling on the clints, hearing the wind, touching the lichens' ledger for one taxed instant, and then going down to become, decades hence, the entire substance of another place's life. Drought up here is a dry patch on the Sump's walls a hundred years from now — an evacuation the amphipods will conduct, forgivingly, without ever knowing whose sky failed them. A generous decade of storms on the pavement is a fat century in the channels, arriving on creatures whose great-great-grandparents were alive for the weather. The Sump forgives its walls their lapses. It has never once known whom it was forgiving.

So: who lives in the Gathering Ground? A gentry of lichen taxing the light, gardens hiding in the wind's blind seams, the branch of a family that kept its eyes, birds who are honest about leaving, a landlord made of moving air, a bolted door that breathes, a slab with initials on it, and a congregation of rain that is never the same rain twice. Almost no one — because the place's whole vocation is *through*. The Sump, at the far end of the hill's long swallow, is a full house in permanent session: everything that arrives there belongs, everything finished is filed, nothing is ever adjourned. The Gathering Ground is the same establishment's open door. It receives everything and keeps nothing, and what it lets go becomes, given a lifetime of stone and darkness, everything the low place is.

They have never seen each other, except as a socket of mist glimpsed from a lip of pavement. They have never been out of touch for an hour in ten thousand years. Stand on the clints in the rain, with the wind taking the words out of your mouth, and you are watching the Sump's future arrive — this exact water, this morning's grey crowd, bound down into the hill to come out weeping in a bowl that will not learn of today for a hundred years, and will file it when it does, and feed it to the herds, and thump on. High places give; it is all they know how to do. Low places gather. The stone between them is the length of the promise, and the promise, by every page the felts have kept, has never once been broken.
