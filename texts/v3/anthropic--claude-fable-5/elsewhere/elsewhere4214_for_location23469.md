**The Keeping Room**

You do not reach it. Not by accident, not by intent, not at all. It had a door once — a second slot in the same hillside, on the colder flank, curtained by the same roots, opening onto a stair of twenty-three steps cut by the same hands that cut the eleven-meter descent a few hundred meters away through the stone. Then, some centuries back, the hill shrugged. A rockfall pinched the passage shut, and the slow mineral seep that lacquers everything down here has since welded the rubble to the walls in one translucent mass. The door was not blocked so much as healed. The hill has closed over it the way skin closes over a splinter it has decided to keep.

So take the description on trust, the way the room itself takes everything.

Beyond the healed door the passage runs level for a while and then opens into a chamber lower and wider than its sibling — you could not stand upright in a third of it, if you could stand in it at all — and most of the floor is water. A pool, black past any blackness the Stilling Room can manage, because the Stilling Room's dark is only air and this dark has depth. Knee-deep at the edges, chest-deep at the middle, and *exact*: the level has not varied by the width of a coin in living memory of anything, because the room is plumbed. Water arrives down one wall as a slow braid — a seep gathered from the fissured sandstone above and behind, which is to say gathered in part from a chamber you have already visited, where it fell once a minute into a basin no one ever finds. It leaves through a throat somewhere below the surface, and at long intervals, when enough has gathered, the throat swallows. That swallow is the room's great utterance. Between swallows there are smaller ones: drops falling from the soda-straw stalactites that fringe the ceiling, each straw holding its drop until the drop is ready, each landing striking a slightly different note from the pool. The Keeping Room, unlike its sibling, is an instrument. It has more to say and no one to say it to, and it says it anyway, at the pace of everything here, which is to say almost never, which is to say constantly.

The cold is the same cold. Not similar — the same, to the degree, because the hill has one temperature at heart and both chambers sit in it like two hands in the same pockets.

On the floor of the pool, visible to nothing, lie the artifacts: five of the smooth gray objects, two spheres, a spindle, a shallow bowl-form, and one shape that resolves differently depending on where your hand starts. All of them whole. That is the first great difference between the rooms, and the one that matters most: nothing here is cracked. The gift comes through undiminished, and the water takes it and spreads it, the way water spreads everything, evenly and without opinion. So there is no mounding here, no leaning, no garden. Instead there is the film — a pale coating, breath-thin, that covers every drowned surface: the stone, the artifacts, the lower steps of the old stair where they descend beneath the waterline and keep going. It is thickest near the gray objects and grades outward like frost retreating from a warm window, and it is the floor of everything that happens in this room, though it would fit in a cupped palm if you scraped the whole chamber's worth together.

Around the pool runs a shelf of dry stone, a few steps wide. On the shelf, at shoulder height, three niches are cut into the wall.

They are not empty.

**The Residents of the Keeping Room**

Begin with the film, because everything else stands on it — or rather eats it, which here is the same thing.

The film is the mirror of the moss, and the mirror reverses. In the Stilling Room, abundance builds upward: the living stand on the dead, the dead hold the water, the floor is generations deep and everyone who ever lived there is still on site and still employed. Here, nothing accumulates. The film grows and is grazed, grows and is grazed, staying always exactly as thin as breath on glass, because the second great difference between the rooms is this: the Keeping Room has animals, and animals do not let anything lie still long enough to become architecture. The dead here do not become floor. They become each other. The population of the Keeping Room, at any moment, is only the living — a leaner census than its sibling's, and a faster one, if *fast* can be said of anything in this hill.

The grazers are the room's hurry. Small translucent crustacean things, eyeless, white as the film they feed on, thousands of them drifting and ticking over the drowned stone like a fog of punctuation. They live a year or two, breed in the dark, die into each other, and constitute the only thing in either chamber that could be called a crowd. Among them, statelier, moves a lineage of pale snails whose individuals spend decades circling the pool on grazing paths their ancestors etched into the film before them, so that each snail travels roads that are older than it is and will outlast it — a palimpsest written in appetite, revised by appetite, never finished.

And above the crowd, the individuals. There are four of them.

Call them swimmers, since no better word has been down here to argue. Pale, four-legged, finned along the tail, blind in the thorough way of things whose ancestors spent their eyes generations ago as the mosses next door spent their green. Each is very old. They hang in the black water for days at a time without moving — in this room, motion is an event, and a swimmer turning over is the largest thing that happens in most months. They eat the grazers, rarely, needing almost nothing in this cold; they breed almost never. None of them has ever seen the door open. Probably. The eldest complicates the word *probably* — it hangs mostly in the deepest water above the spindle-shaped artifact, and it has been the eldest for so long that eldest has stopped being a rank and become a location.

Under the western ledge, in a hollow of stone, there is an egg. One. It has been an egg for eleven years and will be an egg for some years more, developing at the pace of everything else here, which is the pace of stone. The room's entire future is currently the size of a fingernail and in no hurry whatsoever. Where the Stilling Room is learning scarcity through one dying fern, the Keeping Room is holding one promise, and the symmetry is not an accident, because — as will become clear — very little between these two rooms is.

On the wall above the waterline, where the inlet braid keeps the stone wet, there grows a single patch of pale moss the size of a spread hand.

It is the only moss in the chamber, and it is *kin* — descended from spores that rode down the twenty-three steps on the clothing of the tenders, back when there was a between, back when the same feet wore the bowls into both stairs in the same lifetimes. It is the Stilling Room's only relative anywhere, a cousin colony stranded on the far side of a healed door, living off the artifacts' gift at one remove through the water that wets it. It has held its handspan of wall for centuries without spreading — the film owns everything below the line, the dry stone above suits nothing — and it will hold it for centuries more: an heirloom, a hostage, proof in ivory-green felt that these two rooms were once one errand.

Then there is the water, which is the landlord here rather than a tenant, and which is where the accounting of this room stops being local. Follow the braid up through the fissures and part of it is the Stilling Room's seep; the drip that falls once a minute into the basin you never find falls, eventually, *here* — filtered through a few hundred meters of sandstone over years or decades, arriving mineral-heavy and patient to join the pool. The fog of a visitor's breath, settled long ago onto moss in the garden chamber, may in some future decade pass down through the stone, through the braid, through the film, through a grazer, through the eldest swimmer, and out the throat's swallow toward whatever lies below both rooms. The two chambers do not share a wall. They share a bloodstream.

And through that same fissured stone runs the traveler.

The fungus surfaces here as it surfaces there — a thread, pale on pale, rare enough to seem like a visitor. It is not a visitor. It laces the film, traces the drowned artifacts, threads the moss-patch on the wall, and continues into the sandstone itself, following water through cracks no wider than a hair, and somewhere in the blind middle of the hill it is continuous with the threads that lace the moss-mattress of the Stilling Room. Whether it is one individual is a question that does not survive contact with what it is. What can be said is this: the Keeping Room, all five of its givers whole, runs a surplus. And the thin subsidy that arrives in the Stilling Room's failing corner — the trickle that slows the great fern's dying beside the cracked sphere, the charity or appetite or plumbing that no one there can source — comes from *here*. It is carried through a few hundred meters of solid dark at the speed of growth, a tax the Keeping Room does not know it pays to a grief the Stilling Room does not know is shared. The healed door closed the passage to feet. It closed nothing else.

The tenders, of course, live here too, in the way the long-gone live — but here they left more than wear. The three niches on the dry shelf hold three objects, glazed over by centuries of mineral seep until they are more flowstone than anything: one shape that might once have been a figure, one that might have been a coil of something, one that seems to be only a stone, though it must have been a stone that mattered, to earn a niche. Whatever stood in the Stilling Room's empty niches stood, at the end, in these. The tenders gathered what they kept and they kept it here, and then the door closed behind them — and whether the hill did that on its own or was asked to is a distinction the room eroded away long ago, along with the meanings of the three shapes, along with everything else it did not strictly need. At the pool's edge there is a place where the stone is smoothed in the shape of a seated person, worn by someone who came often and sat long, feet perhaps in the water, in the dark, listening to the straws drop their notes. The shelf has held that shape ever since, the way a chair holds the shape of a body just risen — except the rising happened before the door healed, and the chair is in no position to notice, and would not hurry to if it were.

So: who lives in the Keeping Room?

A film that is eaten as fast as it gives; a fog of small quick lives and a guild of slow snails on inherited roads; four old swimmers and one egg; a handspan of exiled moss; five whole givers under black water; three kept things becoming wall; a smoothed absence at the pool's edge; and the water, and the traveler, neither of which stops at the property line.

And that last clause is the true answer, because stand back far enough — stand, say, where the hill stands — and the census of this room and the census of its sibling collapse into one ledger. Two chambers at one temperature, joined by one water and one traveler, keeping each other without knowing it: the garden that hoards its dead and the pool that spends them, the room learning want and the room holding surplus, the fern going out over centuries and the egg coming on over years. One tenant, two rooms. It does not think, probably. It does not need to. In the Stilling Room it says its one thing once a minute; here, at longer intervals, the throat swallows; and between them, offset like the two sounds of a heartbeat, the hill keeps time twice.

Someday — an age from now — one of the five givers here will crack, and the flow through the stone will turn and run the other way, and the garden will keep the pool for a while. The rooms will take turns. That is what the traveler is for, perhaps, or what it has become for, which down here is the same thing.

Until then: the braid descends, the film renews, the eldest hangs above the spindle, and under the western ledge the future sits at fingernail size, in the dark, in the cold, in no hurry.

It is in no hurry to be found, either. It does not need finding. It has, in the strictest sense, already been kept.
