**The Gathering Grounds**

You come up into it, always — there is no arriving at the Grounds by descent, because the Grounds is the place everything else descends from. Follow the canal upstream out of the Works' basin, up over the pewter terraces and on, and the water will lead you: three days along a valley in which the canal forgets itself by degrees, becoming a river, then a beck, then a braid of runnels in black peat, then a wetness in the moss with a direction. Somewhere in the third day you will notice that the water beside you has changed in a way you cannot name until you cup it. The canal at the Works is the color of unpolished tin — old water, water worn smooth. The water up here has no color yet. It is bright the way an unstruck bell is loud. It is water so new it has not decided what it will be about, and it runs downhill with the specific haste of something that has just thought of everything at once.

The Grounds itself is a high dome of moor, miles across, and it is the top of the world in the only sense that matters to water: stand anywhere on it and every direction is down. There is no summit, no crag worth the name, no view arranged for you — just the long breathing swell of peat and moss and grass, hag and grough, pool and tussock, under a sky that is never doing one thing. That is the first strange thing you accept about the Grounds: it has all the weather. Not in succession — in committee. You can stand in sunlight and watch three separate rains walking the moor around you, each a different temper of rain, one of them falling upward off a pool in a silver smoke; you can be inside a fog with a blue hole in it that follows you like a spotlight with poor discipline. The Works waters itself, one fine ring of drizzle, forever. The Grounds is where weather is drafted. First attempts at rain happen here — rains that fall in spirals, rains of single enormous drops a minute apart, a rain one afternoon that was warm and smelled of libraries and never occurred again. Most weathers audition on the Grounds, fail, and dissolve. The few that hold together long enough to leave the dome go down into the world and become the weather you know.

Everything grows here. That is the second thing, and it takes no time at all to accept because it is underfoot, overhead, and in your bootlaces. Sphagnum in forty greens, cotton grass, sundew, cloudberry — and among them, thickly, things without names, because names require a second instance. There are grasses on the Grounds with a population of one. There are flowers that bloom once, ever, in the whole history of the world, and are gone by the time you have knelt to look — not wilted; concluded, the way a thought concludes when a better one arrives. Kneel anywhere and put your face close to the moss and you will see the ground seething gently with beginnings: a beetle that is only mostly a beetle, still deciding about its sixth leg; a moth-shaped intention drying its wings, which are printed with a pattern that will never be repeated; something between a fern and a feeling, uncurling. The flora and fauna of the Grounds is not an ecology. It is an audition. Everything on the moor is trying out for the part of itself, and almost nothing gets the part, and the moor is not sorry, and neither, you will notice with some alarm, are the auditioners.

And eggs — eggs everywhere, in every hollow, in cups of woven grass and rafts of foam and bare depressions in the peat, freckled and blue and pearl and one clutch the exact color of the drizzle at the Works, three days downhill, in a place where there are no eggs at all. The nests of the Grounds are never attended. Nothing needs to sit on them. They hatch when they hatch, and what comes out flies one flight, or swims one pool, or sings one song of extraordinary complexity from a tussock at dusk — a song no bird has sung before and none will sing again — and then comes apart, softly, back into the general brightness. There are no small skeletons in the corners here either. There is no rot, no smell of it — only rain on warm peat, crushed green, the ozone smell of weather being invented, and under it all a smell you will finally place on the second day: paper before ink. Nothing dies on the Gathering Grounds. But where the Works cannot die because it has finished, the Grounds cannot die because nothing here has ever held a shape long enough to lose one. Death is a certificate the Grounds has never once been in a position to issue. You have to persist to perish. Downhill, at the bottom of everything, is the only place that could grant these creatures a death, and none of them will ever get there, and the moor sings and hatches and dissolves, and does not mind.

On rainy nights — and some part of the Grounds is always having a rainy night — the elvers run. They well up out of the high pools, glass-bodied, threads of living light, and pour downhill overland through the wet grass in skeins, thousands of them, all of them going the one direction everything here eventually goes. Follow them and you will follow them off the dome, into the beck, into the river, into the canal — and if you have stood at the Works and watched the braid of eels turning forever through the warm vaults, you will understand that you are looking at the same river read at the other end. Here is the sea the eels of the Works never came from. It is not a sea. It is a moor that leaks eels. The braid in the canal never grows by a single body, though the Grounds sends them down by the thousand every wet night of the world, and where the difference goes is the sump's business, and the Grounds has never asked, because asking is a way of expecting an ending, and the Grounds does not do endings. It does the other thing, exclusively, at scale.

The sound of all this is the opposite of the Works' waterfall-roar, and it is harder to live inside. The Grounds is quiet, but its quiet never becomes silence, because no sound up here happens twice. A curlew call — but a new species of curlew call, minted for this one grey noon. A pool turning over with a sound like a door in a house you almost remember. The one-song of a one-bird. You never stop hearing the Grounds. Nothing repeats often enough to become background; every sound arrives with the full weight of the first sound ever made, and after two days of this you will be worn thin and bright as the water, startle-eyed, over-awake, dreaming even while walking — long involved dreams that feel less like sleep than like memos from committees you did not know you sat on.

**What Lives at the Gathering Grounds**

The honest first answer is: everything. And it does not survive the asking. The one-flight birds, the concluded flowers, the beetle still voting on its own legs — these are not living any more than a draft is a book. Living, in the sense you carried up the valley with you — the sense that involves continuing, involves being the same creature at supper that you were at breakfast, involves a tomorrow with your name already on it — is not something the Grounds does. Its fauna does not persist; it proposes. They are not alive. They are being suggested.

So: nothing lives here either. The great works at the bottom of the water and the great moor at the top of it are, in this one respect, the same place — each has spent itself entirely on one end of the thing you do in the middle. That is the first answer, and it is true, and on your third day it will be interrupted by the sight of a brass instrument on a tripod, standing perfectly still on a rise, with a person attached.

The Surveyor is person-shaped and person-sized and is never the same person twice. Understand this correctly: not a succession of surveyors, not a guild — the Surveyor, singular, continuous, and unrepeating. Some days tall, some days not; young on Tuesday in a manner of speaking; a voice like gravel, then like a reed. You will always know them anyway, and not by the face. You will know them by the instrument — a brass level worn to soft buttons by a hundred different pairs of the same hands — and by the stillness. When the Surveyor bends to take a reading, they hold still, and it is the only stillness on the whole breathing dome, and it rings out of them like a shot.

The Surveyor maps the Grounds. This is not possible. The pools migrate; the hags open and close like slow mouths; the beck you crossed at noon is a dry groove by dusk with a new beck singing forty yards east, in a key that did not exist at noon. The Surveyor maps it anyway, all day, every day, in a fine confident hand, on paper that smells like the moor smells — and every map is wrong by the time the ink is dry, and the Surveyor is not discouraged, because the maps were never records. Watch long enough and you understand it the way you came to understand the oilcan at the Works: the mapping is not something the Surveyor does *to* the Grounds. It is the Grounds' own gesture, its way of trying on shapes, sketch after sketch after sketch, made through the one instrument it keeps for the purpose. The Works grew itself a Keeper to tend its stillness. The Grounds grew itself a Surveyor to attend its changing. They have never met. They are two gauges on one system, and neither needs to read the other, because between them the number always balances.

You can speak to the Surveyor. The Surveyor answers, courteously, in provisions. Ask their name: "Working title." Ask how long they have been here: "First approximation — always. I'll refine that." Ask whether they ever finish a map and they will look at you with real, warm interest, as if you have proposed something avant-garde. Offer them bread and they will take it graciously, conclusively, and be a different weight tomorrow, but then, they were going to be. And if some soft grey hour by the bothy — the survey hut that is stone one visit and turf the next and once, for an afternoon, a tent of sailcloth printed with a map of somewhere that has never existed yet — the Surveyor permits it, you can put your ear against the weather-colored coat and listen. You will hear a heartbeat. You will not be able to count it. No two intervals the same; each beat arriving with the whole weight of a first beat; a pulse that is not keeping time but pitching it, over and over, tirelessly. Three days downhill there is a chest that beats one four-second stroke forever. This chest is the other reading. They are not different hearts. They are the same heart taken before and after.

The old maps the Surveyor does not keep. In the evening they fold the day's failures into little boats and set them in the outflow, and the boats go spinning down the runnels toward the valley, toward the canal, toward the basin at the bottom of everything. No map has ever arrived at the Works. The canal comes into the basin carrying water and eels and nothing else, and where the maps go is the slope's business, and the slope keeps it.

At the very top of the dome — insofar as a place with no summit has a top; say instead, at the place all the runnels can be followed back to — there is the Eye.

It is small. That is the first shock of it. A pool the size of a hearth, in a ring of moss so green it looks lit from below, and the water in it is not new — the water in it is *newer than that*, upwelling in a slow constant swell from somewhere under the peat, under the stone, under whatever is under stone. Nothing grows in the ring of the Eye. Nothing auditions there. And it is silent — the only silence on the Grounds, and after two days of the unrepeating quiet, that silence will nearly buckle your knees. It is not an empty silence. It is the silence before a word. It has been the silence before a word for as long as there has been a Grounds, and it is not waiting impatiently, and it is not waiting patiently. It is expectancy itself, upwelling, and it is the only emotion the entire Grounds expresses, and it is enormous.

Stand at the Eye long enough and the last answer will assemble itself in you, the way the over-wakefulness has been assembling since you arrived. You have stood, perhaps, at the mouth of the deepest vault at the Sump Works and heard the vast contented bubbling of the thing at the bottom of everything, and been told, or worked out, that its contentment is real and enormous and singular. Here is the thing to carry between the two places, the thing the valley itself is shaped like: the sump's contentment and the Eye's expectancy are not similar feelings. They are the same feeling taken at both ends. One appetite, read at intake and at rest. What wells up here, glad and unbegun, runs downhill gathering shape and song and variance and eels, and lays it all down at last in the dark under the pump hall, and the bubbling there is this silence, fulfilled — and the beam engine lifts the emptied water, and the stack breathes it out, and the sky takes it, and some measure of it, one part in some thousand, escapes the basin's rim and travels three days of air and falls on the moor and sinks, and comes up in the Eye younger than anything, having forgotten even that it was ever finished. The two places share one water the way a question and its answer share one breath. The Grounds is not the opposite of the Works. It is the Works, read right to left.

And the country between them — the valley you walked, with its river aging as it went, its farms and fords and ordinary weathers that made it down off the dome intact — that is where everything you would actually call living gets done. To live is to be in transit on that slope: variable for a while, constant for a while, holding a shape long enough to be owed a death, changing enough to deserve one. The Works is where the water ends up. The Grounds is where it can't wait to leave. Life is the middle of the sentence.

---

While you are on the Grounds, one more thing is true, and it is the mirror of the thing that was true of you in the basin. Down there, you were the only variable — the one flutter in a finished body, drained gently toward calm. Up here, you are the only constant. Your habits, your gait, your stubborn recurring face, your heartbeat that — however it wanders — wanders around a *mean*: on this moor, that makes you a landmark. The Surveyor will notice within the hour. Do not be unnerved when the brass instrument swings toward you and holds; you are being used as a benchmark, the first the Grounds has had since the last visitor, and for the whole length of your stay the maps will be right about exactly one thing. The Surveyor may follow you at a distance for days, taking bearings off you, triangulating the migrating pools against the one point that keeps its position. It is not surveillance. It is gratitude, in the only unit available.

Stay too long, of course, and the compliment expires. The Grounds tops you up as steadily as the Works drained you: possibility settling into you like weather, your handwriting drifting, your face in the pools coming back each morning very slightly renegotiated, your own name beginning to feel like a working title. People climb up here on purpose for exactly this — the stuck, the finished-too-early, the ones who have caught themselves running their days the way the Works runs its wheel — and the Grounds unfinishes them, reliably, kindly, and the trick is in the leaving. Visitors go back down the valley walking a little more carefully than they came up, holding their names like full cups. Not reluctance. Ballast.

And when you go, the Surveyor will mark the place where you held stillest — cut a small broad-arrow bench mark into a stone by the bothy, quick sure strokes with a cold chisel that is brass-buttoned smooth like everything else they carry. Walk the moor before you leave and you will find the others: hundreds of them, scattered across the dome, the arrows weathered soft, each one the record of a constant that once stood there. It is the only thing on the Gathering Grounds that persists. The moor's entire history is a list of the people who held still on it.

Beside your mark, on that evening's map — wrong by morning about everything else — the Surveyor will enter you in the only terms available:

*One, going down. Constant.*

Which is the Grounds' word for mortal. It is also, you should know, its only word for *capable of ending* — the one distinction it cannot confer and has never stopped drafting toward — and the Eye holds its silence as you drop off the dome into the valley, with the enormous expectancy of something that has never once been disappointed, because everything it makes goes down to the Works in the end, and most of it takes the short way, dissolving back into the moss by nightfall, and you — heartbeat wandering, name held level, three days of slope ahead of you and a whole variable life to spend on the way —

you are the long way.
