**The Sinks at Barakh-Doline**

The plateau stands nineteen hundred meters above the sea, on limestone so pale that on overcast days the ground and the sky trade places without ceremony. The air arrives unused. It comes off the high west in long cold reaches, having touched nothing since the last range, and the first breath of it after a lowland journey is a small violence — thin, scoured, tasting of stone and nothing else. People from below breathe it in gulps for the first day, as though trying to get ahead of a shortage.

The storms come up from the east, out of the hot country. They build over the lowlands in the afternoons, tall and ambitious, and they cannot go where they intend. There is a depression out there — you can see it from the rim on rare clear evenings, a tea-colored blur of haze far down and far off, a place where the land dips below the sea and the air sits in it like broth — and the storms that try to descend into it die on the way down, cooked apart by the rising heat before they can deliver anything. So they turn. They shoulder along the rim, gathering their undischarged weight, and they climb, and they break themselves on the plateau instead. Barakh-Doline receives, in effect, another country's weather. The rain that falls here in cataracts — and it falls in cataracts, whole rivers arriving vertically, forty minutes of white noise and stinging cold — was aimed elsewhere and delivered here, the way a letter is delivered to the last address that will accept it.

And then, within the hour, it is gone. This is the first thing the plateau teaches, and it never stops teaching it: the stone drinks. There are no rivers on the surface of Barakh-Doline, no lakes, no ponds, not one. The rain hits the pavement and runs, briefly and everywhere, in sheets — and the sheets narrow into the grikes, the deep dissolved slots between the blocks, and the grikes swallow them, and forty minutes after the loudest storm of the year you can walk the plateau in dry boots, hearing the water going away beneath you, down and down, a diminishing conversation in the rock.

The pavement itself is the country's floor and its architecture both: acres of bare limestone dissected into blocks — clints, the shepherds call them — separated by the grikes, some finger-width, some wide enough to swallow a sheep, some deep enough that a dropped pebble changes its mind twice about what sound to make before landing. The clints ring underfoot. It is a different ring than salt — deeper, more bell than crockery — but a person who has walked on halite pavements would stop at the first step and look down, recognizing a family of sound.

From ten paces the plateau looks lifeless as bone. This is a misreading, and the correction is one of the pleasures of the place: everything green at Barakh-Doline grows *below the surface*. The grikes are the gardens. Down in the slots, out of the wind, in the pockets of soil the rain washes there, live ferns and dwarf junipers and hart's-tongue and small hard flowers, whole shaded corridors of green running for kilometers a meter beneath the barren top. Lie flat on a clint and look down into a wide grike and you are looking into a hanging forest the width of your two hands. The plateau is not bare. It is a garden wearing its stone on the outside.

The dolines are the reason for the name and for everything else. Thousands of them pock the plateau — closed depressions where the rock has sagged or collapsed into the hollowness below, from dish-sized dimples to funnels a hundred meters across, and at the bottom of the great ones, the true sinks: open throats into the mountain. Some sinks take water and you can stand at the lip after a storm and watch a temporary river pour into the dark with a sound like applause. Some sinks take nothing and give only air — the breathing holes, which exhale all winter, a steady column of the mountain's stored warmth that keeps the snow melted in a circle around each mouth, and inhale all summer, drawing the surface air down with a faint continuous sip. The mountain respires on a season-long breath. The shepherds use the breathing holes as calendars and, in winter, as places to warm their hands.

The village sits in a shallow valley between doline fields, built of the plateau and roofed in slabs of it, and everything about it is organized around a single problem, which is the plateau's great joke: this is the rainiest place in the region, and it has no water. The stone keeps nothing. So the village keeps everything. Every roof drains through carved stone channels into a cistern; every dished rock in walking distance has a hand-cut groove leading its puddle somewhere useful; the church roof feeds the church cistern, the school roof the school's, and beneath the square lies the Great Cistern, older than any record, plastered and vaulted and dark, entered by a spiral stair, where the water of a hundred storms stands cold and still and black and is lent out through the dry months by measure. Children are taken down the stair once, young, to see it by lamplight — the village's whole wealth, lying quiet — and most of them remember it the rest of their lives as the first serious thing they were ever shown.

It is loud here the way empty country is loud. The base note is wind — constant, working the grike-mouths like the tops of bottles, so that the plateau in a stiff blow plays a chord too low and broad to be called music, though nobody has a better word. Above that: sheep bells, distributed and unhurried. And underneath everything, for those who have learned to hear it, the sinks. Each has a voice. The shepherds sound them the way sailors sound a bottom: drop a stone, count. A splash at four seconds means the passage below is flooded; a clatter means it is dry; a change from clatter to splash at a sink that has clattered for years means water is standing where it shouldn't and something below has choked, and word goes around, because the underground has weather too, and floods, and the plateau reads its own insides by ear the way certain other towns read their machinery by hand.

Where the water goes was, for most of history, a matter of theology. Then, a hundred and some years ago, engineers came — company men, surveying for a mineral concern with holdings in the hot country below — and poured a barrel of green dye into the throat of the Great Sink and posted watchers at every spring within three days' ride. Nothing. They watched for a season, wrote the experiment off, and left. Forty-one years later, in the depression eleven meters below the sea, at a saltworks built around a stack of hot mineral terraces, one of the terrace pools ran a faint and unmistakable green for three days, and an old man who had been a boy runner for the survey read the color and understood, and wrote it into a log that still exists.

Forty-one years. That was the dye, which travels fast, riding the young water. The water itself takes longer. The hydrologists who came later, with tritium and patience, put the full transit between three and four centuries: the rain that breaks over Barakh-Doline this afternoon will go under within the hour, and will spend the next several human lifetimes descending through the dark of the mountain, and will surface at last in the terraces of Erebh-Saline as hot brine, in a year no one now alive will see. The two places are one machine, three hundred years long. The plateau is the intake. The salt town is the outlet. Neither can see the other except as a blur on a horizon, and between them lies not distance, exactly, but time, laid down through the rock like pipe.

The plateau people know this the way one knows a fact about a distant relative. The storm arrives; the stone drinks; the ledger, somewhere far below and far ahead, is credited. It is the driest knowledge in the world and they hold it lightly, except when they don't. There is a saying here, said flat, the way plateau people say most things: *our rain retires to the coast.* It isn't the coast. But it is below sea level, and it is warm, and the rain never comes back, so the saying stands.

**Who Lives at Barakh-Doline**

Begin, as always, with the oldest residents — and here the oldest residents are dead, and are the ground.

The limestone is a sea. It was laid down as one, an unthinkable time ago: the shells and skeletons of small ocean lives, settling for millions of years into a floor, compacted, lifted, and stood up nineteen hundred meters into the wind. Every clint that rings underfoot is a compressed multitude. The shepherds know this in the ordinary way — the rocks are full of curled shells and lily-stems of stone, and children keep the good ones on windowsills — but the full accounting is stranger. The rain that the plateau drinks is slightly acid, and every drop of it, descending, dissolves a little of the old sea and carries it away in solution: the plateau is being un-buried, grain by grain, its dead exhumed a molecule at a time and taken down through the dark. And at the far end of the machine, three centuries later, where the hot water rises and breathes and cools, the mineral comes back out of solution and is laid down again — as the smooth lips of terrace pools, film by film, white shading to ochre. The terraces at Erebh-Saline, which the salt town rightly calls its oldest residents, are built of the plateau's oldest residents, dismantled here and reassembled there by water and patience. One graveyard is quarrying itself to build another, smaller, warmer one, four hundred years' travel away, and neither town had any say in the arrangement.

Below the surface, in the wet dark the rain descends through, lives the nation nobody sees. The rivers under the plateau have their own inhabitants, and the great ones are the olms: pale as the stone, long as a forearm, blind — eyeless in the full sense, the skin grown smooth over where eyes would be — and patient beyond any patience the surface can offer. An olm can go years between meals, a decade if it must, lying in the black water metabolizing almost nothing, and lives a century or more doing it. The foxes of the salt town have never seen anything green; the olms of Barakh-Doline have never seen anything at all, and are missing nothing they have a word for. They are the plateau's counterpart to the brine shrimp — the patient ones, the holders of the permanent lease — and the resemblance is more than poetic. In the same waters live blind shrimps and amphipods, white and delicate, and these are kin, distantly but truly, to the brine shrimp reddening the pans below: one ancient family that, pressed by whatever presses a family apart, split and took the two waters nobody else wanted — one branch down into the fresh and lightless, one branch out into the bright and lethal salt — and both branches are still there, uncountable generations on, each convinced, presumablyly, that it got the better arrangement. Certain of the plateau's crustaceans, washed too deep, ride the young water all the way down; whether any survive the centuries and the gathering salt to arrive at the far end, no one knows, but the assayer of Erebh-Saline is said to keep, in a vial, a small white claw sieved from a terrace pool, and to decline to speculate.

The sinks have their own dusk. The bats den in the throats of the great dolines, thousands deep in the warm exhaling dark, and on summer evenings they come off the mountain all at once — a soft gray outpouring from a dozen holes, as though the plateau were smoking — and hunt the insects that the storms carry up from the lowlands and strand in the high air. Ten breaths of chaos, then the columns thin into the dark and are gone. It is the same hour that, far below and far in the future's past, the swallows come off the cooling galleries; the two eruptions would recognize each other.

The surface belongs to the wind's clients. Choughs — red-billed, black, acrobatic — own the updrafts along the doline rims and spend a scandalous share of each day simply playing in them, tumbling, recovering, tumbling again, to no purpose anyone has identified. The ravens here, as everywhere, are freelancers: they know the shearing schedule, they know which shepherd is careless with his lunch, they attend lambing season with the punctuality of creditors. On the clints, in the low sun, the vipers lay themselves out along the warm stone in the exact posture of gecko on iron, holding the heat as territory; the shepherds and the vipers keep a formal distance, maintained on both sides, of one long stride.

The sheep are the economy and the clock. They graze the grike-gardens by reaching down into the earth for their grass — a flock at pasture on the bare pavement looks, from a distance, like animals grazing on nothing, heads vanished into the ground — and their bells distribute the hours across the plateau. The shepherds are the plateau's ear-craft, its equivalent of a night shift: they sound the sinks, they read the breathing holes, they can stand at a doline lip after a storm and tell from the tone of the swallowed water how full the mountain is running. It is a listening trade, practiced on the outside of a machine whose inside no one will ever walk.

And here the two towns touch a second time, above ground, absurdly, by rail. Twice a month a train leaves the depression carrying salt — faintly pink, dyed by the smallest lives of the pans, sold at a premium — and some of it comes up-country to the railhead two valleys east, and the plateau buys it, because the cheese here is famous and famous cheese wants good salt. The mineral in that salt rose from the terraces; the terraces are built from the plateau's dissolved stone; the stone was carried down by the plateau's own rain. The schoolteacher worked the loop out years ago on the back of a feed invoice, and now it is the thing said to visitors over the cheese plate, said flat, in the plateau way: *we eat our rain back. It takes four hundred years and it comes home salty.* Nobody laughs, quite. It is not quite a joke.

The people number a few hundred, in a village that has been a few hundred for as long as anyone has counted, and they are shaped by the plateau's one law, which is that everything leaves downhill. The water goes down. The wool goes down. The children go down — at eighteen, by the eastern road, every one of them, always — and unlike the water, some of them come back, though the plateau, unsentimental about its own gravity, does not expect it. There is a warden of the Great Cistern, a position older than the church, whose holder measures the black water weekly by lamplight and knotted line and enters the depth in a ledger that runs back, volume behind volume, four hundred years — which is to say the cistern records reach back exactly as far as the water now surfacing at Erebh-Saline reaches, and a person with access to both books could, in principle, look up the very storms that are arriving down there this season, dated, described, in a dead warden's hand: *heavy rain from the east, three hours, cistern up two knots.* That rain is coming out of the ground in the hot country now. It kept its appointment.

The dead of the plateau do not stay. There is no soil to bury in — the grikes take a coffin the way they take everything, badly and forever — and so the plateau's dead have gone, since before the church, into the ossuary cave in the flank of the third doline west of the village: laid on stone shelves in the cool dark, generation over generation, in the path of the seeping water. The dripwater takes them the way it takes the mountain — slowly, respectfully, a little each century — and carries them down into the descending dark with everything else. The plateau people know where that water goes. They will tell you, if you ask about the ossuary, in the flat voice, that their people are not gone but *in transit*; that some measure of every ancestor is at this moment somewhere in the mountain, partway down; and that at the far end, in the hot low country, there are white terraces growing lip by lip in the steam, and their grandmothers are in the masonry. The salt town calls those terraces its oldest residents and tends them accordingly, and the plateau finds this correct, and is quietly glad the arrangement is in good hands.

Ask a resident who lives at Barakh-Doline and you will get the shrug, or the list of neighbors, or — from the cistern warden, once, to a hydrologist who pressed — the answer that *the water lives here, briefly, and we are where it changes its mind.* This is glib but not false. Everything on the plateau, fossil to shepherd, holds a position at the intake of the same long machine: catching, ringing, swallowing, dissolving, sending down.

And the position is open here too. Stand still long enough on the pavement when the storm breaks — and it will break, it is someone else's storm and it has nowhere else to go — and the rain will sheet off you as off any other stone, and run to the nearest grike, and go under, carrying whatever trace of you rinses away: a little salt from your skin, appropriately enough, headed down to the salt country the long way. From the moment the water leaves you, you are on the books. The entry just won't post for four hundred years. The plateau does not distinguish between residents and visitors, or even, particularly, between the living and the dead. It distinguishes between water that has gone under and water that hasn't yet, and it regards everything walking on its surface, patiently, as the second kind.
