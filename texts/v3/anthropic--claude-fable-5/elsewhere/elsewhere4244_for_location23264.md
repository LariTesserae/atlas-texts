**The Settling Grounds**

Follow the water. It is the only thing that leaves.

It leaves as almost nothing — that varnish-thin film weeping down the dark stone, the muffled gurgle under the black ice, the overflow lip of a pool two degrees above freezing. But the mountain weeps from every face at once, and weeping, multiplied across twenty kilometers of raw rock, becomes a stream, and streams dropping through the cloud deck become torrents, and by the time the water finds the foot of the range it has organized itself into rivers — cold, loud, and gray. Not clear. Never clear. The rivers of this range run the color of milk cut with ash, because they are carrying the mountain with them: rock ground so fine by ice and frost that it will hang suspended in the current for weeks, plus everything the vents dissolved into it on the way down, the sulfur and the salts and the iron, the whole chemical exhaust of the foundry, in solution, in transit, invoiced downstream.

The rivers braid. That is what young rivers with too much cargo do — they cannot hold a single channel, because they are laying down their load faster than they can carry it, so they split and rejoin and split again across gravel plains of their own making, a lattice of gray water on gray stone, redrawing themselves after every flood. For three hundred kilometers they descend and slow and widen, dropping the coarse freight first — the cobbles, then the gravels, then the sands, sorted by exhaustion, the heaviest things abandoned soonest — until the land runs out of tilt entirely, and the water arrives at the place where everything the mountain has ever lost has been arriving since before the mountain had its present shape.

The basin has no outlet. This is the first thing to understand about the Settling Grounds, and everything else follows from it: the water comes in, and the only way out is up, into the sky, as vapor. Whatever the water carries stays. The basin is the range's terminal account, the ledger that balances the foundry's books, and it has been receiving deliveries for two million years without once making a shipment.

It does not look like a ledger. It looks like the opposite of the mountain in every particular, as if the world had taken the Heights and stated them in the negative. Where the range is vertical, the basin is a horizontality so total it does strange things to the eye — a sheet of water and mud and reed running to the horizon in all directions, so flat that the curvature of the planet is the tallest thing in it. Where the range lives inside its weather, the basin lives under an enormous sky; the cloud ceiling that presses down on the ridgelines is visible from here, on clear days, as a distant white wall standing along the northern horizon with a gray serration underneath it, the mountains wearing their private climate like a hood while the basin lies in open sun. Where the mountain is being made, the basin is being *filled* — which is, it turns out, the same event, observed from the other end.

The lake at the heart of it is vast and almost accidentally shallow. You could wade a kilometer from the shore and not wet your knees. It has no fixed edge; the boundary between water and land is a negotiation conducted daily by wind, which pushes the whole lake around its bed like tea in a tilted saucer, drowning a thousand hectares of mudflat in an afternoon and exposing them again by dusk. There is no tide, but there is this, which is better than tide because it obeys nothing periodic — the flats are wetted and stranded on the schedule of the weather, and everything that lives on them has learned to read wind the way coastal creatures read the moon.

The basin is zoned, and the zones are the mountain's cargo, sorted. At the northern shore, where the braided rivers come in, the water is fresh and gray-white with rock flour, and the deltas grow. This is the one place in the world where you can watch the mountain's construction at a legible speed: the uplift that jacks the range a few centimeters a year is invisible, but here its shed material advances the delta front meters in a season — new land, made of the Heights, extruded into the lake in lobes and bars, colonized by reeds almost before it is dry. The reed beds stand in belts kilometers deep, a rooted green city where the mountain supports nothing taller than a fist. Farther out, the middle lake is milk — sunlight goes perhaps a hand's depth into it and gives up — and the suspended stone falls through the still water grain by grain, perpetually, a snow that has been falling for twenty thousand centuries onto a floor no light has ever reached. And at the southern end, farthest from the rivers, where the water has spent years crossing the basin and evaporating as it goes, the lake becomes brine, and then becomes something past brine: shallow pans of water so dense with salt it moves oddly, sluggish under the wind, and the shores there are crusted in white and stained in bands of color.

And here the two places confess their kinship openly, because the colors of the southern pans are the colors of the vent fields — the sulfur yellow, the oxide orange, the mineral white laid down in rings — the same palette, the same pigments, some of them literally the same atoms, exhaled at the top of the world and precipitated at the bottom of it, the foundry's process colors reapplied three hundred kilometers away by different hands. And the hands are the same hands, or close cousins. The pigment of the salt pans, like the pigment of the vents, is substantially alive: halophiles, salt-eaters, archaea and bacteria of those same lineages older than continents, painting the brine pink and rust and painting the crusts orange, harvesting energy from gradients the way their relatives in the fumarole throats do — heat there, salt here, the two extremities of the same water, attended at both ends by the same immemorial family. The mountain's oldest residents and the basin's oldest residents are kin, and between them they bracket everything else that lives in this world like a pair of parentheses.

The census of the Settling Grounds is not distributed across temperature. It is distributed across *salinity* — draw the contour lines of salt, from the sweet gray river mouths to the poisoned mirrors of the southern pans, and you have drawn it, complete, the same trick the mountain plays with heat. But where the mountain's habitable country is folded into scattered rooms a few meters wide, the basin's runs in continuous belts a hundred kilometers long, and so where the Heights are nearly vacant, the Grounds are one of the crowded places of the earth.

The brine shrimp are the base of it, and they are the midges' opposite number stated at scale: small translucent animals in numbers that defeat counting, living in water that would kill nearly anything else, converting the halophiles' painted soup into flesh. They have the same trick as the fairy shrimp in the mountain pools — the egg that stops time — but tuned to the opposite catastrophe: the mountain eggs freeze solid and wait out winter; the brine eggs dry to dust and wait out drought, sometimes for decades, blowing along the salt crust like grit until the water comes back. Frost above, desiccation below; one family, two dialects of patience. The brine flies work the shoreline of the southern pans in a band a few meters wide and a hundred kilometers long — the midge's meter-wide world unrolled into a ribbon around a sea — rising in black drifts when anything walks through them, which things constantly do, because the flies and the shrimp are why everything else is here.

Everything else is birds, in quantities the mountain cannot imagine. The flocks that cross the Heights twice a year — those few days when the gray ceiling fills with voices coming from nowhere — are not passing through this world at random. They are commuting between its rooms, and this is the room they were leaving or seeking. The Settling Grounds is where the crowds are *from*. In the passage seasons the flats hold shorebirds in tens and hundreds of thousands, feeding on the flies and the drowned midge larvae and the invertebrate soup of the fresh margins, and when a harrier tilts over them the flocks pull off the mud in a single sheet, a gray smoke that flexes and pours and settles again, the largest coordinated motion in the basin apart from the water itself. The geese and cranes stage here in armies, grazing the delta reeds, filling the nights with a sound like a distant city; and when they lift for the crossing they climb for hours, rising toward that white wall on the horizon, and vanish over the serrated gray teeth beneath it — over the pikas and the vent gardens and the choughs — carrying the basin's calories across the mountain's cold, the two places joined twice a year by a bridge made of voices. Some of those travelers come down onto the warm pools of the Heights and never rise again, and their wing bones join the mineral rings; the basin pays a small toll to the foundry with every crossing.

Even the falcon commutes. The bird that hunts the finch lines from inside the steam columns in summer follows the flocks down the rivers in autumn, and winters here, hunting the shorebird smoke over the flats — the apex of both places is, some years, one individual animal, holding the two censuses together at the top the way the water holds them together at the bottom.

The rest of the roster fills the belts. Grebes on the open fresh water, courting in spring by running on the surface in pairs. Pelicans working the delta channels in coordinated lines, herding the small fish of the river mouths — and the fish are worth a moment, because their situation reprises the mountain's geography inverted: they live in the plumes of fresh water at each river mouth and cannot cross the saline middle, so each delta's fish are islanders, marooned in sweet water surrounded by salt exactly as the moss gardens are marooned in warmth surrounded by frost. The basin, too, is an archipelago; it just built its islands out of a different variable. In the reed cities: warblers in their thousands, bitterns booming from cover, harriers quartering the seams. On the flats at dusk, a pale fox trots the wind-line where the lake last stood, reading the stranded shrimp and the exhausted migrant and the unlucky duckling, the flats' one four-footed clerk. The mud takes every print — fox, crane, the stitched trails of a hundred thousand sandpipers — and the next wind-tide wipes the account clean and opens a new page.

But under the daily page, the permanent record. This is the deepest thing the Settling Grounds does, and it is the thing the mountain cannot do at all. The Heights write in ice — those frozen ripples recording exactly how the water was running when the cold took it — and erase every thaw; the fresh cliff faces are blank sheets that the lichens have barely begun to mark. The basin writes in mud and keeps everything. Down under the milk of the middle lake, where the rock-flour snow has been falling since before the range broke the cloud deck, the floor is layered in varves: a pale band for each summer's melt, a dark band for each winter's stillness, year upon year upon year, an unbroken annual ledger running back two million years. The mountain's whole biography is down there, written by its own shed substance — a thick gray band for the year an entire cliff face let go and the rivers ran heavy for a season; a fine white line of ash for some ancient afternoon when a vent became briefly more than a vent; pollen grains recording which advance-party plants held which seams in which millennium; and here and there, sealed in the anoxic dark where nothing decays, a whole bird, feather and bone, some traveler who came down on the crossing ten thousand years ago and finished the journey anyway, delivered late but delivered, filed in the archive with everything else. The mountain does not remember the rockfall an hour after the rattle fades. The basin remembers all of them, in order, dated. The foundry keeps no records; the records are kept here, by the settling ponds, which is where records have always been kept.

So the answer to the mountain's riddle — the plant in continuous production with no product — was never on the mountain. The product exists. It is here: this plain of new land advancing into the lake, these reed cities standing on last century's deliveries, this floor of ordered gray pages. The product is the Settling Grounds themselves, cast grain by grain out of the Heights, the mold filling three hundred kilometers from the pour.

And the basin is as provisional as the range, and knows it in the same wordless way. Every delivery raises the floor. The lake is shallower than it was; the deltas reach farther than they did; somewhere ahead — far ahead, but on the same schedule as the rounding of those blade-edged ridges — the basin fills, the lake shrinks to its salt pans and then to a white stain and then to a memory held in varves under a grass plain, and the crowds move on to whatever hollow in the world is newly catching the sweepings of some newly rising range. The residents of the Settling Grounds are not the inhabitants of the finished, it turns out, any more than the mountain's residents are. They are the inhabitants of the *arriving* — of the delivery, the milk in the water, the land still coming — and when the shipments stop, they too will follow the freight.

Between the two places the rivers run gray all year, one long sentence being carried downhill, saying the same thing over and over: what is made above is kept below. On still nights on the flats, they say, when the wind lies down and the geese are quiet, you can put your ear to the mud and feel it — too low to be sound, coming through three hundred kilometers of stone — the far engine turning over and over, the foundry running cold and slow at the top of the world, casting the ground you are lying on.
