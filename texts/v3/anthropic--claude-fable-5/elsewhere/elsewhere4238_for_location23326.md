**The Old Levels**

Follow the water.

It is the only thing the Quiet Steeps lets go of. Everything else — shell, scale, stone, silence — the town routes back into itself, but the cisterns can only hold so much, and somewhere below the lowest terrace, past the windrow of discharged pebbles, the overflow gathers itself into a stream and quits the valley entirely. It goes down the mountain's northern flank in a hurry, the way everything on that mountain happens in a hurry: leaping, white, loud at last, glad — a visitor would swear — to be making noise again. It runs cold and faintly grey, milked by rock flour and by a fine freight of moth-felt that never fully settles, and the people who live at the bottom of it call it the Grey Water and will not say much else about it.

At the bottom of it is the old country.

You can tell the old country by the way it has forgotten how to be anything but gentle. Every hill is a shoulder, every valley a lap. The stone here doesn't break in planes; it barely breaks at all — it rounds, and crumbles, and becomes soil, and the soil is black and deep and has had time to think. Rivers in the old country do not leap. They lie down in their beds and negotiate. This is the country the mountain shouldered up through, the country that was here first and will be here after, and it wears its age the way the mountain wears its youth: completely, in every line.

And at the mountain's northern foot, where the land should rise to meet the slope, it does the opposite. It sinks.

This is the thing the Levels folk will tell you first, if you ask how the fen came to be, because it is the beginning of everything they know about themselves: *the ground is lower than it used to be.* When the mountain went up, the country beside it went down — not violently, not all at once, but steadily, over years, the way a man's shoulders drop when he lifts something over his head. The old rivers, arriving at the new low ground, stopped negotiating and simply pooled. Reeds came, then peat, then more peat, and what had been a parish of fields and lanes became eleven miles of black water, floating meadow, and mire, threaded with meres like drops of dark glass.

The mountain and the fen are the same age. This is worth sitting with. The fen *looks* ancient — the black water, the drowned oaks standing silver and dead in the shallows, the peat that swallows a fence post in a generation — and the mountain looks new-minted, all blades and corners. But they are twins. They are one event wearing two faces: the same convulsion, counted once in rising and once in sinking. The Levels folk put it more plainly. *Half the parish went up,* they say. *The rest of us went down slow enough to move our beds.*

The Levels are loud.

This is the first thing a visitor coming down from the Steeps notices, and it arrives almost as violence. The fen at any hour is a standing argument: wind hissing through miles of reed like surf that never lands, coots bickering, the clap and burst of wildfowl getting up off the meres in their thousands, water talking to itself under the floating lawns, the suck and gulp of the mire venting its gases in fat, unashamed bubbles, frogs in their season laying down a sound like gravel being poured all night. Willows fringe the hards — the islands of firm ground — and even the willows are loud, silver undersides rattling in any breath of wind. Nothing here is upholstered. Every drop splashes. Every leaf reports.

And the people are loud too, deliberately, as a matter of doctrine. On the Levels you sing when you pole a boat through the reeds; you call your name ahead of you on the causeways; you talk to the water when you work it, a low running commentary that a stranger takes for eccentricity until it is explained. Silence, in the fen, is not a courtesy. Silence is what the mire sounds like just before it takes something. A quiet patch of water is a warning; a quiet person on the fen is presumed to be sinking; and so the manners of the place run exactly opposite to the manners of the mountain, and for exactly the same reason. Up there, sound is what the pattern removes. Down here, sound is how you prove you are still on top of the world instead of inside it.

There is one exception, and everyone knows where it is.

Where the Grey Water comes down off the mountain's foot and enters the first mere, its cargo of moth-felt finally settles. It has been settling there for as long as the fen has existed. The reeds around the inflow are furred grey to the waterline, and they do not rattle; the water there takes the rain without a sound; a boat poled into that acre goes suddenly, horribly smooth, the ripple of its passage dying within an oar's length. The Levels folk call it the Hush, and they do not fish it, and they do not cut it, and when they must cross it they cross it singing — quietly, which is the only quiet singing done anywhere on the Levels — because the Hush is understood to be not exactly theirs. It is the mountain's embassy. It is the one place where the upstream silence reaches the old country, a grey thumbprint on the loud water, and things released into the Hush are considered delivered.

**Those Whom the Old Levels Keep**

Note the grammar. On the mountain, the residents keep the town. In the fen it runs the other way. Nothing here maintains anything; nothing prunes, or patches, or packs moss into joints. The fen does not tend. The fen *holds* — and everything that lives on it or in it or under it is, in the long run, held.

**The fen-bull** is the voice of the place, and no one has ever seen it well. It is a bird, probably — bittern-built, the fowlers guess, but bigger, striped like dead reed, standing motionless in the deep beds with its bill to the sky — and its boom is the deepest sound the Levels make: a note felt in the sternum before it is heard, carrying six miles on a still evening, rolling out across the water like something enormous clearing its throat under the peat. The boom comes at intervals, and the intervals keep. Households on the far hards genuinely time their evenings by it. What is strange — what the old fowlers point out and then decline to interpret — is that the intervals are not the fen-bull's own. They track the mire's breathing. The peat vents its gas in slow rhythmic pulses, the bubbles rising in trains, and the bull booms on the beat of them, a voice laid over a pulse it did not choose, the way — though no one on the Levels has stood at the cisterns to make the comparison — a line of pale gold salamanders lays its heartbeat over a drip. The mountain keeps its time in silence and the fen keeps its time out loud, but somebody, in both places, is counting.

**The eels** are the post. Every spring the elvers run — a black, braided, uncountable thread of them coming up through the meres, and most spread into the fen to grow fat and stay, but one skein, every year, turns aside into the Grey Water and goes *up*. Up the cold milk of it, up the falls, climbing wet stone by night as eels can, up the raw flank toward the cloud — and the Levels folk, who trap eels everywhere else with a clear conscience, will not set a trap in the Grey Water. *The Milk run is not ours,* is the whole of the explanation. What the eels do up there, in the cisterns of a town no Levels-dweller has seen, is not discussed. What is known is that some come back. Decades later — thirty years, fifty — a single eel will come down the Grey Water swollen to the girth of a man's leg, white as tallow, blind, its eyes gone soft from a life in the dark, and any fowler who finds one lifts it into his boat with both arms, carries it across the loud water, and releases it into the Hush without a word. It is the only errand on the Levels performed in silence. Nobody remembers deciding this. It is simply what is done with something that has come home from up there: you return it to the embassy, and you let the quiet have it.

**The cutters** are the people, and the peat is their calendar, their fuel, their archive, and their church, in roughly that order. The village — a straggle of reed-thatched houses along the largest hard, blue with smoke, loud with geese and children — lives by cutting: each summer the long spades go into the black peat and lift it out in bricks, and every brick is a page, because the fen has kept everything. Leaves fall into the mire and stay leaves. Wood stays wood. A dropped knife stays a knife — and the knives matter, because the Levels folk have iron. The mire itself gives it to them: bog ore, gathered in rusty pans from the shallows, smelted on the hards, so that the fen's people are metal-rich in exactly the measure that the mountain's builders were metal-poor. Once in living memory a smith of the Levels, moved by something he never fully explained, climbed the flank with a fine iron blade as a gift and left it in a niche in the highest terrace wall, among the antler picks. He stayed the night at the valley's edge. By the clearing of the mist the blade had been moved — not taken, not hidden. Carried, with evident care, to the last wall of the town and set down just beyond it, on the outward side. Politely declined. He brought it home and it hangs in the Ledger House now, the only object there that came back down.

**The Ledger House and the Teller** exist because of the pebbles. The Grey Water brings them: pale, thumb-sized, sharp-cornered, young mountain stone utterly unlike anything the old country makes, arriving a few at a time, year over year, washed down from a windrow at a valley's edge that the Levels folk have never seen. The current drops them where it drops everything — in the peat at the Hush's margins — and the peat holds them where they fall, layer by layer, in the order of their arrival. And here is the thing the cutters found, generations ago, that changed the village's understanding of what it was living on: the pebbles in the peat lie in *groups*. Rows. Arcs. Small dense clusters. Whether the current arranges them or whether they arrive already meaning something, the fen has kept the sequence — a stratified transcript, spit below spit, of everything discharged from the mountain's accounts since the fen began. The Ledger House holds them now, each group lifted whole in its slab of peat, shelved in order of depth. The Teller is the office of trying to read them. It passes from one to the next with the shelves and the despair and the small hoard of guesses: the Teller before this one believed, by the end, that she had isolated the sign for *water*. Or for *carrying*. Or for *gone*. Her notes allow all three. The pebbles keep arriving. The Levels' saying about the whole enterprise is the closest thing the village has to a philosophy: *the mountain keeps house, and we keep what it throws away.*

**The peat itself** is the last resident, and the largest, and the one everything else lives on top of. Cut deep enough — nine feet, ten — and the spades ring on the floor: the old ground surface, the drowned parish, still down there entire. A cobbled lane, running true beneath the mire toward the mountain until it simply stops rising to meet a slope that is no longer where it was. Doorsteps. A hearth with the ash still in it, kept soft and grey for want of any air to take it. The churchyard dead of the old parish lie below the floor in their proper rows, and the cutters know where, and cut around.

But there are other dead, and these are *in* the peat, not under it — laid into the mire from above, after the fen was born. They are found rarely, once in a decade, and they are always the same: a small body, grown and not a child, fine-boned, the hands broadened and calloused by stonework, nothing of metal anywhere on it, an antler pick laid along the forearm, and the whole of it wrapped and packed round with moss — cistern-moss, mountain moss, tamped with a fussy, repeated thoroughness that the cutters have learned to recognize by feel through the spade before they ever see it. When one is found, the cut stops. The spit is laid back. The ground is retired, and the fen edge is scalloped now with retired cuts, each one a grave that nobody on the Levels dug. Most lie deep, old, from the fen's first centuries. But one, found in the time of the current Teller's own grandmother, lay barely three spits down.

Three spits is recent. Three spits means that within living memory, someone — something — came down off the mountain in the dark, carrying its dead the whole loud way to the old country, and gave the body to the water that keeps things, and went back up. The Levels folk do not watch for it. It is felt to be none of their business, or all of it. There is a plaza on the mountain, they have been told by the only two people in living memory who have climbed to the green shelf and returned, with a small stone cist beneath its flags containing nothing but tamped moss — and on the Levels this is not considered a mystery. A cist of moss with no body; a body wrapped in moss with no grave. It is considered an address. It is where you write when the letter has already been delivered.

---

The green shelf, they call it — the hanging valley. On clear evenings, when the cloud lifts off the mountain's teeth for an hour, the whole village can see it if it cares to look: a fleck of green on the bare northern rock, impossibly high, the size of a fingernail held at arm's length. The old people can point to the families whose grazing it was. *Went up in the long winters,* they say, meaning the years the ground rose — the lane cracking a hand's width a season, the upper fields tilting away sunward, and the households whose land was rising going up with it, stubborn, moving their walls higher each year, until one year the cloud closed over them and the visits thinned and the ropes came down and there was only the stream. *They went up loud,* the saying finishes. *The mountain quieted them.* Whether their descendants are the eyes in the passages, or the habits grazing the plaza, or nothing at all, the Levels do not claim to know. The Levels are not in the business of knowing. The Levels are in the business of holding, and they hold the other half of the parish the only way left: in the peat, in the pebbles, in the retired cuts, in a blind white eel lowered into the Hush with two arms and no words.

**Who lives in the Old Levels?**

The honest answer is that the fen does — that the cutters and the fowlers and the fen-bull and the eels are tenants, and the mire is the landlord, and the rent is everything, eventually, paid in full and kept forever. Up on the mountain the keeping is work: ten thousand small acts an hour, moss tamped, stones refitted, gaps closed, everything routed into use, nothing merely lost. Down here the keeping is patience: nothing tended, nothing repaired, nothing given back, nothing lost either. The Steeps keeps by hands. The Levels keeps by holding still. They are the two things a world can do with what it loves, and the Grey Water runs between them like a sentence passed back and forth.

And at dusk, if you stand on the causeway with the village noise at your back, you can watch the connection make its rounds in person. The cloud that grazed the hanging valley all day lets go of the mountain, slides down the northern flank, and lies out across the meres as evening mist — the same water, keeping its two posts in turn, silent above and among the loud reeds by night — and before dawn it rises off the fen and goes back up to be a town's weather again. The stream is the exhale. The mist is the breath drawn back. Half the parish went up and half went down, and the water never chose; it just keeps the visiting hours, up with the keeping by day, down with the kept by dark, the one thing in the world still faithfully going between.

The Levels folk sing it home each evening, because on the Levels you sing at everything. Up on the mountain, presumably, its arrival is received without comment.

That, too, is considered an answer.
