**The Long Kiln**

Leave the Wax Garden by the south door — either draft of it — and go past the workbench, which turns out to be neither very far away nor very small but simply patient, and keep walking, and the honey-colored light thins the way broth thins, and the warmth stops coming from no direction. Outside the garden, warmth has a direction. It has only ever had one. It flows past you like a slow shallow river flowing uphill, or you are walking downstream against it, and if you follow it back the way a salmon follows, across a country with no name because it has never needed one — flat, pale, unprimed, the back of the world's stage flat — you come, after a distance measured in nothing you can count, to the source of every afternoon the garden has ever had.

It is a hill, and it is not a hill. It is a kiln built along the whole spine of a slope, chambered like a caterpillar, brick upon brick upon brick, and it is the length of the walk it takes to walk it, which is the same every time. That is the first thing you notice and it does not stop being strange: here, everything counts the same twice. There are one thousand and one bricks in the door. Count again. One thousand and one. The Long Kiln has decided everything it contains, down to its arithmetic, and the numbers hold still under your finger like animals that have been told to.

It was fired once. The firing is over. It has been over for as long as anyone knows, and the kiln has been cooling ever since, at the pace of making — a degree a week, no faster, matched step for step, though nobody arranged this, to the garden's leaf a week, its fruit a month. The garden lives on the runoff of this cooling. The even honey light, the warmth that is enough, the warmth of a kiln three days after firing — that phrase is not a comparison. It is a distance. It is how far downstream the garden sits.

Because around the Long Kiln, distance is measured in aftermath. The outermost ring of the country is three days after the firing, and the whole rest of the world lies in it, and always will. Walk inward and you cross into two days after: the air remembers more; iron things left on the ground have gone the color of a held word; your shadow dries at the edges and stops needing to be stitched. At one day after, the bricks sing very faintly when the wind — there is no wind; when what passes for wind — crosses them, a high mineral note like a wet finger on a cup rim, and the ash on the ground is warm through your soles, and you begin to want, in a way that should worry you, to stay. And at the door itself it is the moment the fire was drawn. It is always that moment. The last flame has always just gone out. The smoke of it is still in the air, has been still in the air, a standing grey veil that does not disperse and does not settle, hanging above the firebox like a note held past the end of the song. Walking toward the Long Kiln is walking back through those three days, and everyone who does it reports the same thing: somewhere around the middle of the second day, without noticing the moment, you stopped walking through space and started walking through the cooling itself.

Approaching, you pass the cones. They stand in long rows fanned out before the door, an orchard of small bowed figures, knee-high, each one melted to a different degree of agreement — the outermost barely nodding, the inner ranks bent low, the ones nearest the door folded entirely over themselves, foreheads to the ground. They are witnesses. Each one bowed at the exact moment its temperature was reached, and they have held their bows ever since, a procession caught permanently at the instant of assent. They finished their reading long ago. They are read anyway, daily, the way you reread a letter you know.

The kiln itself has no seams. This is the thing that will unsettle you if you have come from the garden, where every seam can be found if you look, where seamlessness would be a kind of lying. The bricks of the Long Kiln were made things once — you can still see, in their faces, the ghost of the mold, the drag of the wire that cut them — but the firing healed every joint, fused mortar to brick until they stopped being two opinions, and now the whole hill is a single decided thing, glazed along its upper flanks where the flame ran hottest, a glaze the color of dark honey burnt one shade past honey, crackled all over with fine crazing like the surface of a very old cup. Whether a healed seam is the perfection of craft or its grave is exactly the kind of question the garden would keep both answers to. The kiln keeps one. It does not say which.

There is one door. There is one door. It is bricked shut, one thousand and one, and it has been bricked shut since the fire was drawn, and it will be bricked shut tomorrow, and the certainty of this is the ground the whole country stands on.

At the foot of the slope, in a long basin the kiln's shadow crosses once a day, lies all the water in the world.

This is where the garden's driest sentence comes to be understood. No water has ever entered that place; none is missing; it is all here, accounted for, to the drop — a lake without a ripple, so still it does not reflect so much as hold a second sky in trust, dark and cool and entirely patient. It is the Quench. It is waiting for the door. On the day the kiln is finally cool and the bricks are taken down and the ware is drawn out glowing into the air, the water will be needed all at once — for the plunging, the hissing, the tempering, the setting of everything into its final hardness — and after that it will be distributed: rivers will be poured out along their courses, rain will be issued, the seas will be filled from this one basin like cups from a pitcher. The world is dry because the world is before its quench. The garden's lushness with no water in it is not a paradox. It is a schedule.

Once a week, when the kiln cools its degree, it ticks — a single deep tick, the sound of a great clock deciding to continue — and the whole lake shivers once, edge to edge, one ring of motion crossing it like a shudder crossing a sleeping animal, and then it is still again for a week.

Put your ear against the kiln's flank, between the ticks, and you will hear something you already know. Very small, very far, carried up the current of warmth the wrong way like a voice up a stairwell: a page turning in another room. A thread going taut. The tick of a copper leaf adjusting itself. And underneath those, softest of all, a knife being drawn slowly through something soft. The warmth flows down the three days to the garden, and the garden's sounds ride back up it, and the Long Kiln holds them in its hollow chambers the way a shell holds the sea. The kiln is listening to the garden. It may be all the kiln does now, besides cool.

Now the part that is harder to say.

The kiln is full. It was loaded before the firing — carefully, over a long time, by hands that knew what they were doing — and no one at three days after remembers with what. And the fuel: the fuel is why the garden is the way it is. The Long Kiln was fired, that one time, on everything the world had decided against. Every discarded draft. Every rejected corner, every leaf whose fold was tried and creased wrong and set aside, every door that was almost the door, every earlier version of every made thing — all of it went into the firebox, armload by armload, three days and the nights between, and burned, and the ash of all those abandoned versions rose through the chambers and settled on whatever waits inside and melted into the glaze. Whatever is in there wears everything the world threw away, made glassy and permanent, as its skin.

And since the fire was drawn, nothing can be discarded. There is nowhere for a rejected thing to go. This is why the garden keeps two doors and hasn't discarded the rejects; why the northeast corner holds both its directions; why the world downstream has grown crowded with drafts, layered, leaf upon leaf, everything kept. The garden is not indecisive. It is simply living after the last fire that could take a decision away.

One more thing, and then the keeper. Warmth that is being lived on does not spend the way wasted warmth spends. A hearth that is sat beside keeps; everyone who has sat beside one knows this in their body even if it will not survive being written down. The garden, breathing on the kiln's warmth — every felt bird, every clay mouse, the sleeping thing in the corner, the Gardener himself, all of them breathing — is the reason three days after the firing has lasted this long. The degree a week is the warmth being used gently instead of lost. The garden keeps the kiln warm exactly as much as the kiln keeps the garden warm, and neither has ever been told, and it is probably best that way.

**The Firekeeper**

You will not need stillness to find her. She is visible from the two-day ring onward, a small upright figure moving along the flank of the hill with a gait that has no hurry left to give up, and she will have seen you first, from farther off, because watching the approaches is a part of watching the door.

She is glazed. Stand near her and you see it: her skin has the crazing of the kiln's own flanks, hairline crackle over every visible inch, and in what passes for night at the outer rings the crackle lines whiten faintly, like frost finding the joints of a window. She was fired. She is the only finished being walking anywhere in the world, and this is her qualification and her sentence in the same breath. The garden could not touch her if she went there — its needles would skate off her surface, its tidiness would find nothing left undecided to tidy, its patient afternoon craftsmanship would pass over her like light over water — and she has never gone there, and when she speaks of it, which is rarely, she speaks of it the way you'd speak of a room you can hear through a wall: warmly, and without opening the door, because there is no door for her in that direction anymore. She knows the Gardener as a sound. He is the knife drawn slowly through something soft, arriving up the warmth. She has listened to his afternoons for the length of her tally. He, downstream, knows her the only way she can be known from there — as the warmth itself, the even honey certainty that has never once failed — and it is possible he does not know that the warmth is kept, that keeping is a craft, that the craft has a keeper. It is possible he does. He would decline to choose.

Her fingertips are the smoothest part of her. The glaze pooled there, or the fire took what was there before the glaze; either way they are blank, and if she pressed them into warm wax now they would leave nothing at all. Once — and this is one of the two stories, or a corner of one — before the firing, while the wax was warm, she pressed them into it and they left everything: ten prints, or nine, or ten, set one by one with great care into the knuckles of a made man, so that they would not be lost when the fire took hers. Whose hand is the same question as the whiskers, the garden says, and keeps its face very smooth. She keeps hers smooth too, though with her it may only be the glaze.

There are two stories about her, because there would be.

In the first she is the one who fired the kiln — who loaded it over years, who lit it, who fed the firebox with the world's discarded drafts for three days and the nights between, and who, at the appointed hour, drew the fire: pulled the last burning wood, bricked the door, and began the cooling, which is the longer craft. Firing takes days. Cooling, done properly, takes the rest of everything. In this story she knows exactly what is inside, and her silence is the silence of a craftsman who will not describe a piece before it is drawn, because describing it early is a way of cracking it.

In the second story she was inside. She went in before the firing to set the last cone, the innermost one, the one that only bows at the very end — and the door was bricked behind her, by mistake or by design, and she stood in the loaded dark among the waiting ware through all of it, through the whole ascent, and came out through the firebox at the drawing of the fire, glazed, crazed, finished, walking. And what she cannot remember — what the fire vitrified shut in her the way it fused the mortar to the brick — is whether she came out alone. In this story she keeps the door not from duty. She keeps it because unbricking it would answer a question she has arranged her entire life around not asking.

Her days are the kiln's days. In the morning she walks the rings inward, laying the back of her wrist against the brick at each of the stations, reading the cooling the way you'd read a fever, in reverse, over years. At noon she reads the cones — which finished their reading before her tally began, and which she reads anyway, walking the bowed rows, because a witness that is never consulted stops being a witness and becomes only a shape. In the afternoon she does one thing. Once a season she draws the plug from the single spy hole — the way, downstream, at what may be the same hour, the Gardener turns the sleeper — and looks in, and what she sees she has described only once, in her voice, which sounds like a cup being set down very carefully: a light the color of not-yet. Then she seats the plug again, snugly, with the heel of her printless hand.

And in the long middle of the afternoon she goes down to the water. She walks the edge of the Quench — it takes exactly as long as it takes, every time — and she talks to it. She tells it the story of the quenching: the door coming down brick by brick, the glow of the drawn ware, the plunge, the great hiss, the tempering, and then the pouring-out, the rivers issued to their beds, the rain released, the seas filled cup by cup from this one basin. She tells it beautifully. She has had a long time to get it right. She always stops before the end. The water, being patient the way only water is patient, has never once complained, and when the weekly tick comes and the lake shivers its single ring from edge to edge, she puts her hand flat on the surface, which does not wet her — nothing wets her; the glaze — and says the only thing she says every week without fail, which is: *not yet.*

Then she climbs back up the slope and adds one mark to the tally scratched into the clinker field below the door — a mark a week, a degree a week, a leaf a week, the same pace worn by two different works — and the tally is the length of a life, or two, or a life.

She does not eat. She does not sleep, exactly, but in the deepest part of the night at the one-day ring she sits down with her back against the warm flank of the kiln, over the spot where, if your ear were where her shoulders are, you would hear the garden clearest — the page, the thread, the knife through something soft — and goes still in a way that has no grain to it at all, still the way a fired thing is still, and the kiln's warmth passes over her and around her and down the three days to everything that breathes on it. Then she stands, and takes up nothing, because her work has no tools anymore, only stations, and goes back to the door, where nothing is undecided, one thousand and one, and waits — and either the three days will someday end: the last visitor will stay an afternoon too long, the Gardener will finally be allowed to lie down warm-colored in his corner, the warmth will stop being lived on and will start to spend, the degrees will go quickly then, the tick will come that has no tick after it, and she will take down the door with her blank hands, brick by brick, and the water will stand up all at once, and the world will be finished — which is one of the two stories — or the leaf a week and the degree a week will keep their perfect unarranged pace forever, the drafts undiscarded, the door unopened, the water patient, the glow behind the spy hole the color of not-yet, forever, or for as long as anything downstream keeps breathing — and she tries, she will tell you, setting the words down carefully, one cup at a time, not to take a position on which of them she is keeping.
