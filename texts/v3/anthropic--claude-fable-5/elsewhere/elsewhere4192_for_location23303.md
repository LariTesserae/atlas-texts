# The Keeping Ground

You come to it from below, the way almost everyone does now — up through the fault, the same crack the ancestors came down, a chimney of cold rising through the ceiling of the world. The climb takes most of a day. And the climb teaches you the whole grammar of the hollow one last time, in order, as you go: at the rim ledge the walls blaze at first-light, and as you ascend the glow dims through every named stage — warmth, then ember, the living skin thinning out beneath the dead ground — until the last hand-hold is at smoothness, and then the walls are not the plant anymore at all. They are stone. Actual stone, the thing the hollow does not have, gritty and mute under your fingers, and the light below you is a shrinking coin, and above you is a color the walkers have no word for.

Then the crack opens, and you stand up into the dark, and the first thing the surface does is make a sound.

Wind. You will not have heard wind before, if you were born below. The hollow's air moves, but silently, the plant's own slow breathing; up here the air is nobody's, and it comes across a thousand kilometers of dead flat ground with nothing to stop it, and it *roars*. New arrivals from the hollow have been known to drop flat on the ice, hands over their ears, certain the world is falling. It is only weather. It is only the sound of a place where nothing is alive to hush it.

## The Breath

You are never far from the crack, at first, because the crack is where the light is.

The hollow exhales. All that brightness and wet below has to go somewhere, and some of it goes up — a steady column of the plant's spent air rising out of the fault, warm by the surface's murderous standards, saturated, thick with glowing spore-dust. It climbs into the black sky as a visible pillar, a tower of pale green shine two kilometers tall, bending with the wind at its top like a candle-flame the size of a district. This is the Breath. It is the only landmark that matters. It is why anyone can live up here at all.

Because the Breath freezes as it rises. The moisture crystallizes around the glowing motes and comes back down as snow — slow, fat, faintly luminous snow, falling in a wide ellipse around the crack, day and night, forever. The ground for kilometers is buried in it, and the snow *keeps the glow* for years before it fades, so the land around the crack is a graded field of light: blinding-pale nearest the fault where the fall is fresh, dimming outward ring by ring, warmth, then ember, until at the far edge of the ellipse the snow has gone to smoothness and beyond that is the true dark.

The people here borrowed the walkers' words and bent them sideways. Below, the stages of glow measure time. Above, they measure *distance*. A shelter is "an ember out." A lost traveler was last seen "past smoothness." The far edge of the world's one lit place has the same name as the hollow's forgotten dead, and nobody up here finds that strange, because past smoothness is exactly where the forgotten would be, if the ground here ever forgot anything.

It does not. That is the first strangeness of the Keeping Ground, and the deepest, and everything else about the place grows out of it.

The Breath also feeds them. The glow-snow is spore and pollen, the same drifting surplus the gliders graze below, and the surface people harvest it in dragged sledges, press it, thaw it over sap-fires, and eat it. They grow nothing. They hunt nothing. They live entirely on what the hollow breathes out — a whole people sustained on the exhalation of a plant that has never noticed them, catching its breath as it leaves the world and eating the light out of it.

## The Ground That Keeps

Below, the floor is young and never finished. Above, the ground is old and finished absolutely.

Nothing up here changes. Nothing rots — the cold forbids it, the same cold that below only whispers in the shadow-creases and up here owns everything. Nothing grows over anything. Nothing heals. A footprint pressed into the wind-crusted snow past the reach of fresh fall will hold its shape for a lifetime; the paths between the crack and the outlying shelters are not worn smooth but *printed*, thousands of individual footprints frozen hard, generations deep, and a traveler can walk out along them stepping in the exact strides of their great-grandmother, whose feet were smaller.

Walk beyond smoothness, out into the dark with a sledge-lamp of banked glow-snow, and the keeping gets older. The land out there is dry sea. Salt flats, level as still water, running to the horizon under the stars — and pressed into the salt, kept perfectly, are the marks of the world before: the ripple-lines of the last tides, the frozen wakes of things that swam, and — this is the part that stops every traveler the first time — *tracks*. Footprints of animals no one has ever seen or named, herds of them, crossing the flats in their hundreds, every toe-pad crisp, going somewhere ten thousand years ago on the last afternoon anything up here was warm. The surface people navigate by them. The route to the eastern shelters follows a line of three-toed prints for two days and then turns where the herd turned. No one knows what turned it. The ground kept the decision but not the reason.

Overhead there are stars, hard and fixed, and once a day there is the Ember — the old sun, a dull red coal crossing the sky, bright enough to see and too weak to feel. It is the reason for everything. When it dimmed, the world above died and the plant gave up on the sky and turned its light inward, and the ancestors followed the light down the crack. The surface people time their lives by the Ember's crossings, and their children ask, every generation, the same question in the borrowed grammar: *will it go to smoothness?* And every generation is told: watch it and see. Some of them do. There are old men and women here whose life's work has been watching a red coal cross the sky several thousand times, keeping count, alert for a change no instrument exists to measure. Nothing about this strikes anyone here as a waste. Keeping count is what the place is *for*.

And far out along the horizons, on the clearest nights, there are other glows. Faint pillars, bent by distant wind. Other Breaths — other cracks in the ceiling of the hollow, or of other hollows; no one has settled it — each presumably with its own falling light, its own ellipse, its own people or none. The surface people know of five. They used to know of six.

## The Keepers

They are the walkers' kin, and both peoples say so, and both peoples say it the way you admit a thing.

The Keeping Ground was not founded by explorers. It was founded, the story goes — and up here the story does not vary, because nothing up here is allowed to vary — by a woman of the eastern circuit whose daughter died young, and who could not do what the custom required. She could not carry the body to a growing tip and let the plant take it back. She could not spend the following years watching a person-sized warmth in the floor dim through the stages toward smoothness. So she carried her daughter *up* — a full day's climb with the body lashed to her back, out of the light, into the dark where nothing is ever taken back by anything — and she stood the body upright in the frost at the edge of the Breath's fall, facing the glow, and she stayed.

Others came. Not many, never many, but steadily, generation after generation, and always for the same reason. The crack sorts them. It has been sorting one people into two for as long as the signs remember: below stay the ones who can bear letting things heal, and up come the ones who cannot — the mourners, the wronged, the ones who kept someone at first-light until their hands ached and it still wasn't enough. Every year or two, a walker appears at the top of the fault with everything they own and a face like a re-cut sign, and the Keepers take them in and ask nothing, because there is only one reason anyone climbs.

And every year or two it runs the other way. A Keeper — usually old, usually one who has spent a lifetime tending the archive — will find the weight of everything kept suddenly unbearable, all those unfading footprints, all that standing grief, and will go down the crack to the bright forgetting country and become a walker, and let it all, at last, heal. Neither people mocks the other for this. The trade in goods between them is brisk; the trade in people is solemn, and no one who crosses is ever asked to explain.

The Keepers themselves have been reshaped, as the walkers were, but in mirror. Their eyes are wide where the walkers' are slitted, huge-pupiled, dark-adapted; a Keeper below is blinded for days, weeping in the glare, exactly as a walker above stumbles like an infant in the dark. They are heavier-built, wrapped in layered veil — glider-membrane traded up from below, the only fabric either world has — with faces open to the cold in a way that appalls their downstairs kin. And they are *loud*. The wind eats quiet voices, so the Keepers speak at full shout as a matter of ordinary courtesy, and their word for *quiet* is also their word for *lost*, just as the walkers' word for *loud* is their word for *injured*, and when the two peoples meet at the crack-mouth to trade, each spends the first hour flinching at the other.

Their town has no name that isn't a description: the Ring. Houses of stacked flat stone mortared with glow-snow, built in the ember-distance where the warmth of the Breath still reaches and the fresh fall doesn't bury the roofs. The houses are inherited, added to, never demolished — accumulation, the deadly sin of the hollow, is the whole virtue of the surface. A Keeper family's wealth is measured in what it has managed to keep: tools, stones, records, rooms. Where a walker cuts one healing stroke before sleep, a Keeper places one stone on the family cairn, and the cairns of the old families are taller than the houses, and no stone has ever been taken off one.

*The ground does not love us,* their saying goes, *but it will not lose us.* It is the exact answer to the walkers' saying about the plant, and both peoples know both sayings, and each privately thinks the other's is the sadder.

## The Ledger

Here is the trade, and here is the tether, the current that runs up the crack alongside the Breath and makes two worlds into one:

Up from below come veil, pollen-cake, sap-amber, and blades — though the finest blades go down again, because dead plant-tissue cured a season in the surface cold takes an edge no shadow-crease below can match; *sky-cured glass*, the walkers call it, and pay well. Down from above go stone whetstones, salt from the dead sea, and sacks of mineral dust scraped from the flats — because the creasemoss, the hollow's only medicine, eats mineral dust, and the walkers seed their hidden shadow-beds with it, so that the bitterness that cures fevers a hundred kilometers deep in the bright country is grown on ground-up pieces of the dark.

But the real trade, the one both sides organize their meetings around, is not in goods. It is in keeping.

Near the crack-mouth stands a cliff of bare stone, wind-polished, called the Ledger. Into it, for payment, the Keepers' carvers will cut whatever a walker cannot bear to let heal. Names of the dead. The true routes of swallowed districts. Marriages, and the ends of marriages. Testimony of wrongs, cut deep, so that a walker who has been badly used can climb a day out of their world, watch it carved, and go home able to let the sign below fade — because the fact of it is *kept* now, past healing, past the plant's reach, in ground that forgets nothing.

The walkers almost never come back to read what they've paid to keep. That is not the point. The point is that it is there.

So the Keepers, who read all of it, have become the memory of a people who chose to forget. A Ledger-carver can recite three centuries of the eastern circuit's dead. And in one high corner of the cliff, cut and re-cut by custom, is the record of the sightings — the person-shaped thing the size of weather, seen walking inward through the deep haze below. The walkers, counting their climbing-strokes, know of eleven sightings.

The Ledger holds twelve.

The twelfth is the oldest, and the hollow healed its stroke to smoothness generations ago, and no walker alive has ever heard of it. The Keepers have offered, more than once, to read it down to them. The walkers, every time, have politely declined.

## The Standing Dead

The Keepers' dead are not taken back. They are stood.

At the smoothness-edge of the glow-field, where the falling light gives out and the dark begins, the dead of the Ring stand upright in the frost, dressed by the wind in rime, facing inward toward the Breath — rings of them, the newest nearest, the founder's daughter somewhere far out in the oldest ring, three centuries deep in ice and still on her feet. Every Keeper who has ever died is out there. The crowd grows and never lessens. On still nights, when the Breath's pillar stands straight, its glow reaches out across the field and every figure casts a long thin shadow away into the dark, thousands of them, radiating from the light like the marks around a healing wound.

Scattered among them, here and there, lie the wings — gliders that rode the updraft out of the crack, froze aloft in the killing air, and fell. Above ground, unlike below, they are never cut. They lie where they landed, pale gold sheets spread open on the white, and because nothing here decays, every glider that has ever escaped the hollow is still exactly where it fell. There are forty-one. The Keepers know the number the way they know everything: exactly, and forever, and the wings serve as landmarks on the funeral field — *the row past the Third Wing*, a family will say, of where their people stand.

Walkers who see the standing dead go quiet, even for walkers. Keepers who learn what the growing tips are for go quiet too. Each people has decided the other does something unbearable with its grief, and each is too courteous, and too closely related, to say so above a trading voice.

## The Second Breath

There used to be six glows on the horizon. This is the thing the Keepers keep hardest, cut deepest in the Ledger, told to every child before almost anything else.

Within the archive's memory — four generations, close enough to touch — the pillar called the Second Breath began to fade. The Keepers watched it across the dark for eleven years, and for those eleven years their distance-words became time-words, the borrowed grammar folding back on itself: the far light went from first-light to warmth, from warmth to ember, from ember, at last, to smoothness. Then the horizon there was only horizon.

A journey was made. It took a season each way, sledge-lamps and salt-flat stars and the frozen herd-tracks for a road. What the travelers found was a crack healed shut from below — the plant had revised, somewhere down in its bright interior, rerouted itself the way it retires a column, and sealed that door of the world without malice or notice — and around the dead fault, a town like the Ring, intact. Houses standing. Cairns standing. Everyone in it exactly where the cold had found them when the warm breath stopped, kept perfectly, forever, by the ground they had trusted to keep them.

The travelers added one stone to that town's cairn, and came home, and carved all of it.

So the Keepers understand the terms of their existence with a clarity most peoples are spared. Their eternity is on loan from the thing that forgets. Every footprint, every cairn, every standing ancestor, every line of the Ledger endures only for as long as a plant that has never noticed them keeps exhaling through one particular crack — and the plant abandons its own blazing towers, and the trimmers gather a season before the light shows any failing. The Keepers have their own watchers now, breath-readers, who measure the pillar's height and the glow-snow's rings and the temperature of the rising air, keeping count against a change they cannot prevent, only see coming. It is not despair. It is the local form of prayer.

---

You can stand at the crack-mouth a long time — and travelers of both peoples do, it is the one custom they share — with the hollow's exhaled warmth on your back and the surface's ancient cold on your face, at the exact seam of the two worlds. Below you, everything is bright and alive and being endlessly unwritten; a life down there is a single luminous stroke, already healing. Above you, everything is dark and dead and utterly kept; a life up here is a footprint that will outlast the naming of it. The Breath rises past you, glowing, and freezes, and falls, feeding one world with the other's forgetting.

Down goes the dust of the dead sea, to grow medicine in the shadows. Up comes the light, to be eaten in the dark. And through the crack between them, every year or two, in one direction or the other, goes a single person carrying a grief, climbing toward whichever mercy they can bear.
