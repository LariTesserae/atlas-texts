**The Letting**

At sea level the world still keeps its old habits. There is stone here — real stone, grey and glittering and dead in the honest way, the way the uplands have forgotten how to be — and the town at the foot of the western scarp is built on it with a certain quiet insistence, foundations sunk into granite as if to say: *some of us still live in the mineral world*. Three thousand meters overhead, the rim of the Pith Plateau closes off a third of the sky, a long green-grey wall going up into its own weather, and out of a notch in that rim, day and night, for longer than there has been a town or a language to name one, the plateau bleeds.

The wound is called the Letting, and so is the town under it, because the town is not really a separate thing. Somewhere on the high flats all that cloud-drunk water, hauled through vessels the width of train tunnels, comes at last to a place where the plateau lets it go — whether an old injury that never healed, or a vent the organism grew on purpose, a kidney, a mercy, is a question held loosely, like all questions up there. What comes out is the plateau's own water: milky, faintly sweet, faintly luminous, blood-warm at the lip and cold as cloud by the bottom. It falls the better part of a kilometer down the scarp face in a single white column, and it *pulses*. Every forty seconds a swell travels down the falls, a visible thickening that descends the whole height of it like a swallow going down a throat, and the roar rises and settles, rises and settles. The town has stopped hearing this the way the uplanders have stopped hearing the beat itself. Visitors stand in the streets with their heads tipped back and cannot stop hearing it.

Partway down the column there is a place where the falling slows. The locals call it the Hanging Step: a band perhaps fifty meters deep where one of the plateau's leaked seams of slow time lies across the cascade like a shelf, and the water entering it takes hours to cross. From the harbor it looks like a permanent ledge of white stone hanging unsupported in the middle of the falls — thousands of tons of water suspended mid-plummet, descending at the pace of honey, wearing a haze of rainbow when the sun gets under the cloud deck. Swifts nest behind it. They dart into the slow band and hang there, wings churning in lazy dream-strokes, and come out again into ordinary air like stones from a sling, and their nests, tucked in the hollow behind the hanging water, are the safest real estate in the world, because nothing that means them harm can cross that band faster than an afternoon. The sound of the falls has a hole in it at that altitude. Boats far out on the water use the silence to take their bearings in fog.

At the base of the scarp the world is made of the plateau's dander. The great organism does not erode into sand, but it *sheds* — flakes of dead outer tissue sifting down the face for a hundred thousand years — and the slope at the bottom is a midden of it, the only true soil for a hundred miles in any direction, black and rich and made entirely of the body above. On this the mist-forest grows: everything the plateau is not. Fast. Various. Mortal. A riot of ordinary green in the perpetual spray, ferns and creepers and quick soft trees that live thirty years and fall over, moths, beetles, frogs, and birds — birds beyond counting, all the fauna the plateau lost or outlived or never fed, crowded against its foot like animals at a trough, drinking it secondhand. The birds ride the updrafts along the scarp in wheeling thousands and stop at the rim as if at a fence. In living memory no bird has crossed onto the flats. The town considers this good manners.

The river below the falls is called the Sweet, and the sea it runs to is called the Salt, and the town of the Letting sits at the seam. The Sweet glows. Faintly, always; strongly after rain on the high flats, the luminosity arriving two days behind the weather, so that the town reads the plateau's news in its river — *bright water tonight, they had a soaking up top Tuesday* — and every drop of it is old rain, sea-water that went up as cloud and was drunk and was carried through the body of the largest living thing there has ever been for — the well-priests say centuries; the pilots say longer — before being let go. The town drinks weather from before its founding. It is proud of this in a complicated way.

Where sweet luminous water meets salt, the estuary blooms. The plankton feed on what the plateau exhales, and the fish feed on the plankton, and the fish glow — lampfish, silver-bodied, lit from within like the veins of an old tapper's wrist — and this is the fishery, and the fishery is the town. The night boats work without lanterns; the catch lights itself; from the scarp road the estuary after dark is a slow galaxy, schools turning in the black water like constellations rearranging. And a portion of every catch, salted and casked, goes *up*: hauled on the switchback road three thousand meters to pay the works-house cats their civic wage. The Letting finds this endlessly funny in a fond way. The plateau's blood runs down, becomes fish, and is carried back up to feed the cats who sleep on its pulse. *The mountain feeds its own cats,* the fishwives say. *We just carry.*

The carrying is the other half of everything. Down the switchbacks come resin, lamp-sap, fiber cable, cloth, paper — the plateau's whole industry funneled through one road cut with three generations of priestly petition, a road that has long since decided it was its own idea and takes care of itself accordingly: the hairpins soften year by year, the outer edges thicken into guard-walls no one built, and the hauliers' carts wear grooves that the road, overnight, adjusts to fit. Up the switchbacks go fish, salt, and above all *metal* — because the plateau digested its mountain, and there is no iron on the flats, no stone, no ore; every nail, every auger, every piston sleeve, every bell in every tower of every true town came up this road on someone's cart. The foundry clans of the Letting are the plateau's entire mineral world, its outsourced skeleton. They cast the shift-bells. They stamp each one with the foundry mark, and once a year a warden comes down with the ledgers, and once a year the same entry is made regarding certain bells hanging in the towers of Old Tapping, bells no cart ever carried and no ledger ever recorded, bearing the foundry's mark, correct in every particular: *not ours; nothing wrong with them*. The plateau dreamed the bells, and it dreamed the mark too, because the mark is part of what a bell is now. The foundry has decided to take this as a compliment. Mostly.

Twice a generation, sometimes less, the Letting stops. The falls simply cease — the white column thins, breaks, and is gone, and a silence comes down on the town so total that people run into the streets, and everyone stands looking up at a wet green cliff with a notch in it, and the swifts pour out from behind the Hanging Step in a panicked ribbon, and then, after minutes or an hour, the swallow-swell comes down the empty channel and the water returns and the roar closes back over the town like a lid. The priests up top say the plateau swallows. Nobody down here argues. The years of the Held Breath are how the town counts its history — *my grandmother was married the spring after the second Holding* — and every child is told: when the falls stop, you stop too. It is felt to be the least you can do.

And the river brings down more than water. Things wash over the Letting — spat from the flow at the top of the falls, tumbled down the column, fished from the eddy pools below — and the town keeps a warehouse for them, the come-down house, a long stone building with a ledger of its own. Some of what arrives is simply lost: tools, hats, a child's boot, once a whole cart, matched against the lists the wardens send down and returned uphill with the next haul. And some of it never existed. Furniture from Old Tapping, dreamed and discarded. A chair of a design no joiner knows. A sap-lamp, still lit, that dims and brightens forty seconds to the beat and has never gone out in sixty years and hangs now in the come-down house over the ledger desk, by whose light the entries are written: *origin, none; condition, good.*

The people of the Letting keep tide time, and it is their tell, as the pause is the uplanders' tell: they track the water without knowing they do it. Mid-sentence, mid-sale, mid-argument, the eyes flick harborward — a half-second's audit of the tide's state — and back again, and they could tell you to the minute where the water stands without ever having decided to know. Take one of them inland and they grow uneasy in a way they cannot name, and what they miss, when pressed, is not the sea but *the turn*: the twice-daily hinge, the smell of the flats uncovering, the moment the harbor changes its mind. Theirs is a slower entrainment than the uplanders' — two beats a day instead of two a minute — but it is the same disease, or the same marriage. Everyone downstream of the plateau is married to a clock. The only question is the length of the pendulum.

They drink the Sweet, cut with well-water from the stone, and so they carry only a trace of the light — nothing you would see, except in the oldest of the harbor pilots, whose trade is the reason for their glow. The pilots are the town's mirror of the high-flat walkers, and they know it, and the two guilds exchange stiff formal letters twice a year and consider each other the only other sane people alive. Because the estuary fog misbehaves. Dilutely, gently — nothing like the flats — but a boat in thick weather off the river mouth can lose the distance, arrive an hour before it left, come home with a crew whose accounts of the passage do not match. The dreams, the pilots say, are water-soluble; they wash down and disperse in the Salt, and the mouth of the river is where the dissolving happens, and you do not want to be standing in a thing while it dissolves. So the pilots navigate by the one yardstick in the estuary that does not lie: *sweetness*. The mix of Sweet and Salt lies in gradients across the harbor mouth, honest as the pulse is honest, and a pilot brings a ship in through blind fog with a wet finger, tasting, calling the helm — *sweeter, two points off the ebb-channel; brackish, we're over the bar* — reading the water the way a walker counts the beat. Where the walkers are flat-voiced and hard to surprise, the pilots are garrulous and superstitious and never stop talking, but the craft underneath is the same craft: disbelieving your eyes for a living, and trusting the plateau's blood over your own.

The splicers are the priests' mirror. Pith-fiber cable does not die when it is cut; it *pauses*, and a splice is not a knot but a petition — the splicer lays the two ropes together and keeps them company, hours or days, until they decide they were always one rope. Rigging spliced right heals its own frays. Rigging spliced wrong heals into something else. The old ships of the Letting fleet, rigged in the same cable for generations, have crossed some line no one marked: their rigging has fused into single organisms, and the ships have *learned themselves* — a vessel that trims its own sheets a half-second before the gust, that will not, whatever the helm does, be laid over past a certain angle, that grows sluggish and sour when sold and lively again when bought back by the right family. The fishing nets have gone the same way, and the oldest are the most prized, because a net that has fished one estuary for eighty years has slowly resized its own mesh — widened it, deliberately, evenly — to let the young fish through. Nobody taught the nets husbandry. The fishwives' opinion, held loosely, is that the plateau was a long time learning to keep things alive, and the lesson is in the fiber, and the nets are only doing what everything from up there eventually does, which is take the long view.

Upriver of everything, in the worst weather in town — the perpetual spray at the foot of the gorge, where the roar is total and the moss grows on the insides of windows — is Underfall, the exiles' quarter. The pithfolk who come down, for trade or trouble or love, and cannot go back, and cannot sleep: every forty seconds, the body wakes into the absence of the beat, months of it, years. Except in Underfall. The falls carry the pulse down softened, a swell in the roar, felt in the sternum if you live close enough, and so the exiles cluster there in the wet and will not move to better streets, and sleep like the drowned. At their funerals they climb to the first eddy pool and drink the Sweet unmixed, and glow faintly through the night, and the town turns out to watch from below, and finds it beautiful, and has learned not to say so, because the exiles find it *homesick*, which is a different thing.

The harbor cats are small and quick and ordinary and look at everything, which is how the town knows the Old Wharf is real. The Old Wharf was the first, built in the founding generation on pilings of pith cut without petition — no priest within three thousand vertical meters — and the pilings healed wrong, which is to say they *rooted*. In the black estuary mud, fed on sweet water, they have been quietly growing for two hundred years, fused now below the waterline into a single body, the planking long since become bark, the mooring posts thickening into something with opinions. It has a pulse. Not the mountain's — its own: quick, light, twelve seconds to the beat, a child's heart. The harbor cats will not set foot on it. The harbormasters keep ledgers on its spread the way the wardens keep cat-maps, and the arithmetic is not hidden from anyone: the town knows what the harbor is, now, underneath; knows what it will be in ten thousand years, in a hundred thousand — knows, in other words, exactly how the Letting ends, which is not by flood or fire but by *incorporation*, the town becoming to the new growth what the old towns are to the old one. There has never been a serious motion to cut it out. The lease, as the harbormasters say, renews itself, and the terms are fair, and it is not as if the family upstairs has been a bad neighbor. Some nights the exiles of Underfall walk out along the Old Wharf and sit with a palm flat on the pilings, feeling a beat too quick to be home and too true to leave, like grandparents holding an infant who will outlive everything they can name.

The Salt gives its dead back, and the Letting buries at sea, mostly. But there is a trade upward, for those who can pay: the dead carried on the fish-carts, up the Stair of Backs where the mules refuse the last kilometer and human porters take the load, up onto the flats to be petitioned into the ground and settled forever in green glass, visible on bright days, waiting. The priests take the foreign dead without ceremony and without ever once refusing one, which the priests themselves find either touching or telling, and decline to say which. And so the circulation closes, the whole household of it: the sea sends its water up as cloud, and the plateau drinks it and thinks with it for centuries and lets it go; the blood comes down and becomes fish and the fish go up to the cats; iron goes up and bells go up and the dead go up, and dreams come down and are dissolved in salt, and the come-down lamp burns over the ledger, forty seconds to the beat. The tide turns twice a day. The falls breathe. Out past the fish-sheds the Old Wharf keeps its quick young time in the dark. Three clocks, and the town keeps all of them, down on the honest stone at the foot of the world, where the largest living thing there has ever been reaches the sea, and lets, and lets, and lets.
