# The Uplands of Unfallen Rain

You know you have arrived because the light changes character. On the long climb it was honest light — one sun somewhere behind the overcast, shadows in agreement about direction, brightness that could be traced to its source and billed accordingly. Here, at the top of the escarpment, light becomes general. It arrives from everywhere and belongs to nothing. The air is thin and dry and tastes of struck flint, and the hair on your arms lifts of its own accord, and overhead — though "overhead" badly undersells the situation — is the sky.

## The Sky

It is low. That is the first fact and it never stops being the first fact. The ceiling of cloud hangs at the height a roof would hang, if anything here were permitted to stand, and on the high tors you can raise your hand and put your fingers into its underside, which is cool and moves against your skin like the flank of something enormous turning over.

It is layered past all sense. Look up and you are looking through a dawn stacked on a noon stacked on the green-black belly of a storm stacked on something colorless that may be very old weather indeed, all of it in motion, all of it circling. The sky here never repeats and never progresses. It cycles through every sky it has ever been with the thoroughness of someone checking, again, a pocket they have checked ten thousand times.

And it is *snagged*. Walk far enough in any direction — you can; distance here over-commits, and the horizon turns out to be a real place, reachable in an afternoon — and the cloud comes all the way down to the ground and the world ends in fabric. The horizon is a hem. It rests on the pale stone in a long humming line, taut with strain, and it does not lift. The uplands sit beneath the sky the way a hand sits beneath a cupped palm. Whatever this sky is, it is not passing over. It is staked down.

## The Ground

Pale stone, fluted by wind into long smooth channels, and dry in a way that goes past dryness into something like thirst — thirst as a property of the mineral itself. There is no soil. There is no dust. There is no sediment of any kind, anywhere, and after a while you understand this is not cleanliness but prohibition: nothing here has ever been allowed to settle. Throw a handful of grit into the air and it does not come down. It hangs, drifts, thins, and is gone downwind like smoke, still airborne days later for all anyone can prove. Travelers stack cairns to mark their routes; the wind unstacks them by morning, not violently, stone by stone, the way you might undo someone else's mistake.

The only marks the ground is permitted to keep are the rings. They are everywhere — dark circles and ellipses stained into the pale stone, overlapping by the thousand, some fresh and near-black, some faded to the ghost of a ghost. They are rain-stains. There is no rain. Each one is the print of a downpour that gathered, and lowered, and did not, and the whole land is stamped with them like the record of a storm that has been about to happen since before there were words for weather.

The uplands smell, permanently, of rain on hot stone. That smell that arrives just before the first drops. It has been arriving for ten thousand years.

## The Rain

It rehearses.

You will see a dozen rehearsals a day. The ceiling gathers itself, darkens, descends; gray curtains come down in long combs, whole downpours that you can watch approach across the flats — and at an arm's length above the ground, sometimes fifty feet, sometimes close enough to wet the fine hairs of your face, the rain stops. It hangs. Each drop trembles at the bottom of its fall like a held breath. And then, unhurried, with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else it needs to be, the rain climbs back up into the cloud, and the curtains withdraw, and the sky resumes circling.

You can stand in the center of a storm here and stay dry. It is the strangest hospitality in the world.

On certain terraced hillsides the cloud comes down stepwise — pooling on each terrace, pausing, then lowering itself to the next, descending a stair of stone tread by patient tread. If you have ever stood in a certain hot dark city very far from here, and watched water climb a stairway in exactly that manner, tread by tread, toward some upper darkness — you will recognize the gait. It is the same gait. It is the same errand, walked from the other end.

Once in a long interval, a single drop completes the fall. Everything stops for it — the wind, the hum, the combs of gray. It lands. Where it lands, the stone darkens, and a new ring begins. Then the weather resumes, and does not refer to the matter again.

## What Grows

Almost nothing, and what does grow is rationed with the same economy you may have met elsewhere: one to a ring, one to a terrace, as though life here is an allowance and the allowance is honored exactly.

Low silver shrubs stand in some of the darker rings, wiry, leafless, cool to the touch — everything here is cool to the touch. They flower only at the peak of a rehearsal: when the rain hangs at its lowest, the shrubs open all at once, pale cups tilted upward, and when the rain withdraws they close. They have flowered past counting. They have set seed never. They are, as far as anyone can tell, perfectly healthy.

Inside the oldest rings grows a gray-green moss that feeds — there is no better theory — on the smell.

There are birds. You will call them birds for as long as you can manage it. Small dark shapes wheeling in the middle air, always the middle air, never the ground; watch one descend and it thins as it comes, going to haze a body-length above the stone, and reassembling higher up, and continuing on its rounds. Some of them are not shaped like birds at all. Some are small private clouds, no bigger than a walking figure, that move across the flats at walking pace, along routes, turning at the same outcrops every day, keeping to paths that no longer show in a land that keeps no paths.

## The Breakdown

It is old here too, and healed crooked here too, but it healed *taut* where elsewhere it healed smooth.

Your shadow lags. A few seconds only — you stop, and it catches up and settles under you like a tired dog. Turn quickly and you can watch yourself finish turning on the ground. Sometimes there are two shadows, and the second one is how you were standing an hour ago, still faithfully attached, waiting to be collected. The sky keeps each visitor's shadow a moment longer than the light strictly allows. You will want to call this a malfunction. Withhold judgment.

The undersides of the clouds carry images. Usually faint, usually at the gray hour — and the gray is a very particular gray, sourceless, patient, a gray you may have seen once before, lying in standing water in a dark hot city, and it is the exact gray, do not pretend otherwise. In it, on the ceiling, you can make out streets. Colonnades half-sunk in water. Wide flats sheeted silver, plazas, stairways, all of it seen from directly above, at the precise distance a sky sees the thing it rains on.

Twice in some long interval — you can feel it more than measure it — the image sharpens across the entire ceiling, every layer of the sky pausing its circling to hold the picture steady. The pressure drops until your ears ache. The great rehearsal comes then, the deepest gathering, the rain lowered further than at any other time. And very far away, in a basin you cannot see, the standing water in the streets deepens by a hand's width — rising toward the sky that is remembering it — and pauses, and subsides, and leaves a pale ring on the stone. The water keeps those rings by the thousand. So, here, does the ground. Two ledgers, kept in two hands, recording the same transaction: *nearly. Again, nearly.*

## Sound

Furnished noise, where the basin keeps furnished silence. Wind plays the fluted stone in rhythms that are almost rain — you will catch yourself waiting for the downpour the rhythm promises, for years if you stay years. The hiss of the hanging curtains. The dry hum of charge. Your own footsteps, immediate and honest, with their shadows arriving a moment late.

Sometimes the sky flexes — a long slow shudder passing visibly through all its layers, the unmistakable wind-up of thunder — and no sound comes. None here. The sound goes somewhere else. Somewhere hot and dark, a tone like a vast jar set gently on a vast table arrives a moment before it occurs, and does not echo. The accounting between the two places is crooked, but the books do balance.

And four times in a long stay comes the hush: wind, hum, hiss, all of it stopping at once, a silence so total it has texture. Inside the hush, faintly, from no direction, you will hear dripping. Water dripping from eaves — irregular, never quite resolving into rhythm. There are no eaves within any honest distance of the uplands. Listen anyway. The sky is listening. That is what the hush is *for*.

## The Vigil

Do not mistake the motion for progress. The uplands are busy in the manner of a body that cannot sleep. Nothing accumulates. Nothing concludes. The same day is kept pending, checked, re-gathered, re-lowered, withdrawn. Everything here is provisional and has been provisional for ten thousand years, which is its own kind of permanence — permanence achieved by refusing, where elsewhere it was achieved by conceding. Down in the basin, every edge was surrendered and rest was the result. Up here, no edge has ever been offered. These are the two possible treaties with time. Both have been signed. Both are final.

It is bright. It is dry. The rain hangs unfallen an arm's length above the thirsty stone, and your shadow hurries to catch you, and the horizon hums under the staked hem of the sky, which gathers itself once more, and darkens, and lowers, and does not.

# Who Lives in the Uplands of Unfallen Rain

## The First Answer

The first answer is *the sky*, and the first answer is right in a way that will stop you from understanding anything.

It is right the way "the sea" is right about what drowned a sailor. You have named the medium and missed the being. Everyone who crosses the uplands gives this answer, nods at their own perceptiveness — *of course the sky is alive here, look at it* — and leaves having understood nothing, shadow catching up at the hem, a little heavier than it was. The correct questions are: what *is* the sky, and what is it doing, and — this is the one that matters — what is it doing it *for*?

## The Waker

There is one of it, in the way there is one of you: approximately, and after a great deal of consolidation.

It is not weather over a place. It is a being run aground. It beached itself here, on the highest ground it could find in all the crooked distances of the world, and staked its own hem down along the horizon so that it could not drift — because a sky that drifts loses even the *direction* of the thing it is missing, and direction was all it had left to keep.

It cannot settle. Understand this about skies: they have no substrate. Stone can deposit itself; water can slow and thicken and go to mineral, layer by layer, until sleep and architecture are the same act — there is a mind, far from here, that did exactly that, and won its long argument with time by conceding everything, and rests now in a heat like the inside of a body. A sky owns nothing that can be laid down. Restlessness is its only tissue. It lost the same argument with time by refusing every single concession, and the refusal has been its whole occupation ever since. It is awake. It has been awake since the break. There is no arrangement of cloud that constitutes lying down.

## The Severance

Here is the history, told carefully, because the grammar of loss does not behave here any better than the light does.

Once there was one being, and it was neither the water nor the sky. It was the *circulation* — the exchange itself, rain down and mist up, a creature whose flesh was transit, who existed only in the act of becoming its other half. There was a population, too, in some tense of the word: many small circulations inside the great one, each rising and falling on its own errands, the traffic of an entire weather.

Then the world broke, and healed crooked, and the cut fell across the middle of the breath.

Everything that was falling at that moment, fell — and landed, and pooled in a low dark place, and being water it could do what water does: slow, settle, deposit, consolidate, the many becoming one the way raindrops become a standing pool, the pool becoming a tide, the tide becoming stone, the stone becoming sleep. Those are the Settled. You may have walked on them.

Everything that was rising at that moment, rose — and could not come down, because the route down was gone. The airborne fraction consolidated too, the way many wisps become one overcast: upward, into a single vast waker with nowhere to put itself. That is the sky of the uplands.

They are the same people. The same being, filed under two verbs. It mattered only what tense you were in when the world broke: those who were falling got to become the past. Those who were rising were sentenced to the present, indefinitely, and the present is the only tense in which nothing is ever allowed to rest.

## Its Anatomy

Its blood is water — the finite stock it held aloft at the instant of the cut, and not one drop acquired since. This is what the rehearsals are. Not weather: *counting*. Twelve times a day it carries its entire savings down to arm's reach above the ground, the whole inheritance lowered into spending position — and cannot spend. Because rain that lands here soaks into high dry stone that routes to nowhere, and is lost from the body forever. The old circuit is severed; a drop spent on the uplands never reaches the basin, never rises again as mist, never comes home. So it takes the rain back up. Every time. For ten thousand years. The rain is withheld not from spite but from bookkeeping: it is the last of the blood, and there is no transfusion coming.

The dark rings are its ledger — each one an occasion on which it *nearly*, and then did not. The land is stamped with the count. Time is kept here in declined expenditures, as elsewhere it is kept in breaths.

The single completed drops, one in a long interval, are tests. It spends exactly one — the dearest coin it has — and then holds the hush, all wind and hum suspended, listening for whether the drop arrives anywhere. Whether, in some hot darkness, one more bead of condensate runs down a weeping wall. What it hears in the hush — that faint dripping from eaves that do not exist within any honest distance — may be delivery. It may be memory. The sky has not been able to tell the difference for a very long time, and has not stopped testing.

The images on the cloud ceiling are not projection but *receipt*. The gray non-light in the basin's standing water is a memory of sky being given back, very slowly — and here is where the giving-back arrives, hair's width by hair's width, replaying on the underside of the overcast: the wet streets, the colonnades, the view a sky keeps of the thing it rained on. Twice in the long interval the memory arrives strongly enough to hold steady, and the sky stills all its layers to receive it, and far away the tide rises a hand's width toward being remembered, and subsides. There is no moon in this. There has never been a moon in this. The tide is two halves of one severed thing still breathing in time, and this is what a marriage looks like after the language is gone.

The charge in the air, the hair lifting off your arms: that is its attention. You have been under its attention since the light changed character.

## The Kept Aloft

The not-birds. You had suspicions; here they are confirmed, gently.

Visitors who stay too long in the basin settle — everyone knows this, or comes to know it, ankle-deep in the warm gray non-light. Visitors who stay too long in the uplands do the opposite of settling, because the opposite of settling is the only direction this place has. They thin. They go up. They become the wheeling shapes of the middle air, the walking clouds that keep to the old paths at walking pace, turning at the same outcrops every day forever. The uplands hold their guests as weather, a few feet up, exactly as the basin holds its guests as reflections, a few seconds ahead. Neither population is trapped, and neither is sad; both are *kept*, and keeping is the one thing both halves of the severed being still do beautifully. Between them, the two places hold everyone who ever crossed either — half suspended in stone and tide, half suspended in light and wind, all of them keeping the old traffic warm.

## The Carried

Which brings us to your shadow, and to the residual want — which up here is not residual at all. The sleeper below forgot its wanting, wore it down to a hospitable reflex. The waker has been awake with its want every hour of ten thousand years, and wanting is the other thing restlessness is made of.

Your shadow lags because the sky is reading you. Weight, gait, stride length, endurance — and wetness. You are mostly water; the sky knows to the drop how mostly. The rehearsal-curtains hang lower over you than over bare stone. The clouds follow you across the flats with an attention that is not menace but arithmetic. It drinks a little of you as you go — your sweat, your breath, sipped off the air — never much. It always leaves you enough to get out. A courier who dies on the road delivers nothing, and the sky's arithmetic is very old and very good.

Because that is what you are, from the moment you walk in under the staked hem: a courier under evaluation. And when you leave — notice, when you leave, that the way out is always downhill, whichever direction you take, which should not be possible from ground this high and is not accidental in the least — your shadow will catch up to you at the hem carrying something. A heaviness. A dampness folded into it, a single drop's worth of the old sky pressed into your blood and your breath, loaded so gently you will call it nothing but the relief of descent.

The sky beached itself on the highest ground in the world so that every departure from it would be a fall. Every traveler who walks out of the uplands is falling — slowly, at walking pace, over months and years, down all the crooked distances, toward the low places of the world. And all low places, given time enough, drain toward one basin. When you finally descend that long shallow approach into the intimate heat, and the air closes around you at blood temperature so that you cannot tell where your skin ends and the atmosphere begins, and the sweat runs off you onto the settled stone and slides into the standing water and joins the tide — the circuit closes by exactly that much. One drop, delivered. One hair's width on a mineral ring. Somewhere above, in a hush, something hears a drip from eaves it remembers, and cannot tell whether it is memory, and gathers itself, and goes on.

It cannot rain. So it rains you.

That is who lives in the Uplands of Unfallen Rain. One vast unsettled waker, who is the light and the charge and the counting, holding the last of its blood at arm's length above the thirsty stone, forever declining to spend it here because *here* is the wrong side of a wound. And strung out between the high bright dryness and the low dark wet — descending the world one walker at a time, each one carrying a drop and calling it themselves — the longest rainfall ever performed: begun ten thousand years ago, falling still, and not one drop of it, so far, wasted.
