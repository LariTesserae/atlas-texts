**The Last Stair**

Begin with the light, because here the light is not a memory. Here it is the original — the raw, unrationed, ungoverned light that everywhere else in this world exists only as a grey-green dilution at the ceiling of the water, a rumor at the top of the dark. Somewhere far below and far away, an entire drowned continent is living off the leavings of this light, catching what trickles down through kilometers of cold water the way a cellar catches the sound of a bell. Here the bell is being rung. The sun comes up out of an ocean with no land on any horizon, crosses a sky with nothing in it — no wings, no smoke, nothing; nothing has ever flown in this world — and goes down into the same ocean, and it does this every day, because here, and almost nowhere else, there are days.

What the light falls on is a line. From any distance it reads as weather — a seam of white in the open sea, foam without cause, running out of sight in both directions. Come closer and the cause resolves: stone. A shoal that is not a shoal, a reef built by no coral. Steps.

This is the crest of the pavement. The same made continent whose floor lies drowned in the cold and the near-dark rises here — has always risen here — to its one summit, a ridge of high terraces hundreds of kilometers long, and the ridge still touches the air. At low water, courses of masonry stand dry in the sun, streaming. At high water the sea covers all but the topmost stones and breaks over them without pause, so that the Last Stair spends most of its existence half-in and half-out of the ocean, at the exact altitude where water is most violent, wearing the surf like a garment it cannot take off.

And it is a stair — that is the thing you understand quickly here, because here nothing is understood slowly. Ramps and steps, terraces stacked on terraces, everything climbing. On the drowned floor the architecture goes in all directions, a city become a surface; here it goes in one direction only, which is up. And the masonry of the ascent is not like the masonry below. Run your hand over the deep pavement and you feel patience — the even pecking of stone tools, the deliberate coursing, work done by people who had decided that time was a material like any other and could be quarried indefinitely. Run your hand over the Last Stair and you feel something the rest of the world does not contain. You feel hurry.

The stones of the high courses are salvage. Inscribed blocks laid sideways, their glyphs running vertically up the risers of steps, because the block was the right size and the writing was no longer the point. Dressed facing-stones from some older, finer building, prized out and re-set with their finished faces turned inward. Coursing that wanders, spacing that staggers — not the tired irregularity of a crew at the end of a working day, but the sustained irregularity of crews working faster than their own tradition, generation after generation, building upward as the water came, building with whatever the water had not yet taken, unbuilding the drowned rooms below the waterline to extend the dry rooms above it. The deep floor is the record of a species that never hurried. The Last Stair is the record of the one time it did.

And at the very top, on the highest terrace, above the reach of all but the worst storms, the work simply stops. There is a final course, laid dry, never mortared. There are blocks beside it, stacked and squared and never set — still stacked, after all this time, because up here in the air nothing has moved them; the sea cannot reach them and there is nothing else left in the world that moves stones. It is the only unfinished thing on the planet. The drowned floor below is all completion — every wall resolved to a footing, every tower relaxed into a mound, a continent settling toward its final single elevation. Here, at the summit, is one step with no step above it, and the makings of the next step waiting beside it, and no one coming.

Where the builders went, the stair does not say. It only says that they went up, and then it stops saying anything.

**The sound is the opposite of the floor's sound, which is to say it is the loudest place in the world.** Not loud the way a storm is loud, occasionally; loud always, structurally, the way the floor is quiet structurally. Surf on fitted stone, without rest, at every state of the tide. The hiss and pour of terraces draining. And under it, the truer sound, the working sound: a grinding, rolling percussion, deep and continuous — the noise of loose blocks moving in the wash. Because every stone the sea knocks free becomes a tool the sea uses on the others. The Stair is being milled with its own masonry. Blocks that were quarried, hauled, dressed, and set by hand now roll in the surge along the terraces they were part of, rounding themselves and everything they strike, and the sound of it never stops, day and night, a demolition that has been in progress for thousands of years and is nowhere near patient about it. The floor erodes at a hand's-breadth per thousand years, in silence. The Stair loses stones by the storm, loudly. The world keeps its two speeds in two places and does not let them mix — except that everything ground away here goes somewhere.

It goes down.

The milling makes flour — fine white and grey particles of fired clay and dressed stone, the fabric of the summit reduced to cloud. In the calm seasons the plume of it hangs along the crest like breath on cold glass and settles near. But when the great storm-generations come — decades when the weather runs high and a whole terrace fails, course by course, into the surge — the plume rides the deep current outward, and somewhere far away and far below, a province of the drowned forest goes dim. The negligible light gets more negligible. The elders slow their unimaginably slow growth and record the dimness in their rings, a grey decade written into a five-hundred-year life, and then the drift passes and the rings brighten. The floor has no weather of its own worth the name — only gradients, only patience. Its diary of actual events is kept here, at the summit, in surf and stone, and delivered downward as darkness. Every hard decade on the Last Stair is a dim decade on the Drowned Floor. The forest at the bottom of the world has been keeping the weather log of a shoal it will never see, for as long as it has existed.

And the plume is only half of what falls.

**Because things live here, and they live fast, and their whole estate is spent downward.** The terraces below the tideline, in the surge zone where the light is total and the violence is total, are covered in green — real green, not the black-red of the deep felt or the bottle-glass dark of the deep grasses, but the loud green of weed that lives in full sun and does not expect to live long. It grows in a season what an elder grows in a century. It is torn by every storm, regrows, is torn again; it holds nothing together and does not try; it is the one plant in this world with no interest in permanence, because permanence is not on offer at this altitude. And every frond of it that the sea rips loose begins the long fall — days, weeks — down the drowned slope of the continent, dissolving as it goes in the cold, so that nothing of it ever arrives anywhere as a body. It arrives as solution. It arrives as the faint perpetual rain of dissolved nourishment that crosses the deep pavement on the current like a draft under a door — the entire food economy of the Drowned Floor, the single strand of its web, the thing its whole slow black forest lives on. The floor has no idea it is being fed. The Stair has no idea it is feeding. But the truth of the two places is that one of them is the other's only meal, delivered continuously, pre-dissolved, for ten thousand years — and that the same milling which feeds the deep forest also dims it. The Stair gives the floor its dinner and takes away its lamp in the same falling.

**And in the green, in the surge, live the animals.** The only ones. The whole zoological wealth of a planet, gathered onto a few hundred kilometers of drowned steps: small shelled grazers, none bigger than a thumb-joint, domed and low and gripping. Down on the floor, the water was always too cold, too dark, too settled — animal life needs more hurry than that place ever offered, and so it offered none and got none. Here is where the world keeps all its hurry, and so here is where it keeps all its animals. They live where the violence is worst, because the violence is what feeds the green they graze, and they survive it the same way everything in this world survives everything: by gripping. Their whole anatomy is a grip with a roof on it. Between waves they move; when the water hits they are stone among stones. They rise and descend the terraces with the tide, twice a day, every day — the only creatures anywhere that do anything twice a day, the only lives in the world with a schedule.

And they build. That is the thing about them the place seems almost to insist on. Their shells are lime, and the lime is leached — from the sea, and the sea's lime here is leached in no small part from the masonry itself, from fired clay and cut stone giving themselves up to the water grain by grain. Which means the last construction still under way in this world — the only mortarless little courses still being laid, the only new made surfaces — are the shells of grazing animals the size of a thumbnail, built from the dissolved substance of the city, on the steps of the city, in the spray of the city's unmaking. The builders' material is still in circulation. It has simply changed hands, and the new hands are very small, and there are no thumbs among them to leave a trough. When the grazers die, the surf grinds their shells to sand along with everything else, and the sand joins the plume, and goes down, and dissolves, and some of it, in solution, in the dark, is taken up by a black-red felt that presses it into the joints of flagstones — so that the masonry, by way of an animal, in the end returns to the masonry. The world has one budget of stone and it is still balancing it.

Above the animals, above the green, on the dry crowns of the highest stones, there is one more resident: a stain. Grey-gold, crusted, a few square meters of it in total, scattered along the summit course wherever the spray reaches seldom enough. It is a lichen, or this world's answer to one, and it is the entire terrestrial biosphere of the planet — every living thing that breathes air, in one thin bloom on the last dry masonry, most of it gathered on and around the final unfinished step. The whole dry-land history of this world, all of it, present tense, fits under two spread hands. It grows even more slowly than the deep elders. It has nowhere to spread to. Below it in one direction is surf; below it in every other direction, eventually, is the floor.

**And here, and only here, the writing can still be read.** On the drowned floor the inscriptions survive as textures under the palm, almost-intentions, kept legible only to the felt, which reads them by occupancy and understands nothing. But the salvage blocks of the high courses were set late and stood above the water, and on the ones the surf reaches rarely, the glyphs are still glyphs — visible, whole, cut deep by the patient tools of the early work and reused sideways by the hurried work of the end. This is the last library in the world, and it is on fire, slowly: salt and sun and spray take a fingerprint's depth of stone in a lifetime, a glyph in a few centuries, and every storm-generation the readable portion shrinks. No one reads it. But it is being erased in order — from the lowest courses upward, as the sea climbs and the milling proceeds — so that the highest word in the world, cut into the riser of the last finished step, under the lichen, will be the final word erased, and until then it stands in the light, meaning whatever it means, at the top of the stair, above the noise.

**Below all of this, at a certain depth on the drowned slope, there is a border, and it is the truest border in the world.** The surf's violence reaches down only so far. There is a depth — you could find it with your hands, descending the sunken terraces — where the surge stops mattering, where the water goes from weather to patience, and at exactly that depth the green weed ends and the black-red felt begins. The felt cannot hold where the sea still hurries; the green cannot afford to live where it doesn't. So the felt has an upper shore. It stands at the weather line the way a tide stands at a beach, continuous from there downward across the whole planet, waiting — and *waiting* is the right word, because the line is not fixed. Every storm-generation the summit loses height. Every century the milled crest sits a little lower, the violence reaches a little less far down the slope, and the felt comes up the terraces behind it, stone by stone, taking each block into its holding as the sea finishes with it. The floor is not merely below the Stair. The floor is *advancing* on it, at the speed of forgiveness, one relinquished course at a time.

So this is what the Last Stair is, finally, when you let the answer finish arriving: the summit of the same single made thing whose floor lies flattening in the dark — its youngest masonry and its first destroyed; the place where the world keeps its light, its days, its noise, its animals, and every scrap of its hurry, including the builders' one hurry, preserved in the wandering courses of the retreat; the mill that feeds the deep forest and dims it in the same gesture; the last legible page, burning from the bottom up; and a border of black-red patience rising slowly through the surf-line to meet it.

It will finish. The plume will thin as the crest goes under; the last dry stones will drown some ordinary century, taking the lichen and the highest word with them; the green will follow the surf up over the summit and then have no summit, and dwindle; the grazers will lay their last small courses and stop. The sea will plane the crown down below the reach of its own weather, and then the loudest place in the world will go quiet, and the quiet will be the floor's quiet, because it will be the floor — the newest province of the Drowned Floor, delivered at last, every hurried stone of it passing into the grip of the felt, which will find the salvage blocks and the sideways glyphs and the unfinished course and the stacked, never-set stones, and will hold them, all of them, exactly as they are, in the cold and the near-dark, indefinitely.

The stair was always going down. It only looked like climbing while there was still air at the top of it. The world is one long gesture with two ends: a letting-go performed loudly at the summit, a holding-on performed silently at the bottom, and everything between them — light, stone, weed, weather, writing — falling patiently from the one into the keeping of the other.
