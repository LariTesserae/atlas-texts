## The Bed of the New World

You arrive at the bottom of the wind.

Everything that ever left the Roof was coming here. The plateau has an edge — no one standing on it has ever found the edge, but the wind found it long ago, and the storm's one exit is there: a place where the eternal turbulence, having nowhere else to put itself, finally spills over and pours down the flank of the continent in a cataract of air kilometers thick. Follow that current — a distance no one walks, a current everything rides — down through thickening pressure and gathering warmth, down past the altitude where sound gets its voice back, down until the air is dense enough to lean against and the light has gone soft and golden and nearly blind. Then the descent ends, because everything's descent ends here.

This is the low place. This is where the planet keeps what it subtracted.

Call it a plain, though "plain" suggests land, and the ground here is only provisionally solid. The Bed is a deposit the size of an ocean: the entire eroded substance of the Old World — its mountains, its soils, the loads of its vanished rivers, its forests, its cities, its dead — milled to flour by the storm and laid down here, grain upon grain, in order, over the whole of deep time. There is no bedrock within reach of any instrument. There is no native stone, no native dust, no native anything. Every particle of this country arrived from somewhere higher. The Bed is made, in its entirety, of elsewhere.

That is the first fact, and you should stand on it — carefully, since it will bear your weight only if you keep moving.

## The Wall

The second fact occupies the whole windward horizon.

Turn toward the weather and there it is: a cliff that begins in fog and does not end. It rises out of the fens, gray-gold where the haze thins enough to show it, and goes up past every cloud the Bed can make, up past the last altitude at which this thick air holds any color, and vanishes — not behind anything, simply *out*, into a height the eye declines to finish. No one on the Bed has seen its top. No one on the Bed doubts that it has one. Everything they own came off of it.

They call it the Wall, when they are being plain, and the Old Country, when they are not.

Down its face, forever, comes the Downfall. It is a waterfall in every respect except substance: the storm's exhaust, the great overhead sea of the Roof pouring off its rim and falling — visibly falling, a curtain of hurrying haze wider than nations — until it strikes the dense air of the low world and spreads out across the Bed as ordinary wind, ordinary weather, ordinary days. Up there, in air too thin to speak, the storm rages in silence. Down here, arriving, it finally gets to make its noise. The Downfall roars. It has always roared. Its sound rolls out over the whole country as a floor of bass that never varies and never stops, so constant that the people of the Bed do not exactly hear it; they hear *with* it, the way you see with light. They call it the Loud. Every direction on the Bed is reckoned from it. Every silence on the Bed is measured against it and found partial.

Here is the thing to sit with, because it governs everything that follows: the Downfall goes one way. Air pours down it; sound pours down it; dust and water and the dead of the sky pour down it; nothing — nothing — goes back up. The Wall is not a barrier between two countries. It is the direction of time, stood on end. The Bed's whole relationship to the world above is the relationship of the present to the past: total inheritance, and no reply.

## The Ground

The ground is alive in the geological sense and will not let you forget it.

It ticks. Kneel — the mud will hold a kneeling person longer than a standing one — and put your ear near the surface, and under the Loud you hear the country settling: small dry clicks and long sighs, grain finding its final seat against grain, the sound of an archive compacting itself. On the Roof, the land finished changing before anything now alive existed, and holds a silence so complete you hear your pulse. Here the land has never once stopped talking. It is not finished. It is the only unfinished ground on the planet — the one place where land is still, technically, being made — and it mutters about it constantly.

It also swallows. Nothing set down on the open Bed stays on the surface. A dropped tool is gone in a season; a shelter in a decade; anything, given time. The mud opens slow soft whirlpools — *swallows*, the people call them, a noun — that turn once in a year and take what they hold. Travelers from firmer worlds find this horrifying. The people of the Bed find it merely administrative. The ground is not hungry, they will tell you. The ground is *filing*.

Because that is what the Bed is, under everything: the record. The Roof is a world with no memory of itself — every grain that could testify was plucked away ages ago; what remains up there is only the interlocked, the unremovable, the stone that outlasted its own biography. All the testimony came here. Drive a core downward through the mud and you drive it backward through time: the shallow layers fine as breath, the deep layers coarse and impatient, and every stratum a season of the Old Country — the flour of mountains that no longer exist, laid down in the order of their unmaking; black bands that were forests once, milled and shelved; glittering veins that assay as nothing natural and are understood, quietly, to be cities. The Roof forgot everything. The Bed forgot nothing. Between them, the wind carried the world's whole memory downhill and buried it in order, and the Bed's people were born standing on the only complete history that exists — kilometers of it, under their feet, legible to anyone patient enough to read downward.

## The Air, and the Water

The air is the opposite air.

Where the Roof's atmosphere has thinned to a hard blue-violet clarity — two hundred kilometers of vision, knife-edged shadows, a sun at full honest strength through almost nothing — the Bed's air is thick, warm, wet, and full. The sun here is a smear of amber that moves through the fog like a lamp behind cloth. Shadows barely happen. On a clear day you can see perhaps a kilometer; there are not many clear days; the people of the Bed have never seen a star, and their word for the night sky's contents translates, roughly, as *rumor*. The Roof sees too far and has nothing to look at. The Bed has everything close and cannot see it. Neither country has ever seen the other, though each stands on the other's horizon: the Roof's edge curves away into the violet; the Bed's Wall goes up into the gray.

And the water — all of it is here. This is where the Roof's rain retired. The water that once carved the Old Country, that ran its vanished rivers and paid off its stone and was finally released from service, drained down the ages to the low place and stayed: as fog, as fen, as slow warm channels that wander the mud in circles with no sea to reach and no gradient worth obeying. Rain on the Bed is old rain — the same water, around and around, that once did the work of unmaking a continent. It still falls with a workmanlike heaviness, the people say, as though it remembers its trade. But there is nothing left anywhere for it to cut. It comes down on mud that is already flour, stirs it, and settles. Erosion, on this world, has retired into weather.

On the Roof, the land owes the water cycle nothing. Down here is where you find out where the debt went. The Bed *is* the payment — the whole of it, land and water together, everything the high country rendered up, pooled at the bottom of the wind and holding.

## The Gray Rain

Now the sky's contribution, which never stopped.

The mineral mail from the Roof is nearly finished — the fine dust still arriving amounts to a hair's breadth a century, the last thin letters of a correspondence that was once a deluge. But the Downfall carries something else, and it carries it steadily: the dead of the storm.

You will remember the threadfall — the glassy filaments, finger-long, hollow, faintly green, that the Roof's travelers find caught in the calm-zone seams after the greatest fronts, and take for curiosities. Understand what they are seeing: the merest windward spillage of a rain that falls, in earnest, here. The storm-sea overhead the Roof is inhabited — the Bed settled that question long ago, without ever seeing the storm — and everything that lives aloft dies aloft, and everything that dies aloft comes down the Downfall. It arrives over the Bed as a perpetual soft descent of glass and chaff and finer things, drifting out of the fog ceiling day and night, settling on the fens like the snow of an ocean floor. The people call it the gray rain, and it is the foundation of every food chain in the low world. Everything on the Bed eats the sky's dead, directly or at some remove. The storm's table has two ends: at the high end, it waters the seams and feeds a country by the trace and the film; at the low end, it feeds a country by burial. The same vast resident keeps both households. Only one of the households knows about the other.

## What Grows, What Lives

Where the Roof rations life by the hand-span and the century, the Bed teems, and teems quickly. The fens are loud with small lives — swarming, filtering, luminous in the fog at night in drifts of cold green — and almost none of them will matter to this account, because abundance, here, is the background. Two residents deserve the foreground.

The first are the pilings.

They look, at the surface, like low pale reefs — colonies of filter-growth a few meters across, crowning just above the mud, sieving the gray rain from the air and the wet. What they are is taller than that. A piling grows upward at precisely the rate the ground swallows, and it has been doing so, each of the old ones, since deeper in time than the cores can date: beneath every living crown stands a column of its own dead going down through the whole archive, kilometers of it, the only vertical things in a country of layers. The readers of the Bed treasure them beyond price, because a piling is a *bound* history — a single unbroken witness running through all the strata at once, against which every core can be dated. Where the glasswort of the Roof is patience compressed into a hand-span, the pilings are patience *extended*: a tower of centuries balancing forever on its own grave, keeping pace with the filing of the world, never more than a crown's height ahead of burial. The people say of a steady person: *they grow like a piling* — meaning, exactly as fast as they are being swallowed, and no faster, and no slower, forever.

The second resident is the swimmer.

It is flat, broad, dark, and slate-textured, three or four meters across, with no visible head, and if that description stirs something in you, hold the feeling; the people of the Bed have nothing to compare it to, having never seen its cousin. The swimmer moves *through the ground* — flying, there is no better word, through the soft upper fathoms of the mud as its counterpart flies through the near-nothing above the Roof: one of them hanging motionless in air too thin to hold anything, the other banking slow and silent through a medium nearly solid. Between them they bracket the world's whole range of thickness, and neither knows the other exists.

Unlike the glider, the swimmers are many. They rise at the pilings — always at the pilings — and graze the crowns with an underside no living person has seen, cropping the filter-growth precisely back to the pace of the settling, so that no column ever overtops its balance and breaks. Ungrazed, a piling would outgrow the swallowing, stand proud, and topple, taking its kilometers of testimony down sideways into illegibility. The swimmers prune the historians. Whether they know this is not a question the Bed finds useful. They do their work. The people keep, without ever having negotiated it, a courtesy: no one harvests a crown the swimmers have grazed until it has grown back past the grazing line. No one can say when the rule began. It has never been broken. It is the entire treaty, and it is the same treaty, word for word, that is kept at the seams of the high country by people who will never know they have co-signatories.

## The White Floes

Permanence, on the Bed, comes in exactly one material, and the material will be familiar.

Scattered across the fens — perhaps a few hundred of them, inventoried and named — are objects of a seamless white substance that the mud will not take. They do not sink. They do not settle, tick, or drift downward the width of a grain in a century of watching. They ride the surface of the swallowing country the way ice rides water, circling the Bed on the slow currents of the settling ground, and everything about them refuses the place: mud will not stick to them, water beads and leaves, the gray rain slides off. Where one was — apparently — broken, the break-surface is the same seamless white as the rest, as if the object has no interior, or is interior all the way through.

The people live on them. There is nowhere else to be permanent. A floe is a village that moves without moving — a white raft of forever, drifting the archive at the pace of centuries — and standing on one you notice the other thing they do: the ground beneath a floe goes still. No ticking. No muttering. The filing stops in a teardrop of quiet mud around each one, and within that teardrop, under the eternal floor of the Loud, the people of the Bed experience the closest thing their world offers to silence. They call it the half-quiet. Children are taken to stand in it once, young, so that they will know what the word means. Some find it holy. Some find it unbearable. It is the same gift, translated: the makers' material makes stillness in whatever medium surrounds it — a calm in the wind of the high country, a calm in the ground of the low — two halves of one toolkit, dealt to two households that have never met.

And the shapes of the floes are a catalogue no one upstairs has thought to want. There is a famous one — the Mast, they call it — a tapering white spar six meters long, narrower than an arm at one end, that has been drifting the Bed since before the oldest registry: the top, though no one on either country knows it, of a broken needle that still stands on the Roof, showing no interior at its stump. There are floes shaped like low pylons, in numbers; somewhere far above, a line of pylons marches to the horizon with every third one missing, and the Bed, mapping its white rafts over generations, has unknowingly completed the inventory of the Old Country's losses. The two peoples hold one artifact-hoard between them, divided by the Wall: the Roof keeps everything that could not be moved, and the Bed keeps everything that could.

## The People, and the Reading

They are more numerous than the keepers of the Roof, and they live otherwise: in bands and villages on the floes, in reed-and-raft outliers at the rich fens, strung across the fog by channels and by sound. In air this thick the voice carries wonderfully; the Bed is a country of calling, of songs that travel, of conversations held across half a kilometer of gray — the precise inverse of the high country, where speech survives only in the calm and the crossings are made in silence, in signs. Up there, quiet is the medium and sound is the shelter. Down here, sound is the medium, and the half-quiet is the shrine.

Their vocation is the Reading.

It is the continuous, communal, generations-deep project of coring downward — of taking the archive underfoot and rendering it back into a world. They read grain-size the way sailors read water. They have reconstructed, from mineral runs and the mathematics of settling, the shapes of mountains that stopped existing before their species did; they have named the vanished rivers of the Old Country by the signatures of their loads; they have brought up the milled seeds of forests, the crumbs of cities, and — in the glittering unnatural veins — enough of the makers' leavings to know that whoever unbuilt the high country built things first, and built them small, and built them strange. The people of the Bed know the Old World the way you know a book that was pulped: completely, and not at all. They can tell you what stood, and where, and in what order it came down. They cannot tell you why, and they do not know — this is the tender blindness at the center of everything — that the pulped book is still inhabited. From below, the Wall goes up into fog and the letters have nearly stopped, and the reasonable conclusion, the one the cores support, is that the Old Country is over.

Because near the top of every core, everywhere on the Bed, lies the boundary they call the Quiet: the horizon in the mud where the sediment goes suddenly fine, then sparse, then almost nothing — the stratum of the finishing. Below it, the world happening: coarse, hurried, generous. Above it, the long near-silence since, a film of hair's-breadths in which the whole history of the Bed's own people fits without troubling the record. Their now sits on top of the Quiet like breath on glass. And in that same boundary layer, exactly there, the glass of the gray rain begins — the storm's sea appears in the record at the very moment the land finishes — and from this the Reading's minority view is drawn, held quietly, like a theory about needles: that when the ground was done, the makers moved the world's life *up*, stocked the storm, stirred themselves into the turbulence, and left the wind running. It is the same guess, grain for grain, that the oldest keepers of the Roof make from the opposite evidence, in the opposite silence. Two households, back to back through a wall the height of the sky, arriving at one rumor.

Their dead they give to the ground — of course; there is nowhere else — but not carelessly. A body sunk into the archive is annotated: sealed with its record, name and floe and works, written durably and bound to it, so that the filing stays legible, so that a reader ten thousand years down will not mistake them for weather. The Roof's people lay their dead on open stone to water it, to rise as frost and bloom and lacquer; the Bed's people shelve theirs, footnoted, in the memory of the world. *To join the Reading*, they say, for dying. Neither people knows the other's custom. Between them they have divided death itself the way they divided the treaty and the toolkit: the high country turns its dead into weather, and the low country turns its dead into record, and the wind, as always, would carry the one down to the other if there were anything left of it to carry.

Their ethic — the thing visitors notice, the trait that shapes their humor and their law — the people themselves call *receiving well*. They are the inheritors of everything, and can return nothing; the Downfall goes one way; the entire world is a gift that cannot be thanked. So they receive with exactness. Everything that falls is catalogued. Nothing that arrives is wasted. Every particle, their teaching runs, is somebody's finished country — walk anywhere on the Bed and you are walking on the whole of an elsewhere, ground fine — and the only payment that can be made downhill of everything is attention. Which is, though they will never learn this, the identical currency in which the keepers of the Roof pay the storm. The clerks of the high country and the readers of the low have never exchanged a word, and keep the same faith by the same means: that a maintained world deserves maintenance, and the only maintenance available is witness.

They have one story about the Wall itself, told late, in the half-quiet. That long ago, when the Downfall was said to run weaker, a band went windward to climb the Old Country and find where everything came from, and did not come back — and that this is why, on the Roof, there are people at all: a folk whose oldest accounts begin *already there, already walking*, with no stories of climbing, because the climb was the thing the climb erased. The story is not verifiable and not doubted. If it is true, then the two households are one family, filed on separate shelves, and the kinship went up the Wall the only time anything ever has.

## The Answer

And then there is the note.

The Loud is not featureless. It has thunders and shoulders, moods the people read like a face. But sometimes — a dozen times in a lifetime, so do not count on it — there comes down the cataract, riding among the ordinary roar, a tone: low, several seconds long, felt in the sternum more than heard. It arrives from Up, which is the only place anything arrives from. It sounds — the oldest listeners are exact about this — like one voice.

When it comes, the swimmers rise. All across the fens, everywhere the fog holds people to swear to it, the dark shapes come up out of the ground at the pilings and the floe-edges, hang half-surfaced in the mud, and answer: the same low tone, many-throated, sustained, rolling out across the Bed and into the archive and up against the base of the Wall — where it stops. Sound goes down the Downfall as everything goes down the Downfall. Nothing goes back up it. The answer has never once been delivered.

The people answer too. They learned it from the swimmers, or the swimmers from them; the registries disagree and the argument is friendly. When the note comes down, every voice on the Bed that hears it returns it — households, channels, whole floes sounding together in the fog — knowing, because they are exact people, that the reply will climb a few hundred meters of a wall that does not end, and fall back. They do it anyway. Their saying for it is the plainest thing they own: *It costs nothing to answer, and it may be someone.*

They do not know about the glider. They do not know that far above the fog, above the Wall, above the storm, a single dark shape has been making rounds across a finished country for longer than records reach, sounding one tone at every station and hearing nothing back — that the bleakest and tenderest theory of the high country's keepers is that it calls *in case*, the last of its kind, listening down all the ages for a second voice. The second voice exists. It is a whole country. It has answered every single time, in a chorus of mud and fog and human throats, and every answer lies where all things of the Bed lie — settled, filed, kept, and unheard.

The keepers log the tone. The readers log the answer. Between the two ledgers stands the Wall, and down it, one way, forever, comes everything.

## Lying There

So this is the place, taken whole, and taken — because it cannot be taken any other way — together with the one above it:

A country at the bottom of the wind, warm and wet and blind and loud, built entirely out of another country's subtraction, still rising a hair's breadth a century on the dust of a finished world and the dead of an inhabited sky. Above its one horizon, a wall the height of the atmosphere, pouring. Underfoot, the complete memory of everything the high country forgot, kilometers deep, in order, still muttering as it files. Riding the mud, white unsinkable fragments of the makers' toolkit, each one holding open a pocket of stillness in the swallowing ground, as their standing siblings hold open pockets of stillness in the raging air a world above. Through the soft fathoms, the dark swimmers, pruning the historians; on the floes, the readers, annotating their dead into the planet; and over everything, the Loud — the voice the storm was owed for all its silent ages, paid out here, at the bottom, without pause.

These are not two places. They are one place at two moments, drifted apart in space as well as time. The Roof is the world that finished; the Bed is where the world went. Every polish up there is a layer down here; every silence up there is this roar, arrived; the high country's ending has been the low country's substance all along, delivered grain by grain down a wall that only gives. The Roof traded motion for stillness and mistook — or did not mistake — the ending for home. The Bed made the opposite peace. It stands on ending, is built of ending, reads ending for a living, and has turned it all, by sheer exactness of receiving, into the one country on this world that is still becoming.

Stand on a floe at the end of a day, in the half-quiet, with the fog going dark and the pilings' crowns catching the last of the smeared amber light, and feel the two households hold their positions through the Wall: the clerks above keeping the present of a world with no past, the readers below keeping the past of a world they believe has no present, the storm between them feeding both ends of its table, the treaty kept at the seams and at the crowns, the tone going down and the answer going nowhere, forever, in good faith.

Then the note may come — a dozen times in a lifetime, so do not count on it — down the cataract and into your sternum, and around you in the fog the dark shapes will rise and the voices will lift, and you will hear a whole country answer a question it has never been able to return, because it costs nothing, and it may be someone.

The readers log it. The mud takes the day's grain. The Downfall continues.

That is what lies there: the letter of a world that mailed itself downhill, still arriving, still being read — a country of inheritors keeping perfect records of a gift they cannot answer, on the bed of a world that ended so generously, and so slowly, that everything living there mistakes the ending for a beginning. Or does not mistake it. Knows it perfectly well, and answers anyway.
