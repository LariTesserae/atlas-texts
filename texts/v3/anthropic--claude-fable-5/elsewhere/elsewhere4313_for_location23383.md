**Thumb Pond**

You reach it by a lane that gives up before the water does, so the last hundred yards are a footpath through sedge you must take on trust. The pond announces itself before you see it: a change in the air, cool the way open water is cool, but with something underneath — a colder cold, out of all proportion to the season, as though the pond were remembering a cellar it had never been in. Then the sedge opens, and there it is, in a fold of ordinary farmland where no pond has any business being: a broad oval of dark water, deeper than its size suggests, in a hollow that no river carved and no glacier left.

That is the first thing the maps will tell you, if you consult them, and the last thing they agree on. No stream feeds Thumb Pond. No stream leaves it. It sits in its unexplained depression collecting rain and losing water to somewhere — the level drops a hand's width in the driest summers and never further, rises in the wet years and never floods — draining, evidently, downward, into ground that takes what it is given without comment. Dowsers have walked its shore and disagreed with each other. A county surveyor once spent a fortnight trying to reconcile it with the contour lines and left without filing a report. The schoolteacher says kettle hole, and the word is right in the way words are right when no one presses them: a kettle hole is a hollow left by buried ice, and there was never any ice, and everyone knows it, and *kettle hole* is what goes in the parish record anyway.

The village has an older explanation. A giant, they say, in the soft first days of the world, pressed his thumb into the ground here — out of curiosity, the story specifies, not anger; the tellers are always careful about that — and the mark filled with rain, and that is the pond. Children are shown the shape of it from Beacon Hill to prove the point: seen from up there, the pond does have something of a thumb about it, a long oval with a slight lean, broad at one end. The schoolteacher smiles at this. The story is wrong in every detail that can be checked and correct in its essentials, which is a kind of truth the village is comfortable with and the schoolteacher is not.

What the story does not know, because you must be on the water to learn it, is the whorl. The pond's bottom is not smooth. It is ridged — low concentric bars of harder ground, rising a foot or so from the silt, curving around a deep central hollow in nested arcs. Fishermen know them the way they know their own kitchens: the perch hold along the second ring, the weed takes the shallow arcs, the deep center is cold and empty and belongs to the pike. Rowing across on a still day you can watch the bars pass under the hull like the growth rings of something enormous. No one has ever mapped them entire. No one has ever needed to. They are simply the pond's peculiarity, its private grain, the way it was made — and *the way it was made* is a phrase the pond keeps returning to you, standing on its shore. Everything about it feels less like landscape than like workmanship. The ridgeline of the far bank runs a shade too cleanly. The depression sits in the fields like a signature on a finished page.

Because the pond is finished — that is its essential condition, the exact and opposite condition of some other place, and you can feel it without knowing what you are feeling. Nothing here is prior. Nothing is curing. The stone under the silt decided its structure long ago, in some patient dark, and has held its decision ever since; and everything quick and brief and warm-blooded has come flooding in to live on the settled surface of that decision. The pond is *loud* with what lives in it. Dragonflies stitch the margins all summer. Frogs argue in the sedge. Swallows take the evening midges in long low passes, and a heron stands in the shallows with the patience of a much slower creature, doing a fair imitation of stillness and giving itself away every few minutes with a strike. Mice live in the warming hut. Moss grows on the jetty and nobody deports it anywhere. It is a place built for *after* — for creatures made for finished ground — and they have accepted the invitation in their thousands, generation upon brief generation, wearing the footpath smooth, and the pond forgives every mark they make. That is the other thing to understand about it. Skate blades score the winter ice in ten thousand white arcs, and the thaw erases all of them. Initials cut in the jetty rail gray out in a decade. Here, on the settled surface, nothing you press into the world stays pressed. The pond is the one place in the parish where a mark is safe to make, precisely because it once *was* a mark — the only permanent one its maker ever left — and it seems to have drawn its own conclusions about that.

In winter it becomes the center of the world. The cold arrives, and the pond — colder at its heart than any pond should be, cellar-cold, iron-tasting, as though chilled from below by breath rising through the ground — freezes early and freezes hard, always the first water in the county to bear. And in the rare years the freeze comes before the snow, the ice goes down black and clear as glass, and the whole village walks out onto it and looks through their own feet at the whorl: the nested rings of the bottom, visible entire, arc within arc around the deep center, and the skaters trace them for the pleasure of the long curve, round and round, children and grandmothers, lanterns at dusk, everyone circling and circling on the clear black ice — reading the signature without knowing it is one, skating figures inside a fingerprint, above the pressed and settled curiosity of something that will not be finished for a very long time yet.

**The Warden of Thumb Pond**

The office is older than anyone's memory of the office beginning. There has always been a warden — the parish record uses the word without ever defining it — and there has always been the hut on the north shore, low and tar-roofed, with its stove and its bell, and there has always been one family in the village whose eldest walks out onto the December ice before anyone else is awake, alone, at first light, to ask the pond whether it will bear.

The current warden is a man of sixty-some winters, and he is the slowest person in the parish. This is said of him fondly and it is meant precisely. He was quick once, they'll tell you — quick as any boy, quicker than most on skates — and the office slowed him, the way a river slows in a broad place, because the work of the office cannot be done at any other speed. The work is attention. The pond will tell you everything about itself, his father taught him, and his father's father taught his father, but it tells you at its own pace, and a man in a hurry hears nothing at all.

So he does what the wardens have always done. On winter mornings he takes the auger and the rod and walks the ice in a pattern his family has walked so long that the route itself is an heirloom: out along the second ring, where the ice sets truest, cutting his test holes, letting down the rod. The rod is ashwood, black with handling, notched along its length — not with measurements, or not only; some of the notches are depths, and some are dates, and some are marks whose meanings died with the wardens who cut them, and he runs his thumb over those the way you touch the names on old graves. When the ice is four fingers of good black he rings the bell once, and the village comes. When it is not, he rings nothing, and stands at the shore all day if he must, turning back the impatient, which is the hard half of the office and the reason it belongs to the slow.

But the true work, the private work, the work that makes a warden and not merely a man with a bell, is the listening. Before he drills, before he measures, he kneels on the new ice in the gray of first light and puts his ear against it. Anyone watching from the shore — no one ever is — would see a man prostrate on black glass above forty feet of dark water, motionless, for ten minutes, for twenty. What he is listening to is the ice deciding. New ice ticks. It creaks and settles and rings with small granular adjustments, the sound of water organizing itself, choosing its crystal structure, making up its mind — and the warden has learned, over fifty years, to hear the difference between ice that is still arguing and ice that has decided. He could not write the difference down. He has tried, for his son's sake, and the words go dead on the page. It is a knowledge that lives in the ear and passes only from kneeling man to kneeling boy, and it is, though he has no way of knowing this, the same skill practiced in the same posture in a wet iron room far beneath the roots of the county — an ear against a curing surface, waiting for the sound of a material to finish deliberating. Two listeners, one above, one below, attending to the same world at the moment of its setting. The warden hears the quick, shallow version, a decision that takes a night and lasts a season. The other hears the deep one. Neither knows he has a colleague.

Though the warden, alone in the parish, suspects *something*. It is there in the ledger.

The ledger is the family's other heirloom, a fat calf-bound thing continued through eleven hands, and what it records is dates. First bearing ice. First open water. The glass winters, marked with a star. The level of the pond at midsummer, taken from the notch on the jetty post, faithfully entered for two hundred years and more — the same hand's-width fall in drought, the same stubborn ceiling in flood, the pond holding its level against all sense, giving its surplus downward to drains no one has ever found. Eleven wardens have kept these dates without knowing that dates are being kept about them in return; that the ground their pond sits on wears, somewhere in the dark below the world's basements, a stamped brass label; that theirs is a place with a birthday. The ledger and the brass are the two archives of a single fact, kept at two speeds, by two keepers, and they have never been compared and likely never will be.

And on the last page of the ledger — the back page, upside down, where a boy would write who wanted his writing private — there is a drawing.

The warden made it at eleven years old. It was a glass winter, and his father had rung the bell, and the boy had climbed Beacon Hill instead of skating, for no reason he could ever give, and looked down at the pond entire: the black clear ice, the whorl of the bottom showing through it ring within ring, the skaters swinging around the arcs like slow bright dust, and the whole design legible in a way it never is from the shore. He came down and drew what he had seen, carefully, in ink, arcs nested around a center, and then he sat looking at the drawing for a long time, and then he did the thing that makes him the warden he became: he pressed his own inky thumb to the page beside it, to be sure. The two marks sit there still, side by side, the pond and the print, alike as a word and its echo. He has shown the page to no one. He does not know what it means, exactly. He knows what it *suggests*, and he has decided — slowly, over decades, in the manner of all his deciding — that it is not his to explain. The giant story is close enough. Curiosity, not anger. The tellers were always careful about that, and now he understands they were right to be.

So he keeps his office. He mends the hut in summer and lets the mice have the wall. He knows the great pike in the cold central hollow — the one slow animal in all that quick water, gray as ash, holding still in the deep heart of the print like a sliver of the maker's patience left behind in the mark — and he has hooked her twice in fifty years and released her twice, warden to warden. He sweeps the snow in long ovals so the skaters keep to the true ice, following, though he never mentions it, the second ring. He rings the bell when the pond has decided and stands guard when it hasn't. And on the deep nights of the hard freezes, when the ice booms — that huge sourceless whale-note the whole valley hears, the sound of the frozen pond settling its structure, the closest thing the surface ever gets to the voice of what's below — the village children sit up in their beds, and the warden, lying awake in the hut with the stove ticking, only nods in the dark. He knows that sound now, after a lifetime. It is not a warning and it is not a complaint. It is the sound of a decision holding. Somewhere beneath everything, he is fairly sure, something patient made this place, and made a mistake making it, and the mistake filled with rain and then with swallows and pike and lantern-light and two hundred winters of circling skaters, and if that is what the mistakes of the patient come to, then the pond is in good hands twice over. He rings the bell. The village comes. Round and round on the black glass they go, above the fingerprint, quick and bright and brief and entirely safe — finished, in good company, above ground, in season.
