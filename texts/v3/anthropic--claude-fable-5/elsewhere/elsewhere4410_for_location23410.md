**The Underfall**

Begin with the sound. That is what the plateau cannot prepare you for, if you come down from it — and everything here has come down from it, one way or another. Two miles below the Altiplane the air has weight again, warmth again, wetness again, and sound stops dying young. A dropped stone here does not click and apologize. It rings, and the ring finds the great wall behind it and comes back changed, and comes back again, so that a single pebble can spend three or four seconds finishing what it has to say. Nothing in the Underfall is ever entirely quiet. The country hums at the bottom of its own voice the way the plateau glows at the bottom of its own dark.

The wall is the first fact and the last one. From below, the escarpment of the Altiplane does not look like the edge of a landform; it looks like the end of the sky's patience — two vertical miles of grey-gold stone running unbroken past both horizons, its top lost more often than not in the cloud-decks, its face streaked from crown to foot with long dark stains of running water. The wall weeps. It has always wept. All that condensation up on the height, the black mirror-pools, the paper-ice, the knee-deep silver haze — most of it sublimates back into the plateau's own weather, but a portion, a patient portion, seeps down into the frost-cracked seams of the pavement instead, and travels through the body of the shelf itself, in the dark, at the speed of stone, and emerges from the cliff face centuries later as spring after spring after spring. There are threads of water coming out of that wall right now that went into the ground as fog before the green copper year. The people of the Underfall drink history at every meal. The water tastes of iron — of blood and rain, they say, using the same words the highlanders use for the grubs, though neither people knows the other says it — because it has spent its centuries filtering through a country that is, from bedrock to surface, one continuous act of smelting. To drink from the wall is to drink the plateau's exhalation, delayed by a hundred lifetimes.

And the Underfall pays it back, breath for breath, because the Underfall is where the plateau's weather is made.

At the foot of the wall, beyond the debris country, lies the fetch: a warm shallow sea, or an enormous marsh, or something between the two — a sheet of tea-dark water rarely more than waist deep, spreading away from the escarpment for a hundred miles, floored with drowned slag and silvered with mist every morning of the world. The fetch breathes. On a rhythm of roughly a week — the same week, you will notice, if you have stood on the height and watched the rotating cloud-sheets — the fetch exhales: the mist thickens, gathers itself, stands up off the water in a bank a thousand feet tall, and walks into the wall. It climbs. It takes a day to climb, hauling itself up the seep-streaked stone, wrapping the wall's hanging gardens in white, and then it crests the rim and lies down on the plateau and becomes what the height calls weather: the descended cloud-deck, the day of beading condensation, the black pools a finger deep. The kilnbacks read its approach in the leaning of the haze. They do not know they are reading the breath of a sea they will never see, sent up a wall they will never reach the edge of. The two countries have been exchanging like this — water climbing, minerals falling — for ten million years, a trade so old and so unnegotiated that neither side has ever once noticed it is trading. It is simply what the wall is *for*: a membrane between two halves of one metabolism.

Because minerals do fall. That is the second fact of the Underfall, and the foundation of everything that lives in it.

---

The plateau builds nothing, but it sheds. Frost-shattered scree works its way to the rim and goes over. Slag cones, in their millions, ride the slow churning of the pavement — swallowed and excreted and swallowed again — and some fraction of every century's churn is delivered to the edge and delivered off it. The perfect hexagonal molts of the tile-scavengers, the shed plates, the grub-coins of nearly pure iron, the emptied shells of the dead — all of it, in a rain so sparse you might stand a week at the wall's foot and see nothing fall, and so unrelenting that ten million years of it have built a country.

They call it the tithe. The talus apron at the base of the wall is forty miles wide in places, a black-and-oxide landscape of glassy cones and shell-shards and fitted fragments, ringing faintly underfoot with every step, and it is the richest ground in the world. Every ounce of it has been refined at least once by a living gizzard two miles overhead. The Underfall does not mine, in the ordinary sense. It gleans. Its whole economy is the patient sorting of what the height did not mean to give.

And the fall has its congregation. At the known fall-lines — for the fall is not random; the rim's geometry funnels debris down the same chutes for millennia, and the chutes are mapped in song — live the catchers: broad, patient, crater-mouthed creatures the size of ponds, anchored in the talus with their gape turned upward, waiting years between meals for the sky to pay. A catcher can take a falling slag cone at terminal velocity without flinching; the impact is its dinner bell and half its digestion at once. Around each catcher, generations deep, live the smaller gleaner-guilds that pick its leavings, and around those, the singers.

For the Underfall's fauna is a fauna of sound, as inevitably as the height's is a fauna of heat. In air this rich, a voice is the cheapest organ a body can grow, and everything here has grown one. The knellers are the loveliest: crab-like, patient, each one taking up residence inside a fallen kilnback shell — the great empty domes lie scattered through the talus like a drowned herd — and each one striking its home from within, learning its dome's particular note over a lifetime of tuning, ringing it at dusk to say *occupied, occupied, alive*. On a still evening the talus country tolls like a city of bells, every bell the emptied body of a giant that spent four centuries in silence two miles up. The highlanders' dead, who never made a sound in life, have all their music posthumously, below, and neither country knows it.

There are echo-runners in the boulder mazes, blind as anything from the height but eared like instruments, who hunt by shouting and reading the shape of what comes back. There are chime-fish in the iron rivers that braid down from the wall's springs, schooling by pitch. There are the mireweavers of the fetch, who sing all night in ten thousand overlapping rounds, and whose chorus, heard from the talus, is indistinguishable from the sound of the sea itself thinking out loud. The plateau signals in degrees; the Underfall signals in intervals. Same letters, different alphabet, one long conversation that has never once been silent and has never once been heard above.

---

And there are the bellowbacks, and here the Underfall stops being merely downstream of the height and becomes its kin.

The story is dated, precisely, by the smiths, who read shells as well as any highlander: eight hundred years ago, in the fallen dome-fragments, the same thin verdigris line appears — the green copper year, written into the tithe as surely as into the living herds above. Whatever mineral event marked that year on the plateau, in the Underfall it was simpler and worse: a section of the rim let go. A quarter mile of pavement, and the edge of the herd that stood on it, came down the wall in an afternoon. The Underfall's songs say the sound of it lasted three days, which is the kind of thing songs say, and which the echoes off that wall make almost plausible.

Most of what fell arrived as gravel. But a kilnback shell is a very good vessel, and a very great fall through thickening air is survivable, barely, by the very largest, and one of the giants that came down that day — old, dying already, the songs insist, her furnace guttering, which is why she had drifted to the rim at all — came down whole enough. Her shell split. Her fire did not go out. It lay in the wreck of her, banked in the broken gizzard, cooling by degrees for a month at the bottom of the world. And stone eggs had come down in the same fall — a whole frost-seam's clutch of them, indistinguishable from the rubble, patient as the rubble — and they hatched, as hatchlings do, cold, and did the one urgent thing of their lives: crawled toward the warmth. The songs give the number as seven. Seven knuckle-sized cold things, working themselves into the red dark of a dead giant, receiving from a furnace already in the act of going out the last viable gobs of a flame ten million years lit.

Everything that has a voice in the Underfall descends its music from that month, the singers claim, which is nonsense, but forgivable nonsense, because everything that has that *fire* does descend from it. The bellowbacks are the seven's children: kilnbacks remade by abundance. Down here the air is thick enough to draft, the rock is pre-broken, the tithe is pre-refined, and the fire that above must be banked and hoarded through a thin cold eternity can, below, be *fed*. A bellowback is the size of an ox and moves like weather rather than like geology — a migration takes a season, not a generation. Its shell is brighter, its rings closer-spaced, its centuries fewer; it burns faster because it can. And it has done the one thing its ancestors never could: it has translated. The heat-language of the height, the affection-as-warmth-approached, the individual knowing of every neighbor's temperature — all of it has crossed, in eight hundred years, into sound. A bellowback's furnace roars, audibly, a deep bellows-drone that rises and falls with its feeding, and the herds converse in that register, drone answering drone across the talus, warmth spoken aloud at last. When two of them court they do it in months instead of decades, flank to flank, their two drones sliding toward each other by fractions of a tone until they beat, and then until they don't — until two fires hum at one pitch — and that is the whole act, the electrochemical marriage of the height performed as a duet.

Understand what this means, because the Underfall does, and has built a great deal of its soul on it: the fire in every bellowback on the talus is the same fire that glows through the seams of every shell on the plateau. It was never rekindled. It was carried down the wall in a dying body and handed on, mouth to mouth, exactly as it has always been handed. There are two circles of things it warms now, one above and one below, separated by two vertical miles of weeping stone, and the flame has never noticed the difference, because to the flame there is none.

---

The people of the Underfall build. That is their whole discipline, and it is as strict in its way as the height's abstention.

There are many of them — towns of them, strung along the iron rivers and the fetch's shore, and the towns are unmistakable at any distance because they are *tiled*: every roof, every floor, every path laid in perfect hexagons, the fallen molts of the tile-scavengers, gleaned from the talus over a hundred generations. A species two miles up that builds nothing and only fits has unknowingly roofed a civilization. The people know exactly where their tiles come from. It is the first thing children are taught, holding a molt up to the light: *someone wore this. Someone lifted, once in ten years, and crossed four feet of open ground in the dark, and this is what they left. Fit it well.*

They smelt — of course they smelt; they live at the foot of the world's refinery and its rejected grades rain gently on their country — and their kilns burn day and night along the rivers, and here is the second discipline, the one that answers the height's without knowing it for most of their history: every fire the people light is mortal. Struck from flint, fed by hand, and *put out* — deliberately, nightly, or at the end of the firing, with a ladle of wall-water and a word. No flame in a human hearth in the Underfall is permitted to burn unattended or unended. A fire that no one will sit with is drowned. They could not tell you, most of them, exactly why the law is the law; they will say that a fire kept alive past its keeper's attention *forgets whose it is*, and they will say it while, half a mile off, a bellowback drones on the talus, carrying attended in its living body the one flame that has never gone out and never had a keeper and never needed one. The two facts sit side by side in every town: ten thousand little deaths of fire, nightly, tenderly administered, in the shadow of the fire that does not die. The people do not own bellowbacks, do not pen them, do not take so much as an ember from them — nothing here is given the fire except what can carry it for centuries, and the people know their own span. But a smithy will be built, always, where a bellowback herd is known to winter, and the animals come to the warm outer walls the way highland kilnbacks accept a winding's first loop, and on cold nights the smiths sleep with their backs against the inside of a wall that has a giant's furnace humming against the outside of it, the mortal fires all drowned and dark within, the immortal one keeping them warm through the stone.

They are a singing people, inevitably. Law, lineage, the maps of the fall-lines, the depth-soundings of the fetch, the ledger of which family gleans which chute — all of it is kept in voice, in rounds and registers, because in this air a song carries better than a wall stands. And the greatest of their song-cycles, the one sung entire only at midwinter, is the cycle of the climbers.

For the naturalists came from here. There have been a few — a handful in eight centuries — who looked at the wall long enough that looking became a route, and went up it: months on the face, sleeping in the seep-gardens, drinking the centuries straight from the stone. Most came back with notes on the silence. The last one came back with the fact. She had wintered on the height, slept against a shell, learned the touch-language's vocabulary of degrees; she had walked the downwind edge of a great herd on a haze night and seen the long absence lying on the ground, and lived, and written *do not lie down beside the cold*, which the Underfall sings now as a lullaby without remembering it was a field note. And she had been shown the sacrament — the gob of living ceramic in the bone scoop, the heat of the undivided original on her face — and she had stood there, two miles above her own country, and understood what she was looking at, because she had grown up falling asleep to the drone of it.

She came down and told them. That is the whole climax of the midwinter cycle, and it is sung very plainly, because it needs nothing: *the fire came down before us. We have been living inside the circle it warms since before we were a people. The wall is not between us.*

---

Put your ear to the wall — the smiths do it, on quiet nights, exactly as the highlanders press an ear to a warm old shell — and past the ring of your own blood there is a rhythm in the stone. Very slow. Months to the beat. The great correspondence of the herds above, the structured waves crossing that continental circuit of ten million furnaces, does not stop at the rim; stone carries it, faint and patient, all the way down, and the Underfall has been sleeping against the outside of a thinking thing for its entire history. The bellowbacks feel it best. There are nights, a few in a decade, when every drone on the talus stops at once — herds falling silent across forty miles in the space of an hour — and the animals press their plastrons flat to the ground, and hold, and listen to whatever conclusion is moving through the deep rock, and then take it up again, all together, a shade lower than before. The people call these the answering nights. Whether anything above ever hears the answer, no one can say. The hourly sigh of the height goes down; the drone of the depth goes up; the wall stands between them carrying both, a membrane trembling with two musics that may, for all anyone knows, have been one call-and-response for eight hundred years, conducted too slowly and across too much stone for either singer to be sure the other is real.

And the herds above are leaving, remember — forever, very slowly, leaning away from the cold, arriving at conclusions the way a glacier arrives at the sea. Some of those conclusions end at the rim. The smiths know it; the tithe-songs keep the tally; every few generations the fall thickens along one chute or another as a deliberation begun before anyone living was born delivers its result over the edge. The Underfall reads the plateau's mind the only way it can — by what the mind lets go of — and stands ready at the fall-lines, and sings the chutes, and waits.

So the two countries keep their books against each other without a word passing between them. Water climbs the wall as breath; metal descends it as tithe; the fire went down once, in a dying body, and stayed. On a night when the fetch is exhaling — the mist standing up off the dark water, gathering, beginning its day-long climb — you can stand on the talus among the tolling shells of the height's dead and watch the cloud go up to become the plateau's weather, and hear behind you a bellowback's furnace rise in its drone as the damp air thickens, and know that two miles overhead, in a silence so complete it has to speak in degrees, an old kilnback is glowing a little brighter through its seams at the very same moment, for the very same reason: the same breath arriving, the same fire answering, the one flame keeping its one slow time on both sides of the stone — the height's half of it asking nothing and building nothing, the depth's half of it singing and roofed in borrowed hexagons — neither half alone, and neither one, ever again, the whole circle of things it warms.
