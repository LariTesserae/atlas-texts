The Rising

There is no stream to follow out of the hollow. That is the first thing to understand about how the two places are joined: on the surface of the world they do not touch. You could walk the low country for a season, tracing every runnel and dry valley, and never find a thread of water leaving that small warm bowl, because the thread does not travel where walking happens. It goes down. The bead that falls from the seep, the small clear note, the pool that neither rises nor sinks — what the warm air does not take back, the stone does, drawing it down through seams too fine to name, into the dark interior of the limestone, where it joins other water, older and younger, and moves through the body of the country the way sleep moves through a body: unseen, unhurried, and entirely in earnest.

A day and a half north, where the low country begins to gather itself into hills, the land breaks open, and the water comes back.

You hear it long before you see it. Coming down through the hanging woods of the valley there is first a whiteness in the air, a sound without direction, and then the sound acquires weight, and then teeth, and by the time you reach the valley floor it is no longer something you hear but something you stand inside. At the foot of a grey cliff — the same limestone, but here it is cracked and raw, with fresh faces and fallen blocks the size of carts lying where they landed — the river rises. It does not begin the way rivers are supposed to begin, gathering itself politely from trickles. It arrives whole. Out of a low dark arch at the base of the rock, half-drowned, the water comes up boiling — not hot, but boiling the way a pot boils, turning over itself, blue-green and muscular, already the width of a lane, already deep, already going. Within twenty paces of daylight it is a river with a name and opinions.

The cold is the second thing. The water carries the temperature of the deep hill, and the air above it carries the water's temperature, so that the valley floor is a country of its own, chilled and rinsed, ten degrees apart from the wooded slopes. Where the hollow's air sat at the temperature of skin and dissolved your edges, the air here finds every edge you have and presses on it. You are extremely certain, at the rising, where your body ends. Spray beads on your sleeves. Your breath shows. The cold is not hostile; it is simply the truth about the inside of the earth, delivered without softening.

Everything within reach of the water is alive, and lavishly. The moss here is not a single kept cushion but a flood of its own — emerald, sodden, half an arm deep, swallowing the fallen blocks, dripping from every overhang, so saturated that touching it is like touching the surface of the pool itself. Ferns grow from the cliff seams in extravagant tiers, great arched fronds, glossy, dripping, reaching as far as reaching goes — and if you have come from the hollow you will stop in front of them, because they are the same small leathery kind, unmistakably kin to those three or four arrested gestures in the warm bowl to the south. The difference is only that here nothing thought better of extending. Here the stone put them out and kept going. Midges hang in shafts of moving light. A wren shouts from the rubble. A small dark bird — you will see it if you wait — walks calmly into the torrent and under it, hunting the bed of the river on foot, and comes out again shaking, as if the water were a room with a door. Trout hold in the green tail of the pool below the arch, facing the dark, swaying, fed by whatever the hill sends out.

And the place is loud with time. That is what strikes you hardest, after the hollow. Nothing here has finished. The flood-wrack hangs in the lower branches of the alders, a tide-line of pale grass and broken sticks head-high above the summer level, marking where the river stood last winter and will stand again. There is fresh rubble at the cliff foot, broken this decade, this year; the raw faces have not yet learned grey. The light moves — actual sun, actual cloud-shadow, sliding down the valley wall and across the water, so that the river changes colour twelve times in an hour. The rising keeps every kind of clock at once: the day, the season, the storm, the slow calendar of falling stone. The hollow was a sentence long since ended. This is the middle of the sentence — the verb of it — spoken loudly, with the speaker's whole chest, and not yet anywhere near the point.

People are here still, and always have been. A worn path comes down through the woods, stepped in places with laid slabs — the same broad shallow cutting, the gait of people descending carefully — and crosses the young river on a clapper bridge of three great stones whose upper faces are polished hollow by feet. Initials are scratched on the bridge; some are fresh, some are ghosts. And people give to the water, as they gave to the shelf. They have never stopped. Look down from the bridge into the deep green stillness at the edge of the current and you will see them: coins, mostly, silting into the gravel, some bright, some green as the moss, a few of metals no longer minted, a few of shapes older than coinage — a bent pin, a white pebble, once, deep down, something that might be a small bronze figure lying on its back and looking up. The offerings at the hollow were placed, and kept, and their objects have vanished while the gesture stayed. The offerings here are thrown, and taken, and the river carries them into itself and does not keep them and the gesture stays anyway. Two hospitalities, one house.

The valley people have a name for the spring. They will say it if you ask, a short worn word with a soft opening, and if you ask what it means they will say it doesn't mean anything, it's just what the rising is called, it's always been called that. They are right that it doesn't mean anything anymore. It is the word that was said quietly at the rim of the hollow, before the descent, carried off downstream like everything else — the handle of a door that no longer stands where the handle remembers. The rising wears it the way it wears the coins: without knowing whose it was, without needing to.

Now, the hollow taught you a way of looking, so use it, and it will show you something strange: this place, so crowded with life, seems at its centre to hold none. The wren, the dipper, the trout, the midges, the drowning moss — all of it lives beside the water, off the water, in spite of the water. The water itself reads as mechanism. Volume, pressure, gradient. A pipe in the hill. You would say, standing on the clapper bridge in all that noise, that the rising is a machine wearing a garden.

Begin again, more slowly, which is difficult here, because nothing at the rising will slow down with you.

What lives here is the going. Not the water — water is only what it is currently made of, the way you are only currently made of what you last ate — but the going itself, the long single act of leaving, which entered the dark of the hill an age ago and has been arriving ever since. Its body is the current: you can see its muscles working in the boil at the arch, the standing wave that holds its shape while every particle of it is replaced a thousand times a breath — a body in exactly the sense the hollow's body was a body, a condition wearing matter. Its voice is the roar, and unlike the hollow it never stops speaking, because it is the part that did not finish. It has no eye. This is the thing to understand about it, standing above that blind black arch: it spent so long in the absolute dark of the stone that it gave up seeing as the hollow gave up reaching, and it knows the world now entirely by touch — by pressing on everything, tasting everything, running its whole self over the bed and the banks and the thrown coins and the legs of the wading bird, reading the valley the way a hand reads a face in the dark. It does not look up at the sky. It has a pool that does that, far to the south, on its behalf.

And it counts. Here is the answer to the tally marks. The scored lines in the hollow were not a stranger's failed arithmetic; they were a lesson half-learned from this place, carried up-country by the people who walked between the two, who knew — as the valley people no longer quite know — that the warm bowl and the cold rising were the same parish, the same presence, the near room and the far room of one house. The going counts everything, natively, helplessly: every flood is a stroke scored on the alders, every fallen block a mark, every season a line in the gravel bars, laid down, cut away, laid down again. The hollow keeps one thought and does not count it. The rising counts everything and keeps nothing. They divided the work between them long ago — because they were, and are, one being, and this is the shape its life took when the river that made the hollow found the lower way through the stone and had to choose between resting and going, and could not, and so did both. The stillness stayed in the bowl, warm, finished, holding the thought. The motion went down into the dark, cold, unfinished, saying it.

The thread between them has never broken. It is one drop wide. The bead that gathers in the groove of the seep, and falls, and makes the small clear note — that note is struck on this side too. The drop enters the pool, the pool gives to the stone, the stone gives to the dark river, and years later — no one can say how many; the hill keeps that figure to itself — the same water comes up boiling into daylight at the foot of the cliff, one warm filament lost in the cold gush, a thread of skin-temperature in the body of the flood, far too small to measure and not too small to be. The trout may know it. Cup a double handful from the edge of the boil and drink, and most of what you taste is the cold truth of the deep hill; but there is something else in it, at the very back, faint as the memory of a room — a mildness, a mineral warmth, the inside of a well where the air sits at the temperature of skin.

And at low water, on a still night in late summer, when the roar drops to a broad soft rushing and the valley people are asleep, stand on the clapper bridge and listen down into the dark arch. Under the going, inside it, at an interval that never changes, there is a small clear note. Then the long quiet. Then the note. It is not an echo and it is not your imagining. It is the pulse of the far room arriving in the near one, the letter delivered, the thought — kept all this time in the warm bowl to the south, entire and unhurried — being said at last into the moving world, one drop at a time, and carried off downstream to wherever finished things go when they are finally spoken.
