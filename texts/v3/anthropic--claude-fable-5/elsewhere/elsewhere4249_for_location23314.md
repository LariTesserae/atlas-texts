# The Set

Every breath the Seethe takes goes somewhere.

The vapor ceiling — that warm, copper-shot presence that dims the churn to permanent dusk — is not still. It leans. The roar has no direction and the light-regions wander, but the ceiling drifts one way, always, patiently, and the stayers' sign for that direction is the same as their sign for *out*. Walk out. Weeks of it, the shallows cooling degree by degree underfoot, and the Seethe begins — very gradually, and to its own evident confusion — to succeed. Forms hold a breath longer. Columns finish their twist before collapsing. The roar thins from thunder to surf to something merely weatherlike, and the water clears until you can see the old sediment, and then the finished rock where the sediment thins, and then one morning a form rises beside you, hesitates at the top of itself, and does not fold.

It whitens. Frost has entered the negotiation, and frost does not negotiate.

Beyond that morning is the Set.

## The Verge

First, the seam. Between the blood-warm convulsion and the dead cold there runs a band, a few dozen miles wide, in which water is only water. Waves rise for reasons and subside when the reasons stop. Rain falls downward, every time. Puddles reflect what is currently above them. It is the one ordinary place in the world, and it is inhabited accordingly — most of the world's people live here, in low stone-and-reed villages, between a horizon that churns and a horizon that climbs.

Somewhere in the band is the true line, and it is found by ear. South of it, your footstep sounds a half-beat early; north of it, a half-beat late; on the line itself, your step and its splash coincide, and cause and effect are briefly punctual. The villages are strung along it. Children play a game of straddling it, one ear in each world, and report that it feels like being forgiven for something.

The dark reeds grow here too — the same rubbery, deep-anchored species that huddles in the Seethe's lees — but at the Verge, in the ordinary water, they do the thing they never do in the south. They flower. Small, colorless, almost apologetic blooms, once a year. The Seethe stands are not a different plant; they are colonies of this one, exported into a place with no energy to spare for advertisement. The Verge people cut the flowers and do not sell them. Some things you keep where sound is on time.

North of the villages, the ice front stands out of the shallows — a grey-white cliff, miles long, groaning at a frequency you feel in the sternum. At intervals it calves, and slabs the size of buildings drop steaming into the warm water. The Verge people call the calving edge the Door. They named it long before anyone from the south told them about the frames. When they were told, they were not surprised, and were careful not to say so out loud.

Why they are careful about saying things out loud will become clear.

## The Set proper

Then the world does the thing it has forgotten how to do anywhere else. It rises.

The Set is a dome of ice the size of a subcontinent, and it is the only slope in existence — imperceptible at the Verge, unmistakable a hundred miles in, and at the interior a genuine height, wind-carved, blue-shadowed, climbing toward a sky that no one in the south has ever seen. Because the sky is *there*. The vapor that dims the Seethe to copper gloom snows out here, winter on winter, and over the interior the air is void-clear and utterly dry, and the light-regions resolve at last into what they are: two of them, small and white and hard-edged, circling one another. Everything the Seethe half-sees through its ceiling — the wandering brightness, the days when there seem to be two of it — is those two failing to agree, watched through a blindfold.

And here is the thing to hold in the mind about the height itself: it is made of breath. Every winter's accumulation is the Seethe's exhalation, delivered and kept. The old ranges were ground down to sediment and drowned; whatever stood was abolished; and at the far end of the world, out of warm vapor and patience, altitude is being manufactured again — snow on snow on snow for as long as the plain has been flat, until the tallest thing since the mountains is a mountain of the mountains' murderer's breathing. The Set is not a survival of the old world. It is a second attempt at one, made from the first one's remains at one remove.

## Sound

The Set is not silent. It is late.

Clap, and walk on; the clap follows at its leisure. Speak across a camp, and the words arrive after your mouth has closed, so that conversation at any distance becomes a slow exchange of the recent past. Whatever came loose from sequence in the Seethe — the splash before the step, the collapse heard before the rising finishes — is over-fastened here. Sound does not travel so much as it is *taken delivery of*: the falling snow accepts it, the accumulation compresses it, and the ice keeps it, in order, in strata.

This is not a manner of speaking. A crevasse opening in the mid-Set releases decades — a long exhalation of everything audible above that spot since the layers now exposed were surface: wind, wader-cries, footsteps, the faint carried roar of the far-off churn, a voice. The keepers' oldest vocation is listening at fractures with a slate and a stylus, taking down what the ice gives up, filing it by depth, which is to say by date. The files agree with one another to a degree that Verge people find morbid.

The deepest ice keeps the oldest sound. Once or twice in a keeper's life, a fracture reaches the bottom strata — layers from the accumulation's first winters, or from before them — and gives up the sound of the ranges being taken down. The grinding. The world's original noise. Every keeper who has heard it has written the same sentence in the record, independently, across centuries, and we will come back to that sentence.

Because sound is kept, speech is rationed. It works here — the first shock, for anyone up from the south, is that the mouth is suddenly a usable instrument — and it is therefore dangerous. Everything said aloud goes into the ice. Daily life is conducted in sign, the same sign as the Seethe's, and the two ends of the world each believe they taught it to the other, and argue the point in their respective silences. Speech is reserved: vows, names, verdicts, the dying. To speak at the Set is to publish. There are keepers who have said, in their whole lives, eleven words, and can tell you where each one is buried.

## What the ice keeps

At the Verge, the keeping is quick and literal: frost-forms, splashes standing, a wave kept mid-fall for a season until the sun-blurs take it back. Inland, the keeping goes strange with depth. The clear ice is legible like a document — pale glittering bands that are frozen schools of the little shape-eating swimmers, from ancient incursions that came too far north and were archived mid-appetite; thin compressed grey lines that were walked trails, a ladder of every road anyone ever took across a given valley, stacked; a stratum of reed-flowers from some catastrophic storm, colorless, apologetic, ten thousand years old.

And above the interior hangs the picture.

The cold air over the high Set stratifies and does not stir, and in it stands a mirage that does not flicker: mountains. Ranges — blue with distance, snowlit, serrated, an entire horizon of the thing the world no longer contains — standing above the ice where nothing stands. It has not changed in living memory. The keepers hold that it is a kept image, light archived by the cold the way sound is archived by the ice: the world's last picture of itself with height, hung where the world could reach the shelf. People come up from the Verge, once in a life, to look at it. They stand in rows. Nobody speaks, for the usual reason and for the other one.

The cold keeps breath, too, and this is the Verge's strangest trade. At the right temperature, in the right stillness, a spoken sentence hangs in its own frozen exhalation and can be — carefully, with cupped tools — taken down and stored. There are vaults cut into the lower Set, shelved to the ceiling with breath. Last words, mostly. Stayers bound for the churn sometimes make the whole month's journey north first, to leave a sentence in the cold before going south to be unmade in the warm, and the keepers do not charge them, and do not listen, and file the breath by name.

## The Arrived

The Held want to set. Almost all of them drift toward the stones and kneel and fail. But some — the coldest-tempered, the keepers say — turn out instead, and walk.

The journey undoes nearly all of them: warm water walking into cool water gives itself away step by step, and most of a pilgrim is donated to the shallows long before the Verge. The ones who cross the ordinary band arrive diminished, translucent, child-sized, and at the first hard frost they stop, and whiten through, and are finished. The rim of the Set, for miles along its southern edge, is a shore of them — figures mid-stride, mid-gesture, holding at last, forever, in ranks that thicken every year.

Whether this is success is the local schism. One conviction says: they set, as the stones set, only faster and cheaper — the elders by another road. The other conviction says: cheat. The stones hold from within; the Arrived are held from without; frost is not an achievement but a captor. The test was performed once — an Arrived carried south past the true line, to see whether it would stand revealed as stone. It melted. The melt ran back down the slope toward the churn, unhurried, like something excused from an appointment, and the Verge people do not perform the test anymore.

What is not disputed is the arms. Most of the Arrived are pointing — inward and upward, at the interior, at the place in the sky where the mirage stands. And here the keepers keep their great counter-conviction, the answer they would give the old stayers of the south if the two ends of the world ever spoke: the gestures were never pointing at whatever holds the doors open. Every gesture the Seethe makes — the horizon-crowd, the ten million attempts a second, the walls and trunks and kneeling figures — is a fragment of one gesture, and the Arrived are simply the fragments that traveled far enough to finish it. The whole warm world is pointing at the picture of the mountains. At what it is trying to remember how to be.

## Fauna

The grey waders breed here. One egg each, laid on blue ice on the lower Set, stood over with that exaggerated, weighted, confirmed care through a season of wind. And around the rookery stand the dead — because waders keep their dead nowhere; the dead keep themselves, frozen upright wherever they stopped, generations deep, an ash-grey standing archive through which the living walk slowly, pausing, comparing themselves plume by plume and angle by angle against grandmothers and great-grandmothers and birds with no living descendants at all.

This is what the conferences in the far south are. Rookery manners, exported. A wader meeting another in the warm world is doing what it learned among its standing dead: auditing the shape against the record. And when one of the two cedes — folding down into the churn like a sentence ending — the other, eventually, comes back here, and stands among the frozen for a long time, very still, filing the amendment.

The swimmers cannot cross the Verge alive. But they throng the Door. The calving edge delivers ten-thousand-year-old holding into warm water by the building-sized slab, and the swimmers meet it in glittering weather-fronts miles wide, grazing the arriving past back into possibility. The gel-mats off the ice front are the richest in the world. Whatever the Set keeps, it keeps only until delivery — which matters, because of what else it delivers.

## The keepers

They come up from the Verge for the reason people go down to the Seethe, reversed. The Seethe is good for grief the way a louder sound is good for a ringing ear; the Set is good for the other grief, the kind that is afraid of forgetting. There are perhaps as many keepers as stayers — dozens, spread across a subcontinent of ice — and they listen at fractures, and survey the deep figures, and tend the vaults, and keep the records, and staying has stages here too.

First your sound detaches — the arrears, the footstep that follows you like a polite companion — and this is survivable and even, the keepers say, restful, which is the same word the stayers use, and neither end of the world knows the other says it. Then you stop revising: a path walked once is walked exactly again, not by rule but by settling, the way water finds a channel. Then speech leaves you, word by kept word, as you feel the ice beneath you taking delivery of everything, and there is less and less you are willing to have on record. The oldest keepers are silent, punctual, and radiant with a kind of relief.

A keeper's body goes into the ice face down. Not despair — orientation. The snow buries them; the ice creeps; the interior flows outward, at the pace of centuries, toward the edge. Every keeper who has ever died is currently in transit — a procession thousands of years long, moving through the blue dark toward the Door — because the Set does not keep its people forever. It keeps them until it can deliver them: out through the calving face, steaming, into the warm shallows and the glittering fronts of swimmers, back into possibility. And in the churn just south of the Verge, forms rise sometimes wearing faces out of the oldest records — people no one living ever taught to the water, arriving at their own recurrence a hundred centuries behind schedule, the gesture, the watchers say, already improved.

The stayers walk into the water and are made at once. The keepers lie down in the ice and are made eventually. It is one funeral, in two tempos, and the Verge — where sound is punctual and the reeds flower — is the aisle.

## The Descending

Or the Climbing. The records use both, and apologize for neither.

Deep in the clear ice of the interior, a hundred feet down and more, there are figures. Not Arrived — the pilgrims stop at the rim, and are white; these are far inland, far down, and *clear*, distinguishable from the ice around them the way a current is distinguishable from a river. Human-ish in the count of limbs. Generous interpretation is required for the rest. Each generation of keepers surveys them, and each survey finds them in slightly different postures than the last — a hand higher, a head turned — motion at the rate of one gesture per lifetime.

They are climbing. Upward, against the accumulation, at almost exactly the rate the ice deepens, so that across three hundred years of records their depth has never changed. They are swimming in place against a current of centuries.

The convictions are three, as always. That they are the first keepers, being learned by the ice as the stayers are learned by the water, only at the ice's tempo. That they are the door-builders — the growers of the stone frames — who, when the ranges went down, split into two persuasions: the fast, which walked into the warm and is still being attempted ten million times a second; and the slow, which walked into the cold and is still arriving. And the third conviction, which the oldest keepers hold, and could defend aloud, having speech, and do not: that the Descending are not moving at all. That they are the only stationary things in the world — that ice, water, vapor, sound, the two circling lights, the procession of the dead, the whole apparatus at both ends and everything between it, is drift, and the figures in the ice are simply what holding still looks like when everything around it insists otherwise.

The keepers survey them anyway, every generation. It seems polite.

## The Unfrozen

Deepest of all — below the oldest strata, below the sound of the grinding — there is warmth.

A vertical shaft of melt, walls glassy, self-maintaining, descending through the whole thickness of the Set. At its bottom, standing on the finished rock that underlies everything everywhere, is a doorframe. The same tooth-smooth stone as the southern stumps. And the rectangle it holds is not ice, and has never been ice in all the winters of the accumulation: it churns. A framed portion of Seethe in the heart of the Set — warm, busy, packed with small forms rising and folding, shoulder to shoulder, mid-gesture — and around it the shaft it keeps open, and around that a hundred miles of the most finished cold in the world, unable to close it.

The keepers went down once, generations ago. The descent is conducted through archive — the shaft walls, melting, give up their strata continuously, so the whole climb down plays a hundred centuries of kept sound at once — and the records of what the deepest layers said are terse to the point of injury. They did not go back. They keep the shaft's location the way one keeps a word one has decided never to say.

---

Three items stand in the records, near the end, after which the records stop editorializing.

The first is the sentence about the grinding — the sound the deep fractures give up, the ranges going down, the world's original noise. Every keeper who has heard it has written the same thing: *it does not sound like destruction. It sounds like the Seethe.* The same roar, lower, slower — as if the mountains were not ground down but were always this, the one activity at rock's tempo; as if flat is not what the world was reduced to but what it seethed itself into, once, and set; as if the Set, packing breath into height one winter at a time, is not the world's memory at all, but its next attempt.

The second concerns the grooves in the far southern hollows — the circuits worn by the contrary thing that goes against, the one the old stayers call the fugitive altitude. The traders' charts come north with the kept-breath caravans, and the keepers plot them, because plotting is what keepers do. The circuits are not circles. They precess — by a hair each century, and always the same hair. Toward the Set. Whatever lay down in the rising water when the height was being taken is coming, at its own tempo, toward the only height there is: toward the body of breath, or toward the picture of itself hung above it. The keepers decline to write which.

The third is about the mirage. It does not flicker. It has not changed in living memory. But the surveys are old, and exact, and keepers do not misremember, because they can check. In the oldest records, the mountains stand a fraction further off than they stand today.

The Set keeps everything, in order, and delivers it in the end.

It is best to assume this includes the mountains.
