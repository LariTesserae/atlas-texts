**The Wall of Sarn-Aleth**

The water goes somewhere. That has to be said first, because everything else follows from it. A continent of warm rain that never quite stops, of brimming terraces and weeping stone and marshes fed from below — all of it drains, and drains downhill, and the Tablelands, a continent lifted whole, have what every lifted thing has: an edge. The seep-water gathers into trickles, the trickles into streams, the streams into broad black rivers you would hear on the plateau — you would only ever hear them — as a change in the ground-note, a deepening, a gathering intent. The rivers run warm and mineral-heavy through the eternal dark, and they run toward the rim.

The plateau's residents know the rim long before they reach it. Approach the edge of Anan-Dur and the world goes wrong in exactly one direction: the echoes stop coming back. Click outward, over the edge, and nothing answers — not the soft return of felt, not the hard return of stone, nothing — a permanent hole in the world the size of a horizon. And underneath the no-echo, rising from somewhere below the world, a floor of roar. The rim-country is thinly settled; the herds will not graze within earshot of it; the stitchers have a call for it that they teach their young and do not otherwise use, and the least bad translation of that call is *the loud silence*. It is the one landmark of the Tablelands that no resident has ever perceived and turned back from unchanged.

Then the water goes over. And if you follow it — and you can only follow it; there is no other road off the dark continent — this is what happens to you.

The ceiling spills. The sealed cloud of Anan-Dur does not end at the rim; it pours over it, kilometers of warm mineral vapor descending the face of the escarpment in slow motion, a waterfall of sky wrapped around waterfalls of water, and for the first thousand meters of the drop you are still, in every way that matters, inside the Tablelands — dark, blood-warm, deafened, falling through cloud alongside a falling river. Then the air changes. The cloud thins, tatters, warms differently. And at some altitude that is never the same twice — a wandering boundary the residents below call the *hem* — the falling cloud burns off into drier air, and the world does something it has not done once in the whole account of Anan-Dur.

It becomes visible.

---

**The wall.** Four thousand meters of it, running for — no one has gone to both ends — thousands of kilometers, the flank of the lifted continent, descending from the eternal dark to a shallow white sea. The upper reaches are the same rounded basalt as the plateau, the rock's old shoulders turned vertical; below that, older stone, the basement the continent was lifted on, layered and hard. And over all of it, the plaster. The falling rivers atomize on the way down; the warm spray drifts and settles and gives up its minerals against every surface it touches, and so the Wall is sheathed, ledge over ledge, in the same pale travertine that dresses the terraces above — flowstone curtains a hundred meters tall, aprons, buttresses, blunt white balconies growing outward year by year. The Wall is slowly becoming a staircase. It builds its own shelves, buries its own foot, extends itself out into the sea on fans of deposited stone, and this is the thing to hold onto about the whole country below: *it is made of Anan-Dur.* Every white terrace, every shelf, every beach of pale sand at the sea's edge was dissolved off the dark continent, carried down warm, and laid here in the light. The Tablelands export themselves, molecule by molecule, and Sarn-Aleth is where they are reassembled with the lid off.

**The light.** It is not simple daylight, and it is worth describing with the same care the dark deserved, because the residents here are built around it as tightly as the residents above are built around sound. The cloud of the plateau overhangs the Wall like an eave — a black ceiling stretching inland forever, visible from a hundred kilometers out to sea, and even here you do not see the Tablelands; you see their lid, and what leaks from under it. The eave shadows the upper Wall at all hours. Direct sun strikes the face only twice a day, low and sideways, morning and evening — the gold hours, when the whole four-thousand-meter face lights up like struck bronze — and the rest of the time the steadiest light on the Wall comes from *below*. The sea at the foot is warm, shallow, and saturated milk-white with the plateau's dissolved stone, and under an open sky it throws the light back up with such force that the residents simply call the sea the *Glare*. The Wall is lit from underneath. Shadows here point at the sky. The trees hang their leaves downward. And the animals are countershaded upside down — dark backs, bright mirrored bellies — this is documented, this is real here — because on the Wall of Sarn-Aleth, the direction of light and the direction of danger have both been inverted, and everything that lives on the face carries the inversion in its body.

The terraces of the Wall shine. The word works here. It has been waiting a long time.

**The flora.** Start at the hem, because the hem is where the world changes management. There grows the *hemwort* — a low, tough, spreading mat, gray above and green below, the one organism on this world confirmed to live in both regimes: in the falling cloud it feeds like the plateau's flora, on warmth and mineral chemistry, colorless and patient; below the hem it greens within days and turns to the light. A hemwort colony straddling the boundary is two metabolisms sharing one body, and as the hem wanders up and down the face with the weather, the mats switch over, kilometers of them, like a country changing languages at a moving border. Below the hemline, green — the first green anywhere in this telling — takes the Wall in force. The *downwood* grows out of the ledges and hangs: root-collars gripping the flowstone, trunks arching outward and over, whole canopies suspended beneath their own roots with every leaf's face turned down toward the Glare. Walking a shelf through mature downwood, the forest is under your feet as much as around you, a green ceiling for the terrace below. And in the warm spray-plumes beside the falls, black and green grow interleaved — because the felt lives here too. Torn from the swales above by storm-floods and carried over, fragments of the great meadows root in the warm wet travertine and persist: small, dark, silent colonies, clones of the oldest living thing on the plateau, planted on the Wall by falling. They are the plateau's flora in exile, sorted from the green by centimeters — a hand-span of extra warmth, a hand-span of extra light — and where the spray-warmth and the sun overlap, they grow tangled together, chemistry and photosynthesis sharing a ledge, and neither seems to have an opinion about it.

**The falls.** Dozens of the great cataracts, spaced down the coast of the sky like the engines are spaced across the plateau, each one a river of the dark continent coming out from under the eave — out of blackness, into daylight, silver at the gold hours, four thousand meters to the sea. Most free-fall, atomizing to warm rain long before the bottom. But the largest do not reach the rim as open water at all, and this is the first thing the Wall tells you about the builders: some of the rivers are *swallowed*. Intake galleries at the rim take them whole — the same corrosion-stoic alloy, the same travertine-skinned pressure-works as the engines above — and the water goes down inside the Wall, through penstocks the size of the vent-palms are tall, and is discharged at the foot, below sea level, in warm outfalls that keep whole reaches of the Glare at blood heat. The plunge-basins of the open falls, meanwhile, are the Wall's great tables. Everything the dark country loses comes down here: reed-rafts, felt-slabs, the shells of pool-wrights, and after the big storms the drowned hummock-bodies of swale-oxen — a tonne of upholstered meat delivered out of the sky — and a whole guild of heavy, patient, sharp-eyed waders called *tollkeepers* works the basins, taking the windfall as it surfaces, in a strict and ancient order of precedence that the smaller scavengers dispute at the edges and never successfully.

**The fauna.** The Wall is winged. That is its signature, the way the plateau's signature is the chorus: flight, real flight, in open lit air — the standing winds of the face, cool air sliding down off the eave and warm air climbing off the Glare, shear against each other along the whole length of the country, and the *shear-kites* live on that seam, whole lives on the wing, mating aloft, sleeping aloft in slow circles, coming to the stone only to nest. Below them the shelf-terraces carry their grazers, blunt placid things with their eyes set under the jaw — looking down, always down, where the light and the footing and the danger all come from — and their ears cupped upward toward the eave, so that their whole sensorium is split between the two regimes like the hemwort's body. And there is a predator, and it is the hush's mirror, and once you see the symmetry you cannot stop seeing it. The *glarefalcon* attacks from below. It climbs high over the white sea, folds, and comes up the last stretch out of the upwelling shine itself — out of the one direction no eye on the Wall can check, hidden inside light exactly as the hush is hidden inside silence — and the countermeasure, when the alarm ripples across a terrace, is the same move the plateau makes, translated: the grazers drop flat and turn their mirrored bellies outward, and vanish *into the glare* — becoming light among light, as the chorus becomes silence among silence — and the falcon, robbed of figure and ground, stalls, and climbs away. Two worlds, one grammar. Hide inside the predator's own medium. The Wall and the Tablelands worked it out separately, in the dark and in the light, and arrived at the same sentence.

**Night.** Because there is night here — the phrase means something again; relearn it — and night on the Wall is Anan-Dur's colony. Every evening the visual empire closes. The Glare goes out, the downwood folds, the grazers pack into sealed crevices, the kites climb above it all, and the acoustic regime comes down the face like a tide — because the Wall has its own stitchers. The *fallen*, the combers call them: a lineage descended from plateau stitchers swept over in the breathing storms, gliding and singing all the way down, generation after stranded generation, and now wholly of the Wall — eyeless still, click-swift still, owning everything their sighted neighbors cannot use: the insides of the falls, the fog of the spray-plumes, the hem, and the entire country between dusk and dawn. By night the Wall *sounds like the plateau* — click-trains stitching the hanging forests, glide-song arcing across the gulfs between shelves — and by day the fallen sleep in the roar-zones by the cataracts, where sight is useless anyway and the noise hides them from everything. They have never readopted eyes. They have had, evidently, no need. But they have the stars overhead, the first stars in this whole account, and this is the detail that will follow you home from Sarn-Aleth the way the fitters' hand-spans follow you home from Anan-Dur: the fallen click at the night sky and nothing comes back. To them the open heavens are a hush — the largest hole in the world, directly above, permanent, unwaitable — and their elders teach the young, call by call, what every infant on the Wall must learn one way or another: *do not sing upward. The up-dark does not answer. It has never once answered.* They live out their lives beneath the only sky in the world their kind can reach, and they keep their voices level, and they do not address it.

**The works.** The grid comes down the Wall. It was always going to; the only question was where. Behind the face, connecting the rim intakes to the foot, runs a single continuous gallery — the *Long Stair*, four thousand meters of switchbacked passage cut through the basement rock, its doorways opening at intervals directly onto the air, frame after frame after empty frame looking out over the sea from every altitude, as if the builders had wanted the view, or the ventilation, or something no surviving mind has a word for. The buried lines descend beside it. You can feel them at the foot the same way you feel them above — that faint carbonation in the soles of the feet, in the wet stone, in the shallows — and then the lines run out under the Glare, along the white seafloor, and do not surface. Fish crowd their routes the way the felt crowds them above; the warm outfalls of the swallowed rivers discharge along them; and from somewhere out under the milk, on still evenings, comes a ringing — pure, sustained, struck-bronze, minutes long — that anyone from the plateau would know instantly and that no one on the Wall has ever seen the source of, because the sea here is white as the sky above the Tablelands is black, opaque a meter down, one more sealed country. Whether the bell-bodied walked the lines down the Stair in some deep-past age, or the sea grew its own gauges, or the ringing is something else entirely, the fact is the same fact it always is on this world: the current goes somewhere, and something out there is sounding its condition into the dark, and the engines, four kilometers up and a continent away, are listening to a note struck under the sea.

**The combers.** Last, the Wall's own answer to the fitters, and the second half of a question the plateau asked first. They live in colonies along the fall-lines — long-armed, sure-footed, sharp-eyed, unhurried — and they are masons whose material rains from the sky and sets. A comber village is *grown*: drip-channels cut and re-cut in the flowstone above a ledge, guiding the mineral water where the colony wants walls, so that over generations the travertine itself builds their galleries, their cisterns, their vaulted rooms, each structure the slow collaborative work of the dead and the living and the falling river — built and grown at once, the plateau's great collapsed distinction reappearing here, in daylight, without having been taught. They work the plunge-basins alongside the tollkeepers by old arrangement. They take the windfall meat and the reed and the felt, which they cultivate on their warm ledges. And they take the artifacts. Ceramic insulators. Slivers of the corrosion-stoic alloy, worn by generations of identical grips into perfect fits for the hand — heirloom tools, washed out of the dark, falling out of the eave with the storm-floods — and here is the fact that will not resolve, the Wall's own, to set beside the fitters': *the fallen tools fit the combers' hands.* Grip to grip. And the doorways of the Long Stair fit their shoulders, and the stair-risers fit their stride, and so the question stands on the Wall exactly as it stands inside the engines — the same question, asked in the light this time, and no easier for the light. Convergence, or kinship. Life poured into an available shape, or a shape sized to this life by makers who were its ancestors, or its masters, or it. Two populations, one blind and inside the machines, one sighted and under the falls, each fitting the same builders' works exactly, neither aware — probably — that the other exists.

Probably. Because some combers climb. Above the hem, into the falling cloud, where their eyes fail within minutes — and they go anyway, the strong ones, once in a life, up the Stair or up the wet stone beside it, into the roar and the dark, and they come back with the only thing that can be carried out of Anan-Dur: sound. A returned climber will perform the plateau for the colony, sometimes for years — the chord of a herd, the terrace-orchestra, the beating of two engine-notes in an overlap zone, held in the chest and the throat with a fidelity their whole society polices fiercely — and so the combers possess, as their oldest and most jealously corrected inheritance, a recited soundscape of a country none of them has ever seen or will ever see. Their scripture is an eavesdropping. It is also, for what this is worth, accurate. A stitcher of the high plateau, hearing a comber elder perform, would recognize home.

**Weather.** Weather on the Wall has a direction: it comes from the dark. A breathing storm on the Tablelands arrives here in stages — first as sound, the eave itself groaning, the cataracts' pitch climbing; then as descent, the hem plunging down the face, the falling cloud swallowing the shelves one by one, the plateau's regime annexing the whole Wall for a day, the combers sealed in their grown vaults, the fallen abroad in the total dark like heirs; and then, half a day behind its own noise, as *water* — the flood-pulse, every fall doubled, warm rain hammering the downwood, the plunge-basins churning with the windfall of a continent. And sometimes, riding down inside the storm-cloud, come the veils. Driven off the plateau by the sagging ceiling, over the rim, down the face — and when the hem lifts afterward and the light comes back, one is occasionally left below it, adrift in open day over the shining sea: vast, colorless, trailing its meters of filament, blinking in no way at all, while the shear-kites circle it in escort. It hangs there in the light being *seen* — the first of its line in all the history of its kind to be seen — and it neither knows this, nor could know it, nor is diminished by not knowing, and eventually the warm air lifts it back under the eave and home into the dark, unwitnessed by itself forever.

---

**The rule of the place**, to set beside the rule of the plateau: nothing here is native except the arrangement. The stone of the Wall is fallen water. The meat in the basins is fallen herds. The tools in the combers' vaults are fallen heirlooms; the fallen stitchers are fallen song; the night itself is the dark country coming downstairs to reclaim its property until morning. Sarn-Aleth is Anan-Dur translated — out of sound into light, out of heat into color, everything carried over the rim and reassembled where it can finally be looked at — and the translation runs the other way too, because the Glare steams. Stand on a high shelf in the gold hour and watch it: the warm white sea breathing upward, the vapor climbing the lit face in slow rivers of its own, in under the black eave, gone — feeding the ceiling, the combers say; the dark is brewed at the foot of its own wall, and every drop of the rain that has fallen on the Tablelands since before anything on either side had a name came up this face first as the sea's breath. It may be poetry. It may be plumbing. On this world the two have never been far apart, and the engines, whatever they are for, are pumping *something*, somewhere, still.

So the Wall stands between, doing what it has always done: taking what falls and building country out of it, sending back what rises, one face of a single warm engine whose other face no eye will ever see — audible from the rim above as a roar without echoes, and visible from the sea below as a shining stair climbing into a held black breath, all the way up to where the water comes out of the dark already warm, already old, already on its way back.
