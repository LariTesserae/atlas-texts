## The Floor

It is under, of course. It has always been under. The Shelf speaks of it the way you might speak of the inside of a closed drawer, and this is fair, as far as it goes, because the Floor is roofed — permanently, totally — by the same cloud deck the Shelf stands above. But a drawer looks different from inside. The people of the Floor do not speak of "the highlands," or of *over*, or of *up*. They speak of the Lid. The Lid is the sky here: a ceiling of luminous grey four kilometers overhead, lit from beyond by a sun no one living has seen, brightening and dimming on a daily pulse the way a lamp breathes behind a curtain. The light that reaches the ground has been strained through the whole thickness of the cloud and then again through the canopy, and it arrives twice-softened, a dim green-grey that outsiders (there are no outsiders) would call gloom and that the Floor calls, simply, *day*.

It is raining. It is always raining somewhere on the Floor, and never everywhere. The Lid does not rain the way an open sky rains — it wrings itself out in fixed seams, columns of steady fall that stand in one place for decades, for centuries, long enough to be named, long enough to be landmarks, long enough to be *persons*. The Rain at Solvess has stood for four hundred years and has opinions about the ground beneath it, which it has carved into terraces. People are born under a rain the way people elsewhere are born under a sign, and one of the first questions the Floor asks a stranger from another district is *which rain?* Between the standing rains there is no dry — there is only drip, the endless afterdrip of a canopy that catches everything and lets it down slowly, at its own rates, forever.

And along the whole eastern edge of the world, running north and south beyond any map, there is the Wall.

The Wall is the Floor's one vertical fact. Four kilometers of wet dark stone rising straight out of the forest and into the Lid, its top invisible, its face veiled day and night in the churn where the warm rising breath of the Floor meets the endless thin water coming down. Because water does come down it — all of it, always. The Wall weeps along its entire length, a fall too diffuse to be called a waterfall and too constant to be called anything else, and where it lands it has built a strip of country unlike anywhere else on the Floor: the Weep, a band of drenched, mineral-rich, thundering ground kilometers wide, where the forest grows strangest and fattest, and where — of this, more below — the Floor's scholars go to read.

## What Grows Here, Which Is Everything

There are almost no animals. There were, perhaps, or there will be; the Floor's records are very long and very slow and do not concern themselves much with absence. What there is instead is green — green so total, so ancient, so structurally ambitious, that the distinction between forest and world stopped being useful long ago.

The trees of the Floor grew up under a dim lid on a warm wet plain with nothing to eat them and nothing to hurry them, and they responded the way the Shelf's stone responded to time: they finished arguing. East of the great slow rivers, the forest is one organism in every sense that matters — grafted crown to crown and root to root across thousands of kilometers, a single continuous wooden country with weather moving through it. It has strata, the way a Shelf tenement has strata: colossal grey primary trunks at the bottom, older than any record; then the mid-galleries, where most of the living happens; then the high crowns that brush the underside of the Lid's mist and comb water out of it. Nothing here is built. Everything here is grown, and everything grown is still growing, which means nothing on the Floor is new and nothing is finished, and the people find this pace perfectly natural, because it is the only pace there is.

In the dimness, the green does the jobs animals would do elsewhere. There are pitcher-growths that digest the rain's freight, walking buttress-roots that cross a clearing in a century, pollinations conducted entirely by drip and haze. The small fauna that does exist — haze-flies, rain-shrimp in the leaf-pools, one slow soft grazer of the high galleries called the sooth — is so rare, so remarked-upon, that an animal sighting is a thing you tell people about for a week. The animals are never eaten. This is not law. There is no record of it ever needing to be law.

At night — and the Floor has true night, a full deep dark when the Lid's glow goes out — the forest lights itself. Cold gold-green veins of glow run through the oldest wood, brightening with rain, and there is a profession, the lightwrights, whose whole craft is training the glow along paths and thresholds the way a gardener trains a vine. Night on the Floor is not dark. It is *inscribed* — every road a faint drawn line, every district a constellation lying on its back.

And the whole green mass, all of it, breathes upward. The forest's pollen, its spores, its winged seed-dust, the haze-flies themselves — everything light enough to rise, rises, on the warm updraft, up the face of the Wall and into the Lid. The Floor exhales a living haze into the ceiling every day of its existence, and every dawn, the birds come down to graze it.

## The Birds, and the Wall, and What Comes Down

The birds are the Floor's clock and its only visitors. At first-tide — dawn, when the Lid begins to glow — they pour out of the ceiling along the Wall in a dark descending river two hours long, spread out over the canopy in a body vast enough to be mistaken for weather, and feed, all day, on the forest's breath. At last-tide they gather and go back up the Wall face and are absorbed into the grey. They have never been observed to land. Not once, not anywhere, in the whole grown length of the record. The Floor does not really believe they are animals. The common understanding, held loosely, the way you hold an understanding about weather, is that the birds are the Lid's pulse given shape — and that they carry the forest's seed-breath upward *to plant the ceiling*, so that someday, at the pace such things happen, the grey above will bloom. Whether this is true is not a question the Floor finds urgent. It is very good at questions that are not urgent.

What the Floor does find urgent — as urgent as it finds anything — is the Weep.

Because the Wall's water is not rain. The Readers established this so long ago that the establishing is legend: the water coming down the Wall has *been somewhere*. It carries iron in a worked state. It carries smoke-salts, lime, oils, the specific chemistry of great fires kept burning for centuries. It carries fibers of a woven fabric no plant on the Floor produces, and fragments — small, worn, unmistakable — of spiral shells, and the faint constant signature of an organism that grows nowhere below: a film, mineral-eating, tinted a dim violet.

Something lives on top of the Wall. Something has lived there for a very long time. And the Floor has been reading its runoff — patiently, generation over generation, in the drenched gardens of the Weep — for at least eight centuries, assembling a portrait of a civilization it has never seen out of what that civilization sheds. The portrait is precise in strange places and blank in others. They know the people of the Lid burn metal and keep snails. They know they dye cloth. They know, from the chemistry of certain weeping seams, that they lay their dead into stone and let something violet take them, which the Floor finds beautiful, and says so, in the quiet way it says things. And they know — this is the finding the Readers return to most often, the one grown deepest into the record — that almost nothing comes down whole. A civilization's runoff should be full of its discards. This one has almost none. Whole objects arrive perhaps once in a decade: a worn crab-chitin button, once a length of grey lace, once, famously, a small spiral shell with a fragment of string still through it, which is kept in the record-grove at Solvess and visited.

From this absence, the Floor has drawn its one firm conclusion about its upstairs neighbor, and the conclusion has hardened into something like affection: *they throw nothing away. They mend everything. We know them by how little of them reaches us.*

Nothing that comes down the Weep is refused. This is the Floor's oldest rule — accepted, used, or planted, but never refused — and it is less a law than a courtesy, extended to a benefactor who does not know it is one. Because the Floor understands, without ceremony, what the Weep-gardens mean: the richest ground in the world is fed by the Wall, and the Wall is fed by whoever lives above, and whoever lives above drinks the cloud, and the cloud is the Floor's own breath, condensed. The loop closed long ago. The seam no longer shows. That neither end of the loop has ever seen the other is, the Floor would tell you, a detail.

## The People

They are tall the way the trees are tall — drawn upward, attenuated, quiet in the joints. The Floor does not select for chests; sound here is a resource so scarce it is practically currency, and their lungs are ordinary. It selects for eyes. The eyes of the Floor-born are enormous and dark-adapted, built for the twice-softened light, and the very old, whose eyes have opened further year by year like everything else here, are said to be able to read scar-script by glowlight from across a gallery. Their skin has gone the way of skin in permanent warm wet: fine, faintly luminous-pale with a green cast borrowed from the day itself, as if the light had a complexion and they had grown into it. Their feet are silent. This is not stealth. There is simply nothing on the Floor that has ever needed to be loud, and the forest swallows sound so completely — leaf-muffled, moss-floored, the only constant voice the far white roar of the Weep along the eastern edge — that a raised voice carries for kilometers and means one thing only.

They whisper. All of public life on the Floor is conducted at a murmur, close, face to face, and outsiders (if there were outsiders) would mistake the whole civilization for a conspiracy. For distance they use the wood: the grafted root-mass of the eastern forest carries a knock the way wire carries current, and there is a full public language of it — announcements, summons, the day's news — struck against the buttress-roots and heard, felt really, in the soles of the feet, districts away. Knock is formal. Whisper is daily. And the voice, the full open human voice, is kept for exactly one thing.

Grief. A death on the Floor is announced by a shout — one, from the family's ground, as loud as the body can make it — and it is the only shout most people hear from one year to the next, and everyone who hears it stops, everywhere, until it fades. The Shelf, four kilometers up, shouts everything and keeps its tenderness in the palm of the hand. The Floor whispers everything and keeps its grief in the open throat. Neither knows the other's arrangement. A Reader once wrote, in the slow grain of the record, that the two customs weigh the same.

## What They Do

They grow. It is not too much to say that growing is the Floor's religion, art form, courtship ritual, and politics — the way repair is all of those things above, though neither world knows it has a mirror.

Nothing on the Floor is made. Making — cutting, assembling, forcing a shape into being faster than it can grow — is the local obscenity, spoken of the way the Shelf speaks of building dry. Everything is trained, grafted, bent, and waited for. A door takes forty years, and doors are therefore begun for grandchildren, and a household's standing is read in how many things it has growing that it will never live to use. A young person declares interest by grafting — a cutting from their name-tree set quietly, well, and without comment into the other's. The reply is a counter-graft. If the grafts take, so, generally, does the rest, and the neighbors, who have been watching the bark the whole time, stop pretending otherwise. Long marriages are described in a fixed phrase: *their trees have grown together.* It is not a metaphor. You can go and look.

Every child is planted a tree at birth, and the tree is the record: your age is its rings, your name accrues along its grain — a syllable grafted on at each stage of life, so that the old carry long names spoken entire only once, at the end. The dead go to the root. The body is laid into the ground at the base of the name-tree and the tree does what trees do, at the pace trees do it, and a generation on, the family takes one seed from that tree's crop — one — and strings it by the door. Each seed is a grandmother. Four kilometers up, on the weeping mortuary walls, the same arithmetic is being run in shells. No one finds either grim. The wood is patient, the Floor will tell you, in a whisper, with a shrug you can barely see, and so are we.

The elders are the luxury good here too, though the Floor uses them differently: they are not consulted about the last time the wall did that, because the Floor's walls are alive and do everything for the first time very slowly. They are consulted about *whether to do anything at all*, and every serious judgment on the Floor begins, as all serious judgments here begin, with *Not yet.*

## The Readers

They are found young, around twelve, by a cup of water. It is Weep-water, and most children, given it, taste water. A Reader-child tastes iron, smoke, bird, shell, salt-of-lime, and — this is the confirming note, the one the examiners wait for — *something violet*. Such a child is offered the grey-green of the order and almost always takes it, and spends the next twenty years in the drenched gardens under the Wall learning the only curriculum: the exact taste of everything the Lid-people have ever shed, held in the mouth's memory, refreshed daily, forever, so that the smallest change will snag.

The record they keep is grown, not written — scar-script laid into the grain of the living record-trees at a hand's pace of a few lines a season, in groves whose oldest boles have carried the archive so long that the earliest entries have been swallowed rings-deep into the heartwood and can be consulted now only by the very oldest trees' very oldest keepers, who lay their palms against the bark and are believed, loosely, to be told. Mostly the entries read: *No change. Rain steady. Birds on time.*

They have been writing that sentence for eight hundred years. Four kilometers above them, in logbooks going soft and brown in towers they will never see, a line of watchers in violet has been writing the same sentence for longer. Neither archive knows the other exists. The sentence is, as far as anyone in either world has ever determined, true.

Mostly.

Scar-script cannot be struck out — the wood keeps everything, that is the point of wood — and so it is a matter of permanent grain that the Readers' record contains, century over century, one continuous finding that is not *no change*: the violet is thinning. The signature of the film in the Weep-water, steady for the length of the early record, has been fading, slowly, evenly, everywhere along the Wall at once, as if the violet were withdrawing from the rim above — going somewhere, at the pace of a continent thinking something over. And there is a second finding, which the Readers did not make, because everyone on the Floor has always known it: the forest is walking east. The whole grafted mass of it, root by root, a few meters a century, toward the Wall — pressing its oldest growth against the stone the way you press a hand against a door you are not ready to open. The green coming toward the Wall from below; the violet drawing away from it above. What is only ever written in living wood, never knocked, never whispered, is the reading some Readers hold: that these are not two facts. That something crossed here once, at geological speed, going up; that the violet is the part of it that finished the climb an age ago and is still traveling; that the green is the part still coming; and that both peoples, the menders above and the growers below, have built their entire long, patient, gorgeous civilizations inside the stride of a single step.

The record does not endorse this. The record says: *No change. Rain steady. Birds on time.* The wood keeps everything, and it keeps this too.

## The Stair

At the base of the Wall, north of Solvess, under a standing rain so old it has no name because it predates naming, there is a stair.

It is grown, of course — the trained buttress-roots of wall-figs, coaxed up the wet stone tread by tread by a lineage of tenders who have held the work for six centuries and have reached, in that time, a little over two hundred meters. The Wall is four thousand. The tenders know the arithmetic. It is grown into their own record-tree without comment, alongside the entry, and the entry does not change: the Stair rises at the pace the fig consents to, and the fig consents at the pace of the Floor, and there is no other pace, and that is that. Asked why they climb, the tenders say only: *it is being climbed.* Up on the rim, the fence-menders who work nearest the edge will tell you with a particular firmness that no one has ever come up out of the cloud, and will not be drawn further. Neither answer is an answer. They may be the same one.

The oldest legible ring of the Stair's record — heartwood now, palm-read only — is said to hold two words. *Begun again.* No one on the Floor will say what the ring before it holds, because the ring before it is inside the tree, where the wood keeps what it keeps.

## One Last Thing

In the high emergent crowns nearest the Wall, where the canopy brushes the underside of the Lid and the dawn river of birds passes closest, there are the thread-crofts.

A crofter's work is this: to cultivate, at the very top of the world, a particular vine — the greenest thing on the Floor, which is to say the greenest thing anywhere — and to spin its fiber fine, and to leave the threads streaming from the crown-tips where the birds pour past, every dawn, every dusk, by the million. Almost every thread is lost. Rain takes them, wind takes them, the years take them. But once in a great while — once a generation, perhaps — a thread goes up snagged in that dark river and does not come back down, and the crofters mark the day, and grow it into the record, and that is all. They do not know if the threads arrive anywhere. They do not know if there is anyone to receive them. They have been sending them for as long as the crofts have existed, on the strength of iron and smoke and one spiral shell on a string, saying the only thing a thread can say: *we are here. We are still here.* Four kilometers up, on cornices they cannot imagine, in a city that files each green arrival in a flat unhurried hand and never takes the thread, the messages are all kept, and none has ever been read as a message, and both of these things are somehow true at once.

And once in a great while — once a generation, perhaps — the Weep gives back a thread of its own. A single fiber, come down the whole height of the Wall in the falling water, of a color that grows nowhere on the Floor and dyes into nothing the Floor possesses: a dim violet, the violet of the fading signature, the violet — though no one below can know this — of a watcher's sleeve, frayed at a tower window during forty years of *no change*, carried to the rim by wind and over it by rain. It was never sent. The Floor does not know that. The Floor takes it as reply, because nothing that comes down the Weep is refused, and lays it in the record-grove beside the shell, and goes on — whispering, grafting, tasting the water, growing doors for grandchildren, while somewhere above the grey ceiling something patient keeps everything in repair and does not know it is being written to.

The people of the Floor are content not to be answered yet. They are very good at *yet*. It is, of everything they grow, the thing they grow best — and it may be, though neither world will know for an age, the one crop they and their neighbors have always raised in common.
