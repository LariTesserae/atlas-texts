**The Braid**

Everything on the Green Table stays. That is the plateau's whole economy — the dew swallowed back before noon, the dead grown over within days, the drummers' rhythms handed down unbroken since before the lowland rivers found their courses. Nothing is wasted because nothing is released.

The cloud-sea it stands in is not so careful.

The white clouds that lap at the rim do not only rise; they also rain, day and night, on the flanks below the edge — on the banded grey-and-rose rock of the table's pedestal, the oldest exposed stone anywhere, which has no cushion to defend it. Down there in the perpetual wet, water works the oldest stone in the world the way water works everything, without malice and without pause, and comes off the mountain carrying the mountain: grain by grain, band by band, a fine flour of grey and rose suspended in every stream. The table ate the mountain's top. The rain is eating its base. The plateau hangs in the upper air on a pedestal that is leaving, at a pace measured in the same geology the cushion thinks in, and everything the pedestal loses goes one direction.

Follow it down. Below the cloud the water gathers into torrents, the torrents cut gorges through the banding, the gorges open onto slopes of gravel, the gravel gives way to sand, and then — a long way out, where the land has forgotten it was ever steep — the rivers arrive somewhere flat enough to hesitate. They slow. They spread. They set down what they are carrying.

This is the Braid.

It is a country of rivers that will not choose. There is no main channel and never has been: the water comes off the mountain in a dozen threads and splits, rejoins, splits again, until from any small height the whole land to the horizon is a plait of brown water and low green ground, over and under, no strand keeping its place for long. Land here is a provisional arrangement between floods. An island that has stood through ten high waters is spoken of respectfully; one that has stood through fifty has a name; nothing in the Braid has stood through a thousand, and the people find the Table's kind of age — age past counting, age settled into a final shape — as hard to imagine as the table-visitors find the idea of ground that moves.

And yet the two countries are the same country, and the Braid knows it, because the Braid is made of nothing else. There is no native stone here. None. Dig anywhere — for a well, a grave, a house-post — and it is silt all the way down, soft and fine-grained, and it comes up *banded*: grey layers and rose layers in patient alternation, because the mountain's flanks shed their bands in order and the floods laid them down again in order, so that the depths of the Braid repeat, in soft translation, the striping of the rim a world above. The lowlanders do not say they live below the mountain. They say they live *on* it. Every field in the Braid is the top of the world, come down. After a flood recedes and the new silt dries, the whole country blushes faintly rose for a season, and the people plow the blush under, and the mountain feeds them.

No one in the Braid has ever seen the Green Table. From below there is only the pedestal, far off, banded grey and rose, going up into its permanent collar of cloud and not coming out again. What the Braid knows of the top of the world it knows the way you know a neighbor through a shared wall: by what comes through.

What comes through, mostly, is weather. The Braid gets all of it — every storm the table serenely sits above breaks down here instead, and breaks hard. The rains that the plateau receives as a few fat warm drops arrive in the lowlands as standing walls of water; the towers of cloud that cast brief shadows on the green country spend whole days emptying themselves onto the plait. The Braid is loud with weather the way the table is loud with life. But its own sound, the one underneath, the one the country makes when the sky is quiet, is stranger and smaller and harder to forgive once you have understood it.

It is a hiss.

The pilots know the trick: lay the flat of a paddle on the water and put your ear to the handle, and the channel speaks — a dry, continuous, sliding whisper, the sound of silt traveling along the bed. Every channel makes it, all the time, day and night, the load of ground mountain moving seaward under the boats. The table's sound is the sound of millions of small lives striking the walls of their home, an archive performed without end. The Braid's sound is the sound of the country leaving. The pilots grow up with it and stop hearing it, mostly. The old ones say you hear it again near the end of your life, and that it stops being sad.

Once a year, the plait becomes one water. The great rains fall on the flanks — the listeners in their pits a world above have already heard them coming, days early, in the ragged drumming, and have already come down the ladders carrying the news — and every thread of the Braid rises together, over the low ground, over the fields, joining across the whole face of the country until there are no channels and no islands, only a single sheet of moving grey-rose water from horizon to horizon with the villages standing up out of it on their mounds like knuckles. The flood is the Braid's pulse: not nightly, like the table's, but annual, one slow enormous beat a year, and the people wait for it, and repair by it, and count their ages in it.

**Who Lives in the Braid**

Begin, again, with the answer too large to miss: the river.

Whether the Braid's water is one river pretending to be many, or many rivers refusing to be one, is a question the lowlanders have never needed to settle — the channels split and rejoin so freely, trade their water back and forth so constantly, that ownership never comes up. It is the cushion's question inverted and answered the same way: nothing has ended; it does not matter where one begins. But where the cushion is the oldest living thing anyone knows of, and behaves like it, the river is the youngest large thing in the world and behaves like *that*. It maintains nothing. It revises everything. It will move a channel overnight, in flood, a full half-day's pole from where it ran before, and leave the old bed as a curving lake that the reeds take within a year. Maps of the Braid are a genre of joke. What the pilots use instead is taste: the grey bands of the pedestal and the rose bands weather into different waters, and every channel carries the flavor of the flanks it drains, so a pilot in the dark, in fog, in flood, dips two fingers and knows — grey water, rose water, mixed and in what proportion — where in the plait they are riding. It is navigation by drinking the mountain. Children are tested on it. The great pilots are said to taste last year's rains.

---

**The siltfish** are the Braid's answer to the drummers, though nobody planned the rhyme: they are the life inside the substance of the place, the animals for whom the country's body is not ground but medium.

They are long, blunt, small-eyed, muscular things, and they do not exactly swim in the water — they swim in the *ground*, through saturated silt, through the soft beds and banks and the flooded fields, moving below the surface of what a visitor would insist on calling land. The ground of the Braid swims; that is simply a fact of the country, and the people have made it a tool. A farmer opening a field for planting does not plow it. She cuts a notch in the field's low bank, floods it a hand deep, and pours in a basket of penned siltfish, and for three days the field churns and dimples and boils softly as the fish work through it, turning the new rose silt into the old, dunging as they go, and then she drops the water and gathers them at the notch, fat and stupid and easy, and the field is made. Half of them go back in the pen. Half go in the smokehouse. Plow and harvest are the same animal here. The table's warmth is a joint production of plant and insect; the Braid's fields are a joint production of flood and fish, a metabolism with two kinds of body in it, and neither country's people find the other's arrangement strange when it is explained. *Of course* the place and its animals run one household. What else would they do.

---

**The birds** the Braid shares with the table, though it took the listeners to prove it, because the two countries knew them as two different birds.

The grey-and-rose chisel-bills that pour off the rim in flocks with their own weather are hatched up there, in the bored tunnels, in the cushion's blood-warmth — but they fledge *downward*. A young bird's first true flight is a fall: off the rim, through the cloud-sea, down the whole height of the world, and it spends its first years in the Braid, a lowland bird among the channels, hammering snails and siltfish fry out of the banks, colored like the water it works over. The lowlanders called them silt-jacks and thought of them as their own. Then a listener, generations back, noticed a banded feather in a pit at the rim and its double in a Braid smokehouse, and worked it out, and now everyone knows what the young birds are waiting for when they gather, in their third or fourth year, on the highest mounds before the storm season: they are waiting to climb. A silt-jack rides weather up. It waits for a storm tower building against the pedestal, enters the underside of it, and goes up inside the updraft, through cloud, through hail, through the whole violent machinery of the sky the table never feels, and comes out above the white sea a rim-bird, and does not come down again except straight down, and never as itself. The Braid is the bird's childhood. The Table is its adulthood. Lowland children watch the storm towers in season and say the same thing their grandmothers said: *somebody's leaving today.*

---

**The bones** are the Braid's deep possession, and the thing the two countries argue about most fondly.

The table erases. The Braid archives — that is the whole difference between a body that heals and a country made of sediment. What falls into the silt stays in the silt, in order, forever, and so the Braid's depths hold what the world above has long since grown over and forgotten. Well-diggers find them; mound-cutters find them; the river, revising, sometimes lays them bare in a fresh-cut bank for anyone poling past to see: bones. Great ones. Long, limbless, close-ribbed skeletons, some of them the length of a large boat, with blind heavy skulls, blunt as fists, built for pushing through something soft. Some of the ribs show healed breaks — old injuries, survived — the same palimpsest of scars the table wears on its skin.

The listeners came down the ladders in numbers the first time word of a whole skull reached the rim. There is a famous one now, dark as tea, kept in the great rope-house at Nine Knuckles, and what the listeners did when they saw it is what listeners do: they tapped it, and put their ears to it, and tapped it again, mapping its resonance the way they map the plateau's, and then they sat back and were quiet in the way that, among listeners, means the argument has gotten worse rather than better. Because the skull rings *low*. Below its size. Below reason. Down toward the pressure-tone, the one felt rather than heard, that the drummers fall silent to attend when the hush passes under the green.

So the old dispute has a lowland chapter now. The school that holds the hush is the last of an ancient fauna points down: here is the fauna, they say, in the ground, in its thousands, laid away in the mountain's own pages — the table has one left and the Braid has the graveyard. The school that holds the hush is the cushion's own wandering organ points down too, and says: then why does nothing like it live in the Braid now, in all this soft swimmable country made for exactly such a body? — and the first school answers, quietly, with the thing the flood-pilots report and do not much like reporting. That in the great flood, when the plait is one water, there are patches where the silt-hiss stops. Moving patches. A silence the size of a field, gliding under the brown sheet at a walking pace — *upstream*. Toward the gorges. Toward the pedestal. As if once a year, in the one season the water is deep enough everywhere, something old tries to go home, and is lost to hearing where the current steepens, and either turns back or doesn't. No furrow is ever found. Nobody has proposed a way to settle it. The Braid-folk, pressed, make the tapped sign they learned from the listeners — *both* — and then the one that means *enough*, and pour the tea.

---

**The people** of the Braid live on their dead, and mean it as plainly as the table-visitors mean that they walk on the living.

A village here is a mound, and a mound is time made solid: every floor packed down and built over, every hearth buried under the next hearth, every generation's debris and sweepings and fallen walls compacted into the platform the current generation stands on, so that a village of any age rises out of the flood-sheet on the accumulated substance of everyone who ever lived there. The table forbids permanence by appetite; the Braid forbids it by water; and the mounds are the lowland answer — you cannot keep anything, so you stand on it instead. Dig down through a mound and you pass your family in order, like the silt, like the banding, grey and rose.

They farm the blush, and pen the siltfish, and read the water with their tongues, and they grow, in the swiftest channels, staked out where the current runs hardest, the tough-stemmed flood-flax whose fiber is worth more the more it has struggled — because fiber that has fought moving water all its life is fiber that can hang a person off the edge of the world. Every braided ladder on the rim of the Green Table began as a flooded field in the Braid. The rope-houses plait it, the climbing families carry it up in coils on their backs, and the strand-by-strand replacement that keeps the ladders weeks old in material and generations old in design is the Braid's steadiest export and quietest pride. The country named for a braid of rivers sends braids back up the mountain. Nobody in the rope-houses thinks this is a coincidence. Nothing here is a coincidence; it is all one water.

What comes down in exchange is the forecast — a listener descending with a season's rain in their head is worth a harvest, and is paid like it — and, less countably, the manners of the pits. The tapped finger-speech the listeners use where voices feel wrong has soaked into the Braid the way everything from above soaks in eventually. Spoken language is for daily matters here. For the heavy ones — a death told to a family, a warning of bad water, certain kinds of love — a Braid-dweller will fall silent and tap it on the table, on a doorpost, on the back of your hand, and it is understood that what is tapped is meant more, not less. The table-timed elders, the ones with the slow pulses, stand in the doorways of the mounds exactly as the old account says, easy to be around and hard to surprise, and the tops of the oldest mounds are crowned with small hoards of rim-quartz — the only stone in the whole country, one pebble per completed climb, carried down through the cloud in a lifetime of pockets. A big hoard is a village that has sent many people up. The pebbles are never traded and never counted aloud.

Their dead they give to the current. It is not thought of as loss. Everything in the Braid is on its way somewhere — the silt, the water, the young birds, the mountain itself, grain by grain — and a person is not exempted from the country's grammar just by dying; the body goes into the channel at dusk, and the flavor of the water is tasted downstream by pilots who dip two fingers and say nothing, and the river takes its passenger seaward along with everything else it is carrying, which is to say, along with the top of the world.

Except the listeners. The great ones. For them the Braid observes the one exception it has ever made to its own direction of travel, and it observes it going *up* — the last climb, the body carried on the ladders through the cloud-sea by the climbing families, the laying-out on the open green at dusk, the descent without them. The Braid-folk who make that climb come back changed, and what they say about the top is always the same two things: that the loudest place in the world feels like the emptiest, and that when the drumming around the laying-place went into the slow trailing rhythm, they felt it in the ladder-fiber all the way down — their own flax, their own flooded fields, carrying the mountain's grief through their hands.

So the accounting stands like this, and the Braid teaches it to children as a bedtime shape rather than a sorrow: everything comes down. Water, silt, storms, birds, weather-news, the mountain itself. And three things only go back up. The water goes up, as mist, climbing the pedestal to rejoin the white sea and fall on the flanks and start again. The birds go up, inside the storms, to become their parents. And the honored dead go up, on braids of channel-flax, to be roofed over by the oldest living thing in the world and visited, some later season, by the hush on its rounds.

As for the long arithmetic — the people of the Braid know it and are not troubled. The pedestal is thinning. The rivers are patient. Some unimaginable day the Green Table will have nothing left to stand on, and the oldest living thing will come down at last to the country that has been made, all along, out of everything it was standing on — and the Braid-folk, asked about this, shrug and say the proverb, which loses something in any language but the tapped one:

*The mountain is not gone. It is early.*

And the flood-sheet spreads, and the mounds stand up out of it, and somewhere under the brown water a field-sized silence swims upstream toward the gorges, going home or trying, while a world above, over the cloud, the green country counts its one slow beat in the dark — and between them the ladders hang, weeks old and generations old, creaking gently, holding the two halves of the mountain together by braided grass and the habit of climbing.
