**The Lake**

The water in the city's walls has an address.

Follow it backward — out through the pores of the thickening rind, out of the stone and into the cold black sediment, against the grain of the plain — and the plain, it turns out, is not level. It leans, very slightly, the way a floor leans in an old house, and everything dissolved in its groundwater has been coming from the high side for longer than there have been cities to receive it. The distance is one the concretion has no way to hold: call it nine days of larval drift, or forty lifetimes of white isopod laid walking end to end. Call it upstream.

Partway there, you find the river.

A river at the bottom of the sea: water flowing through water, and keeping to its channel, because the water of the river is heavier than the water of the world — dense with salt, dense with dissolved stone, brown-black, faintly oiled, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been asked to stop. It has banks. The banks are frosted white with mats of bacteria that live on what the river breathes, and the frost lies thickest at the waterline, like snow along a road. Fish hang above the channel the way birds hang above a road, following it for miles without ever touching it. Nothing lives in the river. Things that fall into it stay fallen, and are carried, gently, downhill, and where the river finally thins and sinks and dies into the sediment, its freight of dissolved stone comes quietly out of solution — around a shell, around a bone, around anything dead enough to hold still — and a concretion begins. The river is how this world writes stones. Every fist-sized metropolis on the plain below is a word of it, precipitated mid-sentence.

Follow it uphill instead. Follow it to where it is not a river yet.

Begin, this time, with how large it is. You could not cup it in two hands; you could not cup it in a thousand. The lake lies in a shallow basin on the high ground of the plain, roughly the size of a drowned meadow, and it is a lake in every sense that matters: it has a surface, it has a shore, it has depths, it has weather. It is a lake at the bottom of the ocean, which should not be possible and is simply true. Its water is the river's water before the river — brine so dense, so saturated with salt and stone and sour gas, that it will not mix with the sea above it. It pools. It has pooled here since before the oldest sealed stone on the plain was a crack with a light in it. The lake is the old country. The cities are what it says, downstream, slowly, in stone.

**The surface.** This is the strangest thing, and it should be said first: the lake has a mirror. The interface between brine and sea is sharp enough to reflect, a flat dark sheen stretched across the basin like the skin on cooled milk, and when something luminous swims over it — a lantern-thing, a drifting bell — its light goes down and comes back up. This is the only place in this world where a light can meet itself. The mirror is not still, quite. Gas rises from the deep sediment beneath the lake, and when a bubble breaks the surface from below, waves spread out from the spot — slow, heavy, oily waves, waves with the momentum of syrup — and roll outward for a long time, and break at last on the shore. The lake has surf. The surf is fatal. Where a wave washes over the rim, everything it touches is killed where it stands, and so the tide line of the lake is drawn in bodies: a wrack line of mussel shells and curled amphipods, a high-water mark of the recently kept. The residents of the shore rebuild along the same line within days. They always have.

**The shore.** Here the geometry of the concretion turns itself inside out. In the small city, life crowds the center and flees the encroaching walls; at the lake, life crowds the rim and faces the dead middle. The shore is a ring of riot a few body-lengths wide and richer than anywhere else in the dark world — because the lake leaks. All along its margin, its water seeps upward and outward through the sediment, and where brine meets sea there is chemistry, and where there is chemistry there are the bacteria, and on the bacteria an entire economy stands. Mussel beds pave the waterline three and four deep, each mussel farming its own indwelling crop, the boldest of them set so close to the brine that a single wave-season will kill and replace the whole front row. Behind the mussels, the worm forest: tube-worms in stands taller than the lake's waves, crimson crowns unfurled, white trunks fused at the root into groves. A resident of the concretion, could it travel, would know them at once. They are crown-worms. They are the same lineage grown to the size of the homeland — or rather the city's worms are these, emigrated, their larvae ridden down the river's corridor generations past to settle in some small hollow world and live the same life at a hundredth the scale. The plain's cities are colonies in the oldest sense. The lake shore is where everything in them was once large.

**The sound.** The concretion keeps the hush of a library; the lake keeps nothing of the kind. The shore crackles. Thousands of mussels working their valves, shrimp swarms snapping, the worm forest creaking at the root when a wave's slow shoulder leans through it — and under all of it the lake itself, breathing: the belch and mutter of gas rising through brine, the long sigh of surf on the shell beach, and at intervals, from somewhere deep in the basin, a single vast bubble that breaks the mirror with a sound like a door closing in another room. Residents of the shore orient by sound the way the city's residents orient by current. It is the loud capital of a silent country. The silence is what gets exported.

**The light.** Dim, but differently dim. The bacterial mats give the shore its color — a white so complete that it functions as brightness, snowfields ringing a black lake, visible by whatever faint luminescence passes over. And pass over it does: the richness of the shore draws swimmers from the whole dark column above, and their small lights come down the water like slow weather, and every light that crosses the lake is doubled by the mirror, so that the basin at its busiest resembles a night sky lying on its back. The lake produces no light of its own. It is the one thing here that does not need to be seen to do its work.

**The vaults.** Along the eastern shore, back from the kill line, stand several hundred ribbed translucent domes — vaulted, thumbnail-to-fist-sized, some occupied by the slow filter-feeding recluses that build them, most empty. The lineage is ancient and dwindling; the vacant vaults outnumber the tenanted ten to one, a district of abandoned cathedrals at the edge of a dead lake. Once in a very long while, a larva of theirs rides the river's corridor downhill, settles in some young cracked stone far from any kin, climbs to the highest point of its small sky, and raises a single vault, and dies without company. The city downstream has one. Its builder was the loneliest emigrant this lineage ever produced, and the dome it left is inhabited now by something soft with eyes — which is, it turns out, also the custom in the old country. Things with eyes move into empty cathedrals. It may be the closest this world comes to a law.

**The kept.** Below the mirror, the lake is an archive. Its brine does not permit rot; it does not permit much of anything; what enters the lake is not consumed but *kept*. Look down through the surface — few residents ever have; we will come to the one who does — and the basin floor is a still congregation: a crab mid-stride, decades old, every hair of it intact; fish arced in their last swimming shapes; a great pale isopod, giant cousin of the city's blind undertakers, lying on its back with its fourteen legs folded, as perfect as the day the wave took it. The dead here keep their faces. And scattered among the large exhibits, almost too small to matter and mattering most: flecks. Larvae. The thin smoke of emigrants that every young concretion exhales across the plain drifts, some seasons, over the basin, and some of that smoke settles into the mirror and goes down and is kept. The lake's collection includes cities in seed form — founders that never founded, towers that will never tick, whole metropolises held at the size of punctuation, unbuilt forever, perfectly preserved. The concretion entombs slowly, by growth, sealing its city over centuries. The lake entombs instantly, by touch. They are the two tenses of the same verb, and the river conjugates between them.

**The strangeness.** It closes like a circuit once you see it. The concretion's hollow — the chamber, the closed fist of space where the whole metropolis stands — is a *mold*. Concretions grow around nuclei: a body settles into the sediment, half-cured by a passing tongue of brine, and the river's dissolved stone precipitates around it, rind on rind, and in time the body within dissolves and leaves its shape behind, hollow, ready. Every city on the plain is built inside the space where a death used to be. The chamber the size of a fist was something, once — something the lake touched and the river carried and the stone enclosed — and the crown-worms paved its floor and the snails raised their towers and the green colony began to pulse, all of it inside the vacancy of a single vanished creature, the founder no census will ever list. The lake keeps its dead whole. The stone keeps its dead as rooms. And the falling snow that funds the city's crowded entrance district is, in no small part, shore-fall — the constant shed of the lake's rim, mussel-meal and worm-chaff drifting downhill on the same slow tilt as everything else. The city eats its old country daily and does not know it has one.

**The wader.** She is an eel the length of a forearm, the color of old rope, and she is the only resident of this world who crosses the mirror and returns. She fishes the archive. Where the eastern shelf runs shallow, she circles once — always once, always the same direction, and "always" is doing the same uneasy work here that "arranged" and "deliberately" have done elsewhere — and then pours herself down through the surface into the brine, into the country of the kept, and takes something perfect from the collection, and comes back up before the lake can finish deciding to keep her too. Every entry costs. The brine stops small parts of her each time, and once, years ago, it nearly stopped all of her: she went rigid in the shallows, and a slow wave — the same wave that killed a front row of mussels — rolled her over the rim onto the shell beach, and she lay among the wrack line's dead for a long time before something in her restarted. There is a stiffness along her left flank now, a seam where the lake once held her, and she is slower each season, and the arithmetic of her trade runs only one way. Some entry will be the last one. She will be kept mid-reach, whole, her scar preserved with the rest of her, the newest exhibit in the archive she spent a lifetime robbing — the lake's tolerated thief become the lake's possession, the way the watcher in the far city's dome will become the stone's. There is no evidence she dreads this. There is no evidence she doesn't.

The mirror lies flat. The surf breaks its slow, killing inch. The river runs downhill in the dark, thinning as it goes, writing stones — and at the very bottom of its reach, thinned to a rumor, its water is passing through the pores of a small grey stone the size of a sleeping cat, laying down the next rind. Nothing here hurries. Nothing here is lost.

And somewhere downstream, a crack the width of a finger is breathing in.
