**Cindermere**

You leave the Kiln Ward the only way anyone ever has — up through the seam, out onto the slag — and you find that the surface is not trackless after all. There are cairns. They start a stone's throw from the crack in the ground: waist-high stacks of black rock, one visible from the next, running away northwest across the lava field in a line so old the glassy rubble has settled around their bases like water frozen mid-ripple. Whoever built them built them leaving, and built them to be followed back.

Three days along that line. The slag country is vicious walking — a crust of ropy stone and clinker that rings underfoot and shears into edges when it breaks, no soil, no shade, no water, the sun by day and a cold like a dropped blade at night. On the second day you notice the cairns are no longer plain stacks. Bricks begin to appear in them. Fired bricks, red-brown, foreign to this black country, wedged among the slag with obvious care — one here, two there, thickening as you go, as if the road itself were remembering how to be masonry the closer it comes to home.

On the third day the land tips. You smell it before you see it — an impossible smell after the ward, after the slag: wet stone, reed, rot, cold fresh water. Green appears in the crevices, real green, indignant little ferns and moss like spilled paint. Then the crust ends in a broken black cliff, and below you is the lake.

The lava, when it ran, ran down a river valley, and stopped, and the river did not. Behind the flow's cooled snout the water backed up for miles: a long grey mere pressed between hills, fog standing on it in the mornings, the surface pocked with rain or fish or both. The flow itself is the dam. And built down the dam's outer face, terrace under terrace under terrace, stepping from the crest of the black stone to the wet meadows below, is the town.

Cindermere fits no hollow. It sprawls the way the ward never could — sheds and drying-racks and boat-slips straggling along the shore, peat-cuttings up the valley, jetties feeling out into the fog. But look at the heart of it, the terraced quarter on the dam face, and you will feel a strange doubled vision, because you have seen this town before, folded up small and turned inside out and buried in the ground three days behind you. The same stacked galleries. The same brick arches. Stairs dished by feet, doorways skirting obstacles, lanes that bend for reasons no longer visible. These people build in terraces the way other people speak their mother tongue — without deciding to. They came out of a bubble in the rock ten generations ago and they have never stopped building the inside of it.

Only now everything runs with water. That is the first thing that assaults you, coming off the slag: the *sound*. Cindermere is as loud as the Kiln Ward and its voice is the exact inversion. The dam weeps through every joint — springs burst whole from the basalt face and are caught in brick channels, split, resplit, led chuckling through the terraces past every door. Launders drum. Cisterns overflow on purpose; letting water spill is how you show you have it, and everyone has it. Rain on ten thousand roof-tiles, the lake slapping the boat-slips, gulls, dogs, oars knocking in tholes, children screaming down the stair-lanes — after the ward's dry ticking, the animal riot of it nearly buckles you. Nothing in the ward had a heartbeat. Everything here does, and all of it is shouting.

Water they hold in contempt, nearly. It falls from the sky, it stands in a lake, it pushes out of the rock uninvited. What Cindermere counts, hoards, tithes, inherits, and steals is *heat*. There is not a tree within a day's walk — the slag killed the near country, and the far valley is peat-bog and reed. Fuel is cut in the uplands and carried down and stacked under lock. The town's ledgers are ledgers of turves and reed-bundles and fish oil. A rich family is a family with a dry peat-loft. A curse here — the worst one, the one mothers slap children for — is *may your hearth go grey.* They have a dozen words for grades of warmth: the warmth of banked coals, of a wall the sun has left, of a body in fever, of brick fresh from firing — fine, technical, ancient words. Their words for colors are poor and all borrowed from the kiln: honey, glaze, ash, ember, scroll-brown. They live in the greenest, greyest country imaginable and they name its colors like people describing a photograph of it, because their language grew up in an amber dark where green did not exist. The old stories say the first generation to climb out wept in the daylight for a year.

And they still fire brick. This is the extravagance that defines them. There is fieldstone everywhere for the taking; timber could be rafted down the lake; either would build cheaper. Instead a full tenth of the town's fuel — tithed from every household, weighed at the fire-house door — goes each year into the communal kiln at the crest of the dam, and out come bricks, red-brown, hard, ruinously expensive, and the town is made of them because the town has always been made of them and being made of them is what the town *is*. Their mortar is burnt lime, and here is the difference that keeps their masons humble: it decays. Frost heaves it, water finds it, joints open, walls belly and must be taken down and rebuilt. In the ward, the masonry heals; the resident grows black glass into every failing joint and nothing there is ever ruined. In Cindermere nothing holds itself. Every wall is an argument with the rain that must be re-made every generation, and the masons have a saying for it, worn smooth as their stairs: *below, the house keeps the people. Up here, the people keep the house.*

They mean it literally. They know the ward is there. They have never stopped knowing.

**The Keeping**

At the crest of the dam, where the terraces top out against the black stone, stands the fire-house: a round building, brick to the eaves, roofed in tile, with one door and no windows and a hole in the roof-peak that has trailed smoke without one hour's interruption for something over four hundred years.

Inside is the Undying Hearth. It is not large — a brick pan the size of a bed, banked low most of the day, tended in shifts by a rotation every family in town serves in turn, fed from the tithe-lofts, screened from draughts, doted on like an invalid ancestor. It is the only fire in Cindermere that may not be allowed to die, because every other fire descends from it. When your hearth goes out — and hearths do; fuel runs short in the hungry months, people fall ill, people grieve and forget — you make the walk of the cold hearth: up the stair-lanes carrying a clay hood, and the tenders give you flame without question and without payment, and mark your name in the book, and you carry it home cupped and shielded like a chick. No one is refused. No one escapes the book. Half the marriages in town began as a lingering glance during someone's walk of the cold hearth, and the elders say that is what the book is really for.

The flame's genealogy is the town's founding document, and it runs like this. When the last generation to live below made ready to climb out — when the scouts had found the lake, and the surface had gone survivable, and the long argument was over — the final thing they did, the very last act performed by human hands in the Kiln Ward, was to gather an armful of fallen scroll-leaves from the corner-drifts and press them, bound as a torch, to the glaze of the hottest kiln-mouth until they caught. They lit their leaving from the fissures themselves, with the resident's own shed leaves for tinder, and they carried that fire up the seam and across three days of glass in a hooded pot, feeding it as they walked, and set it down on the dam-crest, and built the fire-house around it before they built anything else.

So the flame in the round building is, by unbroken descent, the warmth of the Kiln Ward, kindled from the body of the thing that lives there and never once let lapse. The town does not put it quite that way. The town says: *we borrowed the fire, and a loan is kept alive until it can be returned.* They call the ward the Old Warm, or the Under-hearth, or — in lullabies — the Mother's House, and the belief, worn soft by centuries but never discarded, is that the ancestors' warmth still keeps house below, and the doors below stand open, and one day, in some form no one troubles to specify, the fire will go home.

They are more right than they know. You have been down there. You have seen how the doors stand open — every passage held at the height of a walking person, every wall-niche kept clear, the hand-axes untouched in their rings of deferential growth. Ten generations of Cindermere have kept faith with a house they believe remembers them, and the house, which cannot believe anything, has kept their rooms in perfect fidelity all the while. Two households keeping each other across three days of glass, each in its own kind of dark, neither with any way to know the keeping is mutual.

Except — the bricks.

Every year at midwinter, when the fuel-fear is sharpest and the fog lies on the lake all day, the kiln is fired, and the first brick drawn from the first firing is set aside. A bearer — it is an honor, allotted by lot, refused by no one in living memory — carries that one brick out along the cairn-line, three days across the slag, and sets it at the mouth of the seam, on the heap the centuries have made there, and turns around, and walks home. No one goes down. It is not exactly taboo; it is closer to manners. You do not walk into a house you moved out of. You leave something at the door.

They call the heap the letter-pile, and they are not wrong to. Because the seam is a throat, and the ward breathes through it, and every brick added to the pile changes — by a whisper, by almost nothing — the draw of air down into the hollow. The resident has no eye, no ear, no memory as we would grant the word. But it reads its own breath the way you read your own pulse, and once a year, in the season the surface goes cold, the breath changes. A slight new resistance. A fractionally altered note in the organ-tone that stands in its sternum-deep voice. That is the only news the Kiln Ward has had of its people in four hundred years: one brick's worth of weather in its throat, every midwinter, regular as devotion. It has grown its channels around that rhythm by now. If the bricks ever stopped coming, something in the shape of its breathing would go slack, and whether that would mean anything to it is a question the word *meaning* is probably too small to hold.

One more thing they carried out, besides the fire. Seeds.

Every child born in Cindermere is given, at the naming, a string of small glassy beads, black-brown, hard as shot — heat-drinker seeds, gathered from the ward floor in the last years below and hoarded ever since, drilled and strung and passed down. They are called the unlit. The belief is gentle and universal: when the world is warm enough — when the fire is returned, when the dead are comfortable, at the end of the long cold that everyone privately understands their history to be — the unlit will bloom. Old women hold their strings near the hearth on winter nights, not hoping exactly. In fidelity to a form.

The beads will never bloom. The drilling kills the germ; every strung seed in Cindermere is a small polished corpse, and no one knows it, and it would be a cruelty to no purpose to tell them.

But you should know — you, coming down off the Char Road with the fog rising to meet you and the first door-lanterns showing along the terraces — what the custom is here for travelers. Anyone who comes in off the slag is taken to the fire-house. It is the one free warmth in town. You will be fed fish and given the floor by the Undying Hearth for the night, on bricks that have not been cold in four hundred years, and your boots will be set by the fire to dry, because that is what is done with a traveler's boots.

And the seed in your tread — the one whole, unpierced, living seed to cross the slag since the crossing itself, flung by the resident into a world where almost nothing it throws can live — will let go of the leather sometime in the night, and drop a hand-span, and land on warm brick.

It knows what to do on warm brick. It has been waiting in the dark of your boot to be told it is home.

A season, perhaps two. A root of black glass finer than a hair, finding the joint between two hearthstones, growing along the line where warm meets cool, drinking the spill of the fire its parent's leaves once lit. Then some evening — winter, likely, when the hearth is built high and the tender on shift is half asleep and the rain is drumming its one endless sentence on the tiles — there will come, from floor level, under all the water-noise, a sound no one in Cindermere has ever heard indoors and every one of them knows from the cradle-songs:

*tick.*

Like cooling pottery. A small ceramic cup, opening beside the flame, facing it.

On a hundred throats around the town, the drilled beads will hang dead as ever. And on the fire-house floor the living flower will stand open toward the Undying Hearth, the loan and the lender face to face at last, four centuries and three days of glass closed to the width of a hand. What the town will make of it — prophecy, visitation, the Mother's House writing back — is theirs to decide, and they will decide it loudly, being people, being alive, being loud.

Below, in the amber dark, nothing will change that anyone could measure. The pods will crack, the seeds will rain into the void, the breath will rise and fall. The resident throws its children, hard, at a world that kills nearly all of them, and keeps a city open in the shape of the family that left, and once a year feels a brick's weight of love arrive as weather in its throat. It has no way to know that one throw finally landed. But then, the letters were never read in either direction, and the keeping went on all the same. A mere, the old word says, is a lake; it is also a boundary. Something has crossed this one, riding the warmth of you, and taken root beside the fire it was always, in the long slow arithmetic of the Kiln Ward, going to find.
