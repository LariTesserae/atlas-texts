The Drift

Follow the breath. At dusk, when the chamber exhales hard through the seam, the warm plume rises maybe a body-length into the plateau air and is gone — taken by the wind that crosses the tableland every evening, dry and steady, running west. Follow that wind for three hundred miles over stone so flat and so empty that the wind forgets it is passing anything at all, and you come to the place where the plateau simply stops: a scarp, a two-thousand-foot brink where the shield rock breaks off clean, and below it, stretching to a grey ocean, the answer to a question the plateau no longer remembers asking.

Because the mountain range did not vanish. Nothing that size vanishes. Billions of seasons of rain and frost and river took the peaks apart grain by grain and carried them down and west, and when the rivers themselves died, the grains stayed. What lies below the scarp, from the foot of the shield to the cold sea, is the body of the mountains: a sand sea a hundred miles deep, dune upon dune upon dune, apricot and rust and bone, every single grain of it a former piece of the summits. The plateau kept the height. The Drift got everything else.

And the Drift, unlike the plateau, is not finished. It has never been finished for one hour. The dunes are enormous — some of them stand taller than anything left on the shield above — and they move, whole mountains of the old mountains walking seaward a few feet a year, crests smoking with blown sand, slip-faces hissing and settling, the entire landscape rearranging itself with a patience that only looks like stillness because you cannot stay long enough to watch. At the western edge the front rank of dunes marches directly into the surf and the ocean takes it, grain by grain, finishing at last the journey each grain began on a peak that no longer exists. The mountain is still eroding. It is just doing it somewhere else, lying down at full length beside the sea, and the plateau's perfected emptiness is not an ending but an abandonment: the story moved.

You would call the Drift dead, at noon. Nothing grows on the dunes proper — no root can hold in sand that will not stop traveling — and the heat off the slip-faces makes the air stand up and shiver. But the Drift is not dead. It is waiting, the way the chamber's assassin bugs wait, as a body plan.

And then there is the fog.

Offshore runs a cold current, old and deep, hauling polar water up along this coast, and where the desert's hot breath meets the sea's cold one, the air gives up what it carries. Almost every night, in the last black hours, the fog comes in off the water — not rain, never rain; nothing here has tasted rain any more than the chamber has — but a slow grey flood, waist-deep, then dune-deep, rolling inland over the sand sea for thirty, forty, fifty miles, and everything it touches, it beads. By first light the whole western Drift is jeweled: droplets standing on every grain of quartz, every strand of dead grass, every wing-case and pebble and thread of silk, trillions of separate waters, free — actually free, belonging to no body, sitting in the open where anything may come and take them. In the chamber, three hundred miles east, there has not been a free drop of water in geological memory. Here it is given away every morning, to everyone, and by mid-morning the sun has taken back whatever went unclaimed, and by noon the gift has never happened. The Drift's entire living world is built inside those few grey hours, the way the chamber's is built inside its stone.

Begin with the old plants, because everything else in the Drift arranges itself around them.

They grow on the gravel flats between the dune ranges — never on the moving sand, only where the mountain's coarser bones have settled and locked — and each one is a fixed point in a landscape that has none. A single plant is two leaves. That is all it will ever be: two leaves emerging from a low woody crown, growing continuously from the base for its whole life while the wind shreds the far ends into ribbons, so that an old individual looks like a wrecked green tent, a splayed heap of leather straps twelve feet across, sand-blasted, half-buried, ragged — and alive, growing, the same two leaves it unfurled as a seedling in an age when the dunes stood miles from where they stand now. The oldest of them are thousands of years into those two leaves. They drink the fog directly, condensing it along every ribbon and channeling it down to the crown and the deep root, and they drink whatever moisture the old riverbeds still hide far below the gravel — water that fell, perhaps, as the last rains the mountains ever felt, still seeping seaward underground, centuries per mile.

And each plant is a settlement. In a landscape with no shade, no shelter, no fixed address, a heap of living leather is a city-site, and every old plant carries its court: dune crickets under the leaf-straps, waiting out the day; a resident lizard or two, flat-bodied, patrolling the ribbons for whatever the wind has snagged in them; beetles bedded in the cool sand beneath the crown; small spiders holding webs in the lee, rigged to harvest fog as much as prey. If the chamber is one sealed room crammed with everything, the Drift is the same arrangement turned inside out — dozens of tiny open chambers scattered across the flats, each one an ancient landlord and its tenants, none of them aware of the others, all of them running the same economy: condense, shelter, endure.

The fog beetles are the Drift's dawn liturgy. They spend the night buried in the dune flanks, and in the grey hour they climb — long-legged, round-backed, dozens of them ascending the same crest like pilgrims — and at the top, facing the sea, each one does the thing its whole body is a tool for: it stands on its head. Abdomen up into the moving fog, legs braced, motionless, while the mist beads on the ridged wax of its back and the beads crawl together and fatten and finally roll, down the tilted body, along grooves shaped for exactly this, to the mouth. A beetle drinks nearly half its weight in a morning, standing upside down on the corpse of a mountain, taking water out of the open air. It is the plainest miracle the Drift performs, and the chamber, for all its million-year ingenuity, has nothing like it and never will: the chamber's creatures inherited their water and guard it in a closed ring; the beetles are given theirs, new, every day the fog comes, and owe it to no one.

Under the sand, the Drift keeps its own blind nation. There are swimmers in the dunes — small furred things, eyeless, satin-coated so the grains flow over them, who move through loose sand the way seals move through water and surface only to cross from dune to dune. They cannot see and do not need to; they hunt by listening through the mountain. A cricket's footfalls, a beetle's landing, the tick of a burying larva — every small event sends its tremor out through the packed grains, and the swimmers read the sand the way the chamber's soldiers read their masonry, head pressed to the medium, waiting for the news. Three hundred miles apart, in the dark of a sealed room and the dark under a dune, the two halves of the dead range are doing the same work: carrying messages through the mountain's substance for the blind. There are sand-colored vipers too, buried to the eyes — the eyes sit on top of the head, periscopes in a sea of grains — and pale fishmoth cousins of the chamber's silverfish, older than either place, quick in the slip-face shadows. The dune fauna is thin, scattered, solitary; no dynasties, no cities, no queen. Every body in the Drift is its own sealed chamber, carrying its own cupful, and the distances between them are the architecture.

What feeds all of it — and here the wind shows its hand — is debris. The dunes grow nothing, and yet their hollows are stocked: every slip-face is a net, and the wind fills it nightly with the small dry wrack of two worlds. Grass fragments and seed-husks off the coastal flats; spores and pollen off the lichen plains; the wings and husks of insects; dust off the plateau itself, blown west over the scarp with whatever organic freight the tableland surrendered. The detritus drifts into the dune hollows and the fishmoths and beetle larvae mine it, and the lizards eat the miners, and the vipers eat the lizards, and so the whole sand sea runs, at bottom, on deliveries — on the same wind, working the same route, that sifts crumbs down through the seam to feed the chamber. The two places have never touched and never will, and they eat at one table. The wind is the trade, and it collects in both directions: what the sea air lifts off the Drift's coastal fields — pollen, spores, gnats spiraling up on the morning thermals — rides east and high, and some unmeasurable fraction of it, over eons, has come down through a crack in the shield an arm's length wide. The chamber's lichen forests, those coral shelves ten thousand years deep, grew from spores; the spores came on the wind; and the greatest lichen fields in this world are here, on the fog-side gravel plains of the Drift — miles of them, orange and grey-green and black, a crusted carpet on the stones, drinking the mist, some colonies old enough to have watched the dunes arrive. The chamber's forests are, in lineage, colonies of these fields: the sealed room's oldest immigrants, still being fed, one spore a century, by the mother country they will never know they have.

And once in a long while the trade runs the other way, and the Drift receives the chamber's only export. In the season of flights, when the colony releases its winged royals and the spider canopy takes its toll, a few alates each year ride the dusk exhalation up through the seam and out — pale, waxy, weak-flying things, built for a world they have never seen — and the west wind takes them. Nearly all of them fall on the plateau within a mile and dry there like petals on an anvil. But the wind is patient and the years are many, and some mornings, after the fog burns off, there is among the night's wrack in a dune hollow a single wing of a shape nothing in the Drift produces: long, milky, veined like frost. The fishmoths eat it with everything else. The sealed world's failed queens and kings, arriving as detritus, entering the open water-cycle at last — the only citizens of the chamber that ever leave, and they leave as food.

Stand on a dune crest in the grey hour and the Drift stops resolving as a desert and starts resolving as the chamber's opposite and answer. There, one warm room, a million bodies, water hoarded in a closed ring, never spilled, never free, the last busy remnant of the mountain's height. Here, the mountain's whole body laid out under the sky, nearly empty, and the water given loose every morning to whoever climbs for it, held by no one past noon. The chamber's few cupfuls have circled queen to worker to gecko to wall for spans of time that flattened the peaks; the fog's water is new off the sea each night and gone by midday, and both economies work, and neither will ever hear of the other. Between them lies the plateau, perfected and silent, believing itself finished — while at its eastern crack the mountain's last unrest keeps its crowded room, and along its western sea the mountain's actual body goes on traveling, dune by dune, into the water: still moving, still fed by the same long wind, still — grain by grain, breath by breath — not done.
