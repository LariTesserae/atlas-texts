**The Dry Stair of Verren-Above**

There is nothing here but sky, and by local reckoning there was always too much of it. You arrive — if arrival is the word for three days' walking up a switchbacked road through country the color of old bone — over a last ridge, and the place shows itself all at once, from a mile away, the way the chamber far beneath it shows itself all at once from within: a white cascade poured down the mountainside, five hundred meters of it, terrace under terrace under terrace, blinding at noon, blue-shadowed by evening, and not one drop of water anywhere in it.

It is the fossil of a waterfall. That is the honest description, and it takes a while to accept it, because the stone still performs the water — every gesture of it, arrested. Hundreds of shallow basins descend the slope in overlapping scallops, each with a lip like a brimming edge frozen at the instant of overflow. Between the basins the stone hangs in curtains and pleats, draperies of pale travertine laid down a film at a time by centuries of hot mineral water, so that the whole stair looks less carved than *poured*, which is exactly what it was. The water wrote it. The water came up out of the mountain scalding and heavy with dissolved stone, spilled from a bore near the summit, and built its own bed as it fell, thickening it by a hair's width per decade, for as long as anyone kept records and longer.

The silence is the first surprise. A place shaped so entirely by pouring, running, brimming, steaming — and the loudest thing in it is the wind. But the wind here is not nothing. That is the second surprise: the stone is hollow. Behind every curtain of travertine is the void the falling water once occupied, and the drapery healed over it, and now the stair is honeycombed with flutes and chambers the wind cannot leave alone. Knock a knuckle against a basin's lip and it rings like crockery. On days when the wind comes up the valley the whole cascade mutters and hoots, a scatter of low breathy notes with no meter to them at all — and if you have been below, if you have hung in the plaza of warm nothing with the triple knock arriving through your ribs, you will notice the lack the way you'd notice a missing tooth. This is the same instrument. This is what the chamber's voice sounds like with the heart taken out of it: the pipes without the pulse.

At the head of the stair, gathered around the dry bore like lips around a straw, is the town. Verren-Above, though for most of its life it needed no suffix; the Deep was its cellar, not its twin. It is built of its own spillage — the founders quarried the travertine the water was busy making, so the houses are cut from the water's handwriting, and you can see the flow-lines running through doorposts and window sills, the grain of a current that stopped mattering long before the roofs fell in. Most of the roofs have fallen in. The streets are the width of two shoulders. The walls stand.

For the wet centuries, this was a town of stolen heat. It did not farm; the ground would not have it. It *soaked*. Bathhouses in tiers, laundries whose steam could be seen from the next valley, dye-vats, tanneries, winter gardens under salvaged glass where lemons grew at an altitude that should have forbidden them. Invalids came up the switchback road on litters to lie in the terraced basins with the mountain's blood-warm water brimming over them, looking into all that sky. The town took the heat the engines pushed up from the drowned district a kilometer below its feet and turned it into linen, into color, into cures, into a modest sufficiency that lasted longer than most empires. And it kept the engines' time without ever having seen them. The strokes came up through the bedrock — faint, but the floors carried them, and the water carried them better — and the town called it *the knock* and set its life by it. The laundry songs are in triple time. The old grace before meals is three phrases and a held silence, timed, the elders would tell you, to fit inside the pause. Children raised on those floors grew up assuming everyone's heart beat in threes, and were reportedly disturbed, traveling, to learn otherwise.

Then, in an autumn no one alive remembers but everyone can date, the water stopped.

It did not fail. That distinction matters here, is in fact the town's whole theology of its own decline. The heat below never faltered; the beat never stopped; the pool at the town's center is warm to this hour. What happened — as near as the keepers' ledgers and the elders' bitterness can reconstruct it — is that the machine below finished wearing itself into its final shape. Somewhere in the drowned circuitry of pipes, over centuries of mineral crust and patient collapse, the water found a shorter way around: a closed loop, chamber to engine to chamber, perfect, self-correcting, needing nothing and offering nothing. The system arrived at the one motion it could not help making — and that motion no longer included the surface. The stroke that stumbles and rights itself, the rhythm that holds without a single tending hand, the thing that fills a visitor to the Deep with something close to reverence: seen from up here, that is the disaster. The heart perfected itself, and in perfecting itself, withdrew its blood from the limb. The steam over the stair thinned across one summer. The lemons died under their glass. The last hot water went down the last drain, and the stone began, for the first time in its existence, to dry.

The town emptied the way water leaves a basin: quickly at the spout, then slower, then a residue. The residue remains, and it lives around the pool.

Because one warmth was left. In the oldest bathhouse — the only roof in town that has been continuously kept, re-slated, re-beamed, at real cost, for generations — there is a pool perhaps six meters across, round, rimmed in stone worn glassy by the hips and heels of the wet centuries. The water in it is black. Not dirty — black the way a doorway is black. It is the mouth of the shaft: the warm column that drops for the better part of an hour's sinking into the chamber below, and the heat of the engines climbs it by slow conduction, so that the pool steams gently on cold mornings, the town's last exhalation of its old wealth, enough to warm one room. The keepers live in that room. The pool is the throat of the Deep, and Verren-Above has become, in its diminishment, a settlement of the throat: a few households arranged around an opening, the way the machinery below clings to its walls around a center of warm nothing.

The strangeness of the place is quiet and arrives late, and it arrives at the pool. Sit by it long enough — an evening will do — with a lamp low and the surface still, and you will see the water move. Not ripple: *swell*. Three small risings, each perhaps the thickness of a coin, and then a settling, a held smoothness, and then three again. The pool breathes. A kilometer down, the pressure-strokes flex the whole water column, and the column delivers them to the surface intact, softened to almost nothing but never to nothing, so that the beat which is the total condition of the world below arrives up here as the smallest possible gesture: a black circle of water in a lamplit room, lifting and settling, lifting and settling, in a rhythm no one designed and nothing tends. On the rare nights when the engines stumble — the doubled stroke, the early beat — the pool slops its rim, a sound like a fish turning, and by morning it is written down.

**The Ledger of Verren-Above**

Begin, as the town would, with the book itself, and work outward.

**The keepers.** There are two of them now; there were once forty, an order with vestments and a schedule of offices, back when the office meant something — keeping the bore clear, keeping the basins swept of scale, regulating who soaked when. All of that purpose drained away with the water. What survived is the residue of duty: keep the bathhouse roof sound, keep the pool's mouth clear of anything that might fall in, keep the lamp, keep the ledger. The ledger is the office now. Every night, one keeper sits the pool and records its behavior: the count of swells, the depth of the settling, any stumble, any slop over the rim, the correction that follows. The entries go back in an unbroken chain of hands through — no one has counted — perhaps forty volumes, shelved in the warm room, the early ones in scripts the current keepers can no longer read. They do not know what the record is *for*. There was a reason once; it wore away; the recording continued, because the recording had by then become a shape that could only do the right thing. They are, and one of them will say so with a dry look, the surface's answer to the engines: a practice that finished its purpose long ago and never learned how to stop. They keep the count. Below, unknown to them, in the silt of the cemetery floor, the ring-shelled grazers are keeping it too, laying down the same stumbles and corrections in carbonate. Two transcripts of one heartbeat, a kilometer apart, neither aware of the other. The world below writes in stone and the world above writes in ink, and they have never once disagreed.

**The lichen.** Across the dry basins, gray-gold — and if you have seen the film on the cool pipes below, the color will stop you, though it is rhyme, not kinship; convergence, not descent. Where the film farms the engines' exhaust and builds, the lichen farms the sunlight and *unbuilds*: it etches the travertine, roughens the poured gloss, takes the stair apart at a hair's width per decade — the exact pace, as it happens, at which the crust below still thickens. The two worlds are trading substance at the same speed in opposite directions, one growing its stone and one dissolving it, as if a single slow breath were passing between them.

**The swifts.** The hollow curtains of the stair are riddled with openings, and the swifts have taken the water's abandoned rooms for their own. All day they ride the thermals that pour up off the white stone — heat again, but honest surface heat now, the sun's and not the mountain's — wheeling in the enormous empty air above the terraces, which is Verren-Above's plaza of nothing, its void with all the density clinging to the walls. Their festival is the spring return: one evening the sky over the stair is empty, and the next it is a screaming gyre, thousands of them funneling down into the stone at dusk, and the hollow flutes answer with a sound the wind never manages, and for one night the dry instrument is almost played. The old people come out of the two inhabited houses to watch it. It is the closest thing the surface has to the larval diaspora below — life riding the currents out and back — and like everything else here, it happens in the water's absence, in the spaces the water made.

**The snails.** In the basins, in the shade of the lips, small coiled shells that you will decide are pebbles until one, over the course of a morning, is somewhere else. They graze the lichen. Their shells grow in rings — wet years thick, drought years thin — and they are the third set of books kept on this mountain, unread like the others.

**The laminated.** The stair is full of the town's wet centuries, held in cross-section. Coins thrown into the brimming basins for luck, sealed over within a decade and visible now as round shadows an inch deep in the translucent stone. A bone comb. A child's shoe buckle. A bird that landed once in the wrong basin. The water accepted everything offered to it and filed it in stone, exactly as the chamber below upholstered its bolts and gloves in opal. Neither place buries its past. Both wear it, a hair's width down.

**The knock.** Last entry, first citizen. It is still here. Lie down at night on any stone floor in the town — every floor is the same stone, the water's stone — press an ear or a palm flat, and wait past your own pulse, and under it, beneath hearing but not beneath feeling: three, and a hush. Three, and a hush. Arriving through a kilometer of bedrock from the engines that no longer send anything else up. Heat, water, wealth, purpose — the mountain has taken all of it back. The beat is the one export it still makes, and it costs nothing, and it stops for nothing, and every child born up here still falls asleep on it without being told it exists.

So the two places stand, the heart and the scar it once reached the air by: below, a machine dissolved into a creature, deafening, warm, complete; above, its dried limb in the sunlight, silent, cold, kept. When someone goes down — it happens once in a keeper's lifetime, maybe; the ledger has a page for it — they lower themselves into the black pool at dusk and are gone the better part of an hour into the warm dark, and the pool breathes on behind them, three swells and a settling. And when they rise again, days later, carrying the beat in their ribs the way everyone who visits carries it, the keepers towel them off by lamplight, sit them by the warm black circle, and make the entry in the only words the office still requires, the same words for centuries, which are also the truest thing either world has ever said about the other:

*One went down. One came up. The count goes on.*
