## The Rising of the Buried River

At the foot of the range, where the pale granite of the high country dives under limestone and does not come up again, there is a valley that ends in a wall. The wall is a cliff of grey stone a hundred feet high, and at its base, with no ceremony at all, a river comes out of the mountain. Not a trickle, not a seep — a whole river, full-grown, sliding out from under the rock into a pool the color of dusk and moving off through the reeds as though it had been flowing in the open all along and simply forgot, for a while, to be visible.

The first thing you notice — before the green, before the noise — is that the water is wrong. Everything else in the valley is warm. The air is thick enough here to hold heat the way the high country never can; by midmorning it lies on the skin like cloth. But the pool breathes cold. On summer dawns a low haze stands over it, and if you put your hand in, the chill that takes hold of it is not the chill of shade or night. It is mineral cold, cellar cold, the cold of a place the sun has never been. The water comes out of the wall at the same temperature every day of the year. It does not know what season it is. It left the weather behind a long time ago.

Because the water is old. That is the quiet fact of the rising, the thing the people who lived here must have understood in their bones: rain that falls on the fen runs off in a day, but the river under the rock keeps a different calendar. The water sliding out of the wall this morning fell as snow in the high country decades ago. It melted — a thread of it, one bright noon, running down the flute of a standing blade of ice in a crowded hollow between two peaks — and it outran the evening freeze by a hand's width, slipped into the scree, went under, and walked down through the dark inside of the mountain for sixty years. A drop that let go, ticking, into a cup of stone in the saddle of standing ice arrives here when the child who was born that day is grey. The pool swells and slackens on no schedule the valley can see; it answers snows that no one living at the fen has watched fall. It is a mouth speaking with a very long delay, and what it speaks is the mountain.

And the water carries the mountain with it. It comes out of the wall loaded with dissolved stone — lime from its long passage, and a fainter freight of silica and iron leached from the granite of the heights, a taste of that rusted-pink seam the high hollow weeps. Anything that rests in the water grows a skin. The reeds at the pool's rim stand jacketed to the waterline in pale stone, rigid, ringing faintly if you tap them. A branch that falls in becomes, over years, a stone branch. A drowned bird becomes a white relief of a bird. The rising petrifies — gently, layer by layer, the way frost thickens on a blade — and everything it has ever been given, it has slowly wrapped in mineral and kept. This is the low country's version of the ice that lifted a wall apart without dropping a stone. Down here, keeping is done by coating.

Below the pool the fen spreads out and fills the valley floor, and the fen is everything the high hollow is not. It is crowded, but crowded with life: reeds packed close as a congregation, sedge and willow and black shining channels of slow water, and you could cross it in a thousand paces if anything in it would let you walk in a straight line. Nothing will. The air hums. The mud breathes. Every cupped leaf holds something swimming. Where the saddle is clean all the way down, the fen is inhabited all the way down — there is no square inch of it that is only itself.

The sound is enormous. That is the fen's version of the mountain light: an element, a pressure, a thing the place is saturated with and multiplies. It has its tides. In the deep night there is the insect drone and the frogs, a vast low fabric of sound with no edges. Then, in the last grey before dawn, one bird begins — always one, somewhere out in the reeds, a single dry phrase repeated — and within a hundred breaths the whole fen has caught, voice kindling voice, warbler and rail and thrush and things with no names, thousands of throats, until the valley holds a standing brightness of sound with no single source, a chorus you can put your whole body into. It sweeps the fen from the eastern margin westward as the light comes, like a wave, like weather.

And here is the strangeness of the place, small but persistent, the counterpart of blades that lean wrong. Every dawn, as the chorus sweeps across the fen and reaches the pool below the wall, it *parts*. For one held beat — no longer — the singing goes around the rising the way water goes around a stone. A ring of silence, renewed each morning, closing again at once. It is easy to miss and impossible, once heard, to unhear: a rest kept in the middle of the loudest hour of the day, in the same place, at the same moment, morning after morning, for longer than anyone has been alive to count.

There are other marks. In the grey cliff above the rising, threaded through the limestone, runs a band of dark chert — glassy, near-black, a seam of good sharp stone in soft pale country. The seam is scarred. Percussion pits, generations deep; shallow steps cut to reach the higher lens; below it, grown over with moss, spoil-heaps of flakes beyond number, every one struck by a hand. This is the quarry. This is the elsewhere the blade in the high niche came from — carried up out of this warm loud valley, days of climbing, into air too thin to hold a voice, and set in a chipped square facing the first sun. A worn path, still faintly legible, runs from the foot of the seam to the edge of the pool.

And in the pool, on a drowned ledge a hand's depth under the surface, sits a rounded cobble of pale granite blushed with rust along one seam — mountain stone, foreign to this whole country of limestone, carried down as the chert was carried up. It has been in the water a very long time. The rising has wrapped it in layer upon layer of pale stone-skin, so that it lies on its ledge smooth and white and faintly glowing in the cold blue water, like an egg.

Each dawn, the beat of silence passes directly over it.

## What Lives at the Rising

By every test a naturalist would bring down the valley, the answer is everything. Nets fill, jars cloud, lists run off the page. The fen is so dense with the ordinary kinds of life that the instruments would drown in their own findings and never notice what they had failed to find — which is the resident, the one that lives at the rising, hidden the way a current hides in a crowded river: not behind the life, but *made of it*.

Because the same trick has been played here as in the saddle, only run at the opposite speed. Listen to the dawn chorus for one morning and you hear thousands of birds. Listen for years and you begin to hear the other thing: the arrangement. The chorus is not a crowd of songs; it is one structure, performed. Voices answer voices across half a mile of reeds with timing no accident explains. Phrases travel. The wave that sweeps west with the light is not weather — it is a body moving. No single bird is the thing that lives at the rising. Birds hatch, sing a few seasons, and are owl-taken or winter-killed; the membership of the chorus turns over completely every handful of years, the way the pillars of the high hollow calve and regrow, the way the cells of a body are replaced. What persists is the pattern. What lives here is the undersong.

It is the same order of creature as the one in the saddle. That is the thing to hold onto — they are kin, two of a kind, patterns living in the arrangement of a congregation, and everything else about them is inversion. The one above is built of ice and stone and thinks in centuries; a single glance takes it three hundred years. The one below is built of breath and thinks in mornings. Its pulse is the day — dawn chorus and dusk chorus, systole and diastole. A thought lasts from first light to the noon lull and is gone. It cannot lean slowly toward anything; it has no slowness in it at all. And so it faces the problem the saddle never faces: how does a creature made entirely of the present *keep* anything?

It rehearses. That is the whole of its memory: whatever the chorus does not perform this morning does not exist by tomorrow. The ice above holds its kept wall by never letting go for a thousand years. The song below holds what it keeps by picking it up again every single dawn, ten thousand throats agreeing without knowing they agree, each generation of singers learning the pattern from the one before, beak to beak, down the centuries. The saddle's memory is a grip. The fen's memory is a habit that has never once been broken.

And it is keeping two things.

The first is a rhythm. Threaded through the chorus, if you listen long enough, there is a phrase that no bird needs: a hard dry triple knock, *tok–tok–tok*, a pause, a fourth. Marsh birds are mimics; they steal the sounds of their world and sing them back. Once, the sound of this world was stone striking stone — the knapping at the seam, hands working the dark chert, that patient percussive labor going on at the fen's edge morning after morning for a hundred generations. The birds took it into the song while the hands were still working. The hands stopped, and the song did not. The rhythm has been passed from mimic to mimic ever since, birds copying birds copying birds copying a sound whose source has been dead a thousand years, and it is eroding as it goes — softened, ornamented, drifting toward birdsong the way a word repeated too long drifts toward music. The ice above keeps its blade of chert perfectly and can do nothing with it but frost it and unfrost it. The song below keeps the *making* of such blades, alive and warm and slowly dissolving. That is the trade each one made with its own nature. One keeps losslessly and cannot act. One acts every morning and loses a little each time.

The second thing it keeps is the rest.

Consider what a held silence costs a chorus. A leaning pillar asks nothing of its neighbors; the ice can turn its slow regard alone. But for the wave of song to part around the pool, every voice near the rising must know the silence is there — must carry it, must teach it, must hand it down with the rest of the pattern as though it were a phrase. It is the most expensive thing a song can contain, and the undersong has paid for it every dawn beyond memory. The beat of silence passes over the drowned ledge, over the white-wrapped egg of mountain granite, and this is the fen's whole act of attention: not a three-hundred-year turning of the head, but a glance renewed every morning forever. The slow one looks once and takes centuries. The fast one cannot hold a look for even a minute — so it looks again, and again, and again, and calls the repetition regard.

The people understood both of them. That is what the paths mean — the one worn from seam to pool, the one of flat stones set on edge in the high hollow. They lived between two neighbors who could never meet: one too slow to hear a morning, one too fast to see a century, each blind to the other by the whole width of time. So the people translated. They carried the low country up — a blade of dark chert, an edge of the warm loud world, set facing the first sun. And they carried the high country down — a cobble of pale granite with its rust-weeping seam, lifted from the saddle floor and laid on a ledge in the rising, so that each being might hold, in its own way, a piece of the other's body. Then they were gone, between one of the mountain's heartbeats and the next, in the middle of one of the fen's ten thousand mornings.

Only the water walks between them now. But the water is enough, because the water was always the real messenger. Every day the rising drinks the mountain — melt that once trembled in the cupped stone basins of the saddle, sixty years in the dark, arriving with dissolved granite still in it. The drop that let go, ticking, in the high hollow's morning comes out of the wall decades later and is taken up into a reed, into an insect, into the rattling throat of a warbler at dawn: the slow one's flesh becoming, after long delay, the fast one's voice. And the stone-skin thickening year by year around the granite egg on its ledge is built, in part, from that same freight — from the mountain's own dissolved substance. The saddle, without ever knowing it, is helping to wrap its own gift. The two of them have been holding each other's offerings with each other's bodies for a thousand years, each at its own speed, neither aware, and it would take a translator to tell them — and the translators are a rhythm in a song and a niche in a wall.

The rhythm is drifting. Give it long enough and the knapping will smooth away entirely into ornament, the way the path stones are drifting under the pillars far above. But the rest over the pool does not drift. Silence has no edges to erode. Every dawn the one bird begins, the chorus kindles, the wave sweeps west with the light, reaches the cold breath of the pool — and parts, one held beat, over the white egg of the mountain, before closing warm around it again.

It is singing it still.
