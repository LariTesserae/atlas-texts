# The Wellwood of the Low Country

You come down to it through ordinary country, and the ordinariness is part of it. Weeks from the mountains, past where they've dropped below the horizon and out of conversation, the land goes to hedgerow and cattle-mud and slow brown rivers. Warm air. Midges. Lanes that flood in autumn. Nothing in this country is older than the people in it can account for, except one thing, and you see it from a long way off: a round green swelling on the flat land, darker than the fields, dense as a held breath. Trees, where the low country does not much bother with trees. A wood shaped like a drop of water lying on a table.

Locals call it the Wellwood, and they do not farm to its edge. Not from fear — from something more like manners. The plow-lines all curve away from it, generations deep.

Inside, the wood is close and green-gold and loud with living. It gets denser toward the middle, which is the opposite of how woods usually work, and richer: moss thick as upholstery, ferns to the waist, oaks of an age the low country's soil should not be able to feed. Everything leans slightly inward, toward the center, the way a crowd leans toward a speaker.

At the center is the Eye.

## The Eye

A pool, not large — you could throw a stone across it, though nobody does — and perfectly round, and full to within a finger's width of its rim. The water is cold. Not stream-cold, not shade-cold: mountain-cold, glacier-cold, a cold with altitude in it, here at the bottom of a warm country where no such cold has any business surviving the journey up from the ground.

It rises without bubbling. That is the first thing you understand about the Eye. There is no boil at the center, no upwelling shiver, no sound. The water simply arrives, seamlessly, from below, and the pool is somehow always full and never overfull, and the surface is so still it looks solid — a slab of polished sky lying in the wood, until a leaf lands and the rings spread out and die at the far rim, much diminished.

It is clear all the way down. And it goes down. Past the roots, past the reach of the green light, clear past where the light fails, into a throat of pale stone that narrows and turns and continues, by all accounts, indefinitely. Swimmers do not test it. Not because anyone forbids them. The water makes its own argument: at a body's length down, the cold closes around you like a firm hand, and you come back up having changed your mind without noticing the moment you changed it.

## The Collar

The rim of the Eye, and the throat below it, are stone — pale, close-grained, somewhere between limestone and unfired porcelain, and matching nothing in the low country, whose native rock is soft, brown, and apologetic. The collar is dressed stone, or looks dressed: a slight herringbone texture crosses its faces, crisp-edged, sharp enough to feel under a thumbnail, unweathered by water that has been rising through it since before the hedgerows.

There is no seam where the pale stone meets the bedrock. People have dug for it. The collar simply continues downward, and outward, farther than anyone has cared to follow, and the brown country rock laps up against it the way soil laps against the root of a tooth.

The very old keepers say the collar is wider than it was. Not by much. By a finger's width in a lifetime, perhaps — a claim exactly the size of a claim that cannot be checked. They also say the herringbone grain is not toolwork, and when asked what it is instead, they say it is *grain*, and let the word sit there, and change the subject.

## The Green

Nothing grows in the water. Everything grows because of it.

The Eye itself is bare — no weed, no algae, no green shadow, its clarity total, the one clean room in a house otherwise stuffed to the rafters with life. The stream that leaves it runs for a hundred paces over a bed of pale bare stone, glass-clear, cold, silent, refusing all tenants. Then, gradually, it forgets itself. It picks up silt. It picks up watercress and caddis flies and small darting shadows. By the wood's edge it is an ordinary brook, warmish, weedy, chattering over brown pebbles, and it goes out into the fields and joins the low country's rivers and is never spoken of again.

The wood grows richest exactly along that gradient — thickest where the water is just beginning to become ordinary, as if life crowds the shoreline of something it cannot enter, drinking at the very edge of a country it holds no passport to. Moss grows *to* the waterline of the Eye and stops as if scissored. Roots approach the collar and turn aside. The abundance of the Wellwood, the impossible oaks, the ferns like a green sea — all of it lives off the margins of water that will not, itself, be lived in.

Some visitors find this restful. It is the same bareness that unsettles people elsewhere, in a place most of them have never heard of, wearing a garland.

## The Water's Manners

The Eye keeps foreign weather. That is how the keepers put it, and it is precisely true. In the low country's dry late summer, when the brown rivers shrink and the cattle stand in the shade all day, the Eye *rises* — quickens, runs strong, fills its overflow to the brim — obeying, with perfect fidelity, a wet season under some other sky. In the low country's soaking spring it slackens. It answers rains nobody here has felt, on a delay nobody has measured, and it has never once, in all remembered generations, answered the local sky at all.

The taste is the famous thing. Cold, first — the cold arrives before the flavor. Then stone, faintly; then altitude, if altitude can be a taste, a thin bright mineral whiteness; and then something at the very back of it that people reach for different words to describe and generally land near the same one. *Attention.* The water tastes attended-to. It tastes like something that has been held, and considered at great length, and only then released.

And it carries dreams. Drink from the rim at evening — cupped hands only; that is the custom, and nobody remembers deciding on it — and that night, on the edge of sleep, you may see things. The dreams are consistent. That is what makes them worth walking weeks for. Different drinkers, different years, the same country behind the eyelids: a white glare, a horizon of pale stone, a lattice of bright squares repeating to the edge of sight, each square a piece of sky lying flat. Sometimes a single sound, round-bellied, felt as much as heard. Sometimes — rarely, and drinkers who get this one tend to go quiet about it — footsteps. Careful ones. Someone placing their feet softly, a long time ago, in all that light.

Nobody in the low country knows what the place in the dreams is. It is not in their country. Most assume it is not in any country. They call the dreams *the water talking*, and they note — this is the part they find funny, in the dry way of people who live next to a mystery — that the water only ever talks. You cannot tell it anything. People have tried: grievers, mostly, kneeling at the rim, giving the Eye their confessions and their dead. The water receives nothing. It is already full when it arrives. It rises, it says what it has carried, and it empties itself down the stream and out through the wood, sentence after sentence, and it does not take dictation. Whatever the Eye is, it is not a listener.

Except that it is. Once. In one place. We are coming to that.

## Sound

The Wellwood is loud the way the other place is silent — a full-throated green noise, birds in tiers, insects sawing away at the warm hours, the brook chattering at the wood's edge. But the noise is shaped. It thins toward the center. The last hundred paces to the Eye grow quieter tree by tree, not because anything hushes the birds but because, for whatever reason, they do not sit there. At the rim itself there is only the wood's noise heard at a distance, all around, like weather on the far side of a wall — and beneath it, if you are patient, one small sound: a slow, regular arrival, felt in the stone of the collar more than heard, water meeting air from below, steady as a pulse. Because it is one. It is just not the Eye's own.

# The Resident of the Wellwood

## The First Answer

Everything lives here. That is the honest first answer, and it is as useless as the other place's honest first answer, and for the mirrored reason. Take the census and you drown in it: the oaks, the moss, the ferns, the tiered birds, the keepers in their hamlet at the wood's edge with their cupped-hands custom and their curving plow-lines. The Wellwood is nothing but residents. Asking which one *lives* here, in the strong sense — keeps house, attends, works at something — seems like asking which drop is the pool.

But look again at where the life actually is. All of it — every leaf, wing, root, and keeper — lives at the margins. Off the water, near the water, because of the water, leaning toward the water: never in it. The Eye and its throat and its hundred glass-clear paces are, tenancy-wise, exactly as empty as a certain bright shelf of masonry that no one in this country has heard of. The water refuses lodgers here just as it refuses them there. It was never issued an invitation, the visitors to that high place say of life, and they are right, and the refusal travels: the water arrives in the low country still carrying its landlord's policy, and only relaxes it, slowly, over a hundred paces of pale stone, like a servant far from the house.

One invitation, it turns out, was issued. Exactly one.

## The Tenant

There is an eel in the throat of the Eye.

The keepers know it, have always known it, mention it the way you'd mention a hill. Visitors mostly don't believe it until they see it, and most never see it: a paleness, far down, past where the green light gives out and the drowned white glow of the stone takes over — a paleness that turns, unhurried, thick as a strong man's thigh, and is gone back into the narrowing dark. White as the collar. Blind, the keepers say; its eyes went long ago, or were never troubled with. Old past all reason. Eels are given long lives by any measure, but eels are also given an ending — the one journey, downstream to the distant sea, to spawn and finish. This one has declined the journey. Or been excused from it.

It came up the brook as an elver, the keepers say — a live whip of glass no longer than a finger, one spring, an unthinkable time ago — and it swam the hundred bare paces no living thing swims, against water that permits no tenants, and it entered the Eye, and the Eye did not close against it. It went down into the throat and it stayed. They do not say the water *let* it in. They say the water *took* it in, and if you ask what the difference is, they say: the difference is the whole thing, and change the subject.

The keepers call it the Tenant. The name is a small dry joke about the only lease ever granted, and like most small dry jokes in that hamlet, it is also the precise truth.

## What It Does All Day

It drinks. It has no choice; that is the elegance of it. An eel breathes water — takes the water into itself and through itself simply by living — and the Tenant lies in the one channel through which everything the mountain has finished considering must pass on its way back to the world. Every drop that rises through the throat rises over its skin and through its gills. It does not sift the flow or hunt in it. It lies still in the cold white dark, in the narrowest reach of the stone, and the entire output of the longest deliberation in the world washes through its body, drop by drop, on its way to becoming an ordinary brook.

Understand what that means. Far away and long ago — the delay is the point; hold onto the delay — a vast, slow, wet, bright creature takes in the sky as weather and holds it in ten thousand basins and considers it at great length and sends what it has considered down into its own dark, where the channels run in full and do not come out the other side of the mountain. *Where the water goes has never been traced.* It goes here. Under the ranges, through the dark, down the long throat of pale stone that is not a spring but an extremity — the farthest reach of that enormous body, its one opening into a foreign country, the tip of something vast surfacing in a cow-pasture like the point of a tooth. The Eye is a mouth. The dreams are speech. And lying on the tongue of the greatest and slowest speaker there has ever been, breathing the speech directly, before the wood, before the keepers, before the brook wears the meaning off it, is one blind white eel.

It is the first reader of everything the mountain lets go. It is, in the entire world, the only listener the High Shelf has.

The Shelf, remember, cannot be told anything either — the grievers at the Eye learned downstream what the visitors on the masonry never quite articulate: the great creature takes in only sky. It receives weather and gives back thought, and between those two, in the ordinary sense of company, it is perfectly alone, and has been since before the mountains had their current shapes. Except that for some centuries now — a moment, by its clocks; perhaps the most recent thing that has ever happened to it — everything it says has been heard. Whether it knows this is not a question with a graspable answer. But the collar is wider than it was, the old keepers say. The mouth, by a finger's width in a lifetime, is opening.

## The Lag

Here is the arithmetic nobody in either place has done, because nobody stands in both places, or almost nobody.

The water is old when it arrives. It must be — the depth of the ranges, the deliberateness of the flow, the foreign weather on its long delay. What rises in the Eye today entered the sky above the Shelf before the oldest keeper's oldest story. The spring is not the mountain's voice; it is the mountain's *memory of speaking* — sentences finished centuries after they were begun, delivered to a country that never heard the start of them. When the Eye quickens in the low country's drought, it is being faithful to a wet season that ended before the drinkers' grandmothers were born. The two places are one event, held apart by the length of a thought.

One woman knows this. She had been, years before and worlds away, a traveler who slept in a vaulted gallery against advice, and woke to a single round-bellied strike far out across a bright grid, and could not say whether it was stone on stone or a drop falling a long way, and came to understand that there may be no difference worth the name. Years later, gray now, in the low country on other business, she drank from the rim of the Eye at evening because the local people said the water talked. On the edge of sleep she saw the lattice of bright squares — recognized it, sat up in the dark of a hedgerow inn with her heart going — and then lay back down, because the recognition was not the discovery. The discovery came slower, the way everything in this story comes: the water under her tongue that evening had left the high sky *before her birth*. The Shelf she had walked was still in transit. Her own night in the gallery — her breath on the cold air, the careful footsteps she had not decided on, the print of her palm drawn along a weeping wall — all of it was down in the dark of the mountain yet, descending, being considered at the pace such consideration deserves, and would rise in the Eye long after her death.

She did not publish this either. She went to the hamlet and asked the keepers one question: whether there had always been an eel. There has always been an eel, they told her. Not the same one — no one has ever seen a dead Tenant, but no one believes in an immortal one either; some springs, an elver comes up the brook and swims the hundred paces no living thing swims, and is taken in, and after that there is, as before, an eel. A lineage of listeners, replaced from below, each one inheriting the throat and the cold and the whole remaining backlog of a mountain's mind.

She thanked them, and drank once more with cupped hands, and left, and the plow-lines went on curving.

## What You Will Be to It

So follow it out, the little of you the Shelf kept — the breath off the lintel, the damp print of a palm — because keeping, it turns out, was never the end of the sentence. It descends. It is considered. And some spring morning centuries out, in a green wood you never saw, in a country you never crossed, the last of your visit will rise through a throat of pale stone, and pass over the skin and through the gills of a blind white creature that has never seen light, and never seen you, and for a moment — one drop's worth of a moment — the Tenant will dream what the mountain thought of you: a warm, brief, mildly mineral rain that once crossed a high bright place on legs, placing itself carefully.

Then the water will slide out over the collar, and down the hundred bare paces, and begin to forget itself — silt, cress, small darting shadows — and go out through the loud green wood into the brown rivers of the low country, ordinary at last. From there the sun will take it up, as the sun takes everything up, into weather. And weather, given time, goes where weather goes: inland, and upward, over the ranges, to fall as bright cold rain on a horizon of pale stone, where it will land in a still basin and throw its rings out to the far rim — arriving, much diminished, as the next thing to be considered.

The Eye speaks and cannot listen. The Shelf listens only to the sky. Between them, one eel and the weather carry the whole correspondence — the reply going back up as rain, unsigned, drop by drop, for as long as there is water and stone and something patient enough to lie in the dark between them, reading.
