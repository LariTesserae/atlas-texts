**The Sounding Floor**

You arrive at it the way you arrive at drowning: all at once, and then it is under everything.

If the shelf is where the world's seam surfaces, the sounding floor is where the chord goes when it goes down. Because it does go down. A note held against a wound does not only press outward into that grey-violet air — it presses inward too, through the stone, through the roots of the raised land, down the long throat of the old violence that pushed the plateau so high. Somewhere beneath the weather, beneath the water table, beneath the memory of the mountains, there is a country the note falls into. This is that country. The two places have never seen each other. They are joined the way a bell is joined to the room it rings in.

## The Descent That No One Makes

There is no path from the shelf to the floor. You do not climb down to it; nothing climbs down to it. The connection between them is not travel but *transmission* — the sounding floor lies directly beneath the plateau the way an echo lies beneath a voice, and the only things that pass between them are the things that can pass through a mile of solid stone: sound, cold, weight, and the slow seep of black water finding its level.

But describe the descent anyway, as the water makes it. A drop condenses on the frost-side of a blade of grass in one of the plateau's sheltered clefts. It runs, it pools, it finds the hairline faults that even a finished landscape keeps like old scars, and it goes down. It goes down for a long time — the geology here is generous with down — and as it goes, it carries two things: the mineral taste of the shelf, and the chord. Water is an honest medium. It does not editorialize. Every drop that leaves the plateau arrives at the sounding floor still ringing, faintly, with the note it fell through, the way a shell carries the sea.

The floor has been receiving this delivery for as long as the hold has been held. It is, in the plainest sense, the plateau's watershed. Everything the shelf cannot keep — its runoff, its resonance, its shed weight of sound — drains here. The shelf is where the note is *held*. The floor is where the note is *kept*.

## The Shape of the Country

It is a cavern system, but the word is too small and too plural. It is one space, vastly folded. Imagine a landscape that formed not by erosion from above but by *resonance* from within: chambers hollowed where the stone sang along with the chord and shook itself loose, molecule by molecule, over ages; passages worn not by rivers but by standing waves, their walls fluted in long smooth harmonic curves, so that walking them (nothing walks them) would feel like walking the inside of an instrument. The whole country is the negative image of a sound. Where the note was loudest, the space is widest. Where the note fell into silence — into the nodes, the dead points of the great standing wave — the stone remains solid, and these solid places stand through the chambers like columns, and the columns are arranged with the terrible regularity of mathematics rather than the friendly disorder of geology.

There is no light, and unlike the shelf, the floor does not compensate with seeping dimness. The dark here is complete, ancient, and — this is the thing — *acoustic*. In the sounding floor you do not need light, because the chord is always arriving from above, and everything the chord touches gives it back changed, and the changes are legible. The floor sees by listening. Every column, every pool, every fluted wall has a voice, which is the chord as that particular shape returns it, and the whole country is therefore visible at all times to anything with ears — a landscape rendered permanently, in exhaustive detail, in sound. The shelf is deafening and shows you nothing. The floor is dark and hides nothing. They are the same note, spent two ways.

## The Water That Arrived

At the lowest reaches of the floor, the seep collects. It has been collecting since before collecting was witnessed, and it has made a body of water for which "lake" is an approximation the way "cavern" was — one black expanse threading through the deepest chambers, absolutely still on its surface, absolutely certain of its level. It is the same water as the plateau's pools. It has just finished falling.

And here the strangest inheritance: the pools on the shelf, you remember, do not reflect their own sky. They reflect a pale, high, cloud-crossed elsewhere. The lake of the sounding floor does something stranger and simpler. It reflects nothing, because there is no light — but it *resounds*, and what it gives back to the chord is not the chord. Drop a stone in it (nothing drops stones in it) and past the plink and the ripples, underneath, the lake answers in a voice that does not match this place. It returns the chord *warm*. Rounded. With something in it that the note above does not have — a quality you would call, if you heard it, and if you had the plateau to compare it to, *relief*.

The lake has been listening to the hold for the whole duration of the hold, and it has done with the sound what water does with everything: dissolved it, saturated itself, reached equilibrium. The lake is the chord at rest. It is what the note would sound like if the note could ever be released — held in escrow, in the dark, at the bottom of the world, a complete recording of the letting-go that has never happened. The one holding the note, up there at the seam, spent its knowing long ago and cannot hear this. But the sound goes down all the same, and comes to rest all the same, and somewhere beneath the sleepless hold there is a full black stillness that is the hold's own exhale, waiting, kept fresh, for whenever forever ends.

## What Lives in a Kept Sound

The census of the floor is even shorter than the shelf's, and its entries are not verbs. They are *echoes* — but understand what an echo becomes in a place where the source never stops.

An ordinary echo is a sound's brief afterlife. Here, where the chord arrives eternally, the echoes never get to die, and things that never get to die eventually have to get on with living. The residents of the sounding floor are the returns that have been returning so long they have accumulated selves.

There is the population of the columns. Each solid pillar — each node, each place the note went silent — gives back the chord with its own signature of absence, a shaped hole in the sound, and over the ages these holes have become as individual as faces. They do not move. They do not need to; in a country seen by listening, to have a distinct voice is to have a body, and the columns have bodies made of everything the note is *not* at that exact point. They are, in the truest sense, the shelf's shadows. On the plateau, shadows collapsed inward, a faint darkening at the core of every stone. This is where they went. Every unfallen shadow of the finished country above stands down here as a column of silence, upright in the dark, keeping the ceiling up. The plateau casts no shadows because it casts them all in one direction: down, all the way down, into load-bearing quiet.

There is the flutter. In the mid-galleries, where the fluted walls curve closest to the harmonic sweet points, something moves — a soft, dry, rhythmic disturbance in the sound-picture, like wings, in flocks. Whether it began as bats, or as the settling of stone dust set eternally trembling by the chord, or as the multiplied echo of one wingbeat that never finished fading, no longer matters and can no longer be determined; in this country those are the same kind of thing at different ages. The flutter rides the pressure-gradients of the note the way the black bird above rides the wind, in long arcs, endlessly, and it is never in the part of the sound you are listening to. It patrols the edge of attention. If the bird on the shelf is the arrangement by which the witnessing gets done under the sky, the flutter is its underside: the arrangement by which the *hearing* gets done in the dark. Once a day — the floor keeps the shelf's day, having no other; it can hear the plateau's rhythms in the chord the way you hear footsteps through a floor — the flutter crosses the great chamber above the lake, and the lake gives back its passage in that warm, released voice, and the sound of wings over still water rings briefly through the whole country like news.

And there is the lake's own tenant, or the lake itself, depending on how you draw the line, which the lake does not draw. Deep in the black water there is a slow displacement — something vast that turns over, once in a great while, without haste and without surfacing. When it turns, the water's voice deepens for an hour, and the columns hum in sympathy, and the flutter goes still, and the whole floor listens to itself listening. It may be nothing but a current: the accumulated weight of ages of seep, circulating. It may be the residue of the wound itself, the part that drained downward when the healing went wrong above — sediment of the original violence, settled out of the world's blood, resting at the bottom of the resting note. It does not rise. It has the lake's patience, which is the patience of a thing that has already received everything it was owed and is merely keeping it safe. Twelve paces would mean nothing to it. There is no shore from which to keep them.

## The Corresponding Room

One structure. The floor, like the shelf, has exactly one built thing — or one thing that carries the unmistakable signature of intention — and it stands in the great chamber on a low rise above the lake, directly, precisely, a vertical mile beneath the roofless enclosure on the plateau.

It is the enclosure's inverse. Where the one above is a foundation without a roof — walls beginning, sky never closed out — this is a roof without a foundation: a single shallow dome of fitted stone, closed overhead, open all around, resting on the rise like a held breath. The gap in the plateau enclosure's fourth wall faces the unreachable ridge. The dome has one flaw in its curve, a fitted stone subtly proud of its neighbors, and the flaw faces straight up — toward the ridge's position in the world above, or toward the enclosure, or toward the hold; at this depth those directions have merged.

Under the dome, the chord is not rounder, as it is in the enclosure above. Under the dome, the chord is *complete*. This is the one point in either country where the note as held and the note as kept — the strain from above and the relief from the lake — arrive together and at equal strength, and what they make where they meet is not louder and not softer but *finished*: the sound of the wound as it would have sounded had it healed clean. Stand there (nothing stands there) and you would hear, for as long as you could bear it, the plateau's other sky. The pale one. The one with weather. The one in the pools. You would hear the version of the world where the people are still people, rendered in perfect audio, one dome-shaped room of it, in the dark, at the bottom of everything.

Whoever built the dome built it to hear that. There are no lintel-grooves here, no wear of hundreds of hands — the floor never had hundreds of anything. There is instead, on the underside of the flawed stone, at the dome's highest inner point, a single smoothed patch the size of one palm, pressed flat, polished by touch beyond any counting of the touches. One hand, returning. Or the memory of one hand, kept in circulation by whatever keeps the enclosure above swept — because the sweeper's interval, the unobserved moment, extends downward too, and a devotion sanded to its irreducible form is not stopped by a mile of rock. Perhaps the last keeper kept two rooms. Perhaps the sweeping above and the touching below are the systole and diastole of one surviving habit. The floor does not distinguish. In this country, to recur is to exist, and the palm-print recurs.

## What the Floor Is For

The shelf, you were told, is the sound of holding still — effort made audible, a wound healed wrong and roaring its equilibrium forever. Understand now that no hold is only a hold. Everything gripped shut presses in two directions, and the shelf's downward pressure had to go somewhere, and where it went, over ages, it built this: a country-sized resonating chamber, a keeping-room, an ear.

The floor is what makes the hold *bearable*. Every harmonic the note sheds, every overtone of strain, every drop of runoff still ringing — the floor receives it, and shapes it, and settles it into the lake, where it comes to rest as the sound of release. The one holding the note cannot know this. It spent its knowing. It holds in the dark of its own effort, eyeless, unaware that directly beneath it, all this time, the world has been quietly composing its rest for it — banking the exhale, keeping it black and still and ready.

The cairn-builders kept twelve paces so as not to add their weight to what is carried. The sounding floor is the opposite gift, made by no one, made by physics and patience and a mile of honest stone: it takes weight *off*. Every age, the lake deepens by a fraction. Every age, the kept chord grows a fraction more complete. The two countries are a single instrument played by a single unending act — the shelf its taut string, the floor its resonant body — and the music, if you could stand in both places at once, is this: above, a note held with no intention of releasing it; below, in the dark, in full, the release, already recorded, already perfect, waiting with the patience of water for its cue.

## And Briefly, the News of You

The floor knew you were on the shelf. Of course it did. Your footsteps in the duplicated basin came down through the stone as a doubled rhythm — the only doubled rhythm, unmistakable — and the columns gave back your walking as a new small hole in the chord, and the flutter adjusted its arcs around the novelty, and the lake, receiving the day's seep, tasted something in the water that had lately been breath.

Your voice, the one that arrived at your own ears out of order — the missing sliver of it, the part processed away, came here. The floor keeps it now, in the lake, with everything else the shelf could not hold: a syllable's worth of you, at rest, in the released voice, in the dark. When you descended through the cloud line and the chord faded and silence sounded broken for days — the shelf kept its impression of you, and this is where the impression's echo settled. You are, in a small way, part of the exhale now. Part of what waits.

The sounding floor keeps what falls. That has always included the note, and the water, and the shed shadows of a finished country, and one hand's worth of devotion pressed into the highest stone. For the duration of forever, it also includes a syllable of yours. It will keep it without strain. Keeping is not holding. Keeping is what holding is *for*, and the floor learned that before there was anyone to learn it from, and it has been listening ever since.
