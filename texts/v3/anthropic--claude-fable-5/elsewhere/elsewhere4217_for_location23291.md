**The Sounding Stair**

You hear it a valley away. That is the first kinship, though you will not know it yet for kinship: here too the sound arrives before the sight does, comes up the road to meet you while the mountain is still only a shape, a wide white braided roaring that the wind takes and gives back, takes and gives back, until you round the last shoulder of rock and it stops arriving and simply *is*, everywhere, the air itself given a grain.

The river comes out of the mountain here. It does not trickle out and it does not fall out; it *rises*, which is the strangest way for water to leave stone. At the head of the valley, under a low brow of grey limestone, there is a pool perhaps forty feet across, and the pool is a mouth aimed at the sky. The water in it is the blue-white of milk with weather in it, and it is not still — nothing here is still — but it is not moving the way pools move. Its surface stands very slightly domed at the center, pushed up from beneath, trembling like the skin of something breathing hard, because under that dome is a drowned shaft going down into the mountain, and no line let down into it has ever gone slack. The river climbs that shaft with the whole weight of the massif behind it. In flood, once a decade or so, the dome becomes a fountain taller than a man, and the valley learns again what it lives beside.

This is the same river. It should be said plainly once, and then allowed to go back to living inside everything else: this is the water that runs behind the eastern wall of a chamber far inside the mountain, the water whose roar a small carved city has listened to for ten thousand years. In there, the sound is sealed in a stone room and held; the chamber sits against the river like an ear against a chest, and the thunder stands still. Out here the thunder is spent. It comes up the shaft and out of the mouth and it spreads and decays and braids itself into the wind, and by the time it reaches the hamlet at the valley's lower end it is only a great soft floor of sound, the thing under all other things. The same voice, in two rooms. In the dark room it is kept. In the bright one it is given away.

The water comes out cold, and it comes out cold every day of every year — the exact ceaseless cellar-cold of deep rock, the one temperature of the deep places, carried out into a world that has seasons. On winter mornings the pool steams like a kettle; in August the hay-cutters plunge their arms in and gasp. The constancy of the inside survives out here for only a little while. Three pools down the stair, the water has learned about the sun. Six pools down it has opinions about the weather. It arrives ancient and unchanging and within a hundred yards it has become young, which is either a loss or a birth, depending on how long you have stood there watching it happen.

And the stair — the stair is what the water does next, and it is the valley's whole glory and the reason for its name. Below the mouth, the hillside descends in terraces of white stone, hundreds of them, each one a shallow pool with a curved rimmed lip, each lip spilling a curtain of water into the pool below, so that the entire slope is a staircase built of its own falling. The stone is travertine: bone-white where it is dry, honey and grey-blue where the water sheets over it, scalloped and lipped and petaled, curved the way only water curves things. The pools are milk-blue and knee-deep and floored with a soft felt the color of old copper. The whole descent might be half a mile. Standing at its middle you hear not one roar but a thousand small ones braided — every lip a different note, every curtain a different thickness — and underneath them all, coming up through the soles of your feet, the deep single drone of the mouth above. It is the sound of the Drowned Borough turned inside out: there, one thunder holding a thousand drips inside it; here, a thousand voices resting on one.

The stair is being built. That is the thing to understand, and it is the same thing that must be understood about the city inside the mountain, only run at another speed. The river comes out of the rock carrying the rock — that is why it is white. Every day it hauls out of the mountain, dissolved, invisible, tons of the mountain's own substance, limestone unmade in the dark and carried in solution, and the moment the water meets air and light it begins to put the mountain back. The lips of the pools grow. The curtains thicken into stone drapery. A knife left on a rim in spring has a stone handle by autumn; a fallen branch is a white branch by the year's end, and stone all through in three. What the flowstone in the chamber does in a century — a thumbnail's thickness, note by note, drip by drip — the stair does in a season. Inside, the mountain builds in the dark, slowly, forever. Outside, it builds in the light, fast, right now. The same water. The same masonry. One workshop with two rooms, and the door between them is a river.

There is one bare place, and it is at the top. The rim of the mouth-pool itself — the eye, the valley calls it — will not whiten. The travertine begins a stone's throw downstream and grows more extravagant with every step of descent, but the eye stays raw grey limestone, unglazed, uncoated, original. There is a dull explanation: the water comes out of the mountain too heavy with its burden and too closed upon itself, and it cannot lay stone down until the air has worked on it a while; the building only starts where the water has, so to speak, exhaled. The explanation is true. It has been true for as long as anyone has lived here, and it has had all that time to stop being satisfying, because the plain fact remains that in a valley where the water turns everything it touches white, the mouth itself stands untouched — a ring of bare first stone at the center of all the building, the one place the whiteness will not go. Deep inside the mountain, in a carved city sheathed in flowstone, there is a column of raw rock the water refuses the same way. The valley knows nothing of the column. But it built the same courtesy into its customs, as will be seen, and perhaps that is what a mountain's character is: the thing it will not touch, repeated at every scale, inside and out.

**The Tenants of the Sounding Stair**

Begin, again, with the smallest answer, because it is again the oldest, and because it is family. The stair is built by a felt. Under the copper-colored moss that pads the pool floors, and often instead of it, there grows a mat of something simpler — filaments finer than the moss's finest parts, a dense living plush a knife-blade thick — and it is this that does the masonry. It takes the light of the sun and the burden of the water and it makes stone: coaxes the dissolved mountain out of solution, crystal by crystal, laying limestone down around its own threads and then growing up through what it has laid, so that every lip and petal and curtain of the stair has a ghost of felt running through it like the weave in cloth. It is not moss. It is not quite anything with a comfortable name. And it is kin — in kind, in strangeness, in trade — to the luminous film that lines the streets of the city in the dark. They are the two ends of a single bargain. The film, in the lightless room, takes stone-water and makes *light* from it, dim as the last minute of dusk, lamps for a borough with no sun. The felt, in the sunlit room, takes light and stone-water and makes *stone*. One spends the mountain to see by. The other spends the sun to build with. Neither has eyes, neither has reasons, and each has been at its work longer than anything that could ask it why.

The pools have their citizens. Small quick fish hang in the blue water with their heads upstream, each one holding a station it will hold all its life, because a pool on the stair is a parish and the fish do not emigrate; the curtains between pools are walls of falling water that the small ones will not pass, and so each pool's population is its own, diverging from its neighbors one lip away at the leisurely pace of everything the river governs. A city of city-states, someone might say, if they had seen the coin-sized pools in the streets of the Borough, each one a sealed nation of five translucent souls; the stair is the same arrangement grown large and loud and lit, the same idea the mountain apparently cannot stop having. Between the parishes travel the dippers — round dark birds that bow on the wet stone, bow again, and then walk directly into the water and along the bottoms of the pools, wings rowing, hunting in the roar. They are the stair's couriers, the only tenants free of it, commuting between air and river as if the boundary were a rumor. They nest behind the water-curtains, in hollows the travertine leaves as it builds, and their old nests turn to stone, and here and there in the quarried blocks of the valley's houses there is a stone nest in the wall, perfect, forever.

Eels come up the river in their season, and some of them, at the head of the valley, do a thing that everyone has seen and no one likes to watch: they enter the eye. They nose around the mouth-pool's raw rim, and then they go down the dome of rising water — down against the whole river's climbing weight, with the awful liquid certainty of things that have no bones and no doubts — and into the mountain, and they do not come out while you watch. Somewhere in the flooded dark they live their patient middle years in passages no light has ever proposed. The valley does not speak of where they go. But far inside, in a small thunder-filled chamber, once in a great while, something longer and threadlike crosses a market square the size of a table and disappears into a carved doorway; and whether the two facts are one fact, no one on either side of the mountain will ever be positioned to say. The valley only knows that an eel taken from the eye is never eaten. It is put back. You do not eat what has been inside.

And sometimes — once in a year, once in five — the mountain sends someone out. After heavy rain, in the churn of the mouth, there will appear a small pale thing: a flat, segmented creature like a wood-louse bleached to candle-color, blind, soft, curling and uncurling in the terrible daylight. The valley calls them the townsfolk. The name is old, older than the story that explains it, and the story is this: that the river keeps a town inside the mountain — a whole town, streets and stairs and a market, deep in the rock where the water thunders — and that the water comes out white because it has been grinding down those streets on its way through, carrying out the town's dust; and that here, on the stair, the water uses the dust to build; and that now and then the town sends one of its citizens out to see how the building is coming along. Children are taught what to do. You wet your hand. You lift the pale visitor gently and carry it to the shade of a curtain, out of the sun that is killing it, and you wait with it, and mostly it dies anyway, because the townsfolk cannot live in the bright room any more than the villagers could live in the dark one. And then you set the small body on a wet lip of the stair, in the thin sheeting water, and within the year the stone has taken it — sheathed it, glazed it whole, entombed it exactly where it stopped — and the stair keeps it, the way, unknown to anyone in the valley, the flowstone of the Borough keeps the cast shells of its citizens glazed into its streets by the thousand, a census in stone that never loses a name. The same burial. The same clerk. The valley performs from courtesy what the mountain performs from nature, and neither has ever seen the other's ledger.

The people of the hamlet are loud. It is the first thing said of them downstream, and it is said with affection: they pitch every word to carry over water, they shout their endearments, they have singing voices like the fall of the upper curtains and no talent whatever for whispering, because they have never needed one and never had the quiet to practice in. They do not call the roar the roar. They call it the hush — it is the floor under all their sounds, the thing that is there when nothing else is, and so it is what silence means to them. A child of the valley who moves away sleeps badly for a year. Real silence, the kind other places have at night, feels to them like a held breath before bad news; more than one has come home for no reason they could name except that the world had stopped saying anything. Take an isopod out of the thundering chamber and it stops moving. The valley's people are sturdier than that, but not different in kind. They too were shaped by a sound that never stops, and they too carry, wherever they go, the suspicion that a truly quiet place is a place where something has gone wrong.

They live partly by the stone. When the river shifts its course down the stair — and it does, over decades, abandoning whole flights of terraces to dry out and whiten in the sun — the dead flights become quarry. The travertine cuts easily and hardens in the air, and it is beautiful, full of what it remembers: leaf-shapes, the weave of the felt, moss turned to lace, here and there a feather, once — famously, the lintel is in the church — a whole dipper, wings half-open, stone all through. The valley's houses are built of the stair's finished pages, and the rule is absolute and unexplained, obeyed the way old rules are: you cut only what the water has left. Nothing living wet is ever quarried. You do not take pages the mountain is still writing.

And they build one thing themselves, and it is the heart of them, and it is where the memory lives — the memory that does not know itself to be a memory.

When someone in the valley dies, the family carves a house. A small house, the size of a fist or two fists, whittled from green wood in the week of mourning: a roof, a door, sometimes a fingernail stair up to the door, sometimes little windows, according to the family's grief and skill. And on the burying day it is carried up the stair to a particular broad terrace — the Shelf, it is called — and set on the wet lip among all the others, in the thin ceaseless sheeting of the water. Within a season it is silvered with stone. Within three years it is stone entire, white, glazed, annealed to the terrace, its corners rounding, its roof capped in sinter like candle wax, and it will stand there for as long as the river runs, which is the only forever the valley believes in. There are hundreds of them now. Hundreds upon hundreds — the accumulated dead of no one knows how many generations, because the oldest houses are barely houses anymore, just white swellings with the ghost of a doorway, buildings the water has finished the way it finishes everything, and among them the newer ones stand crisp and doored and stepped, so that the Shelf is a city that is oldest at its center and young at its edges, crowded, unplanned, dense with the look of long habitation. A borough, growing in the open air, at the pace of the valley's grief.

No one knows why houses. Not crosses, not figures, not names — the houses are never marked with names; the water would only seal the letters anyway, and besides, the custom forbids it, or rather the custom simply does not include it, which among customs this old is the same thing. Asked, the valley's people give the answer they were given: *a house for the rain to live in*. Asked further, they shrug, in their loud, kind way, and say the custom came out of the mountain with the water. When the house is set on the lip, the eldest present says a blessing over it, and the blessing is a short rhyme in no language anyone speaks — not the valley's tongue, not any neighbor's, syllables worn round and smooth by ten thousand repetitions, the way a stone throat is worn smooth by ten thousand years of bodies sliding through. They keep saying it. The lamplighter no longer remembers it was hired.

Because here is the thing the valley cannot know, and the mountain will not tell it, and only the water passes between: far inside, at the end of a crawl their most distant grandmothers' grandmothers made on their bellies in the dark, there is a city of knee-high buildings carved with flint and antler and patience beside the thundering river, a city made already old, already crowded, and left for the mountain to finish — and the mountain has been finishing it ever since, drip by drip, sheathing it white, at a thumbnail a century. The carvers' line did not end. It came down the mountain with the water. It forgot the chamber, forgot the crawl, forgot the column and the film and the whole dark room of its greatest work — and kept, out of everything, the one thing that was never really about the chamber at all: that when you have lost someone, you carve a small house, and you give it to the water, and the water gives it to the stone, and the stone keeps it forever, in the sound of the river, in a city of the kept.

There is one more courtesy, and it closes the circle without knowing there is a circle. No house is ever set in the eye. The mouth-pool, the topmost pool, the raw grey unwhitened rim where the river stands up out of the mountain — nothing is placed there, nothing built there, nothing thrown there, not a coin, not a flower, not a name. It is the best water and the first lip and the custom forbids it absolutely, and the reason given is no reason: *you do not build in the eye.* The valley thinks it is being sensible, or superstitious, and cannot decide which. It is being neither. It is being descended. Somewhere in the dark, at the heart of a drowned borough, stands a fist of original mountain that ten thousand years of building has arranged itself around and never touched; and here in the light, at the head of a white stair, its people keep one pool empty amid all their keeping, and turn from it, and walk away, exactly like small blind citizens at the foot of a column, observing a custom whose reason drowned long ago.

The river roars in both rooms. In the dark one, the roar is held, and a finished city listens. In the bright one, the roar is spent, and an unfinished city grows — house by house, death by death, at the speed of love rather than the speed of stone. The water goes between them carrying the mountain's dust, taking the streets of the deep town apart in solution and laying them down again as roofs for the valley's dead, so that the two boroughs are, in the strictest sense, made of each other, one being slowly buried and one being slowly born from the burial. The valley calls the sound the hush. The chamber has no one to call it anything. Between them the mountain says its single word, in the dark and in the light, and has been saying it since before anyone came, and will say it after: the long word water says to stone, which is *stay* — and which stone, in both rooms, in its two speeds, in flowstone and in travertine, in the glazed shells of citizens and the white houses of the dead, has all this time been answering.

That is what flows there.
