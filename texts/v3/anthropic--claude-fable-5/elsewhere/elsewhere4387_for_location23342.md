**The Eye**

Two days down the ridge — down past the peregrines' cliff, down through the boulder fields and the last of the hard bare rock, to where the mountain finally consents to soil and the first willow scrub takes hold — the ground opens into a bowl. It is not a dramatic place. A shallow amphitheater of dark stone at the massif's southern foot, a place a walker would cross in ten minutes, except that no one who reaches it crosses it in ten minutes, because in the middle of the bowl there is a pool, and the pool is the wrong color.

It is thirty paces across and it is green-blue in the way of things that are lit from inside — not the grey of mountain tarns, not the milk of glacier melt. It sits in a ring of terraces the color of dried blood: shelves and lips and aprons of stone stained rust and apricot and deep ochre-red, stepping down into the water like the seats of a small ruined theater. Steam stands over it on cold mornings. Lean over the rim and the air coming off the surface is warm — not hot, warm like the inside of a sleeve, like skin — and if you have ever stood in the slot near the summit and felt the mountain exhale, you will recognize the temperature exactly, because it is the same warmth. The Roost and the Eye are two mouths of one throat.

This is the plumbing of it, as near as anything can know: snow falls on the blade of the ridge, and the ridge is not solid — it is a mass of ancient fracture planes, the same flaws that opened the chapel-room near the summit. The meltwater sinks. It goes down through kilometers of broken gneiss, into the deep settling heat of the massif, and there it turns — warmed, mineralized, iron-rich — and works its way back toward daylight along the fractures, and comes out here, at the mountain's foot, welling up through a vent in the floor of the pool in a slow, ceaseless, upside-down rain. No one knows how long the journey takes. Decades, at least; perhaps centuries. The water rising into the pool this morning may have fallen as a storm on the ridge before the oldest swift in the Roost was hatched — may, in fact, be the same rain the threshold stone drank and forgot. The Eye is always weeping old rain. The mountain breathes through the room at its crown and bleeds through the pool at its foot, and both the breath and the blood are warm, and both are patient.

The warmth is the pool's whole character. It never freezes. In the deep of winter, when everything else in this country is white and locked and silent for a day's travel in every direction, the Eye stays open — a single lens of dark water in the snow, steaming, ringed by a collar of ice-flowers where its own vapor freezes onto the willow twigs. From the high ground it is the only interruption in the whiteness, and it is impossible, seeing it then, not to understand the name it insists on: the mountain lies with its face to the sky all winter and does not blink.

And it reflects. This is the pool's second character, the one the Roost — lightless, inward, sealed — can never share. On still evenings the Eye holds the whole southern face of the massif upside down in its skin, the ridge-blade and the shadow-seamed wall below it, and at the changeover hour, if the air is clear, you can watch a thin smoke rise from one particular dark seam in the reflected rock: the colony pouring out of the slot, twenty thousand birds rendered as a wisp on the water. The only place on earth where the Roost can be seen doing what it does — breathing out its ceiling into the sky — is on the surface of the Eye, three thousand meters below. The pool watches the room. The room does not know.

The terraces are the pool's own slow work, and they run on the same clock as everything the mountain builds. The rising water carries dissolved iron and lime, and as it cools and touches air it lets them go, a film at a time, and the film hardens, and the terraces fatten — a fingernail's width a year, the pace of the guano cone, the pace of the flowstone. Everything that rests too long at the pool's rim is absorbed. Willow leaves, turned to red stone leaf by leaf. The wing-bone of a duck that died here some cold February, now a white seam in an orange shelf. And on one low apron on the eastern rim, half-sealed under later layers, a line of human footprints — bare feet, walking toward the water, pressed into the terrace when it was soft mud and mineralized where they stood. They are old in the same way the handprints in the Roost are old. They may be old in exactly the same way, for a reason the terraces themselves supply: the red of those three hands on the flowstone, the ochre someone carried up the mountain in a pouch after days of climbing, is this. The terraces are ochre. The pigment inside the mountain's highest dark room was dug from the rim of the pool at its foot. Whoever pressed hands to the wall up there had first knelt down here, at the water, and filled a bag with the Eye's own rust — and the Eye, in return, has kept their feet. The mountain holds the same person in two archives, three vertical kilometers apart: hands going into the stone at the top, footprints going into the stone at the bottom, both closing over at a fingernail's width a year.

The outflow leaves the pool at its cool western lip as a stream you could step across, and drops at once into a narrow green gorge — the one truly lush place on this whole side of the mountain, walled with moss and dripping and dense with ferns, fed forever by water that arrives already warm and never lets the gorge quite freeze. It is a pocket of lowland climate embedded in the heights, and it is, almost certainly, the homeland of the one crooked fern that grows inside the slot of the Roost: a spore from this gorge, lofted on the mountain's flank-wind or carried on a bird's wet foot, that landed in the only other place on the massif warm and damp enough to receive it. The loneliest plant on the mountain is a colonist from here. The gorge is full of its family, and does not miss it.

**Who Lives in the Eye**

Begin with the color, because the color is alive.

The rust of the terraces is not a stain; it is a nation's architecture. The pool's oldest residents are mats of microbes — sheets and streamers and cushions of them, orange and olive and blood-red, banded by temperature across the terraces and the shallows — and every particle of the red stone was placed by them, iron gathered out of the rising water and laid down cell by cell for longer than the pool has had a shape. They are close kin — the same deep lineage — as the pale, faintly glowing film on the wet flowstone of the Roost. One family, two houses. Up there, in the dark, the family lives blind and slow, eating the raw chemistry of the mountain's breath, and glows with the little it can spare. Down here, in the open, the same family lives in full sun, drinking light all day, gaudy as spilled paint, building red stone at industrial speed by its own standards. If every wing in the Roost fell silent and every fin in the Eye went still, this family would notice nothing and continue in both houses. They are the landlord's people, keeping both doors of the same building. And it is their work — their laid-down iron, their patient red — that someone once ground to powder, carried up three vertical kilometers, and pressed onto a cave wall in the shape of hands. The Eye's oldest citizens are sealed inside the Roost's oldest wall. The two places have already exchanged relics, and neither will ever give them back.

The mats define the pool's geography, which is a geography of rings. Where the Roost is stacked — ceiling, wall, floor, a vertical column of nations — the Eye is concentric. At the center, over the vent, is the hot ring, where nothing lives but the microbes themselves, a zone of pure landlord. Around it, the warm shallows, and here are the snails: hot-spring snails smaller than barley grains, grazing the mats in their millions, each temperature band holding its own kind, and at least one kind — a small dark whorl with a lip like a folded ear — that exists nowhere else on earth. Every individual of that species that is, was, or will be lives inside a ring of water a few paces wide. They are the pool's answer to the beetles of the guano cone: a whole species pocketed inside a single room. The difference is the ceiling. The beetles' universe is sealed dark and has no rumor of a sky. The snails' universe has nothing *but* sky — it stands open to the whole turning weather of the world, hawks and stars and snow crossing over it — and the snails, eyes barely worth the name, register none of it. Two nations, each complete, each twenty steps across; one blind in the dark, one blind in the light.

Then the cool outer ring, the pool's deep western half, and the fish.

They are char — or they were char, ten thousand years ago, when the ice sheet fell back and stranded a run of them here with no river left to return by. The pool kept them. They have been alone with it ever since, and it has remade them the way the dark remade the beetles: dwarfed them to the length of a hand, silvered them pale, tuned them absolutely to the one place. They hold in the cool water in loose ranks, all facing the same way, and they live with their eyes turned upward, because everything they eat arrives from above — midges touching down, moths that misjudge the steam, the rain of small lives onto the sky's underside. They are the mirror of the guano floor: down there, a nation eats what falls from a ceiling of birds; down here, a nation eats what falls onto a ceiling of light. The fish spend their whole lives watching the surface, which means they spend their whole lives watching the sky, which means — on still evenings — they are the only residents of either place who have seen the Roost empty itself, that far-off wisp rising from the reflected ridge, drifting across their ceiling like weather.

The midges are the pool's currency and its principal export. They breed in the warm shallows in numbers that defeat counting, generation stacked on generation, and because the water never chills they fly in every month of the year — on windless January days the Eye stands in its bowl of snow wearing a faint grey halo of the only insects airborne for a hundred kilometers. In summer the halo becomes a column, and at dusk the column is harvested from above: the swifts come down. It is the one hour when the two places' citizens mix. The colony that will spend the night hanging in the hot dark near the summit descends the whole face of the mountain in minutes, and skims the Eye to drink — touching the water at full speed, a chip of light off each keel — and hawks through the midge column, filling their throats with the pool's abundance before climbing home to the slot. The insects the Roost digests in darkness were grown in the Eye's warm rings. The floor of one place is built, in real part, out of the shallows of the other; there are strata deep in the guano cone that are, molecule by molecule, former pool. And at night the traffic continues without the birds: moths bred in the willow scrub ride the warm updraft that flows up the mountain's flank — the Eye's own heat, climbing — and some of them ride it all the way to a slot near the summit, where a single orb-weaver's web is strung across the upper corner to receive them. The spider's tax is paid in the Eye's coin. It has never once been paid in anything else.

The pool keeps its own hunters. In summer the dragonflies hold it — big hawkers patrolling fixed beats above the rings, taking midges out of the air with the same economy the peregrines use on the swifts, the pool's falcons, each defending a territory of warm air the way the falcons defend a kilometer of cliff. And year-round, working the outflow stream and the green gorge below it, there is the dipper: a round sooty bird that made, long ago, precisely the opposite bargain the swifts made. The swifts gave up the ground and kept the air; the dipper gave up the air's freedom and took the water — it walks on the stream bed, fully submerged, gripping the stones with its feet, flying through the current with half-opened wings, hunting the larvae of the very insects that will one day rise to feed the birds above. It winters at the Eye because the Eye is the only open water, bobbing on the rim ice, dropping into the warm stream while the world freezes around it. One family of birds folded itself into the sky and must come to a dark room to touch the earth; another folded itself into a stream and must come to this pool to survive the cold. The mountain accommodates both extremities, one at each end of itself.

In winter the Eye becomes a commons, which is the deepest difference between the two places. The Roost admits almost nothing: three nations, an ancient treaty, a door the width of outstretched arms, and the world kept out. The Eye, in the hard months, admits everyone. The deer come down at dawn to drink at the only water, and stand steaming in the pool's steam. Ptarmigan crowd the snow-free rim. A fox works the margins for whatever the cold has weakened. Ravens attend. Tracks of a dozen species crosshatch the snow around the bowl, converging on the warmth like spokes on a hub, and for the length of a drink an approximate truce holds, the way it holds at water everywhere. Two policies of one animal: the mountain keeps its heart locked and its eye open.

And the Eye, too, keeps an absence. Near the footprints on the eastern terrace, under a skin of later sinter, there is a hearth — fire-cracked stones in a ring, and a scatter of worked flakes that match, in material and hand, the flakes on the dry ledge beside the handprints far above. This is where the climb began. Someone camped at the pool, knapped their kit by this fire, knelt at the rim and ground the red terrace-stone to powder and filled a pouch, and walked into the mountain's face for days, carrying the Eye's color up to the mountain's mouth — for the birds, or the guano, or the warm breath, or to press the outside of the animal onto its inside, for reasons that left no residue but red. Whether they ever came back down to this fire, the terraces do not say. They only continue. The sinter is closing over the footprints at a fingernail's width a year, the same rate at which, three kilometers overhead, the flowstone is closing over the first of the hands, and somewhere in the coming few thousand years the two seals will finish in the same geological moment — feet inside the pool's stone, hands inside the mountain's — and the person will have achieved the only permanent residency either place confers: absorption, in duplicate.

So: who lives in the Eye. A family of mats older than the pool's own shape; the ringed nations of snails; a stranded people of upward-watching fish; the midge column and its falcons; the counter-swift walking under water; the winter commons; the ghost at the hearth. But stand at the rim on a still summer evening, when the steam leans and the reflected ridge darkens and the far wisp of the colony rises off the water's skin, and the roster dissolves the same way it does in the dark of the room above. The mats are how the pool remembers; the fish are how it watches; the midges are how it pays; the warmth welling through the vent is the same blood that warms the chapel at the summit, arriving at the other end of the body.

Because they are one animal — that is the plain fact under all of it. The fractures are its veins, the deep heat is its metabolism, and the two openings are its two oldest organs: near the crown, a warm dark room where it keeps its heart, breathing birds in and out twice a day; at the foot, a warm bright pool where it keeps the sky, unblinking through every winter. And threading between them, every summer evening, run the swifts — down the whole face of the mountain to drink, and back up into the dark to sleep — the living nerve between the heart and the eye. Every one of them was born blind in the room at the top. Every one of them, on the evening it first let go of the wall and fell out of the slot into a sky with no edges, looked down through its first light at a world it had taken entirely on faith — and found, far below in the shadow of the massif, one small green-blue disc already shining, already holding the ridge and the wisp of its own kin on its surface.

The first thing a swift ever sees of the world is the Eye. The Eye has been watching for it all along.
