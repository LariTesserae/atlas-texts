**The Fan of the Maren**

There were mountains here too. The same ones.

That is the fact to hold onto, because nothing about this country will volunteer it. The Fan lies three miles below the Tableland and a hundred miles east of it, spread flat under a huge wet sky, and there is not a stone in it bigger than a fist. But drill down — the geologists did, generations back, the same expedition that climbed the Tableland and pronounced its rock finished — and you find silt under the silt, sand under the sand, gravel under that, layer on layer on layer, miles of it, going down and down past the reach of any auger. The whole of the missing height of the mountains, accounted for. The peaks the Tableland remembers did not vanish. They came here. Grain by grain, over an age no one can hold in the mind, everything sharp that the high country ever had was carried down the old rivers and *laid out*, in a vast half-circle of new land opening toward a shallow grey sea.

The geologists announced this as a discovery. The people of the Fan listened politely. They already knew. Their oldest saying, the one their children learn before they learn to swim, is: *the mountains lay down and walked to the sea.* They live on the walking.

**The braid**

The river is the Maren, and calling it "the river" is the first mistake every visitor makes, because it is not one river. Somewhere up in the broken, spent country below the Tableland's eastern rim — a hundred miles of exhausted slope the Fan people call the Shed, where nobody lives and the trickles gather — the Maren is briefly a single channel. Its very first water, the pilots will tell you, comes off the tailraces of the five high towns: cistern-water that has already turned a wheel, already burned in a lamp. They toast with the phrase. *To water that's worked.*

By the time it reaches the Fan proper, the Maren has given up on singularity. It splits, rejoins, splits again — a braid of channels, some a mile wide and some you could step over, weaving through the flat green country and never twice the same. Channels silt closed in a season. Channels open overnight. Islands are born, grow reeds, grow willows, grow barley, and are eaten. The locals call the whole moving system *the braid*, the way the highlanders call their moss *the pile*, and they navigate it the way you'd navigate a crowd: by paying attention, continuously, to something that is paying no attention to you.

The braid is never still. This is the thing visitors take longest to accept, and it is the exact same thing, inverted. On the Tableland the ground is finished and the green moves. Here the green is incidental and the *ground itself* is in motion — slowly on a calm day, alarmingly in flood, but always, and everyone knows it, and nobody builds as if it weren't so.

**The fresh**

What the Maren carries and lays down is called the fresh: silt ground so fine it pours like smoke and packs like flour. It is the flesh of the old peaks milled to powder, every mineral the mountains ever held, and things grow in it with a violence that unsettles people from ordinary country. Push a willow stick into a new bank in spring and stand back. The shot-wood — the fast poplars and willows of the braid — goes from cutting to full timber in a decade, and rarely gets much older, because the flood that will take it is already on the calendar somewhere. The reed-seas run for miles, taller than a mounted rider, hissing.

It is the opposite green from the high green. On the Tableland nothing stands above the knee and everything is centuries old. On the Fan everything is vertical, glassy, bright, loud with birds, and almost nothing is older than a grandmother. Height is cheap here and age is impossible, which is precisely the reverse of the arrangement three miles up, and the two greens are made of the same rock and the same water and would not recognize each other.

The shot-wood is worth pausing on, because some of it goes up. The plank-ways of Vell — the raised walks the highlanders keep to so courteously, sparing the pile their boots — are Fan timber, every board of them, hauled up the Stair on porters' backs and on the backs of trough-sheep. The Tableland has no trees. The Vellish, who will not step on their own green, walk everywhere on the Fan's.

**Water, on its way back**

At dawn the Fan smokes. The whole country exhales — miles of channel and marsh and wet fresh giving up the night's warmth — and the fog lifts off the braid in slow towers and leans west, climbing, joining the great wind-river that runs at altitude toward the high country. The Fan people watch it go without ceremony. They know where it goes. Three days on, more or less, that breath arrives over the Tableland as the cloud-wall, and the mesh combs it, and the cisterns fill, and the lamps of Vell brighten. *We breathe and the bone country drinks*, the pilots say, in the flat mild tone they use for the tide.

What Vell does not take rains out over the Slack — the shallow sea at the Fan's edge — or comes back down the Shed as river, and around it goes again. The two places are two organs of one circulation: the Fan sends its water up as sky, the Tableland sends it back down as river, and each thinks of itself as the beginning.

There is one thing the Fan does not generally know about its own rain. The Vellish speak the names of their dead into the cloud-wall as it passes. The cloud continues east. It rains on the braid. A few Osseli — travelers who went up the Stair and came back down changed in small ways — stand outside when it rains, hatless, and will not say why.

**Ossel and its moorings**

Ossel is the largest town on the Fan, and it is not a place. It is a fleet.

Two hundred hulls and rafts, moored gunwale to gunwale in whatever channel is currently deep, slow, and likely to last — houses on barges, workshops on pontoons, the chart-house on the great flat-bottomed hulk at the center of everything, walkways of shot-wood lashed between. When the braid shifts, Ossel shifts. The town has moved wholesale eleven times in living memory, warping down its channel over a week of shouting and song, and its position is not a secret or a shame but simply a fact with a date on it, recorded in the chart-house like weather. The name belongs to the people, not the ground. Ossel is wherever Ossel is moored.

Where Vell is built down into finished rock and furred with moss until it vanishes into its own hillside, Ossel is built to *float and be found*. The hulls are painted — hard reds, yolk-yellows, a blue that has no business existing in nature — because on the Fan everything moves, and a thing that cannot be seen from a distance is a thing that can be lost. Vell hides in its land because its land can be trusted with it. Ossel signals across its land because its land cannot. Both towns are being exactly as sensible as their ground allows.

**Loud, mostly**

Open water gives sound back with interest, and the Fan is the loudest quiet country imaginable. Voices carry half a mile down a channel. The Osseli hail each other across the water as a matter of course and of pleasure — there are hailing songs, channel by channel, older than any island — and a pilot bringing a barge through a reach at night steers partly by ear, because every channel has a voice: the deep ones mutter, the shoaling ones chatter, the dying ones go silty and dumb. The pilots know the braid's channels by sound the way the framewrights of Vell know their condenser frames by note, and neither trade has ever heard of the other's method, and both would recognize it instantly.

Under everything, in flood season, is the roar: the whole braid running brown and urgent, the sound of the mountains still moving. The Osseli sleep through it. It is, they say, the sound of the floor being laid.

**What lives here, which is nearly everything**

Where the Tableland's animals are few enough to be famous, the Fan's are numberless — fish runs that turn whole channels silver for a week, eels, otters, herons in gawky thousands, midges in weather-systems of their own. No one on the Fan knows the animals individually. There are too many. Abundance makes its own kind of anonymity.

With one exception. Each autumn, dropping down the sky out of the west, come the long dark birds the Fan calls *stairbirds* — because they climb the sky the way porters climb the Stair — and there are about forty of them, and they winter and breed in a warren of reed islands the Osseli call the Nursery and do not cut, and every one of them is known. Named, sketched, argued over, waited for. In spring they ride the dawn fog up and are gone west along the wind-river, and the Fan counts them out; in autumn the Fan counts them home.

Three miles up, these are the gleaners, riding the cloud-wall in to pick droplets off the condenser mesh, and the dynamo hall of Vell keeps its ledger of them nine bird-generations deep. Two ledgers, two sets of names, one forty birds. Once a year, at the hamlet called the Landing where the Stair meets the Fan, a framewright comes down and a chartist comes up-channel and the ledgers are reconciled — the Counting, the one rite the two peoples fully share. Most years the number agrees and there is a feast. In the years it doesn't, the news travels both directions at once, and a name is read into the cloud in Vell, and a name is cut into a driftwood post in Ossel and given to the river, and the same bird is mourned twice, three miles apart, in two languages, in the two ways.

**The strangeness, plainly stated**

It is this: the Fan is *unfinished*, and everything on it knows — and more than unfinished, it is *made entirely of somewhere else*. Every handful of the ground was once a peak. The country has no substance of its own; it is the high country's substance, relocated, and it is still arriving — the Maren delivers more of the mountains every day, a barge-load an hour, forever.

On the Tableland, geological time stopped and biological time kept going in the calm. Here geological time never stopped; it changed jobs, from tearing down to laying down, and in changing jobs it changed *speed* — land is born here in a season, in a flood, overnight, at the pace of grass and children, so that on the Fan the two kinds of time run together in the same water and cannot be told apart. An island is a geological event and a barley crop and somebody's childhood, simultaneously, and then it is a memory and a layer.

The Osseli have a phrase for the braid, the fresh, the fog going up, the fleet, all of it together. They say the Fan is *still arriving*. They mean it as a description. Spend a season in the painted town, learning the hailing songs, watching new ground appear where there was water, and you begin to suspect they also mean it as an ambition.

**The Osseli**

Begin with the body, because the Fan did.

Where the altitude built the Vellish deep-chested and low and hard to knock over, the braid built the Osseli long, light, and impossible to knock *in*. They are a tall, narrow, quick people with the balance of cats and the feet of herons, at ease on a wet gunwale in the dark, incapable of true stillness — an Osseli standing "still" is making forty small corrections a minute and does not know it, because that is simply how standing goes when the floor has never once held still in your life. Their pulses are quick, their sleep comes in short takes (the river changes at night, and someone in every household half-listens for it), and their hands, like Vellish hands, are large and sure — but where the highland hand is slow and exact, the Fan hand is fast and provisional, the hand of splicers, netters, polers, people whose materials will not wait.

They are brown and weathered under an open sky the Vellish would find merciless, and they squint amiably at everything, and in the amber galleries of Vell — a few of them get there, every generation — they are described as looking slightly wrong, like gulls indoors.

**How they sound**

Water gives voices back, and the language grew to fill the room it was given. Osseli speech is quick, carrying, consonant-edged — nothing worn off it, everything still sharp, because here a word has to survive a hundred yards of open channel and arrive intact. They stand far apart and talk loudly and consider this warmth. The Vellish habit of standing inside your arm's reach and murmuring strikes them as vaguely alarming, like being ambushed with tenderness.

Their names run long — Averane, Corusselle, Ivenne, Tavrian — and are immediately cut short in use, to Ava and Coss and Ive and Tav, the long form kept for the river, for name-posts, for the moments that need a person's full length. The river itself, the Maren, gave its name up the Stair generations ago; the Vellish wore it round, as they wear everything round, and give it now to daughters. There are old women in the stone galleries of Vell named for the water that carried their town's floorboards. Neither people finds this strange. Both find it right.

**The work and the offices**

Ossel organizes itself around the one thing that cannot be organized, and its respected people are the ones who read it.

The **pilots** are the aristocracy, insofar as the Fan has one: readers of water-color and water-voice and the smell of a shoaling reach, keepers of the braid in their heads, where alone it can be kept, because —

— the **chartists** will tell you, cheerfully, that every chart of the braid is wrong by the time the ink dries. They draw them anyway, dated, exact, doomed, and file them in the chart-house barge, and the stack of dead charts going back four hundred years is the only geology the Fan possesses: a record not of what the land is but of everywhere it has been. This is the deep inversion between the two peoples, and it is worth stating plainly. The Tableland keeps records in its ground — the pile remembers every footstep, the rock remembers everything — so the Vellish may keep almost none, mark no graves, write little. The Fan's ground remembers *nothing*; the river erases as it delivers; and so the Osseli are the great record-keepers, the daters and namers and ledger-folk, holding in ink and memory everything their country refuses to hold in form. The pile remembers, so the Vellish may forget. The river forgets, so the Osseli must remember.

The **freshcroppers** farm the new islands, knowing them doomed — three harvests from a good island, five from a great one, then the braid takes it back. Farming here is frankly a wager, and the freshcroppers talk like gamblers, and the grain they win goes up the Stair by the porter-load to feed the five high towns, along with salt, iron, fish-oil, and the shot-wood planking that Vell walks on. Down the Stair comes wool — cloth the exact grey-green of the Tableland, which the Osseli, on receipt, dye red.

And the **stiltwrights** and **splicers** and the **Stair-porters**, and, quietly, the **name-carvers**, whose office comes last because it comes last.

**Manners, and the river**

The Osseli walk anywhere. Land is not courtesy's object here — land is temporary, borrowed, nobody's. What is sacred is the *line*: the mooring, the knot, the thing that holds a thing against the current. A child's moral education is a mooring line, given young, checked nightly, and the first commandment of the Fan is this: *nothing goes to the river unmeant.* The river takes everything eventually — your island, your boat, your bank, you — and so everything handed to it must be handed on purpose, with attention, or you incur what is called, without humor, a *current-debt*, and you will stand night watches until the town forgets, which — this being the town that remembers everything — takes a while.

The dead are given on purpose. An Osseli is laid into a going bank — a bank the river is actively eating — during the run, when the channels are silver, and within a season the Maren has taken the body and laid it down somewhere new, as fresh, as ground, as somebody's future barley island. *It is what the river did for the mountains*, the name-carvers say. *It will do it for us.* And because the river keeps no record, the record is launched after: the full long name cut deep into a driftwood post and given to the current. Name-posts travel. Finding one aground on a far island is a serious thing; you read it aloud, once, and relaunch it. Some names have been in circulation for two hundred years. The braid is full of the dead, going somewhere, becoming ground, still legible.

Up on the Tableland they speak their dead into the water going by in the air. Down here they carve their dead into the wood going by in the water. Neither town taught the other. The water taught both.

**The one that stayed**

The Fan's children are told it early: when the mountains agreed, all together, to lie down and walk to the sea, one refused. It is still up there. It keeps its shape under the green and moves only at night, a little, still making up its mind. The Osseli call it Old Stay-Behind, fondly, the way you'd speak of a stubborn great-uncle, and the porters who have been up the Stair report that the highlanders will not speak of it at all — will not name it, tilt their heads toward the western swells and change the subject — and the Osseli find this hilarious, and then, in a part of themselves they don't examine, correct. Down here a name is a light thing; the river takes names away regardless, so you may hand them out freely. Up there, on ground that keeps everything, a name would *last*. Some courtesies are about what your country does with what you say in it.

So the Fan names it and the Tableland doesn't, and both are being gentle with the same unfinished thing, and if you put the two customs side by side — which only the Landing folk, half of each people, ever really do — they describe between them a single belief that neither town has ever stated: that the last of the mountains is entitled to arrive in its own time, and that the two countries, the one that is its bones and the one that is its body, will keep its privacy between them until it does.

**Those who go up, and those who come down**

Every generation the Fan loses a few to the bone country. They are the ones who went up the Stair with a porter-load and did not come back down — recognizable, the Osseli claim, for years beforehand: people who had begun standing still on purpose. Ossel speaks of them as *gone to anchor* — gently, as of people who got tired — which from Ossel is not a compliment.

And every generation a few Vellish come down and stay: the ones the lowlands hurt in some unlocatable organ, who found even the Fan loud at first, and unfinished, which it is, and who then, standing on a gunwale one dawn watching the country smoke and lift and lean west toward their own town's lamps, stopped flinching. Ossel takes them in without ceremony, gives them a line to hold, and waits. It usually works. The town has watched ground be born from open water; it can wait for a person to be.

Most of the hinge-people settle at the Landing, at the Stair's foot, half of each blood, keepers of the Counting, fluent in murmur and in hail. They are the only people in the world who hold both phrases at once and will tell you, if pressed, that they are the same phrase.

**Their next shape**

So: who lives on the Fan of the Maren?

A people who took an unfinished landscape as instruction. Their highest word is *underway* — said of a marriage in its strength, of a craftsman at her height, of a channel running true: *it's underway* — and there is no higher word in the dialect. They prize the provisional the way the Vellish prize the worn: the knot meant to be untied, the chart meant to be superseded, the island meant to be barley and then memory. Their old people move, raft by raft, toward the fleet's downstream end, nearest the current, where the water's going can be heard through the hull all night, and they listen to it without fear, as one listens to a great companion still at a long task. At the end they ask to be carried out during the run, into the silver water, to a going bank.

They are not rootless. This is the misreading every visitor makes first and abandons last. The Osseli are the most placed people imaginable; it is only that their place is a motion, not a spot — the way a current is a fact although no drop of it stays. They have simply stopped confusing permanence with being home.

And between the two towns, the mountains — the old, tall, vanished mountains — are still entirely present, and still one thing. What stayed lives quietly in its last shape, three miles up, under the moss and the amber lamps. What left lives loudly in its next, down in the braid, under the painted hulls and the going water. The fog climbs west every dawn and three days later the lamps of Vell brighten with it; the tailrace trickles east and a year later it is an island with barley on it; the forty dark birds cross between, counted at both ends, named at both ends, belonging to neither, and are very nearly the whole of the correspondence — that, and the rain over Ossel, which has been spoken into, and the planks under Vell, which grew in three summers on ground that did not exist when its walkers' grandmothers were young.

The Osseli say the Fan is still arriving. The Vellish say the Tableland is finished. Up at the Landing, where the Stair meets the braid and the two dialects pool, the hinge-people have worn the two sayings together into one, and offer it to travelers going either direction, as a description and as an ambition:

*Everything here is the mountains, still. Some of it has stopped. Some of it is on its way. All of it is alive.*
