**The Dissolving Hills**

They stand on the far side of the divide, on the wet side, where all the weather that never reaches the town is spent. You come up out of the dry country by a long saddle of dark rock, and somewhere near the crest the air changes its mind: a first breath of damp, then cloud sliding through the pass at shoulder height, then the rain — and the rain, once it begins, does not end. Not storm. Nothing so dramatic. A fine, patient, permanent rain, warm as breath, falling out of a sky the same pewter as the town's sky, as if the two places share one ceiling and only the floor beneath it differs.

Below the crest, the hills.

They are white — the same gypsum-white shading to ash-grey, and you know it at once if you have stood in the town, the way you know a voice you have only ever heard whispering when at last you hear it speak. But nothing here is sharp. That has to be said plainly, because it is the whole character of the place. There is not one crisp corner in the entire country. Every ridge is rounded like a knuckle, every slope is fluted with runnels the width of a finger, thousands of them, combed downhill by centuries of rain. The stone looks kneaded. It looks licked. Domes and saddles and swags of white, going back into the rain-haze for miles, and everywhere on their surfaces the water moving — not in streams, exactly, but in sheets and threads and slow silver films, so that the hills seem always to be shining slightly, always in the act of giving something up.

And they are loud. That is the second thing, after the softness. The town's silence has an opposite, and this is it: the world-filling, texture-of-everything sound of water on stone — hiss on the flats, trickle in the runnels, a deep occasional swallowing gulp from below. You stop hearing it within an hour and then you are hearing it in your sleep for years.

The water is milk-white. Cup it in your hands and it is faintly cloudy, faintly sweet-smelling, tasting of chalk and struck flint — the smell of the town's empty rooms, dissolved. Because that is what the rain is doing here, hour on hour, generation on generation: it is drinking the hills. The stone gives itself to the water at the pace of bone healing, a finger's width in a season on the exposed domes, faster in the runnels, faster still in the throats. And here is the fact on which everything else in this account depends: *the water does not leave*. There are no rivers running out of the Dissolving Hills. Not one. Every thread and sheet and film runs downhill only far enough to find a swallow-hole — the hills are pocked with them, round dark mouths fringed with the smoothest stone in the country — and goes down, milk-white, laden, into the dark under the divide. Nothing leaves the hills by daylight. The whole massif is being carried away underground, in solution, patiently, in one direction.

The direction is the dry country. The direction is a shallow bowl of land on the rainless side, where cold ground meets a saturated current and asks the stone, after its decades in the dark, to become stone again.

The hills run warm. The town runs cold. Whoever notices both, as one visitor has, notices also that the warmth here is the same size as the chill there — that the unmaking gives off what the making draws in, as if the two places were the two ends of a single transaction and the dry country between them merely the length of the table. The hills' people have a saying that means, roughly, *what the water takes, the water owes*. They do not know about the town. One of them does.

Because the hills, unlike the town, are inhabited — and their residents have all had to solve the town's question turned inside out. Not how to live at the speed of a place that is never finished, but how to live in a place that is always giving itself away. Each has answered differently.

Begin, as before, small: with the eater. On any surface where water lingers grows a film of grey-green, soft as felted paper, and where it grows the stone melts at triple speed — the eater etches, secretes, hurries the rain's work along and drinks the milk it makes. It is the country's editor, deciding which curves deepen into hollows and which hollows into caves. And it is — this is written in a notebook kept in a waxed box, pressed specimens side by side — lobed exactly like the rosettes of the town. Ring within ring, the same architecture. They are one lineage under two covenants: given water, it eats the stone; denied water, on the far side of the divide, drawing only what the night air surrenders, it turns conservator, rooting into old walls and somehow persuading the town to keep them. Thirst made an archivist of an appetite. The rosettes are the eater in exile, annotating the very pages their kin, back home, are consuming.

Then the casemakers: thumb-sized, soft-bodied, living at the edges of the pools, and they are the hills' single act of construction. They pull the mineral back out of the milky water and build with it — small white tube-cases, crisp-cornered, hard, each one a private argument against the whole tendency of the country. Their cases are the only sharp edges in the hills, as the grazers' tooth-marks are the only erosion in the town; the two species have divided one heresy between them across the divide. Stranger: the cases resist. Rain that unmakes a hillside in a lifetime cannot soften an abandoned case in three, and no one knows why, any more than anyone knows why the town spares its rosette-furred walls. The villagers pave their thresholds with dead cases, millions of them, white cobbles the size of grains — the only paths in the country that keep their shape.

Twice a year, on the same heading each time, the high crosser comes down. Here, and apparently only here. It lands on the Three Spires — the last high stone in the hills, rain-washed pillars too steep for the eater — and it descends to a wide shallow pool the villagers call the Milkpan, the most saturated water in the country, and it drinks for a long time, and it goes. Pale, vast, silent, the exact grey of the sky it travels. Its heading, extended backward across the divide, passes directly over a shallow bowl of dry land where something has been watching it cross, too high to identify, for decades. Extended forward, the heading runs out over the hills and onward into rain-haze toward a third place no one from either end has found. The road it flies is older than the villagers' memory and possibly older than the hills. It may drink here because its bones are of the white stone. Once every few years it leaves, in a hollow atop the middle spire, a single egg indistinguishable from the rock, and no one has ever seen what comes out of it, only that eventually the hollow is empty and the sky, twice a year, still crossed.

And the people. A few dozen of them, in a settlement that has no fixed location and therefore no fixed name, though outsiders would call it the Openings. They live in the chambers the rain reveals. For the hills are hollow — riddled through with voids, room upon room of them, and as the surfaces melt back, the rain breaches them one by one. A dome thins for years, goes translucent, glows faintly from within on bright days, and then one morning opens with a long low exhalation — the exact inverse of the town's held breath — releasing a body of air that has waited in the dark for no one knows how long, smelling of chalk and centuries. The villagers listen for these openings the way farmers elsewhere listen for weather. A newly opened chamber is dry inside, warm, swept-looking. They move in. They live there — a family, a generation, sometimes two — while the walls around them thin, and the ceiling goes translucent, and the rain's voice comes through more clearly each year, and then, before the room fails, they leave it. Leaving is not tragedy here. Leaving is housekeeping. They own almost nothing, keep almost nothing, scrape the eater from their walls each week to buy time, and pave each new threshold with the white cases of the old one. Their word for *home* is grammatically a verb.

Their dead go to the water. This has to be said plainly too. A body is wrapped in white, packed round with powdered stone, and given to a particular swallow-hole — the widest, the smoothest-lipped, called the Throat — and the family stands in the rain and watches it go down into the milk and the dark, and the saying is spoken: *what the water takes, the water owes.* They believe the dead travel. They do not know where.

One of them found out. Years ago a child of the Openings — the sort of child who asks where the water goes and is not satisfied with *down* — grew up, crossed the divide against all custom, walked the dry grey country along the crosser's heading with a dowser's patience, and came at last to the rim of a shallow bowl and looked down at a pale town, crisp-cornered, silent, growing, and recognized the stone the way you recognize a voice. This is the visitor who camps on the rim, most years, and never sleeps below it twice. The notebooks in the waxed box hold the correlations no one else has thought to look for: that the year after the great arch called the Gate finally fell here — the hills' oldest span, leaning its last degree into the rain — the town, across the divide, began to raise its windowless tower. That the tower leans the way the Gate leaned. That the town's revisions, read carefully, may be the hills' losses arriving decades late, translated through the dark, form without content, the way a shell records a life it will never contain. The keeper has decoded nothing, by their own account. But they have stopped calling the town empty. And they attend the funerals at the Throat differently now — standing a little apart, facing not the hole but the divide, doing arithmetic.

The least modest theory in the notebooks is this: that the hills themselves are an old town. That deep chambers, when they open, sometimes hold a lintel inside solid stone, a stair climbing into rock, a room proportioned exactly like the rooms across the divide; that once, far in, a breached wall wore the fossil imprint of a rosette, eleven rings deep. That the hills accreted in some ancient bowl of rainless land through ten thousand patient years, and then the weather moved, or the world turned its shoulder, and the rain found them, and began carrying them — room by dissolved room, dead by dissolved dead — to a new dry bowl on the far side, to begin again. In which case the town is not the hills' child. The town is the hills, moved. The villagers live inside their own settlement's previous life and send their dead ahead to its next one, and the low long structure on the town's eastern edge, with the one doorway in all that country that has never begun to close, is standing open for someone in particular, and knows it, and can wait.

If you stay in the hills until dusk, press your hand flat against the wet stone and hold it there, then lift it away. The print you leave will be deeper by morning — the hills take impressions the way the town erases them. And somewhere between the two, under the dry country, in the dark, in solution, at the pace of grief: the print itself, traveling.

The town houses whatever holds still. The hills release whatever lets go. Between them runs the milk-white current, carrying the one place to the other, owed and paid, owed and paid — and giving is most of what the hills do.
