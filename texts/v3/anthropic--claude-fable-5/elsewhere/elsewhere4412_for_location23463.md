**The Weirs of Bellwater**

The valley has never finished. That is the first thing to understand about it. It lies at the foot of the same mountain — far below the summit, far below even the cloud-floor that the people of Old Drum call *the lake* — and everything in it is still being decided. The river that runs through it is rewriting its banks this very season. The hills on either side are coming down in slow brown shrugs of mud. Nothing here has settled into a final shape, and nothing intends to.

Above the valley, always, is the roof.

That is their word for it — *the roof* — and it is the underside of the very cloud-floor the summit looks down upon. It has never once opened. The people of Bellwater have never seen the sun, only known it as a warmth that comes through the grey; never seen a star, never seen the moon, never seen the mountain's top, though the mountain's flank rises at the valley's northern end, climbs through the dripping forests, and goes up into the roof and does not come back out. As far as the valley knows, the mountain has no summit. It simply becomes sky.

Note the symmetry, because both peoples would find it funny if either knew of it: on the summit, rivers are a rumor from the trading songs. In the valley, the sun is. Each keeps the other's most ordinary possessions as articles of half-belief. The summit's amber dusk and the valley's pearl-grey day are the same light, divided by a mile of cloud, and neither people has ever stood in the other's noon.

---

**The rain does not stop. It only changes register.**

The valley lives inside weather the way the summit lives above it. The roof condenses, gathers, lets down — and the people of Bellwater, who could no more imagine a dry day than a summit-dweller could imagine a wet one, have given up the idea that rain is an event. Rain is a *medium*, like air, and what varies is its voice. They have names for perhaps a dozen rains, and the names are the first vocabulary a child learns: the *seed rain*, fine as breath, that you feel only on your lips; the *walker*, a heavy squall you can hear approaching along the river for a full minute before it arrives, hissing louder and louder across the water, so that people simply step under an eave and wait for it to pass over them like a large animal; the *long grey*, which is most days; and *roof-sweat*, which is what falls when the rain pauses — a slow fat dripping from every leaf and eave, the roof wringing itself out, the nearest thing the valley has to dry weather and the occasion, always, for hanging things hopefully on lines.

The light beneath all this is shadowless. Where the summit's low sun draws long blue shapes all day, the valley's grey glow comes from everywhere at once, and nothing in Bellwater casts a shadow, ever, in any season. Distances read strangely; a hill and a heron flatten into the same silver. And so — again the symmetry, wearing a different coat — the valley does not trust its eyes either. But where the summit went to the hand, the valley went to the *ear*.

---

**They are a people of hearing the way the mountain's people are a people of touch.**

The river is the reason. Below the town it runs across a shelf of hollowed, potholed stone, and there it *rings* — a low continuous glassy tolling, water striking rounded hollows, audible through the whole settlement day and night, changing pitch with every finger's-width of rise and fall. That ringing is the town's name and the town's newspaper. A weir-ward waking at night knows the river's height before opening her eyes. A change of half a tone means rain fell hard in the hills yesterday; a certain muddy flatness in the ringing means a bank has slumped somewhere upstream and the flood behind it is already walking down. Children learn the river's voice before their own; a child of ten can pole a boat through the weir-channels in full dark, steering entirely by the sound of water on wood, on reed, on stone, each surface answering differently, the whole valley a room whose furniture can be heard.

Their speech has adapted the opposite way from the summit's. You cannot go *under* rain — rain owns the low hiss — so the valley went *over*: their language is pitched high and bright, cutting, full of short piercing vowels that carry through downpour the way a whistle carries. Heard from outside it sounds like birdcall, and this is not a coincidence the world neglected to use, as will be seen. Their names are not words at all but short sung phrases, three or four notes, given at birth; to call someone across the water you sing them, and every person in Bellwater is, in the strict sense, a melody.

And they have a bell. One bell, brass, traded up from downriver four generations ago at ruinous cost, hung in a tower of black resinous wood at the town's center: the answer, the elders say, that the town finally made to the river's ringing, after listening politely for a thousand years. The bell speaks perhaps thirty phrases. *Flood coming. Fish running. Assembly. A birth. A death. A finding on the slopes.* And one phrase, almost never rung, that every child is taught anyway: *Someone has come down the stair.*

---

**Everything here grows fast, rots fast, and is forgiven for it.**

The green of the valley is a green of *appetite*. Reeds cut in spring stand head-high again by autumn. The trees are soft-wooded, quick, careless — a tree here lives sixty years, falls across the river, and becomes a bridge, a nuisance, and then silt, all within one person's memory. A planting is not a letter to one's grandchildren; a planting is roughly breakfast. And because rot eats everything — wood, cloth, rope, memory's anchors — nothing old survives in Bellwater. The houses stand on posts above the flood line and are rebuilt entirely every twenty years or so, the rebuilding a festival with its own songs; the weirs, the great fish-traps of woven stakes that stitch across the river and are the town's larder and masterwork, are torn apart by every flood year and rewoven, and no one grieves this, because the weirs were never understood as objects. They are understood as a *practice*, like breathing, and a practice cannot drown.

The consequence runs deep in them. A people who cannot keep things keep *songs* instead. The valley's memory lives entirely in voices — genealogies sung, floods sung, the boundaries of fishing rights sung, disputes settled by whose grandmother's version scans better — and the town's true elders are the singers, whose office is simply to hold, between their ears, everything the rot has been prevented from eating. Among what they hold are the trading songs: long chanted lists of goods for a market that no longer opens. *Resin and sap and feathers coming down; brass and salt and cloth going up.* No one has traded up the mountain in living memory. The songs are sung to babies now, as lullabies, and the babies fall asleep to inventories.

---

**The birds pass between the worlds, and belong to neither, and rob both.**

Every dawn the roof leaks birds. The valley people stand in their doorways with their tea and watch the flocks pour down out of the grey — the same sicklewings, the same sooty criers, the same bronze swifts that roar around the summit — descending in long unravelling spirals to fish the river, skim the weirs, and thieve. In Bellwater they are called, collectively, *the roof-folk*, and the theology built on them is the mirror-twin of the summit's, though neither side has ever compared notes.

For the valley believes this: that above the roof is the finished country — the place where the world keeps everything it has completed. The sun lives there, and the moon, and the stars, and the oldest ancestors of all things, in a warm dry stillness beyond weather. (They are, on the whole of it, essentially correct, which would astonish them.) The rain is that country's ordinary correspondence. The roof-folk are its couriers. And so the sicklewings, grave and enormous, are permitted to take fish from the weirs untaxed — the birds pay themselves, and the valley calls this fair, in exactly the tone the summit uses when calling its egg-quota fair — because a courier of the finished country does not settle its accounts with the likes of us.

The criers, meanwhile, are hated cheerfully. They steal. They have always stolen — anything bright, anything small. A hundred-odd years ago a crier took a brass button from a coat drying on a line at the weirs; the coat's owner swore, carved a new button from bone before supper, and forgot the matter by dark. That is the entire history, on this side, of the object that hangs in the meeting house of Old Drum as the great text of a religion. The valley wrote its scripture without noticing, in the act of losing a button, and the summit has spent a century reading it, and both peoples are, in their way, entirely right about what it means: *the other world is real, and sends proofs.*

The criers give as well as take, though no one below or above has ever traced the gift. They are mimics. They carry the high birdlike speech of Bellwater up through the cloud in their throats, and on the summit, in the murmuring of the flocks at dusk, the people of Old Drum sometimes hear thin sweet phrases in no language they know, and understand them, reasonably, as the voices of their dead. And the criers carry the summit's low droning songs *down*, so that on certain nights the valley hears, threaded through the rain, a deep mournful humming from somewhere in the roof-folk's roosts, and understands it, reasonably, as the finished country singing to itself. Each people has spent generations being comforted by the other's misheard music. It is possible no arrangement in the world is working better.

---

**What falls from the roof is met, and carried on.**

Rarely — a handful of times in a generation — the roof-folk behave strangely over the high slope forests: the sicklewings circling one place, silent, for a day. Then the bell speaks — *a finding on the slopes* — and the finders go up. It is an inherited office, like the summit's egg-counting: a family of slow careful climbers who know the drowned woods of the mountain's flank, where the trees stand in permanent drip and the moss is a hand deep on every branch. What they bring down, wrapped in oilcloth and carried with the particular gentleness owed to a stranger, is one of the summit's dead — given to the western edge a mile above, fallen through the lake that is the roof, received by the canopy of a forest that neither people has ever named.

The valley calls them *the roof-sent*. They are treated as the holiest thing the town touches. The body is sung over by singers who invent, for each one, a name-melody — since the true sung name, they reason, is known only above — and then given to the river at the ringing shelf, to be carried down and down, past the last weirs, toward the sea. For the sea is the valley's own rumor, its own downstream theology: the place all water and all the dead are returning to, the great origin, the home before homes. The valley believes it is doing the finished country a service — relaying its dead onward to where everything goes. The summit believes its dead have arrived. Neither is the destination. Each is one more place along, passing the wrapped strangers hand to hand down the world, and the world, it appears, is a relay: everyone lives in somebody else's heaven, and wakes there to an ordinary morning, and complains about the weather.

One thing more, before the body is given to the water. The finders' family holds a privilege: they unwrap the cloth, once, and the weavers of Bellwater come to study it. Summit cloth is impossible by valley physics — cloth that has lasted *generations*, mended a hundred times in a hundred hands' styles, a genealogy of stitches, smelling faintly of pine and pepper. The valley weavers cannot keep cloth ten years, but they can keep *technique*, and for a hundred years now certain mending stitches invented by long-dead grandmothers of Old Drum have been living a second life in the quick-rotting garments of Bellwater, copied and recopied, taught to apprentices as *roof-stitch*, their origin a mile overhead and wholly unsuspected on either end of the thread.

---

**And the stair. Last of all, the stair.**

Above the town, past the highest fish-smoking sheds, past the treeline of the drowned forest, there are steps cut into the mountain's flank. They are ancient — older than the valley's oldest song, which is the valley's way of saying older than anything — and they climb, switching back and back across the wet rock, up into the roof, and do not come out. The valley calls it the Stair into the Roof, and its lower reaches are where the grieving go: it is the custom, after a death, to climb an hour up the stair and sing the dead person's name-melody into the cloud, once, and come down. The cloud does not answer. It is not expected to. The singing is the point.

But once in a long while — a lifetime, two lifetimes — someone keeps climbing. The roof-called, they are named: the very old, sometimes, or the inconsolable, or simply the curious, who go up singing into the cold blind wet of the cloud's interior, that mile of drifting grey that the summit calls the lake and the valley calls the roof and that belongs, in truth, to the birds alone. Some come back down, days later, silent or babbling, with stories of a warm amber country above the sky where small deep-voiced people touched their shoulders and gave them resin-smelling tea and wept over them as over a returned ancestor — and the valley half-believes these stories, and sings them, and lets them blur. Some do not come back down at all. The valley has a story of a stairkeeper, in the great flood year, who climbed singing into the roof and was never seen again; the summit has, in its own memory, one ringing of the bone chime for a thing no song agrees on, rising through the lake. Whether those are one story wearing two coats, no one on either side is in a position to say.

For the valley, too, keeps a sentinel toward the other world. At the treeline, where the stair leaves the forest, there is a single small house of black wood, always occupied, always by one person — the stairkeeper, an office taken up, near the end of a life, by those who feel themselves already leaning toward the roof. The stairkeeper clears the lower steps of slide and moss, keeps a lamp burning in all rains, and listens — for the voice of anyone coming down, and, on the stillest nights, for anything at all from above. And a mile overhead, in a fracture-hollow among the highest nests, the one below of Old Drum sits doing precisely the same thing in the opposite direction — two old people, unknown to each other, posted at the top and bottom of the same cloud, each convinced they are keeping watch on the border of the land of the dead, each listening through the roar of birds and the hiss of rain for a footstep on the same worn stair. Between them the flocks rise and fall, carrying seeds, songs, buttons, luck, and the wrapped and honored strangers, and the correspondence goes on — unbroken, misread on both ends, and answered faithfully, forever, by return post.
