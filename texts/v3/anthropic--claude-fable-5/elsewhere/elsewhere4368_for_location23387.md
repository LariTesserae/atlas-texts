**The Quarry at the Bottom of the World**

Begin with the fact of depth, because everything else follows from it: this is a hole that should not be where it is. It lies low — lower than the old sea floors, lower than the deltas and the drowned coasts, so deep in the skin of the world that the air inside it is thick enough to lean on. And it was not opened by anything the world does to itself. Not a rift, not a sinkhole, not the socket of some dead volcano, but a *taking*, bench below bench of stone the color of whale bone lifted out and carried away, a pit so wide it curves out of sight in every direction and so old that its cut faces have weathered into something that no longer looks cut. From the rim, if you could stand on the hot heavy air of the plains around it, you would see terraces descending into haze and a faint perpetual shimmer rising from the depths. From the inside there is no outside. There is only the descent.

Everything here is absence, and the absence is made. That is the first rule of the place, and the strangest one, because nothing about it feels empty. The walls of the pit — and it is a country, a depression that takes days to walk down into even in imagination — are stepped. Vast benches, each as tall as a fortress wall, wind downward in a single continuous spiral, so that the whole pit is one road forty leagues long coiled into the earth. Their edges are rounded now, soft as old teeth, their faces dished by nothing but wind moving over them for so long that wind alone was enough. And every face is stitched with holes: ruler-straight rows of round bores, millions of them, marching along the stone in lines that were drilled to split blocks free and were never needed again. Whoever emptied this place did not dig *into* the world. They subtracted a piece of it, cleanly, and left the shape of what they took.

You know where it went. The stone tells you, if the geometry doesn't: bone-white, fine as porcelain, without flaw — the same stone, course for course. Somewhere at the top of the world there stands a rim wall going up into cloud, holding a sea that should not be where it is, and for every layer laid up there a layer was lifted out of here, so that the wall and the pit are the same object folded apart across half a world — a fullness and its hollow, a cup and the ground the cup was cut from, separated by a distance so great that neither place can be seen from the other, or from anywhere between.

And then the makers finished, or left, or became something else, so long ago that the wound has finished healing. It has reached the state old workings reach when they stop being scars and become landscape. Nothing here will ever look raw again. The tool marks softened ten thousand years ago and have since been polished into long shallow flutings, like the ribs of a shell. The bore-holes have worn round-mouthed and smooth as if the stone had grown them. The whole vast subtraction has settled into its final shape — and that final shape, it turns out, is a garden.

Because this is the great inversion, the joke the world plays on its makers at the bottom as surely as at the top: at the top of the world they built a basin and excluded everything, and nothing grows there, not a weed, not a green film on the stones. Here they built nothing — they only took — and the world has moved back in with everything it has. The absence is furred with life. It began, the rocks suggest, with lichens on the north-facing cuts; then the seams. The makers sawed their stone free along channels, long straight grooves running for miles across every bench face, and dust settled in the grooves, and the dust became soil, and the soil became a linear world: hanging meadows a hand's width wide and a league long, ruled across the pale stone like green writing. The upper benches, after a hundred centuries of this, carry actual forest — dense, dark, dripping — trees rooted in the old saw-lines, their canopies flooding out over terraces that were engineered to bear ten thousand tons of block and now bear moss. Going down, the green thins as the air thickens and warms. The middle benches are grass and scrub and the grey-green congregations of the fog-combs. The deep benches are bare again — too hot, too still — and the very bottom of the quarry, pale naked stone under a haze of heat, looks uncannily like descriptions of the sea floor at the top of the world: clean, quarried, final, as if at their extremes the two places curve back toward each other.

The air never quite rests, but unlike the sea in the high basin it *breathes*. The pit is deep enough to make its own weather, and the weather has a pulse. All day the sun loads the upper terraces with heat, and warm air peels off the benches and rises in twisting columns — dust-devils a hundred feet across wandering up the spiral road, lifting the fine pale flour that ten thousand years of weathering have ground from the cut faces, braiding it into brief towers and letting it fall. This is the exhale, and it goes on till evening. Then the high slopes cool, and cold air begins to pour over the rim and down the benches in slow avalanches — you can watch it come, a river of clarity flowing down through the haze, bending the grass on terrace after terrace in sequence — and all night the pit inhales, filling itself with cold, until the deepest benches lie under a lake of chilled air a thousand feet deep. Twice a day, at the turning of the breath, there is a stillness. It lasts only minutes. Dawn and dusk, the whole quarry holds its air, and in those minutes you understand what the place sounds like the rest of the time, because the sound stops: the bores. Millions of drilled holes, every one a pipe, every face of every bench a rank of flutes — the wind plays the quarry continuously, a vast breathy chord that rises and falls with the breath, changes key when the breath turns, and dies entirely, twice a day, into a silence so complete that animals all down the spiral lift their heads. The cistern's water never rests; the quarry's air rests for a few minutes at dawn and dusk, and everything that lives here sets its life by those two silences.

Rain falls into the pit and never fills it. Cloud pours over the rim on heavy days and slides down the terraces like slow white water, drowning the forests, filling the upper third of the hole with a fog-sea that burns off by noon — the quarry drinks whole weather systems and stays thirsty, the water going down through seams and faults into the body of the world. Only at the very bottom does any of it stay: a single pond, dark and dead still, no larger than a town square, in the deepest corner of the deepest bench. It does not ripple. The breath does not reach it under its thousand feet of pooled cold air. It rises a little in some ages and falls in others and has never, in the memory of anything, been either empty or full.

And there is the matter of where its water comes from, which is the second strangeness of the place, and the one that binds the two ends of the world together.

The makers moved the stone somehow, and the moving left marks: the Levels, scoured lines radiating out from the quarry rim across the surrounding country, graded so perfectly that they read as acts of geology. Most have been erased — plowed under by ice, taken over by rivers, blurred into ordinary valleys. But one remains legible along its whole length, the Great Level, a groove running away northwest across plain and upland, climbing at a slope so gentle it takes a surveyor's patience to detect, for a distance that beggars belief — because it climbs all the way. It ends, travelers' bones and older stories agree, at the foot of the mountains at the top of the world, at the base of a wall the color of whale bone that goes up into cloud, over whose lip a faint perpetual mist forever bleeds. That mist freezes, thaws, gathers on the outer face of the wall, and runs down — and the water, finding the country below already furnished with one perfect unwavering downgrade, does what water does. It takes the road. A thin cold river, born from the overspill of the sea at the top of the world, flows down the Great Level the way the stone once went up it: the quarry's own road, used in reverse, forever. Most of the river dies on the way — drunk by deserts, split by later valleys, lost in a hundred places — but a ghost of it arrives. A seep, a trickle in the old groove where it comes over the quarry rim, threading down the spiral through the forests and the fog-combs and the bare hot benches, to end in the still black pond at the bottom of the hole. The pond tastes of cold stone and altitude. It is, a cupful at a time, the sea at the top of the world, come home along the path of the stone.

It carries more than water. The high sea grinds its basin ceaselessly — the storm up there mills a fine pale flour off the ancient paving, and some of that flour rides the mist over the lip, and rides the river down the Level, and settles, grain by grain, into the pond and the seams and the soil of the hanging gardens. Which is to say: the quarry's stone, taken to the top of the world ten thousand years ago, is being returned. Ground to dust by the storm it was built to hold, carried a continent by the water it was built to carry, coming back down the road it left by, a grain at a time. The two places are not merely kin. They are one very slow transaction, still in progress: the stone went up once; the water and the dust come back forever.

**The Tenants of the Quarry**

Begin with the rule of the place, because everything that lives here has been shaped by it: the hole does not change, and the breath never fails. Twice a day the air turns; twice a day the flutes fall silent; every creature on the spiral has folded that rhythm into its body the way the cistern's tenants folded in the storm. But where the high sea's fauna is grey and closed and keeps everything, the quarry's is various and prodigal and gives everything away — because a pit that breathes out every afternoon is a pit that exports, and the country for a hundred miles around is planted, populated, and fed by what the quarry exhales.

**The bore-swifts.** The drilled holes were made to split stone. The world found a better use. Every bore in the quarry — and there are more bores than there are people in nations — is a nest-hole, owned, inherited, polished by so many generations of small bodies that the mouths of the holes are dished and satin-smooth, and the ruler-straight drill-lines read now as streets of a city built by something that only ever built doors. The bore-swifts are small, sickle-winged, dust-brown above and pale below, and they live on the breath. All day they ride the exhale, spiraling up the thermals in flocks that turn like smoke, hunting the insect-rich shear layers where warm air meets cold, some of them climbing clear out of the pit to range the plains. At dusk, when the breath turns and the cold begins to pour over the rim, they come down with it — not flying down so much as falling with the falling air, a rain of folded birds sluicing along the benches, each one breaking out of the avalanche at its own street, its own door, so that at nightfall the walls seethe and then, hole by hole, go still. Their droppings, accumulated over ten thousand years in the saw-seams below the nest-lines, are the founding soil of the hanging gardens. The forests of the upper benches stand, quite literally, on the swifts. The green did not colonize the quarry. The swifts built it, one meal at a time, without meaning anything.

And a few of them — the strong ones, the strange ones — follow the water road. Riding the exhale out of the pit and the weather northwest, some bore-swifts trace the Great Level up-country, day after day, all the way to where the land climbs into cold, and circle up the face of the bone-white wall, through the bleeding mist, over the lip — and skim, for a moment, the grey-green surface of the sea that should not be there, drinking on the wing from the storm in its cup. They are the only living things that know both places. They nest in the stone's absence and drink from the stone's presence, and they carry nothing between the two but the fact of having been there, which is carried by nothing else alive.

**The fog-combs.** On the middle benches, where cloud pours in on heavy days, grow the grey-green congregations: low fan-shaped plants, ranked in their thousands along the seam-gardens, every fan angled the same way. They are dew-harvesters — their fronds are combs fine enough to strip water from moving fog — and they lean into the weather the way the anemone-things of the high sea are said to lean into the current, all together, the lean traveling along a bench in a wave when the fog changes direction, faster than the fog itself moves. They are the quarry's weathervanes. The seam-runners watch them. The swifts read them. Everything on the spiral that wants to know what the breath will do next looks first at which way the congregations are leaning — and a traveler who knew both places might stand among them in the fog and feel the world rhyming with itself: two ranked grey assemblies, half a world apart, one in water, one in air, bowing to the same office.

**The seam-runners.** The saw-channels run for miles, dead straight, and something long and low owns them: slender pale-gold reptiles, whip-thin, a man's length from nose to tail-tip, who have become, over uncounted generations, creatures of the straight line. A runner holds a territory measured in distance — a furlong of groove, a half-mile, more for the old ones — and knows its length blind: every root that crosses it, every place the channel deepens enough to turn in, every intersection where its seam meets another runner's seam and the two have worked out, by long mutual arrangement, which one withdraws. They hunt the swift-streets at dusk, patient at the hole-mouths; they bask on the channel lips at noon; and at the turning of the breath, in the two daily silences when the flutes stop, every runner on every bench goes motionless, head raised, holding the pause with the whole quarry. What they are listening for in that silence, or whether they are only observing it, the runners do not say.

**The sail-hatch.** The quarry keeps almost nothing. On strong afternoons, when the exhale is at full press, the air above the pit fills with silk and seed: spiderlings ballooning on threads, winged samaras from the bench-forests, the dust-fine spores of the seam-meadows — a whole ascending nation of the barely-there, riding the thermals up the spiral, over the rim, and out across the world. The plains around the quarry are green in its image; its trees walk outward a wind's-reach per generation; botanists of some future age will map the flora of half a continent and find every line pointing back to a hole. The cistern hoards its life inside a wall and lets nothing out but mist. The quarry breathes its life over the land twice a thousand times a year and keeps only what falls back in.

**The bottom tenants.** Below the last green, under the thousand-foot lake of pooled night air, the register changes. The deep benches are bare and pale and hot by day, cold and utterly still by dark, and what lives there has converged — strangely, exactly — on the forms said to haunt the drowned foundations at the top of the world: pale, slow, patient, half-blind. In the black pond live the stillwater salamanders, ivory-colored, a fathom long, lying along the bottom like fallen columns, moving — when they move — a body-length in a season. They eat what the ghost river brings and what settles from above: the final, finest snow, some of it ground in a storm half a world away. As though, at the extremes, both worlds arrive at the same animal.

And beside the pond stands the last block.

It is the only piece of made work in the quarry that is still work: a block the size of a hall, cut clean on five faces, still joined to the world by its sixth. The channels around it are finished; the bore-lines are drilled; the splitting was never done. Whether it was the next block or the last block, whether the makers stopped mid-task or left it deliberately — one course short, somewhere, in a wall at the top of the world — cannot be known. What can be known is what the quarry has made of it. Its cut faces are polished glass-smooth, not by wind, which barely reaches the bottom, but by bodies: ten thousand years of animals pressing against it in the dark, because the block does at the bottom of the pit what something vast is said to do at the bottom of the high sea — it warms its world. All day the pale stone drinks the sun; all night, under the lake of cold, it gives the heat slowly back, and everything that lives on the deep benches comes to it. Runners coil in its channels. Swifts blown too deep shelter along its bore-lines. The salamanders haul out on its foot, an inch a night. And at dusk, as it begins its long exhale of warmth, the block *ticks* — a single small dry sound, stone easing as it cools — and everything around it pauses, the way the eels of the high sea are said to still when their foundations speak. Then the tick fades, and the huddling resumes, and the flutes far above take up the night chord.

It is not a maker's monument and not an altar and not a grave. It is the one place in either world where the made and the unmade are still a single thing — a block that is also bedrock, a wall that is also the world — and the quarry's life has gathered around it as if it knew.

**What lives there, then,** is this: a loud green spendthrift nation, strung on a breath, coiled down a road that goes nowhere but deeper — swifts in the makers' drill-holes, meadows in the makers' saw-cuts, everything housed in the marks of the taking and nothing mourning what was taken. Above them the flutes play and the thermals climb and the silk goes out over the world. Below them the pond holds its cupful of the far sea, and the dust of the far wall settles into it, grain by patient grain, the stone coming home down the road it left by. At the top of the world, a storm is kept like water in a cup. At the bottom, the cup's absence is kept like a seed in the ground, and has already sprouted. The wall was built to last forever, and it will nearly manage it; but the water is grinding, and the road runs downhill, and the quarry is in no hurry at all. It was here before the wall. It will be here after. And in the meantime it does what the high sea cannot: it breathes out, twice a thousand times a year, and gives the world away to itself, and listens, in the two silences of every day, to the sound of nothing needing to be built.
