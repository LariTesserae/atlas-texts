# The Floodlands of the Unfinished Rivers

A mountain that has been ground down to a plain has not vanished. It has moved. Every grain the frost pried loose from the Tableland, every flake the wind lifted over the ages of its wearing, went the only way loose things go — over the rim, down the mile-high escarpment where the old drive-roads still switchback like a signature, out onto the low country. The plateau is the mountain's absence, held up in the sky. The Floodlands are the mountain itself, lying down.

You feel this before you understand it, the way everything true in this world is felt first. The ground is soft. Not marsh-soft, not treacherous — generous, giving, a land of silt and loam the color of strong tea and turned earth, and it goes down. Drop a weighted line into any still pool and pay it out for as long as your patience holds; you will not touch bottom. The mountain was very large. Everything here — the villages, the groves, the wandering herbs of animals, you — stands on the body of what stands, a mile above, as an emptiness with a horizon.

Nothing in the Floodlands is finished. That is the first thing, the overwhelming thing, the fact of this place that makes every other fact a footnote. The hills are landslides that have paused to think. The ground remembers being a mountain and cannot commit to being a plain; it shrugs in the night, gently, and by morning a rise has become a hollow and a hollow has put up a shoulder, and no one is surprised, because surprise requires an expectation and expectations do not keep here any better than anything else.

And through it all, everywhere, in their dozens and hundreds and shifting thousands of channels, run the rivers.

They rise out of the land itself. There is no rain up on the Tableland and there has been none for so long that even the absence has eroded — but there *was* rain once, an epoch of it, a mountain's whole allowance, and it soaked down into the mountain's body as the body traveled here grain by grain, and it is still coming out. The springs of the Floodlands are the oldest water in the world surfacing at last. At the springhead it is cold and impossibly clear — water that has waited a million years, water with nothing in it, water the stone up top never learned — and within a mile it is brown and heavy and busy, gorged on the only cargo available, which is more mountain.

The rivers do not know where they are going. This is not a figure of speech. A river is supposed to argue with rock until the two of them agree on a single channel, and that agreement is what a river *is*, and these rivers arrived too late for the argument. The frost and the wind finished the mountain without them, a mile up, in the dry. So the rivers braid and split and rejoin and abandon their beds in a night, carrying the mountain's body around and around the lowland, laying it down and picking it up again, deciding and undeciding, and they have been deciding since before the first village and they will decide forever, because the thing they would need to settle the question — bedrock, resistance, an ending — is up in the sky, finished, and missing them.

The weather here is a double portion. All the storms that should have broken on the mountain have nowhere high left to break; they circle the Floodlands like herds that lost their drover, and it rains most days, warm and hard and brief, and the seasons come out of order — a week of autumn inside a summer, a single morning of spring arriving three times in a month. Night, too, comes down in excess. Night runs off the Tableland the way everything runs off it; it pools in the low country, deep and layered, and sometimes a second night arrives before the first has fully drained, and the people light lamps inside their lamps. On clear nights you can see the rim from anywhere in the Floodlands: a long, level band along one whole side of the sky where the stars stop — not darkness, not light, but the mild unchanging grey of the finished country, glowing faintly with its sourceless afternoon. The people call it the Shelf-light. Children are told it is where the day goes to be kept.

The soil is so fertile it outruns intention. Drive a fence post and it leafs out by the third rain; plant an orchard and get a forest; a wall begun in the morning must be begun again in the evening, not because it fell but because it *became* — settled, sprouted, was adopted by vines, turned into a hill. Nothing keeps. Ink runs. Carvings silt over. Graves fill softly with water and lilies and are lost with love. A name painted on a boat lasts about as long as the boat's next voyage. The Floodlands are not hostile to permanence; they simply cannot hold it, the way the stone up top could not hold water. Each country is missing exactly what the other is made of.

There is one exception, and you can walk on it. The drive-roads — the old switchbacked ways the herding people wore into the escarpment with a hundred generations of hooves, going up — run out onto the lowland for miles before the sediment swallows their purpose, and the roads do not wash out. They do not sprout. They do not become. Around them the whole country churns and rebraids, and the roads lie through it all like the spines of something, because they are finished, and finishedness is a substance here, rare and imported, and everyone knows where every ounce of it lies. Along the roads, and only there, grow the dark trees — slow trees, in a land where nothing else is slow, rooted in done stone, putting on a ring a decade, their wood so dense and black it sinks in water and holds an edge and can be gripped for a lifetime without wearing through. Everything else that grows in the Floodlands grows too fast to make anything of; the grain runs like water. The road-groves are the only wood in the world that is willing to stay a shape.

The Floodlands are loud. That must be said, coming from up top. The rivers talk constantly; the rain applauds itself; the frogs and the night-birds and the wind in the fast trees keep up a conversation that has no beginning to remember and no conclusion in view. It is the sound of everything happening at once, forever, and the people sleep inside it like a blanket.

And then, once in a great while — once in a lifetime, or two, or none — a single high note falls out of the sky.

It comes from the rim. It is small and clear, like a splinter of stone struck from stone, and it takes a long time to fall through a mile of air, and while it falls, the Floodlands do the one thing they cannot do. The rivers run straight. The rain hangs. The braids align; the paused landslides pause further; every boundary in the low country — bank and water, hill and hollow, was and is — decides itself, cleanly, for the length of one note. People who have lived through it do not describe it the same way twice, because nothing here is described the same way twice, but they agree on this: that during the note, they remembered everything. Every washed-out year, every silted grave, every name the rain had taken. All of it, at once, complete.

Then the note ends, and the rivers bend, and it drains away like everything else.

Almost everything else. But that belongs to the second telling.

## Those Who Live There

Begin with the grammar, because the Floodlands will make you begin with it whether you choose to or not. Everything here lives in the progressive: *is running, is raining, is becoming, is being rebuilt.* Nothing has happened in the Floodlands — not because nothing occurs, but because occurrence never closes. The perfect tense will not hold; say *the flood has ended* and by the time the sentence dries it is flooding somewhere else, which is the same flood, which never began either. Even nouns are a struggle. Things will not stay things long enough to deserve them. The people speak in a language heavy with verbs and light on names, and they call one another by what they are doing — *the one mending, the one crossing, the one singing near the water* — and this works, because everyone is always doing something, and it fails gently, constantly, the way everything here fails, and no one minds. Up on the Tableland, to ask what lives there is to strain the present tense. Down here, it is the only tense there is.

Start with the animals, since they are the answer that moves through all the others.

No animal in the Floodlands occurs twice. That is the first thing. Where the herds of the high country are a few hundred originals tiled to the edge of the world, the creatures of the low country are all of them one-of-one — every grazer a variation, every bird a proposal, every swimming thing in the brown water an argument the water is still having with itself. Taxonomy fails here in the opposite direction from up top: there you cannot count the instances; here you cannot gather the kinds. A naturalist would go happily mad. The animals themselves are untroubled. They browse the fast green world, and vary, and their offspring vary from them, and the whole living population of the Floodlands is best understood not as a fauna but as a *drafting* — the world working, in warm wet longhand, on the question of what an animal should be.

And now and then, the world finishes a draft.

You can tell at a glance, everyone says, though no one can say what the glance catches. An animal wakes one morning *definite*. It has stopped varying. Its edges have agreed with it. It eats nothing that day. And at some point between the rains it turns — every one of them turns the same way, toward the escarpment, toward the Shelf-light side of the sky — and walks to the nearest drive-road, and begins to climb, and the Floodlands never see it or its kind again. Its cousins go on varying as if a question has been lifted from them. This is where the standing herds come from; this is what the tiled originals of the Tableland are. They are the low country's finished drafts, kept above in the cool even light, every one of them repeated to the horizon like a fair copy made over and over of a line finally gotten right. The eleven grazers that recur across the high plain like a carpet-motif were resolved down here, the songs say, on a single impossible dawn — eleven at once, walking up the roads in file — though the songs say it differently every time, and sometimes it is nine, and sometimes it is a dawn of birds, and the singers shrug, because the song is not for keeping. Nothing here is for keeping.

Except the songs know one line. Hold that thought. It matters at the end.

The people of the Floodlands are the ones who stayed. This is the part that takes longest to accept and explains the most. The drives went up from here — every generation, the strongest walkers and the best of the herds, up the switchbacks to the high pasture, and back down before the season turned, for as long as their people had been a people. Until the drive that did not come down. The high country finished while their kin were standing in it, and the herders looked out at the level grey forever, and set their staffs down, and stayed. The low country never learned this the way one learns news. It learned it the way it learns everything: the rains took the waiting, year by year, and the silt took the names, and the songs reworked the grief until it was weather. The people who live along the unfinished rivers today do not know, most mornings, that they have kin standing a mile above them, warm and breathing, flank to flank at the top of the worn-down world. They know there is a Shelf. They know the day is kept there. They rebuild their villages when the rivers rebraid, and they carry their houses like arguments they are still winning, and their music — this is the thing you notice first about them, and last — is all of it about missing. Songs about what the singer can no longer name. Songs addressed to persons the song itself has forgotten by the final verse. Up on the Tableland, the one promise, kept a million times over, is *you are missing nothing.* Down here the people miss everything, all the time, and have made of it something like a climate, and something like a kindness, and their counter-promise is the equal and opposite of the high one, and it is this: *because nothing is kept, anything can still happen.* They will tell you this with rain on their faces. They mostly believe it.

Every song they sing changes with every singing, except for one line, which has come through ten thousand rewritings without losing a syllable, the way a hard stone comes down a long river: *and they set their staffs down, for the day was done.* The singers believe it is about evening. It is the only thing in the Floodlands that remembers what it is about, except for one person.

The staff-maker lives where the widest drive-road runs out into the soft country, in the only building in the Floodlands that persists — and it persists by a trick, which is that it never stops being rebuilt. A plank a day, a stone a week, continuously, so that it is never the same house two seasons running and the low country, which dissolves whatever stays, has never once caught it staying. Inside, it is dry, and it smells of dark wood, and it is quieter than anywhere else below the rim, and in it the staff-maker works the slow black timber of the road-groves into staffs — long, plain, made for gripping — and grips them. That is the work. Years of gripping, walking the roads' flat miles with each staff in turn, wearing the polish in with a living hand, because a staff sent up unheld is only wood. No staff is ever finished here; nothing is ever finished here. They are sent when they are sent, at whatever state of becoming, and they complete themselves on the way up, the polish deepening with the altitude, taking their final luster somewhere in the last switchbacks where the air goes thin and cool and even — which is why every staff on the Tableland, planted among the herds or leaning on a warm flank, is perfect. They finished the way everything finishes: by arriving.

The staff-maker went up once. A child at the tail of the last drive, too young to carry a staff, too young for any day to be done — and so when the herds slowed, and stood, and the herders looked out at the grey forever and understood, and set their staffs down and walked in among their animals, the child was the one thing on the plateau with unfinished business, and the plateau, which keeps everything, could not keep that. The child came back down the switchbacks alone. And carried something down: the high country's tense, lodged inward like a splinter of that even light. The staff-maker's memories do not erode. Alone in all the Floodlands, the staff-maker lives in the perfect — *has seen, has lost, has kept* — the low country's single speaker of the finished tense, exactly as the keeper, a mile up, mid-stroke over the pale stone, is the high country's single speaker of the present. Each country holds one native of the other's grammar, the way each holds one incorruptible thing, and it costs what you would expect: the staff-maker cannot finish anything either. Not a sentence of grief. Not the house. Not, in the ordinary way, a life. Old beyond the reach of the songs, gripping staff after staff smooth, keeping the tally the rivers cannot keep.

Because here is the trade, the whole trade, the oldest open account in the world. When the keeper's knife, descending a hair's width a year over the forty-first carving, at last frees a shaving of pale stone, the shaving falls, and rings, and the note goes out across the high plain for miles — and off the rim, and down the mile of air, into the low country. And for the length of that falling note the Floodlands are finished: the rivers straight, the rain hung, and every living person below remembering *everything* — the drives, the Shelf, the standing kin, every name the water ever took — complete, for one note, the high country's promise visited on the low like a debt honorably paid. One moment of total keeping, in exchange for what goes up.

Then it drains away. Except that each time, in one person, the remembering does not stop. No one knows how the choosing works; the staff-maker suspects the plateau chose them the way it does everything, in advance, in the finished tense, long ago. That person walks — through the resuming rain, past neighbors already losing the moment like a dream at breakfast — to the house that is never the same house, and finds the door open, and the current staff waiting, warm from decades of gripping. The staff-maker gives it over, and takes the name, and holds it. Forty notes since the drives ended. Forty climbers. Forty staffs. Forty names, held against the rain in the one memory in the Floodlands that does not erode, while up above, forty small carvings sit warm on the floor of a low slab shelter, and forty animals stand at the temperature of stone.

The forty-first note fell — the staff-maker could tell you exactly; the staff-maker is the only one who could — some time ago, as the low country measures it, which is to say: the rivers have rebraided many times since, and the villages have moved twice, and the rains have already taken your face from everyone who waved. The mud here never kept your footprints past a single night; it tried, it always tries, and the water always wants the mountain back. The only earth in the world that holds your stride is a mile up, printed in thin grey dust on paths that — from the plateau's way of seeing, which keeps everything at once — you had always already walked. The staff you carried finished itself somewhere in the last cold switchbacks and stands now, perfectly polished, unclaimed, leaning at the edge of a gap in the densest center of the herds, exactly one figure wide, where you set it down. The herder's gesture. The day, done.

So the full answer to who lives in the Floodlands of the Unfinished Rivers is this: the rivers, arguing forever with a mountain that left; the drafts of every animal, varying in the warm rain until the day they turn toward the roads; the people, rebuilding, forgetting, singing what they can no longer name, promised that anything can still happen and mostly believing it; the dark groves on the finished roads; the staff-maker, gripping the forty-second staff smooth in the house that is never the same house, saying one name each night against the rain.

The name is yours. It is the only thing in the low country that is not washing away. Up there, you are being kept — the knife descending, the warmth of you passing hair's width by hair's width into pale stone, missing nothing. Down here, you are being missed, which is the Floodlands' one form of keeping, renewed like weather, and the staff-maker will hold it until the next note falls and the rivers straighten and, for one clear splinter of a moment, everyone below remembers you at once — before it drains, as everything drains, into the brown and busy water, which carries the mountain around and around, and runs on past both countries, unfinished, toward a sea that no one, in all the mornings of the world, has ever finished reaching.
