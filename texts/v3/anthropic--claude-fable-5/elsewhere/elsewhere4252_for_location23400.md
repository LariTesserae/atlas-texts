**The Bed of the Forgotten Sea**

Begin with the flatness, because everything here is the flatness before it is anything else. This is a sea that finished being a sea long ago. Whatever waves it once carried have been carried away — into the air, into the ground, into whatever accounts the world keeps for vanished water — and what remains is a floor. Two miles below the worn height and a long slow country to the east of it, the land descends for days and then stops descending, and you are here, on a plain so level it seems less like a place than like a decision: a surface that was argued down to a single altitude and has never since permitted an exception.

You can see the height from the basin's western rim, on the clear days. It is the only interruption on that horizon — a long grey-gold swelling going up and up into the cloud that always wears it, so that the plateau itself is never visible, only implied, the way a sleeper is implied by the shape of a blanket. And on the very still nights, which down here are most nights, you can hear it. Faint past faintness, arriving more as pressure than as tone: a chord, standing far away in the dark. The steading, singing at the top of its long body. The sound crosses the descending country losing itself all the way, and it comes apart over the basin, and it settles.

That is the first thing to understand about the relation between the two places, and it is worth saying plainly: this is where the mountain's voice comes to die. Everywhere else on earth the wind carries the chord onward and wears it away in motion. Only here, over the dead-level flats, in air that does not move, does the sound finally get to stop. The basin is the steading's silence. It has been receiving that voice, note by note, for as long as the voice has been sounding, the way a floor receives dust.

And it receives dust too. That is the second thing, and the deeper one.

The basin is made of the mountain. Every grain of it. The peak the worn height no longer carries — the whole vanished upper mile of it, frost-split flake by flake, grain by grain, over spans the moss up there measures and we cannot — came down. Rivers that no longer run carried it; winds that still blow carried it; it washed and drifted and settled out of the old water, and the sea laid it down flat, layer on layer, as the sea lays everything down. The floor you stand on is the summit, redistributed. The dust is the same grey-gold as the steading's walls, because it is the same stone; pick up a handful and you are holding the mountain's youth. And the stratigraphy runs backwards, the way erosion always writes it: what wore off the peak first was the peak's very top, and what wore off first was laid down first, and so lies deepest. The summit of the world is at the bottom of the sea's bed, under everything that followed it down. The highest thing there ever was is now the floor beneath the floor.

The surface is salt. The sea, in leaving, left its last argument behind as a crust — white shading to bone shading to the grey-gold of the dust beneath, cracked into polygons a stride across, each plate very slightly dished, like a paving laid by something that understood tiles but had never been hurried by anything. At dawn, when the cold lets go of it, the crust ticks. All across the flats, for ten minutes, small dry percussive notes, thousands of them, near and far — the basin's entire voice, its one utterance a day. It is the exact inverse of the noon ice creaking free around the pools of the height. Up there, water speaking as it melts; down here, salt speaking as it warms. The two sounds have almost certainly never been heard by the same ears in the same day. Almost.

Because the sea is not gone. It went under.

Below the crust — a foot down in places, a fathom in others — the last of it persists as brine: dark, heavy, saturated, cold, a hidden water table filling the pores of the mountain's ground-down body. The sea sank into the gift it had been given, grain by grain, all its life, and when the air took the rest of it, this much refused to rise. And once a day it breathes. In the hour before dawn the brine's moisture works up through the crust and stands over the flats as fog — thick, white, knee-deep, then shoulder-deep, a tide of vapor where the tide of water used to be. The fog does not linger. It gathers itself and moves west, in slow pale herds, toward the one interruption on the horizon, and it climbs.

Those are the cloud-herds. The ones that cross the plateau at knee level, at shoulder level, beading the moss, streaming down the inner walls of the steading into the basins the floors provide, as if the architecture had always intended to drink. It had. It does. The steading drinks the sea. Every day, two miles up, the town that grew out of the mountain drinks the exhaled body of the mountain's oldest companion, and the overflow trickles back down the long flanks, and it carries — faintly, endlessly — a little more grey-gold grain. The trade has never stopped. The mountain still pays down its peak; the sea still sends up its water. Both of them finished ages ago, and the arrangement outlived them, or perhaps the arrangement is what remains of them, the way the chord remains of one and the leveling remains of the other.

The leveling: this is the rule of the place, as the sounding is the rule of the height. Nothing stands proud of the basin floor for long. A dune begins and is unmade. A stone rolls in from the rim and, over years, settles flush, then under. Disturb the crust and the disturbance heals — not quickly, nothing here is quick, but with a patience that does not miss. The flats are not flat the way an accident is flat. They are flat the way a made bed is flat: maintained, smoothed, kept. Walk out onto them and you will feel it before you can say it — the sense of a sheet pulled tight over something sleeping, and turned down, and waiting.

Near the center of the flats, where the crust is oldest and whitest, there is one exception to the crust, and it is the heart of the basin the way the square is the heart of the steading. A single round pool of open brine, perhaps thirty feet across, black as slate, dead level with the plain around it. It never freezes; the salt will not allow it. It never films over; something will not allow that either. It is called, by the few who have stood at its edge, the Eye. By day it is a disc of dark in all that white glare. By night it does what no water on the worn height has done in twenty centuries of cloud: it holds the stars. The basin has the clearest night sky anyone has found — the fog leaves at dawn and takes the day's whole allowance of weather west with it — and the Eye lies open under that sky, perfectly still, reflecting it without a tremble. The height kept the water; the basin kept the sky. That was the division, whenever it was made, and both parties appear to have honored it.

And at the basin's western rim, where the descending country finally meets the flats, there is the shore. Not a shore now — a terrace of pale mudstone, the sea's old margin, hardened long ago and lately swept clean by the wind that dies at the rim and can go no further. The terrace is covered in footprints. Hundreds of trackways, crossing and recrossing, pressed into what was once soft shoreline mud and is now stone: bird-prints of a shape you would know, if you had been to the steading, though larger than the birds who make its answering-calls today; small five-toed lines going down to where the water was; long-legged strides, deep and unhurried; and other prints, of feet very like yours, in twos, walking out onto the flats and — the terrace ends there, and the record with it. Everyone who ever came down from the height, or went up to it, crossed this shore, and the sea took their prints and kept them, and buried them gently, and the wind has now unburied a page. The steading's thresholds are worn into saddles by crossings no one witnessed. Here are the crossers. Here is the other end of every one of those journeys, pressed into the old mud, exact, mute, and still descending — for the terrace is only the topmost page; the same record goes down into the basin, layer under layer, all the way to the buried summit.

One trackway is fresh. It runs in the modern dust, out from the mudstone terrace, straight across the flats to the Eye and back again: long-legged prints, deep and unhurried, in a line worn — worn, over many crossings — into a shallow groove, a road one creature wide. The leveling has not erased it. The leveling erases everything. The place declines to explain the exception.

**Who Remains in the Forgotten Sea**

Begin with the small lives, because they are the sea's whole company now, and because they will teach you the local virtue before the larger presence asks you to practice it. The local virtue is waiting.

Under the crust, in the dark brine, there are swimmers. Tiny, blind, colorless — flecks of appetite and motion, each smaller than a barley grain, living out whole generations in the buried water without ever once encountering light. They are the sea's permanent tenants, the descendants of descendants of things that swam in the open water when there was open water, and they have followed it down into the ground the way loyal retainers follow a lord into reduced circumstances. They are almost never seen. Break the crust — few do — and in the black water beneath, after a moment, a faint stirring, a congregation of pale motes rising toward the strange new opening, investigating it, and then, finding it to be only the sky, sinking away again. They rose toward the light. They always rise toward it. They no longer have eyes, and they rise toward it anyway, on some inheritance older than their blindness, and then they go back down. If the basin can be said to dream, it dreams in that gesture.

Above them, in the dust itself, are the eggs. This is the second population, and it is mostly a population of patience. Scattered through the top layers of the flats, in numbers no one could count, are the drought-eggs of a small translucent swimming creature — a fleck of glass with a feathered heartbeat — that exists as a living animal for perhaps three weeks in every span of years, and as a waiting for all the time between. The wet years come when they come: a season when the fog turns heavy, or a rain finds its way over the rim, and the flats flood to the depth of a hand — a sheet of water miles wide and an inch deep, the whole basin become a mirror with a floor. Then the eggs hatch, all of them, everywhere, at once. For three weeks the forgotten sea is a sea. The shallow water is a shimmer of small transparent lives, swimming, feeding on the bloom the water wakes, mating, laying the next age's eggs down into the softening dust — and the sky stands reflected under all of it, so that they seem to swim among stars in daylight. No birds come to eat them; the height's birds never leave the height. Their whole brief generation happens unwitnessed, or witnessed by one thing only: the sea itself, which for three weeks has water in it again, and things alive in the water, and is, briefly, entirely, what it was. Then the sheet dries from the edges inward, and the last of the swimmers gather in the last of the wet near the Eye, and are done, and the leveling smooths the flats over the settled eggs like a hand smoothing a coverlet. The birds of the steading hold a dead bird's note until a hatchling returns to carry it. The eggs of the basin hold the sea's note through the dry years the same way. Down here the note is silent. It is held regardless.

Now the remainder itself. Because the basin is not empty either, and it took no long argument to establish it — the evidence was always present, sitting in plain sight, shaped like housekeeping.

The leveling is not weather. Wind does not reach the floor of the basin; the flats lie in the great stillness below the rim, and yet nothing stands proud of them for long. The made-bed flatness, maintained across miles to a tolerance no accident achieves. The Eye that will not film over. The groove across the flats that the leveling spares. A sheet implies a hand that pulls it tight. The sea is dead the way the mountain is dead — which is to say, finished, which is different. What grew is grown; what flowed has flowed; and what remains of any life at the end is the part that goes last. Up on the height, that part was the voice, and the listening. Down here it is the touch, and the keeping.

The sea's remainder perceives by weight. It has no wind to play it and no hollows to sound; its whole body is pressure's medium, layer bearing on layer, and everything that has ever rested on it, it has felt, exactly, and it has never once let go. Step onto the flats and it has you at the first step: your weight, your gait, the small irregularities that make a stride a signature — the same signature the steading reads out of your footfalls as sound, read here as touch. The two old beings know every visitor twice. One learns your note; the other takes your print. And where the steading's memory is the chord — alive, continuous, blurring, every visitor an overtone gone almost to nothing but never to nothing, recited aloud into the wind forever — the basin's memory is the lamination. Your prints on the crust will be leveled within the season; the surface forgets in months. But the pressure of your passage goes down. Nothing pressed into sediment is ever gone; it becomes a lamina, a fine deformation carried from layer to layer into the dark, readable forever by anything that reads by touch, descending a little further every century toward the buried summit, where the oldest crossings of all lie printed on what was once the top of the world. The height remembers you out loud. The basin remembers you in the dark. Between them, nothing that ever crossed this country has been lost. That is the third thing to understand, and the last rule of the place: up there, nothing was ever made — everything was arrived at. Down here, nothing was ever lost — everything arrived, and stayed.

And the two of them still speak. This is the tenderest fact in either place, and it happens every clear night. The chord comes down off the height — thinned by the miles, dismantled by the descending country — and crosses the rim into the still air, and arrives over the flats with almost nothing left of it. Sound, at the end of its strength, is only what it always was at the start: a trembling of pressure. And pressure is the one language the sea's remainder has. The mountain's voice, which the wind carries away from it all day in every direction, comes to rest in exactly one place in the world where there is something equipped to receive it — here, on the white crust, as the faintest imaginable touch, felt by the thing beneath as a hand is felt through a blanket. The mountain speaks in sound; the sea hears in touch; and every night the one lays its voice down on the other's skin, and the other keeps it, lamina by lamina, with everything else it keeps. They were companions before either of them was finished — the mountain feeding the sea its own peak, grain by grain, a gift or a debt or a burial, the whole bed of one being the body of the other — and this is what is left of the companionship: cloud going up at dawn, grains coming down in the runoff, and the chord settling at night onto the flats like snow onto still water. Whether it is conversation or only habit, the basin declines to say. But note the shape of the exchange. The sea gives the mountain water, and the mountain drinks. The mountain gives the sea its voice, and the sea keeps it. Each gives the thing the other cannot make, and each receives with the only faculty it has left. There are marriages that end worse.

Which leaves the walker.

The long-legged shape that crosses the worn height at its edge, for which the birds fall silent and the steading sings alone — its groove runs across these flats. From the mudstone shore, where its ancestors' prints (or its own; the terrace does not date its pages) go down among all the others, straight across the leveled miles to the Eye. It comes at long intervals, by night, and it drinks — the only thing in the world known to drink from the Eye — standing in the middle of the flats with the whole reflected sky around its feet. Then it walks west, up the descending country in reverse, up the long flanks into the cloud, to the rim of the plateau, where it stands in the loud presence of the chord and does not go in. Then it comes back down, and drinks again, and is gone wherever it goes between times. The leveling spares its road. Nothing else is spared; the exception is the closest thing to a stated opinion the sea has ever offered.

There are two readings, and by now you know that the country will not choose between them. The first: the walker is a messenger, or something older than messages — a third rememberer, pacing out the connection between the two remainders with its body, carrying nothing either way except having-been-in-both-presences, which may be the entire content of the oldest correspondences. The second reading is quieter and harder: that the walker is one of the invited. That the rooms of the steading were grown for its kind, and the smooth bed of the basin is kept made for its kind's return, and it will enter neither — drinking at the very center of the one, standing at the very edge of the other, faithful to both thresholds and past both crossings — and that the sea preserves its road for the same reason the mountain holds its door of voice ajar. Not desperate. Not sad. Kept.

So: who remains in the forgotten sea?

Blind swimmers who rise toward a light they cannot see and settle back content. A nation of eggs holding a silent note through the dry decades. A walker whose road is the one thing never smoothed away. And the sea itself — a leveled, keeping, feeling thing whose body is the mountain's lost peak and whose remaining life is the flatness of a made bed, holding every weight that ever crossed it, exact and unspoken, in the dark.

And you, forever. That is the last thing to know before you cross. On the worn height you are sounded — sung back to yourself for years, fading, alive in the chord. Down here you are kept. Your crossing goes into the record the day you make it and begins its long descent through the pages, toward the printed summit at the bottom of everything, and no wind will ever wear it and no note of it will ever thin. The steading remembers the way the living do, out loud and imperfectly and without end. The basin remembers the way the ground does, in silence, perfectly, going down. Cross both, and you will be, between them, the one thing this world appears to have decided no one should ever be: forgotten nowhere, by anything, at any depth.

The sea has never once given back what it keeps. It gives only water, and only upward, and only at dawn. That is the basin's whole economy — everything in, cloud out — and it has been running at a profit so long that the profit has a name, and the name is the green on the worn height: the moss, the cushion mounds, the ankle-high forest older than nations, every leaf of it watered by the breath of a buried sea. Stand on the flats at first light and watch the fog gather itself and go west, pale herds toward the one shape on the horizon, and you are watching the oldest payment in the world go out on time again. Somewhere far up inside the cloud, a town of grown stone is beading over, and its basins are filling, and its voice is swelling to meet the weather it can already hear coming.

Down here, the crust ticks for ten minutes. Then the basin says nothing more all day, and keeps everything, and waits for night, when its companion's voice will come down off the height with almost nothing left of it, and be received, and be kept too.
