**The Tallow Coast, in the same dark months**

Six hundred kilometers south, on the outer edge of the same sea, there is a coast that the ice also made — but it made this one long ago and has had ten thousand years to forget it. The signature is here too, if you look: the parallel scratches, the polished domes. But the handwriting has faded almost to nothing, softened under lichen and frost-shatter and rain until the scratches read less like a signature than like the memory of one. Everything the Succession Valley is, this coast is the far end of. There, edges are edges. Here, there are no edges left at all. The land lies down into the sea in long low skerries and sounds, worn round as knuckles, and the sea moves between them without hurry, and nothing about the place suggests it was ever violent, which is a lie, because it was — twice. Once when the ice ground over it. And once again, much more recently, at human hands, which is where the coast got its name.

Understand first what the coast is made of. Not rock — or not only rock. The low islands wear a cap of peat up to four meters deep, black, dense, astonishingly rich, and the peat was made by birds. For something on the order of a million years this coast held one of the great seabird cities of the northern ocean: petrels beyond counting, burrowed into every slope, hauling the sea ashore one fish at a time and rendering it, generation over generation, into soil. The islands are, in the most literal sense, built out of ocean, carried up in stomachs and delivered by wing. Cut into a peat bank anywhere and you cut through ten thousand years of occupation — feather, bone, guano, burrow-collapse, feather, bone — an archive laid down in layers by residents who could not write and did not need to. The old accounts, the ones from the first boats, say you could not hear a man shout on this coast in the breeding season. They say the birds coming off the water at dusk darkened the darkening sky. They say — and this is the sentence that matters — that the noise never stopped.

So the coast once sounded the way the valley sounds now. Hold onto that.

The name is from the fires. For two hundred years, boats came in the light months and worked the colonies the way you'd work a seam of coal: the birds were fat — they fed their chicks on rendered fish oil, they were practically fuel already — and the crews clubbed them by the tens of thousands and tried them out in iron pots on the beaches, and the tallow went south in barrels, and the ledgers of it survive, barrels per season in a fading commercial hand, and the coast has been the Tallow Coast ever since, named not for what it was but for what was taken from it. The fires alone might not have finished the job. The rats off a wrecked provisioning ship helped. The foxes finished it — introduced deliberately, farmed loose on the islands for fur, because an island of burrowing birds is, to a certain kind of accounting, an island of self-feeding fox pasture. The colonies did not decline. They fell, the way a roof falls. The original bird — the tallow bird itself, the petrel that built the islands — has a last confirmed record and then a museum drawer, and after that the coast went quiet, and stayed quiet for a very long time.

The *quiet* is the thing visitors are never ready for. That is the exact inversion of the valley, and people who have stood in both places say the two silences — the valley's total absence of it, the coast's near-total supply — feel unexpectedly alike, the way extreme cold and extreme heat feel alike for the first second. The coast's quiet is not empty. It has contents, and you learn them fast because there are so few: the sea on shingle, patient, unranked; wind moving through winter-dead grass with a sound like a slow page turning; ravens — the ravens will come up again — and under everything, at intervals, the soft concussive collapse of a peat bank calving into the tide. Because the coast is being unmade now, gently, a meter or so a decade, the sea taking back its archive. Where the banks fail, the beaches below whiten with what weathers out of them: bones. Wing bones mostly, centuries and millennia of them, light as grass stems, more bones than shingle in places. The valley is a land where soil is being assembled at four hundred times the natural rate. The coast is a land where soil is being disassembled at exactly the natural rate, and the soil is birds, and the sea is taking them home a wingbone at a time.

There is light here in the dark months, which the valley does not get: two hours or so of true sun, low and amber, skating the horizon at what would be noon, and then long blue dusks that last half the afternoon. Snow-light. It is enough to see the coast's one great built thing, which from the headland looks, under snow, like a graveyard, and looks, from the air, like a city plan. Eleven thousand mounds, in rows, across the old colony grounds.

They are burrows. Fifty years ago the restoration began here — and it is important to say plainly that it began *without* a lattice. The coast was offered one. The same capability that is currently unmaking a glacier and feeding a forest in the north could have rebuilt this soil in a decade, rebalanced the whole shore, run the arithmetic of a million years in a single human generation. The coast people refused it. Their reasons were several and are on record, but they reduce to this: the damage here had been done by people who did not have to watch what they were doing — the tallow crews never wintered over, the fox-farmers never heard the colonies fall, the ledgers record barrels and not one single sound — and the coast people had concluded that speed without witness was the exact shape of the original crime, and they would not repeat its shape even in reverse. So they did it by hand. Decades of it. The rat war, island by island, and then the fox war, longer and worse, and the last fox on the last island is a story every child on this coast can tell you, and not one of them tells it triumphantly. Then the mounds: eleven thousand artificial burrows with ceramic nesting chambers, built to the dimensions taken from collapsed originals in the peat, laid out along the old colony streets, which were still legible in the ground as rows of slumped scars. A city rebuilt to spec. And speakers among the mounds, weatherproofed, playing on loop through the light months the only recordings that exist of the original species — forty-one minutes of colony sound captured by someone with a disc recorder in the last years, the actual voices of the actual dead, broadcast over their actual graves as an invitation.

And when their reasons went north — when the valley project was chartered and the treaty drawn — it was the Tallow Coast delegation that put the witnessing clause into it. That is why two people live in a cabin above the flood line six hundred kilometers away, listening to a forest grow. You do not write a witnessing requirement into law for a culvert. You write it because your grandmother's grandmother rendered birds on this beach and nobody was required to look.

Then the lab wrote the bird back. From skins, from the drawer, the gaps in the genome closed with judgment calls, and the reconstruction — the lab has a designation for it; the coast people use the old name, the tallow bird, as if there had only ever been the one kind — was released here, into the rebuilt city, over the playing voices of its ancestors. It was provisioned. It was expected. And within three seasons the bulk of the population had gone north, to a roaring valley nobody designed it for, and the coast had its answer, and the answer was no.

The careful paper about dispersal parameters is careful precisely because of what it steps around, which is that petrels do not do this. Philopatry is nearly the whole of what a petrel is: you return to the burrow you fledged from, or within meters of it, and your species' entire architecture of colony and coast and continuity hangs on that fidelity. The written birds' fidelity is looser — a parameter drifted, or was misjudged, or was closed with the wrong judgment call — and so when they lifted off this coast and prospected north and found a land that sounded *alive*, forty thousand real voices instead of forty-one minutes of dead ones on loop, land steaming and cracking and screaming with the specific unstoppable racket of a place in full becoming, they were free to choose it. The stewards up north call that arrival a favorable review. Down here the same sentence has a different weight. The coast rebuilt the city, cleared the streets, played the old songs, and the child of the dead came home, and looked at home, and preferred the place with no memory in it at all.

Start with the dead, because everything here starts with the dead.

The peat is the coast's deepest resident and its slowest: anoxic, acid, patient, holding its million-year census in better order than any archive humans keep. The living soil community above it works at honest subarctic speed — real winters, real dormancy, everything the valley's springtails have been excused from — and the botany over it is old the way nothing in the valley is old or ever will be: crowberry and heather in mats that predate the tallow fires, and on the polished domes, map lichens whose growth rings put individual colonies at three and four thousand years. In the valley, no tree will ever be old. Here, there are lichens that were old when the ice left the valley's latitude the *first* time. The two places run on the same physics at speeds so different they barely seem related — except that they are related, precisely, by a flyway.

The otters came back within five years of the last fox, which surprised no one, and the eiders winter in the sounds in dense rafts, muttering, the nearest thing the dark months have to a crowd. The ravens are the winter voices, and the ravens are the coast's own quiet scandal, its answer to the valley's climbing foxes: ravens mimic, and these ravens grew up within earshot of the speakers, and some of them have the loop. Not all of it — phrases, fragments, the churring contact call of the extinct original — delivered from the mound rows in the blue afternoon dusk, in raven order, for raven reasons. The only living throats on earth that produce the true tallow bird's voice belong to ravens. No one on this coast calls it mockery. No one has settled on what else to call it either.

The stayers are at sea now. When the reconstruction went north, not all of it went: some three hundred and forty pairs held their burrows here, bred here, and have kept doing so, and they have been studied with an intensity that borders on the devotional, and no measurable thing distinguishes them from the birds that left. Same writing, same parameters, same wings. They simply chose otherwise, which the engineers find uncomfortable and the coast people find steadying. In the dark months even the stayers are gone — petrels winter on open water, hundreds of kilometers out, asleep on the swell — and so the coast in the dark season is at its truest: eleven thousand burrows, all empty, all kept.

Kept by the keeper, who is one person, where the valley's cabin holds two — the coast's arithmetic of witness runs leaner, or lonelier. The current keeper is coast-born, and her descent runs straight through the ledgers: the fading commercial hand that recorded barrels per season belonged to her family, and the ledger books came down to her the way debts do, and she has continued them — this is true, you can hold the book — in the back pages of the last tallow ledger, so that a single binding now runs without interruption from *barrels rendered* to *burrow entrances cleared, row nine through row fourteen; ceramic chamber forty-one re-seated; speakers serviced and silent per winter protocol*. The book turned. Nobody made it turn. She walks the rows daily in the two hours of amber light, clearing snow from eleven thousand doorways that nothing will enter before spring, because the first birds come back off the sea early, in the last of the winter dark, and a petrel arriving at night to a blocked burrow will not dig; it will leave. The whole coast has learned exactly once what it costs when this bird decides to leave.

The speakers run only in the light months. Winter protocol is silence. But at midwinter, every year, the keeper plays the loop through once — all forty-one minutes, out over the snow-covered mounds and the black water, to an audience of ravens and eiders and no petrel within five hundred kilometers — and she has done it long enough that she knows the recording the way the stewards know their glacier's pulse: knows where the wind gusts against the old microphone, knows six individual dead birds by voice and has, privately, named them. She would not call it a service. She plays it once and shuts it off and logs the weather.

And now the returns, which are the reason the coast holds its breath. The flaw travels both ways. The loosened fidelity that let the written birds abandon their designed home is the only mechanism in the world that could ever refill this one — a properly faithful petrel born in the valley would die faithful to the valley — and for four summers now, valley-born prospectors have been turning up in the light months, circling the mound city at night, walking the rows, trying doorways. Seven last season, by their tags. And one pair stayed, and bred, in row nine, mound forty-one, and fledged a chick into the autumn dark, and that bird is at sea right now, this dark month, somewhere out on the water between the two places, carrying a geolocator and the coast's entire question: whether the drift toward home has begun. The keeper's log entry for the fledging night is the coast's answer to the steward's famous one. It reads, in full: *Row nine, mound forty-one, gone to sea. They don't know what this place is. Good.*

Because that is the shape of the whole arrangement, seen squarely. The valley remembers nothing — it is one continuous roaring body wearing temporary faces, and its memory has to be kept for it, by two people in a cabin. The coast remembers everything — remembers it in peat and bone and ledger, remembers it so thoroughly that the memory is the heaviest thing on the islands — and its healing, if it comes, will arrive on the wings of birds who carry none of it, hatched in a screaming amnesiac forest, prospecting these quiet rows as if the coast were simply a coast. Twice a year one supply hull works the route between them, and letters go north and south in it, keeper to stewards and back, the world's two records in slow correspondence — *what it was like when it was young* going one way, *what was done to it when it was old* going the other — and the petrels overtake the boat in both directions, owing nothing to either shore.

The two places are running toward each other. The valley is loudening downward, decade by decade, toward the day it becomes merely a forest. The coast is quieting upward — seven prospectors, then one pair, then whatever comes — toward the day it becomes merely a colony, and you cannot hear a shout on it, and the name Tallow is just a name again. Somewhere in the next century the lines cross. Neither the keeper nor the stewards will see it. That was never the job. The job, at both ends of the flyway, in the dark, is the same job it has always been: keep the doorways clear, keep the record straight, and be there — in person, within the sound of it, as required by treaty and by older law than treaty — when what was set in motion decides, again, where it prefers to live.
