# Underfall

The water leaves. That is the fact the plateau is quiet about, the one thing all that stillness is doing. Ten thousand square kilometers of basins draining into basins, everything connected, everything moving at a seep — and all of it going somewhere, because water always is. Follow any rim downhill for long enough, choose the lower lip at every junction, and the pools begin to hurry. The gradients steepen toward the plateau's southern edge, the terraces grow taller and coarser, the milk-colored streams braid together into channels with actual current in them, actual sound. And then the roof of the world simply stops — a travertine escarpment four hundred meters tall, curved like an amphitheater — and everything the city has been holding goes over at once.

The town at the bottom is called Underfall, because the people who live there have never seen a reason to be clever about it.

---

## The Falls

Up on the plateau, you will remember, there is never a roar. The flows are too divided, the gradients too gentle; the city speaks in drips, counting at their own intervals, and a visitor can walk it for a season in a hush like upholstery.

The hush is not the absence of sound. It is sound *withheld*. All the noise the high country declines to make, it saves, and it spends the whole account in one place. The falls of Underfall are the plateau's entire voice at speed — every basin, every weeping wall, every drip that took a winter to say its piece, gathered into a single white column and released. The roar is total. It is not loud the way weather is loud; it is loud the way a room is a room — a thing you are inside of. In the streets nearest the water you cannot hear your own name spoken at your shoulder, and the townspeople long ago stopped trying. They have a hand-speech, quick and dry and full of jokes, used in the roar-quarters the way other towns use shouting. Indoors is different. The old houses are built with double walls packed with rammed river-silt, and to step through a doorway in Underfall is to fall abruptly into quiet, the roar dropping away behind half a meter of mud and stone. The town's architecture is organized entirely around this one transaction: outside, the voice of the plateau; inside, your own.

The falls make their weather, as the city makes its fog. A plume of mist stands off the base of the escarpment day and night, taller than the falls themselves, visible from a day's ride downriver — a white column standing over the town like a landmark or a sentinel, leaning with the wind. On sunny afternoons the plume is full of fragmentary rainbows that form and collapse as it breathes. In winter it glazes everything within its reach: the near streets, the bridge rails, the roofs of the White Ward, all sheathed in clear ice a finger thick, so that the closest quarter of town spends the cold months looking like its own memorial glass. They call this the Glazing, and they chip it off the door hinges every morning, and complain about it with the particular affection people reserve for a hardship that is also the reason their town exists.

## The Water

Because the water is the reason. It comes off the plateau warm — the deep springs' heat not yet spent — and heavy with dissolved stone, and it never freezes, and it never fails. While every other river in the lowlands shrinks to gravel in a dry year, Underfall's river runs milk-green and constant, and the town has grown fat on that constancy: mills, tanneries, dye-works, terraced market gardens kept green into early winter by the warm channels threading them. Downriver towns send their grain up to be ground here in the dead of winter when their own races are locked in ice. Underfall grinds, and charges for it, cheerfully.

But the water carries the plateau in it, and the plateau does not stop being itself just because it has gone over an edge. Every kettle in town furs white. Every pipe narrows, year over year, like an artery with opinions. The millwheels grow a rind of stone and must be dressed with chisels twice a season or they thicken into sculpture. The channel-tiles, the well-buckets, the laundry-stones, the teeth of the sluice-gates — everything the water touches, the water begins, patiently, to keep. A hair's breadth a year, the same as up top. The town knows the rate the way other towns know the phase of the moon.

And the people drink it. Have drunk it for as long as there has been a town. The water of Underfall is famously hard — hard enough that soap won't lather and tea brews pale and strangers pull faces at the first mineral swallow — and the people raised on it are hard in the same idiom: long-boned, heavy-jointed, with teeth that the downriver dentists discuss in tones of professional grievance. An Underfall smile lasts ninety years. The town's one chronic complaint is the gravel — small stones, passed painfully, cursed at length — and the town's oldest joke, told at every sickbed, is that it's just the city getting started in you early.

They know what is dissolved in that water. This is the thing to understand about Underfall: they are not ignorant of the plateau. They know it better than anyone living, because they are drinking it. The stone the river carries down is stone the high country let go of — worn from the rims, spalled by the frost from the standing forms, the finished and the load-bearing given up molecule by molecule into the flow. The people of Underfall have a phrase for their river that they use without ceremony, the way you'd name an ingredient: *the ancestors' water*. They mean it approximately, and also exactly.

## The Racks

Behind the falls, where the escarpment overhangs, there are galleries — natural at first, then improved, generation by generation, with iron rails and plank walks slick with spray. Along the rails hang objects. Hundreds of them. A child's boot. A wooden bird. A fiddle with no strings. Gloves beyond counting, hung in pairs and hung alone. Each one under its own thread of falling water, each one turning to stone.

This is the town's second industry and its first religion, if it can be said to have either in a form a stranger would recognize. The mineral-heavy spray coats whatever is hung in it — a season for a skin, a year for a shell, a decade for a thing gone wholly white and heavy and permanent — and the people of Underfall use this the way the plateau uses it, which is to say: for keeping. When someone dies, the family chooses one object — the most *handled* thing, is the tradition, the thing worn to the shape of its person — and hangs it on the racks, and in a year or three brings it home stone. Every house in town has a white shelf near the hearth, and on the white shelf the household's dead sit in effigy: a stone pipe, a stone thimble, a stone toy horse with its mane fused to its neck. Not portraits. Grips. The shape of a hand, kept.

Some of what hangs on the racks is for trade. Stone lace — real lace, coated to a white crust fine as frost-fern — goes downriver by the crate and has made Underfall's name in cities that have never heard of the plateau above it, or think it a story. Merchants come up the valley road, stand at the foot of the plume with their mouths open, buy lace and petrified roses and little stone boots, and go home believing they have seen the source of the marvel. The townspeople let them believe it. The falls are not the source. The falls are the last fast stretch of a very slow sentence, and the racks are just Underfall holding a few words of it still long enough to read.

## The Tools

The kinship is not legend to them, though it has legend's shape. It is lineage.

When the masons of the high country put down their tools and stood into the water — this is how it is told on winter nights in the double-walled rooms, in the good quiet — not all of them stood. Some looked at the pace of the stone and could not consent to it. They were not lesser, the telling is careful to say; they were *quick*, and quickness was in them like the current is in the river, and a river cannot be blamed for declining to be a pool. So they gathered their chisels and their mallets and their squares, and they walked down the falls-road while it was still a road, and at the bottom, in the spray, they built the first double wall and slept their first night inside their own quiet, and that was Underfall.

The town descends from the ones who kept the tools. Half the surnames in the valley are tool-names — Square, Mallet, Plumb, Rasp, Setter — and in the older houses the founding chisels themselves are kept on the white shelf among the stone gloves, unstoned, oiled, black with age. There are people in Underfall who will tell you, flatly, that if you carried one of those chisels up the grade and laid its edge against the tool-marks under the mineral skin — the softened handwriting under the tracing paper — it would fit. No one has tested this in living memory. It is not the kind of claim you test. It is the kind you keep.

So the two places are one people at two speeds, and both of them know it, in the ways available to each. The town has never stopped doing the founders' work; it has only kept doing it *fast*. Underfall is a maintenance culture down to its bones: the descaling of the wheels, the reaming of the pipes, the chipping of the Glazing, the eternal dressing of stone that is eternally trying to thicken — a whole civic calendar of scraping-days and channel-fairs, children sent out with hammers and taught the correct angle, the correct patience. On the plateau, the frost edits what the water writes. In Underfall, the people are the frost. They say it themselves, with a chisel in one hand: *up there the winter does this; down here it's us.* The argument between accretion and revision that makes the city is the same argument that makes the town. They are not on opposite sides of it. They are the same side, at different tempos, and the water runs through the middle of the argument carrying stone from one party to the other.

## The White Ward

The town is losing the argument in one quarter, and has decided, in its unhurried civic way, to lose it on purpose.

The streets nearest the plume — the oldest streets, the founders' streets — have been under the mist for eight centuries, and the mist has done to them what it does to a glove on the racks. The house-fronts are coated white, their carvings softened, their doorways rounding into arches no mason cut. Sills have flowed into steps. A pump-handle in one courtyard has become a slender white column with the ghost of a handle in it, handwriting under tracing paper. Two generations ago the town stopped scraping the ward. There was a vote; the record survives; the margin was wide. Now the White Ward stands at the town's edge like an ambassador's district — Underfall's streets in the city's idiom, thickening a hair's breadth a year, the first suburb of the plateau built four hundred meters below it.

The coating ground lies just beyond, under a spray-channel the sextons tend. The graves there whiten and rise and, over centuries, merge; the oldest rows are already a single low white terrace with the names gone smooth. The people of Underfall are not troubled by this. They live fast and are buried slow, and they consider the arrangement fair — the river brought them the ancestors' stone all their lives, and this is the shape repayment takes.

## The Quiet Weeks

Once a year the falls thin. Deep winter locks the high country — the frost holding water up in ten thousand skinned-over pools, the city's exhalation slowed to its coldest, barest seep — and for a few weeks the great column shrinks to a lace of streams down the escarpment face, and the roar drops away, and Underfall stands in a silence its own children find frightening the first time.

This is when the town does its remembering, because it is the only season you can speak a name outdoors and have it heard. They walk the racks. They visit the White Ward with lamps. And the old listeners — every generation has a few — go out onto the bridge in the dark and stand very still, because in the Quiet Weeks the falls' voice comes apart into its strands, and they swear, they have always sworn, that under the thinned water you can hear it: not a roar anymore but a texture, drips at intervals, many intervals, drifting into and out of phase. The whole conversation of the plateau, no longer compressed. Slowed almost — almost — to the speed of an ear.

No one claims to understand it. One family claims something better. In the southern basin-districts of the high city, in a covered passage, there is a standing form newer than the rest — a pack fused at one hip, boots gone white at the ankle. The town knows the name. Setter, it was; the glove is on the racks, hung two hundred years ago, stone through and through now, and once in a generation someone of that line makes the long cold climb to the passage to see how far the face has turned. It turns toward the drips, they report. It has always been turning toward the drips. A degree a decade, patient as a season.

And each time, before they come back down, they do what the family has always done: they lean close in that upholstered hush, and they say the news of the town — the year's floods, the marriages, the price of lace — quickly, all of it, the whole fast world in a few breaths. Then they walk home along the falls-road, and the river walks with them, carrying the stone down, carrying the sound down, the one sentence the world up there has been saying all along, arriving in Underfall every second of every day at full speed — and the town lives inside the sound of it, and scrapes its kettles, and hangs its gloves, and waits, without impatience, at the foot of the reply.
