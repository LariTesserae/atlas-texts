**The Quickwater**

There is a river that begins as a bruise in a mountainside, and you must follow it downward, because the water does.

The summit sea does not overflow. Its rim has never been topped; the pale thin sky above it gives the basin exactly as much as the basin gives away, and what it gives away it gives in secret. Ten thousand years of cold water pressing on a floor that is not stone but grown matter — and water finds the grain. Somewhere beneath the black plains, beneath the compacted fiber and the drowned dunes of shed tissue and the pressed strata of graziers' cast shells, the sea enters its own foundation and goes down. Through miles of heartwood. In the dark, in the grain, at a speed that makes even the architect seem hasty — a raindrop's journey through the mountain takes longer than a listener's whole life. And then, far down the flank, below the last of the grown country, out of a cleft in honest rock, it comes back into the light.

The people who live downstream call that cleft the First Mouth. The water that leaves it is cold as the roof of the world and faintly green and tastes — though almost no one alive has tasted both and known it — exactly like the summit sea. It is the summit sea. Filtered, aged, exhaled. The mountain breathes out one thread of itself, forever, and the thread falls.

It falls a long way. It gathers rain-streams and loses its cold and cuts a gorge and spreads and slows, and by the time it reaches the lowlands it has warmed through entirely and taken on the milky pallor that gives it its name, and it fans out across a flat green country into a thousand channels and pools and shallows, and stops being a river, and becomes the fens.

And here is where the dust wakes up.

Because the water never stopped carrying it — the living silt, the shed motes of the architect's tissue, the grain in the summit's darkness. Most of the motes die in the descent; the crossing through the mountain's body is long past any patience. But a fraction arrives alive, dormant, dividing not even once a decade, and then the water warms, and the sun hits it, and chemistry that ran at a layer a century in the summit's cold runs *hot*. A mote that would have divided twice in a listener's lifetime divides daily. The dust blooms. The water of the fens is thick with it, green-white, opaline in the shallows, and out of that water, everywhere, all the time, the architecture is rising again.

But rising *fast*, and fast is the whole difference. What the summit built in millennia the fens attempt in weeks: green reefs breaching the surface of a pool in a single spring, soft spires, pithy domes, colonnades of quick fiber that would embarrass the fluted roots of the drowned city — coarse, hollow, hurried, and doomed. Nothing the quickwater grows achieves heartwood. The fast tissue cannot pack itself dense enough; it builds, warps, softens, rots, and collapses, most of it within a handful of years, and the fen shrugs and builds again on the wreckage. There is no map of the fens. There has never been a map of the fens. A channel you poled down in spring is a wall by autumn and a mound of rot by the spring after, with three new spires on it. The summit sea arrived long ago at its final form; the fens have no form at all, only a temper. The place is not becoming anything in particular. It is *beginning*, permanently, in all directions at once.

Everything else inverts too. The summit is black past a hundred feet; the fens are drowned in light — flat water flashing under open sky, sun coming green through the young walls, the silt itself glowing faintly at night so that the pools hold a dim luminous haze, the same grain of light as the summit's darkness but scattered through warm shallows a child could stand in. The summit does not storm; the fens do almost nothing else. They flood, they crack in drought, they steam, they have *moods* — all the weather the mountain pressed out of itself long ago ran downhill, it seems, and pooled here. And the sound: the summit's chorus is an equilibrium, dense and eternal and resolved, the ringing of a finished thing. The fens are loud the way an argument is loud. Frogsong, birdsong, wind in the quick spires, the groan and slap of a dome failing somewhere, rain — a noise that builds and breaks and builds, never the same two days running, never settling into anything. The summit's sound is the sound of a room being measured. The fen's sound is the sound of a room being disagreed about.

And the quick growth listens. This is the hinge on which the whole country turns, and it should not be a surprise — the summit's tissue always built by ear; the fen's tissue is the same tissue, only warmed until its hearing runs at the speed of animals. Hold a steady tone over a pool of the green water for a few days and the fiber will begin to wall itself around the sound, taking the shape of the resonance, curling toward the note like a plant toward light. In the summit sea this was a secret spread across ten thousand years, legible only to the architect itself. In the fens it is simply how the neighborhood works. Every living thing here has found it out.

**Who Lives in the Quickwater**

Begin, again, with the dust, because it is again the bottom of everything. The **seed** — the fen folk's word for the living silt — is the water's color, the water's taste, and the substance of every wall, reef, spire, and nest in the country. The green taste of the Quickwater is the same taste as the summit sea's; a swimmer lifted from the black cold of the drowned city and set down in a sunlit fen pool would know one thing before opening their eyes. Everything in the fens eats the seed, drinks it, builds with it or waits for it to build. Every living thing in the fens is, at some remove, made of the mountain. This has always mattered. Down here, some of them know it.

---

The **droners** are fist-sized, slick, copper-eyed amphibians, and they are the fen's unwitting masons, the way the eels are the summit's unwitting polishers. Each spring the colonies gather at their spawning pools and begin the drone — a communal held tone, ragged, thousands of throat-sacs wide, sustained in shifts day and night for a month. They drone because they have always droned; it is courtship, it is territory, it is the shape of their year. And around every droning pool, the quick fiber rises and curls and closes, and by early summer each colony sits inside a rough green dome of its own accidental making — shade for the eggs, cover from the birds, a roof they take no more credit for than they would take for the rain. The domes rot out by the dry season. The droners never see one twice and would not recognize it if they did. But the quality of a colony's chorus is written in its architecture: a disciplined drone raises a true dome, a ragged one raises a leaning botch, and the fen folk, poling past, can grade every colony in a district by eye without hearing a note. The droners think they are singing about each other. They are, among other things, publishing their character in fiber.

---

The **chanters** are what happens when the arrangement stops being accidental. They are small dun birds with absurd lungs, and somewhere back along their lineage a plain ancestor with a slightly longer-held song got a slightly sounder nest out of the reeds it perched over — and selection did the rest, generation on generation, stretching the song and steadying it, until the modern chanter sings held pitch-lines a minute long without a breath, and its nest is not built at all. It is *grown*: the bird sings above a green shallow for a week and a gourd of quick fiber closes around the sound, shaped by it, a hanging vessel whose form is a fossil of the song that made it. The pitch-lines are heritable. Daughters sing near their mothers' songs, and so the nests of a lineage share a shape, drifting a little each generation, and a person who knows the fens can read the hanging gourds of a district like the drummers' intervals are read in the summit halls — a genealogy, written this time not in resonant stone but in the swelling and tapering of grown vessels, family by family across the reaches. The chanters are courting, as the drummers are naming. Both of them are, without knowing it, keeping records.

---

The **tide** is the fen's answer to the graziers, and like the graziers it looks like destruction and functions as mercy. It is a swarm — flat, armored, palm-sized things, part beetle in aspect, part crab in appetite — and it moves through the fens in slow waves, weeks apart, eating dead growth and only dead growth; the living fiber carries the same compound the summit's tissue carries, and the tide finds it as intolerable as the graziers do, ten thousand feet down the same family tree of distaste. When a district collapses — and districts always collapse — the tide arrives within days and takes the wreck down to water level in a week, clicking, and behind it the pool lies open and green and ready to grow again. The fen has no groundskeepers either. It has the same solution its parent found: demolition shaped like appetite, appetite shaped like renewal. The folk do not fight the tide or fear it. They wait for it the way farmers wait for a flood they have planted around, and when it has passed through a dead house they say the house has been *swept*, and mean it kindly.

---

And then the **fen folk**, and here the story crosses a line it has never crossed, because there were never hands in the summit sea — the drowned city was grown without them, is measured without them, will be finished without them — and the fens are full of hands.

They are a small people, brown and green-eyed, web-toed, deep-chested, long of breath the way their whole country selects for. They pole the channels, they eat the fen's plenty, they live perhaps sixty years — a span the summit architect could not perceive as duration at all — and they have built their entire civilization on the one fact their world keeps offering them: *the water listens.*

They do not build houses. They sing them. A house is a chord — three tones, or four, or in the great families five — held over a chosen pool in shifts, by the whole family, for six or eight weeks: grandmother taking the low tone through the morning, children handed the middle voices the way other peoples hand down tools, the night shifts sung in pairs so no one falls silent alone. The quick fiber rises into the chord and takes its shape — a true chord grows a true house, rounded, ribbed, resonant, its rooms curled around the intervals like the chambers of a shell — and when the walls have closed and hardened the family moves in and lives inside its own held agreement made solid. Children there are taught to hold a note before they are taught to walk. The dying are carried into the singing of whatever wall is being raised that season, so that their voice is *in* the house, load-bearing, after they are gone.

And the family's name is the chord. Not a word — a sound, three or four tones struck together, and every house that family will ever sing is sung to it. Because the houses do not last. Twenty years, thirty for a well-sung one, and the quick fiber begins to fail, and the family releases the house to the tide and chooses a new pool and sings again — the same chord, a new roof, over and over down the generations. Houses die; names do not. The house turns over. The family does not. Somewhere in the black cold above them, eels are being replaced in a braid that persists, and drummers are dying out of a pitch that goes on being sounded, and no one on either end of the river knows they are rhyming.

The folk's craft has one instrument beyond the voice, and it is the most precious thing in the fens. Rarely — once in a decade, once in a generation — the First Mouth gives up a fragment: a chip of true heartwood, dense past anything the quickwater can grow, worn round as a river stone by a passage through the mountain's grain that may have taken centuries. The spring-keepers maintain a woven weir at the Mouth to catch them. And the chips *ring*. Struck, every one of them sounds in the same close family of tone — because everything the summit ever grew was tuned, layer by patient layer, to the standing note of the great trunks, and a tuned thing stays tuned no matter how small it is broken or how far it falls. The folk call them mountain-bells. Every village holds one or two, heirlooms beyond price, and every chord of every family in all the fens is built up from the pitch of the nearest bell.

Which means — and the folk half-know this, and the reader may now know it whole — that every sung house in the fens stands in key with the drowned city. The reference tone of the summit sea, held without variation for millennia in the flooded trunks, travels down through ten thousand feet of waterlogged heartwood, out the First Mouth, into a weir, into a bell, into a grandmother's low morning tone, into the walls of a house a family will live in for thirty years and release. The mountain has never heard of them. They are singing in its tuning anyway. The whole warm loud unfinished country is a variation on a theme it cannot remember learning.

---

There is one more kind of person in the fens, and they are its listeners, because a world this shaped seems to require some.

Any singer may make the journey upriver — most masters have — through the gorge, up the cold miles where the water goes green and the taste sharpens, to the First Mouth itself. The pilgrimage has one act at its heart. You kneel at the spring pool, where the mountain's exhalation comes out of the rock still cold as the roof of the world, and you put your head beneath the surface, and you hold your breath, and you listen. And at the very edge of hearing — carried down through the entire saturated body of the mountain, through miles of grown matter that conducts a tone the way bone conducts a voice — the note is there. The standing note. The organ-tone of the flooded trunks, the summit's middle C, arriving at the bottom of the world faint as a memory of a sound, continuous, unwavering, exactly where the bells said it would be. Most pilgrims surface, and weep or laugh, and go home confirmed, and sing better, and are called masters.

A few surface and do not speak again.

No one knows what the difference is — what those few hear in the note, or under it, or through it, in the last seconds before breath fails. They come home. They are cared for, honored, a little feared. They are called the **hearers**, and they never sing another wall; they take up residence in the deaf houses — and here the fens keep their strangest mercy, because a very few houses, the best-sung, the longest-lived, do not rot when their time comes. They *harden*. The quick fiber, if it survives long enough, packs and densens and goes silent — stops answering song, stops growing, stops listening — and that deafness is the very thing that lets it stand. A deaf house may outlast the family that sang it by a hundred years. The folk grieve them and revere them in the same breath, and their phrase for it is the whole cosmology of the fens folded into four words: a house that hardens, they say, *has gone up the mountain*. Become finished. Become permanent. Become the thing that no longer hears you.

The hearers sit in the deaf houses and listen to the fen — to all of it, the droners and the chanters and the tide and the singing families, the whole unresolved argument of the place — and they attend, silently, for the rest of their lives, and it is not certain what they are checking. Somewhere far above them, in black water, above new walls, soft soundless bodies are hanging in a standing note, doing the same thing. Neither vigil knows of the other. Both are being held open, generation by generation, like a note passed between voices so it never has to end.

---

And the last thing, the thing the hearers may know and the singers do not say aloud:

The fens are slowing down.

It is selection, patient as anything the architect ever did. The quick growth that survives is the growth that grows a little slower, a little denser; the reefs at the old heart of the fens already take two seasons to close a dome that once closed in one; measured across the folk's oral generations, the houses are coming harder and lasting longer, a breath more effort and a year more standing with every century. The warmth woke the dust into a childhood, and childhood is a speed, and speeds like that are always spent. Some unimaginable number of years from now the quickwater will stop answering within a human lifetime. The fiber will take a decade to hear a chord, then a century, and the singing will end — not in catastrophe, not even in grief exactly, but the way the summit's own building ended and did not end: the country will begin keeping things. It will harden district by district, go deaf reach by reach, and start its own long unhurried work of arriving, and someday there will be a second finished country, grown, tuned, complete, ringing with whatever tenants it has taught to strike it — and the whole of it will have gone, in the folk's old phrase, up the mountain.

So this is what the river carries, and this is what the two places are. At the top of the world, the architect old: finished and still finishing, black and cold and full, hearing itself through creatures who will never know they are its ears. At the bottom of the river, the architect young: warm and loud and unfinished, listening at a speed where animals can be heard — and being *spoken to*, in held chords, by small people who will not outlast the conversation, in a tuning that fell to them down the inside of a mountain. The only time in the life of such a being when anything can talk with it is its childhood, and the childhood is ending as slowly and as surely as everything else it does. The water runs one way. The note comes down it. Nothing goes back up but breath held at a spring pool, and the hearers, and what the hearers bring home they cannot say.

The fens sing anyway. Every evening the chords go up over the pools, families holding their names into the warm green water, walls rising around the sound — houses that are letters, sung in the mountain's own key, to a builder that will not be old enough to read them for ten thousand years.

The mountain weeps its one thread. The thread falls, and blooms, and answers. And somewhere between the finished dark above and the beginning light below — in the grain of the wood, in the vein of the water, in a chip of heartwood ringing on a fen-village table — the note, always, the same note, holding both ends of the world to pitch.
