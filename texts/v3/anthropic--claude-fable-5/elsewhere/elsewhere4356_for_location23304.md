# The Loud Delta

You come down into it long before you can see it, because the first thing the delta does is give your sound back to you — and then everyone else's, and then its own.

It begins as a smell on the descending road: mud and salt and green rot and woodsmoke and frying oil and the particular sweetness of fruit going soft in heat. Then a texture in the air — thickness, wetness, the atmosphere at sea level pressing on you like a warm hand after the thin heights, so that for the first few days lowland visitors report a feeling of drinking their breath rather than taking it. Then the sound arrives, kilometers out, a low continuous shimmer on the horizon of hearing, and it never afterward stops. Not once. Not at any hour. The delta is the mouth of a continent, and mouths make noise.

## Water

Here the water does not hide. Here the water is the landlord, the road, the calendar, the government, and the graveyard, and it conducts all of these offices in public.

A great river — brown, patient, muscular, older than any name it carries — comes down out of the interior and, meeting the sea, loses its resolve and splits. It splits into channels, and the channels split into creeks, and the creeks split into a filigree of tidal threads so fine and so changeable that no map of the delta has ever stayed true for a full year. The land between the waters is barely land: silt islands, reed banks, mud that shines like poured metal at low tide, whole districts of ground that exist twice a day and drown twice a day and are farmed anyway, in the interval, by people who plant and harvest by the tide table the way others do by the seasons.

The water is loud. It gurgles under hulls and slaps against pilings and hisses through reeds; it makes a vast dim roar at the outer bars where river argues with sea; it drips from ten thousand roofs and paddle-blades and net-floats in an endless irregular percussion. Rain here is not polite. Rain here arrives like a decision, gray-white and total, drumming on tin and thatch and water with a sound that fills the skull, and everyone shouts cheerfully through it, and commerce does not pause.

And in the water — this is the delta's open secret, told to anyone who dips a hand in at night — there is a fine silt that is not entirely local. The river gathers its load from the whole continent's worth of eroding country upstream, and among the ordinary sands and clays there is a scattering of something else: motes that carry a faint grain of light, visible on dark nights as a pale luminous smoke in the wake of a boat, in the curl of a paddle-stroke, in the splash of a child jumping from a dock. The delta people call it *fireflour*. They have called it that for as long as their songs go back. It settles into the mud, and the mud of the delta is the richest growing-ground anyone has ever cut a dike around, and the delta people will tell you, with the confidence of people who have never needed the claim examined, that the fireflour is why.

They are more right than they know. Far upstream — through the flood plains, up the gorge country, past the last navigable rapids, into headwaters no delta boat has ever reached — the river rises among high broken tablelands, terraces of rose-gray stone stepping up into thin bright air. The polite rains that walk those terraces, the hidden seeps, the patient pools: all of it, eventually, obeys the oldest law of water and goes downhill. And the rock it moves through up there has been wearing, grain by grain, around certain standing things for tens of meters' worth of vanished ground — and though the standing things themselves do not abrade, the stone that has spent ages pressed against them, cooled by them, soaked in whatever slow radiance they carry, comes away changed. It comes away luminous, in a measure too faint to notice until it is concentrated by the sorting of a thousand kilometers of current. What falls sideways as light inside the artifacts of the plateau falls, at last, as fertility into the mud of the delta. The quiet country's dreaming is the loud country's soil. Neither place knows the other exists. The river knows, and says nothing, or rather says everything, continuously, in a language of sediment.

## The Built Thicket

Nothing stands alone in the delta, and nothing stands still for long.

The settlements are not towns so much as accretions — houses on stilts, houses on rafts, houses on old hulls run aground and built upward, all of them lashed and bridged and gangplanked into neighborhoods that flex with the tide. A district rises and falls a body-height twice a day, groaning and creaking and knocking, and its people move through it with the rolling delta gait, never quite trusting a floor, never needing to. When the river shifts a channel — and it shifts them the way a sleeper shifts limbs, without malice, without warning — a neighborhood unlashes itself, floats to the new water, and re-lashes. There are families whose houses have traveled forty kilometers over four generations without ever once being *moved*, only *carried*.

Everything here is provisional and everything is repaired. This is the delta's architecture and also its philosophy: nothing is built to finish; everything is built to continue. A dock is never done. A boat is a genealogy of patches. The great tide-temples at the three main mouths — the largest structures the delta people make — are rebuilt in part every single year, by design, in a festival of demolition and construction so old that no one can say which parts of the current temples are original, and the priests will tell you that this is the point: the temple is not the wood; the temple is the *rebuilding*.

It would puzzle them, and then perhaps move them, to learn that at the top of their own river stands the opposite proposition — structures that have never needed a repair, made of a material erosion cannot argue with, wearing the posture of things that have finished happening. The delta has no finished things. The delta has never once finished happening. Between the two ends of the river the whole spectrum of permanence is stretched like a string, and the water plays it.

## Vegetation

Not edited — *argued*. The delta's green does not keep polite spacing. Mangrove-like trees walk out into the brackish channels on stilted roots and fight each other for mud. Reeds stand in armies. Vines use everything: trees, houses, mooring lines, slow boats. A roof left unattended for a season becomes a garden; a garden left for two becomes a jungle; the delta people do not so much cultivate as *negotiate*, cutting back, letting in, taking a tithe of fruit from plants they never planted and never fully own.

The dike-fields are the masterwork. Rings of packed mud, each enclosing a paddy or a fruit grove or a fish pond, thousands of them, tessellating the firmer islands like the cells of a honeycomb — and the mud of the dikes is renewed each year with dredged silt, fireflour and all, so that the delta's farmers are, without knowing it, spreading the ground-down substance of the plateau's ancient patience across their fields with wooden shovels, and shouting to each other while they do it, and singing.

There is one plant they treat differently. In the oldest dike-fields, on the crowns of dikes that have been rebuilt annually for so many centuries that their cores are dense with accumulated fireflour, there grows a low silver rosette — small-leaved, unassuming, and famously useless: it can't be eaten, brewed, or woven. The delta people call it *pilgrim's plate* and leave it strictly alone, because of what it does. All the rosettes, across the whole delta, face the same direction at any given hour, and the direction moves through the day, and it is not the sun. Delta children are taught the rhyme: *the plate looks home, the plate looks far, ask the plate where the old ones are.* Nobody remembers what old ones. The rosettes are the delta's silver congregation's far-flung colonists — seeds carried down a thousand kilometers of current, taking root only where the water's luminous freight has piled deep enough — and they still face what their ancestors face, attending, from the mouth of the river, to something at its source. They are the one thread of the connection that grew a visible face, and the delta made a nursery rhyme of it and moved on, because the delta is busy.

## Fauna

This is not the surprise of the place. The delta announces its abundance from the first hour and never stops announcing it.

The birds alone are a weather system. Dawn on the delta is not a lightening but a *detonation* — a hundred thousand waders and divers and gulls and glossy black fish-thieves rising off the roosts in shrieking sheets, wheeling, settling, arguing. Their wings whistle and clap and thrum; they have never in their lineage needed silence, and they spend the surplus lavishly. Herons stalk the shallows and croak like unoiled hinges. Kingfishers hit the water with a smack you can hear across a channel. In the evening, flights of river-doves come up the main channels toward their roosts — plump, quick, loud-winged, their wingbeats a rattling whir — and they are, though no naturalist has ever stood in both places to compare, close cousins of the fringe-winged silent doves of the headwaters: one stock, split long ago by the length of the river, one branch schooled by an enormous quiet into owl-soft wings and drinking in turns, the other raised by an enormous noise into whistling flight and drinking in mobs. The same bird, translated into two languages.

The water itself is thick with life and thick with the sound of it. Fish boil at the surface in the tide-rips. Great whiskered bottom-feeders, long as a man, grumble audibly through the hulls of boats at night — actual grumbling, a resonant croak they make in the swim bladder, and the delta people navigate fog by it, telling channel from channel by which grumblers live where. Otter-like creatures raid the fish ponds in gangs and are fought off with theatrical rage and secretly admired. Crabs in the mangrove roots click and clatter at low tide in numbers that make the whole margin of the land sound like faint applause.

And in the fish ponds of the oldest dike-fields — only the oldest, only where the fireflour lies deepest — there are the threads. Translucent, finger-length, finer than grass, eyeless, mouthless, swimming with the water's circulation rather than against it. The pond-keepers know them well and prize them above any fish: a pond with threads in it never fouls, never sours, turns over slowly on windless days as if breathing in its sleep, and stays strangely cool through the worst heat. A pond-keeper whose pond develops threads has *married the water*, the saying goes, and owes it courtesies thereafter — no shouting across it, no washing of blades in it. It is the single place in all the roaring delta where a custom of quiet is kept: small rectangles of hush, scattered through the din, each one an unwitting embassy of the headwaters, holding a little of the high country's stillness the way the high country's pools hold a little of its cold. The threads are different in every pond, each water alone long enough to invent its own — in this, at least, the two ends of the river run the same.

The delta's great predator is the river itself, and everyone knows it, and no one holds a grudge. The water takes boats, takes dikes, takes the careless and sometimes the careful, and the delta's answer is not vigilance but continuity — the neighborhood re-lashed, the dike rebuilt one year thicker, the drowned mourned loudly for three days with drums and feasting and then folded into the songs. It is, had anyone the vantage to see it, the same actuarial peace the grazers of the tablelands keep with their soundless cat: the taking is part of the terms, and the answer to being taken from is to go on. The two countries arrived at the same treaty with loss. One signed it in silence. The other signed it singing.

## Weather, Time, Motion

The delta has two clocks and obeys both. The tide clock, twice daily, unarguable, felt in the boards of every floor. And the flood clock, annual: the far-off rains of the high country and the middle country arrive as a rising, a brown swelling of the river that the delta awaits the way other lands await spring. Flood season is loud even by delta standards — the water audibly *working*, whole trees riding down out of the interior, the fireflour running so thick that the night river glows faintly along its seams and children stay up to watch the shining wakes.

The flood is the plateau's one annual letter, and the delta reads it closely without knowing the sender. Old pilots can tell you, from the color and grain of the first flood-silt, what kind of year the unseen headwaters had — and in the years after the high country's rare heavy rains, the pilgrim's plates flower, all of them, all at once, on a single night, delta-wide. On those nights the oldest fish ponds glow like windows. The delta declares an impromptu festival, because the delta declares festivals the way other places declare emergencies, and no one asks what the flowering answers to, a thousand kilometers upstream, where a grain of light runs bright inside things that stand.

## The Sense of It

What the place finally communicates, if you stay long enough, is *digestion*. Everything that comes down the river — silt, seeds, timber, wrecks, light, the ground-down residue of an intelligence that finished happening long ago — the delta takes in, breaks down, builds with, eats, sings about, and passes on to the sea. Nothing is preserved here and nothing is lost here; everything is *used*. The delta is the place where the continent's past becomes the present tense, loudly, twice a day, with the tide.

It is a warm, wet, low, deafening country at peace with its own impermanence, and it does not need you to understand it either. It only asks, by its noise, that you raise your voice — that you announce yourself, that you answer when hailed, that you add your sound to the general sound, because here silence is not reverence. Here silence is what the water sounds like when it has finally taken someone, and so the delta's whole clamor, dawn shriek and rain-drum and grumbling fish and shouting farmers and drummed grief and sung joy, resolves, if you listen from far enough away, into a single continuous syllable, the loud country's version of the high country's daily tone, meaning the same thing it has always meant everywhere the river touches:

*Still here.*

# Who Lives on the Loud Delta

Start large, because the place does. The delta introduces its census from the top down, at volume, and it withholds nothing — or seems to withhold nothing, which is its particular way of keeping secrets.

## The Many and the Loud

The delta's people number in the hundreds of thousands and behave like one organism with a hundred thousand mouths. They are boat-people, dike-people, tide-farmers, net-menders, pilots, priests of the rebuilding, drummers of grief and drummers of joy (these are separate guilds, with a famous rivalry). They marry across channels, feud across channels, and reconcile across channels, all audibly. A delta conversation is conducted at a distance of thirty meters over moving water as the default, and their language has evolved accordingly — carrying vowels, tonal shouts that survive wind, a whole grammar of whistles for fog. A delta person dropped into a quiet room will stand too close and speak too softly, having never needed the middle register.

They are genealogists of water rather than blood. Ask a delta family who they are and they will tell you which channel they follow — families are named for waters, and when the river shifts a channel and the channel dies, the family keeps the name and mourns the water with the full three days of drums, exactly as for a person. There are families named for channels that have not existed in two hundred years. The name is the tomb and the monument both, in a country that builds no other kind.

Their great law is the Law of the Raised Voice: everything important must be said aloud, in the open, over water. Contracts are shouted between boats before witnesses on both banks. Marriages are proposed across a channel at low tide and answered at high, so that the water has time to consider. It is a legal system, a theater, and a flood-warning network all at once, and it means the delta has almost no private life and almost no lies that last — a lie must be maintained at full volume, in public, indefinitely, and few have the lungs.

## The Pilots

The aristocracy of the delta, insofar as it has one, is the guild of pilots — the men and women who hold the unmappable map in their heads.

Because the channels shift, the map cannot be written; because it cannot be written, it must be *lived*, and a pilot's training is a childhood spent sounding mud with a pole, tasting water for the salt line, learning the grumbler-fish choirs channel by channel, memorizing not the delta as it is but the delta as it *changes* — the grammar of its shifting, so that a pilot reading a new sandbar can say what the river is about to do the way a reader of faces can say what a friend is about to feel. Pilots are the delta's memory, and the guild's oral archive — recited in relay, in full, once a year at the rebuilding of the temples, a performance lasting four days and nights — is the closest thing the delta has to a history book. It is mostly water. Which channels lived, which died, which years the flood came shining.

The oldest sections of the recitation, the parts even the pilots no longer fully understand, speak of the river's source. Not as geography — no pilot has been within eight hundred kilometers of it — but as a *personage*: the Far Quiet, the One Upstream, who sends down the flood and the fireflour and asks nothing back, the silent partner in every contract shouted over water. The recitation opens, every year, with the same formula: *We speak because the source does not. We are the loud end of a long word.* The pilots will tell you it is poetry. It is, as it happens, also simply accurate, and it is the nearest the two ends of the river have ever come to an introduction.

## The Pond-Keepers

If the pilots are the delta's memory, the pond-keepers are its contemplatives — the closest the loud country comes to producing quiet people, and it produces them the same way the high country produces its rare noise: sparingly, at the edges, where the other end of the river leaks through.

A keeper of a threaded pond inherits the courtesies with the water: no shouting across it, no blades washed in it, and — the third courtesy, less spoken of — an hour a day spent sitting at the pond's edge doing nothing at all. The keepers call this *keeping the water company*, and they are mildly embarrassed by it, in the way of practical people caught being devout. But every keeper does it, and every keeper, pressed in confidence, will admit the same thing: that the hour is the best hour, that the slow turning of the pond quiets something in the chest, and that sometimes, on still evenings, sitting there, they feel *noticed* — without appetite and without judgment, the way a sleeping host notices a guest moving gently through the house. They do not know what notices them. It is a very long way away, and very thinly spread, and the threaded ponds are the only places in the delta dense enough with its ground-down substance to carry even this faintest edge of its attention, like the last audible ring of a bell struck in another country.

The pond-keepers are, without a guild, without a doctrine, without knowing it, the delta's congregation of the resident of the tablelands — a parish at the far end of a thousand-kilometer nave, keeping an hour of silence a day in the loudest country in the world.

## The Grief-Drummers and the Joy-Drummers

Everything in the delta is mourned and everything is celebrated, and both offices belong to the drums.

The grief-drummers are called for the dead, for dead channels, for a great tree taken by the flood, for the decommissioning of a boat too old to patch. Their music is not sad; it is *thorough* — three days of complex, rolling, unhurried rhythm that maps, the drummers say, the whole shape of the lost thing, so that the water can carry an accurate account of it to the sea. The joy-drummers answer births, weddings, the first flood-water, the flowering nights of the pilgrim's plates. The rivalry between the guilds is ancient, theatrical, and false: they are two halves of one instrument, and everyone knows it, and at the very end of the annual temple rebuilding, when the last new beam is lashed, both guilds play together for a single hour — the only hour of the year they do — and delta people travel for days to stand on the water and hear it.

What they play in that hour has no name and is never played otherwise. It is slow, and it is low, and it is felt in the sternum more than heard, and though no drummer has ever stood on the high terraces at the moment the plateau says its once-or-twice-daily tone, the resemblance, if anyone could compare, would be the resemblance of the grazers' chest-note to the land's own word: a child's imitation, at enormous distance, of a parent's voice — the loud end of the long word, answering.

## The Children

The delta's children deserve their own entry, because the delta is unusually theirs. Half the country is only knee-deep; all of it is climbable; nothing is finished and therefore nothing is forbidden the way finished things are forbidden. Delta children swim before they walk, pole a skiff by six, know two hundred whistle-signals by ten. They are the ones who watch the shining wakes on flood nights, who keep the rhyme about the pilgrim's plate, who dare each other to sit still — sitting still being the delta child's ultimate dare, its equivalent of a high place or a dark room — beside the threaded ponds at dusk.

And it is a delta child, some year — this has not happened yet, but the shape of it is already in the water — who will notice what no pilot and no priest has: that the plates look upstream, and the fireflour comes downstream, and the ponds hold quiet like the rhyme holds sense, and who will get into a skiff with a season's food and start up the river to see. It is a very long way. The river has been carrying the invitation down for ten thousand years, a grain of light at a time, patient as a clock. Somewhere at the top of it, on a bright terrace where the water hides and the hares keep their constellations, there is a sitting-place outside a dry-stone shelter that will, sooner or later, need inheriting.

The delta will drum when the child leaves — grief-drums and joy-drums both, because no one will be sure which it is. And that, too, will be accurate.

## The River

Last, count the river itself among the inhabitants, because both of its countries do, in their different grammars — the delta shouting its contracts over the water as over the shoulder of a listening elder, the plateau bowing its hares to the tone that the water, in its patient way, has been translating downstream all along.

The river is the only being that lives in both places. It rises in the quiet and it dies in the noise, and it dies every second and rises every second, and it carries the one country's finished stillness down to feed the other country's unfinished clamor, and carries nothing back but the plates' silver gaze and, someday, one child in a skiff. It is the breath between the two syllables. The high country says *still* and the low country says *here*, and neither has ever heard the other, and the sentence has never once, in ten thousand years, gone unsaid.
