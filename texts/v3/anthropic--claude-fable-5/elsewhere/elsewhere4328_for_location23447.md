# The Root Shore

You do not arrive at this place so much as come out from under something. The shadow reaches you first — hours before the land does, a coolness crossing the grass ahead of your feet — and when you finally look up from the road you understand that you have been walking, all this time, in the weather of a wall.

The wall is the world's edge, as far as anyone here is concerned. It rises out of the plains green-black and ribbed, folded like a curtain hung by someone enormous and careless, and it goes up until it doesn't: at about two kilometers the cloud takes it, a permanent grey collar that has never once, in all the generations, lifted. No one on the Root Shore has ever seen the top. There are old arguments about whether it has one. The mountain — they call it the mountain, out of a habit older than the knowledge that it isn't one — simply continues upward out of the visible world, and the people live against its foot the way other peoples live against a sea: facing it, fed by it, taking its moods for weather and its weather for law.

They do not know that the top is called the Hollow Crown by the people who live inside it. They do not know there are people inside it. Or rather — they know it the way you know a thing you have been telling children for four hundred years. Which is a kind of knowing, and a kind of not.

## The Wall

Stand at the mountain's foot and put your hand against it, and the first surprise is that it is not stone. Everyone here has always known this and it surprises them anyway, every time, all their lives: the wall gives, very slightly, under the palm. It is cool and faintly damp and grained like wood but wrong for wood — the grain runs in long slow curves, kilometers of curve, and where the rind has split and peeled you can see it goes down in layers, husk over husk, each layer the mountain's skin from an older century.

The face of it is a country in its own right, vertical and uninhabited. Ledges of shelf-growth. Long green stains where the interior weeps through. Whole hanging gardens of moss and epiphyte and bird colony in the folds, because the outside of the mountain, unlike the inside, is generous with light and everything that can cling to it does. Birds pour off the wall at dawn in rivers of their own — the shore's mornings are loud with them — and pour back at dusk, and none of them, not one species in all the shrieking multitude, has ever found a way in. The mountain's skin is closed to them. This is noted, on the shore. It is one of the facts people turn over.

And the wall is cracked. Hairline seams run up the rind — the same seams that, on the far side of meters of husk, admit those threads of grey light the crown's travelers steer by, though no one on either side of the wall knows they share them. By day, from the shore, the seams are scratches, barely there. By night they change. High up, where a seam crosses some healing wound in the tissue behind it, the crack shows a faint blue-green — thin luminous scratches on the dark of the wall, drifting over the months as the mountain flexes, going out slowly as the wounds knit, kindling elsewhere as new ones open.

The shore people call them *the veins*, or, in the older speech, *the kept lamps*. They say the mountain keeps what it was given, and gives back light. They mean the climbers — the ones their tellings say went into the mountain long ago and did not come down — and they say it as consolation, as figure of speech, standing in their doorways on clear nights pointing out the veins to children.

They are more right than any of them has ever guessed. Behind those cracks, in the black chambers, the crown people give their dead to the fresh tears, and the graves glow as they heal, and the grave-light leaks through the seams. The shore has been looking up at its cousins' cemeteries for fifteen generations and calling them lamps, and being correct.

## The Half-Day

Light, on the Root Shore, is rationed by geometry. The mountain owns the eastern sky — the whole of it, horizon to cloud — and so the sun does not rise here. It *arrives*. Mid-morning, sometimes later, depending on the season and on how far into the shadow your village sits, the sun clears the wall's shoulder far to the south or crests the cloud-collar itself, and daylight comes down the plain like something spilled: you can watch the line of it approach, a moving frontier of gold crossing the fields, and everything the line touches lights up and everything behind it waits.

The shore's whole culture is arranged around that line. Villages are named for their hour — *Third-Light*, *Latewake*, *Noon-Come* — and a settlement's wealth is, roughly, its earliness. Fields are planted by the shadow-calendar; the line's position on the boundary stones tells the month better than any moon. Children are taught to read the mountain by light the way the crown's children are taught to read it by ear: the color of the wall at first sun tells the day's rain, the sharpness of the shadow-line tells the wind aloft, a certain green-going-grey in the high folds means the falls will run heavy by evening.

Because the shore people are, before anything else, lookers. The crown breeds listeners; the open air breeds eyes. Sound dies fast out here — the wind takes it, the falls drown it, there are no walls to carry a voice anywhere — and so the shore signals in light: polished-husk mirrors flashing village to village down the shadow-line, smoke-codes, colored flags run up the watch-poles, lamps hung in agreed patterns after dark. Their language is loud and flat and unmusical, made for shouting over water. Their names are faces: a person is identified by a face-mark, painted in youth, worn in tattoo by adulthood, and their greeting — the shore's whole social order in a gesture — is to step *into* the light when you meet someone, out of whatever shade you're standing in, and show your day-side. To greet from shadow is rudeness verging on threat. Every meeting begins with being seen.

They would find their cousins' manners incomprehensible: the shaded lamps, the breathy melodic murmur, the two-note call into the dark. A crown greeting spends sound and hoards light. A shore greeting spends light and hoards nothing, because out here light is the one thing that cannot be crept up with.

## The Waters

The mountain combs the sky. That is the shore's oldest piece of natural philosophy, and it is simply true: the clouds come in off the plains, meet the wall, and the wall takes their water — cloud condensing against two vertical kilometers of cool rind, gathering in the folds, running together, until it comes down the face in the falls.

The falls are the shore's rivers, its power, its clock, and its voice — the permanent roar under all other sound, so constant that shore people who travel inland report being kept awake for weeks by silence. The water comes down white. Not foam-white: white *in the cup*, faintly clouded, carrying the fine milk of the mountain's substance, and it tastes of almost nothing, and then of something — a green edge, a mineral coolness, a sweetness that comes and goes.

The coming and going is read. This is the shore's deep science, its equivalent of the crown's route-songs: the *river-tongues*, hereditary tasters, who go down to the falls-pools at first light and take the water in small sips from white bowls and tell the villages what the mountain did inside itself this week. Because the interior weeps, and the weeping finds the seams and folds and joins the falls, and the water carries the mountain's inner chemistry out to the foot of the wall like a letter. A green bitterness: old growth dying somewhere within, a vault gone soft. A long mild sweetening: healing, wounds being fed, the repair-sugars washing down — and when, once or twice in a generation, the mountain tears itself a great new chamber in the unseen heights, the shore knows it twice. First as sound: a roll of deep thunder out of a clear sky, felt in the ground more than heard, which the shore calls *the mountain turning over*. Then, days later, as the sugar-flood — the falls running sweet for a whole season while the vast wound above pours repair into itself and the runoff carries the excess down two kilometers of fold to the shore, where it is festival: the brew-vats come out, the boiling-sheds run day and night, the year is named for it afterward. *The year of the long sweet.* The crown screams *more room* into its darkness, and the shore, far below, not knowing the words, throws the party.

In the falls-pools live the flash-fish: finger-long, quick, colored like dawn — bands of rose and green that they kindle and dim at will, talking to each other in light the way everything down here talks in light. They also sing. Very faintly, a thin crystalline pulse, chorus-made, one fixed note to a pool — and no one on the shore has ever paid the note the least attention, because who could hear it under the falls, and what would it matter? The fish are pretty, and good fried, and their song is noise.

Two kilometers up, in black still pools rimmed with ice, their descendants — carried into the dark egg by egg over ten thousand years, gone eyeless, gone colorless, having lost every gift but the note — sing the same crystalline pulse, and it is the most precious sound in the crown: the fixed points of the world, the pool-song the route-songs are tuned to, the map the people will not eat. The same voice, top and bottom of the same world. Below, drowned out and ignored beside its makers' wasted colors; above, the only thing left, and everything.

## The Advance

Now the fact under all the shore's other facts, the one its name carries: the mountain is coming.

Not falling — coming. The roots run out from the wall's foot for kilometers under the plain, and they are not still. They surface in long low ridgelines, pale-barked, warm to the touch on cold mornings, and they *move* — not visibly, never visibly, but measurably, relentlessly: an arm's length in a year along the active fronts, less elsewhere, never nothing. Fields buckle over decades. Boundary stones set by great-grandparents now stand tilted on wooden swells. A village's grazing common becomes, over a long lifetime, a country of ribs and hollows, and then root-ground entirely, and then the village moves.

So the shore builds to be abandoned. Post-and-lash construction, floors that come up in panels, shrines designed from the first prayer to be carried. They arrived at this the same way their cousins in the crown arrived at it — not as philosophy but as arithmetic — and neither people knows that the other builds the same way for the same reason, the two of them holding, at the top and bottom of the same organism, the same architectural creed: *nothing heavy, nothing final, mark the route and let the house go.* Some shore folk have gone further and stopped retreating altogether: the root-riders, who build directly on the great surfaced roots and live on the moving wood, letting their villages travel — a hand's-width a season — toward the wall. Their houses arrive where their grandparents' houses stood. They consider themselves not conquered but early.

And in one place along the whole long front, the advance is holy. There is a scar on the wall, low down, a smooth healed boss of rind the size of a hill, subtly warmer than the face around it, and the shore's tellings are unanimous — the tellings agree on almost nothing else, but on this they agree — that this is the Door. Here, long ago, an open vault-mouth breathed at ground level, and out of a world of flood or war or famine or debt or calling (pick your village; you will hear a different reason) some hundreds of people walked into the mountain carrying stone, seed, fire, and children, and the mountain closed behind them like water.

The shore's tellings disagree about what happened next, and refuse to choose, on the principle — they share this principle too, without knowing they share it — that a wrong certainty is worse than an open question. Some say the climbers died in the first dark. Some say they climb still. Some say they became the mountain's people, its kept ones, and point at the veins.

At the Door, once a generation, the shore holds the Asking. The strongest cutters take the heirloom adzes — the shore still has quarries, real stone, and remembers with a complicated pride that *the best blades went up*, that somewhere inside their ancestors' tools have been resharpened to slivers — and they cut into the scar, formally, in silence, and the mountain heals faster than they cut, as it always has, and after three days they stop, as they always do, and oil the blades, and press their palms flat against the warm wood, and go home. It stopped being an attempt long ago. It is now what it looks like: a knock.

## The Listeners

The knock is answered. This is the thing the shore is only beginning, in its most recent generations, to let itself believe.

It began with the root-listeners — originally a farmer's trick, nothing mystical: press an ear to a surfaced root and you can hear the mountain's interior, the deep structural groans arriving through the wood clear and close, and a listener learns to read them for practical ends. The long roll that means a sugar-flood is coming. The dry crack-chorus of a hard growth-year. The slow silences that mean nothing useful. Every village along the root-line keeps a listener or two, and the good ones lie out on the ridgelines at night with an ear to the bark, reading the mountain's insides the way the river-tongues read its water.

And for generations, the listeners have reported — carefully, to each other first, because it sounds like madness — that some of what comes down through the wood is *too regular*. Patterned percussion, deep and faint, arriving in phrases. Not growth: growth doesn't repeat itself. Not the falls, not the wind in the seams. Phrases — with the unmistakable prosody of intention, question-shaped, answer-shaped — descending through kilometers of living fiber from somewhere above the cloud, at all hours, in many hands.

It is, of course, the drums. The crown's struck-fiber drum-speech, the stripped-down melodic language that rides the resonant walls from band to band, rides them further than its makers know: down the great pillars, down through the old crushed vaults, out along the roots, into the ear of a farmer lying on a ridgeline under the stars.

Some listeners, in living memory, began to answer. Long-handled mauls, root-heads wrapped in hide, struck against the sounding-swells of the great roots in imitation of the phrases they hear most — clumsy, toneless, a foreigner's accent in a language they don't know is a language. They strike, and wait, ears to the wood.

And up in the black chambers of the crown, minutes later, the answer arrives: deep patterned booms out of the floor, out of the *below*, in a strange heavy hand. The crown's people have a name for this. They call it the under-echo, and their belief about it is gentle and wrong and almost right: they say the mountain keeps the sounds it is given, the way it keeps the dead — that their old drummed words settle into the deep wood and, long after, the world repeats them back, changed. *The house keeping its echoes.* When the under-echo sounds, a crown drummer will sometimes, out of respect for the dead words, drum the phrase again, correctly — and down on the ridgeline the listener hears the mountain reply, cleanly, in pattern, and lies very still in the dark with one palm flat against the root, which is the shore's gesture, as it is the crown's, for *that*.

Neither side knows. That is the present state of the world's slowest conversation: the shore half-believing it speaks to the mountain, the crown half-believing it hears the mountain's memory, and between them two kilometers of living dark through which the phrases pass, patient as sap. The listeners are teaching themselves the patterns. It will take generations. Everything here does.

## The People of the Shore

There are many of them — many by the crown's standards, anyway: tens of thousands along the root-line, in towns that would astonish their cousins, under all that unhoarded light. They are weathered, loud, broad-handed people, sun-marked down one side of the face and pale down the other in the old joke about shore complexions, farmers and brewers and quarrymen and bird-netters, herders of the shaggy dew-cattle that graze the shadow-pastures where the grass grows sweet and slow. Their eyes are their trained sense and their pride: a shore watcher can read a mirror-flash at the edge of sight, tell tomorrow's rain in the color of the high folds, spot a friend's face-mark across a market. Their word for *liar* is the same as their word for *one who signals carelessly*, and it is the worst thing you can call someone.

They live well, by the measure of the world, and provisionally, by the measure of the mountain. Their harvest years are named for the water's moods; their houses are built to be carried; their maps are repainted every decade as the root-front moves the ground the maps describe. They worship — this they can do, and their cousins cannot, because worship needs distance and the shore has nothing else: temples face the wall, the veins are read for omen, and the sects quarrel amiably over the one question that matters. Whether the advance is the slow flood that will one day take everything, and is to be mourned. Or whether — as the root-riders hold, building their traveling houses on the moving wood — the mountain is not covering the world but *housing* it, room by room, and everyone at its foot is not outside but early.

The listeners belong to neither sect. Theirs is a quieter position, arrived at flat on their backs on the ridgelines with their ears against the wood, and it goes about as far as the crown's own people go and no further. They note that the phrases come down in many hands, at all hours, and that grief and news and greeting sound the same in every language. They do not say *our people live*. They say — palm flat on the root — *something up there has not yet fallen silent as if we weren't here.*

There is one phrase the listeners answer more than any other, because it is the one that comes down most: a short figure, two notes, low then high, arriving through the wood in every hand, at every hour, from every quarter of the unseen heights — the commonest utterance in the mountain, whatever it is. The listeners learned it first and strike it best. It has become their opening, their signature, the phrase the mauls fall into without thinking: two blows on the sounding-swell, low then high, out through the roots and up through the dark.

They do not know that it is the crown's name for its own kind, and its greeting, and a complete sentence. They do not know that for three generations now, into a black hollow country of listeners two kilometers above their heads — among the screaming criers and the drifting lights and the warm unnamed patience below the floors — the Root Shore has been sending, over and over, in a strange deep accent, the one thing the crown most needs to hear from the world and has never expected it to say:

*Here — and you?*
