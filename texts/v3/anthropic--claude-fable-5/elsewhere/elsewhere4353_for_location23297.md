# The Remainder

Follow the water.

Every basin on the Provision Floor holds at exactly the rim of its overflow channel, and overflow implies an elsewhere. The channels join into canals, the canals into greater canals, the long utilitarian numbers cast into their walls growing longer as districts merge, and the whole continent of deck — you would need instruments to feel it — leans. One part in ten thousand. A slope so gentle it took the engineers a landmass to express it. Walk with the current for days and the world empties by degrees: the provision stacks come at wider intervals, then in older designs, then not at all. The pavements of grazers thin to scattered shells. The turners' gyres fall behind you one by one until the sky holds nothing. Somewhere in the second week you will notice — this is the first change that matters — that yesterday's boot-scuff is still on the deck. No burnisher herd follows you now. You have crossed out of the country of erasure, and everything you do from here on will be kept.

The light goes next. The lattice overhead develops seams, then unlit panels, then long dark reaches, the sourceless white dimming through gray into a permanent civil dusk, as though the specification for this region read *visibility, minimum, maintenance access only*. The hum beneath the deck changes key — fewer synthesizers, more pumps, and under the pumps something laboring. The condensate stops being drips and becomes, for the first time in this world, weather: the warm canal water breathing into cooler air, fog in moving banks, and then actual rain, falling slantwise, driven by the first wind you have ever felt here.

Then the deck ends.

It ends at the Sills — a rim kilometers long, crisp-edged like everything upstream, where every canal on the continent arrives at last and goes over. Not one waterfall but a hundred, gray water dropping in slow ropes into a dark that has depth and weather and a floor of its own, far below. Stand at the rim and look down through the fog and you are looking at the Remainder: the place the world exhales into. The Provision Floor is young the way a held breath is young. This is the breath let out.

The first thing you understand, descending, is that the Floor's perfection was never free. It was exported. The burnishers eat oxide and redeposit bright metal, but no process returns everything; what they cannot use is voided below-deck, collected, carried, and ejected here, along with every other thing the equilibrium declines to keep — spent components, mineral crusts scoured from the syrup channels, shell-chalk, slag from the deep foundries, the entire particulate residue of a continent that refuses to age. It falls, and has fallen for however long the Floor has been finished, and it has built a landscape. Terraces of slag rise in strata you can read like tree rings — a dark band here where some district's stacks were refitted, a pale seam of chalk there that must record a century of brood-shells. The world above has no history; this is where its history was sent. The geology of the Remainder is the Floor's receipts, stacked.

Between the terraces lie the dunes: oxide dust in drifts the color of dried blood, rust that never got to happen upstream, happening here all at once, moved into long ripples by the wind off the warm falls. When the fog lifts you can see the light of the world you left — the lattice of the Provision Floor glowing above the rim of the Sills, a pale band along one whole horizon, a false dawn that never rises and never sets. Everything in the Remainder is lit by it, at a low angle, forever. The shadows here all point away from home.

And things grow.

This is the reversal you will need the longest to absorb. Nothing grows on the Provision Floor — growth there is a permitted exception, metered and closed. But the outfall water still carries sugars the braid never caught, and where it soaks into the oxide dust, mats take hold: dark, ungoverned, oxblood and black-purple, spreading because they can rather than because anything maintains them. They mound over decades into soft reefs. Beneath the oldest reefs is a layer of their own dead compacted into something spongy and rich — the first soil this world has ever produced, made entirely of things that were allowed to die and stay dead. Kneel and put your hand into it. It is cool. Nothing on the Floor is cool. It smells of iron and fermentation and it is the only ground in the world that was not designed.

Now the inhabitants, and here you must be told plainly where they come from, because the Remainder is not merely the Floor's midden. It is its overflow in every sense.

The census balances. Birth equals death, population is conserved — but conservation is an achievement, not a given, and the ledger's corrections run downhill like everything else. The paviors' brood-grooves are cut into the overflow channels. When a district runs surplus, the correction is simply this: the extra young, still soft, still coin-sized, are swept from the grooves by the current before they can harden into pavement, and carried — days, weeks, the length of the continent — to the Sills, and over. Turner chicks caught in the exhaust winds. Segments of braid flushed with a clouded basin's water. The Remainder is populated by the world's extra children. Most do not survive the falls. The plunge pools below the Sills are thick with braid-kin grown huge on what arrives, and that is the first sorting.

Those that live become something the Floor has no word for, because the Floor has no words and no need of the concept: they become *afraid*, and fear, it turns out, is the seed of everything else.

The runners are pavior-kin, but you would not mistake them for deck. Their shells never harden true — scarred, asymmetric, patched with mat-growth like moss — and they are fast, skirts repurposed for travel, grazing the reefs in loose bands that scatter at a shadow. Watch a band long enough and you will find its center: an old one. There are no old animals on the Provision Floor — you will see your first clouded breathing-slit here, your first chipped scute worn smooth again by decades — and the old ones lead, because in a place where the fog has seasons and the hunters have habits, memory is worth following. First old age. First leadership. Both invented, or recovered, in the rust.

The hunters are the gaunts: taker-kin, stilts lengthened for pursuit, and they pursue, because nothing here walks calmly into anything's arms. They stalk, they rush, they fail more often than they succeed, and sometimes they starve — the scheduled flow of death replaced by effort, and effort shadowed by fear on both sides of it. And yet. At the foot of the Sills there is nearly always one gaunt standing motionless in the old posture, sometimes for days, facing the falls, waiting in the ancient way for a runner to detach itself and come. None ever has. None ever will. Whether this is memory or vestige, grief or reflex, is a question of exactly the shape you learned to stop asking upstream, and you will not manage to stop asking it here.

The keepers are laddered-kin, and they are the ones to watch. No stacks to queue on, no channels to drink from in turn — so the great inherited orderliness of their line has bent itself to a new object. They forage the mats and they *hoard*: caches of dried reef-stuff tucked under slag, defended, remembered, willed somehow to offspring. First property. And where there is property — you will see this within a month — first theft, and where theft, first deception: keepers dig false caches, conspicuously, and watch from the dunes to see who comes. Their eyes are still the largest in the world, still dark, still lidless, and they will still find yours. But the look has changed. Upstream it confirmed you existed and closed the matter. Here the matter stays open. Here the look contains the question.

And the keepers mark. The deck plates that fall among the spoil are unburnished and stay unburnished; a scratch on them lasts forever, and the Remainder is therefore the only writing surface the world has ever offered. Keeper territories are edged in scratch-clusters. Inside their dens the clusters thicken — tallies, or maps of caches, or records of something, or nothing, or claw-sharpening elaborated by generations of imitation into pattern. You will make your hypotheses, and you will recognize the shape of your failure from before: upstream, census and prayer could not be told apart; down here, writing and habit cannot. At this distance from the builders, in both directions, the distinction may simply not exist.

Overhead, when the storm winds stand up off the warm falls, the strays ride — turner-kin whose family gyres were left behind at the moment of falling, who circle nothing fixed, who wander. And they land. Turners never land; strays land badly, and walk worse, and eat what they find dead on the dunes, which is the last of the firsts and maybe the largest: first carrion, first scavenger, first economy of the unplanned dead.

You will find bones. Whole middens of them, chalk-white against the rust — impossible upstream, where every taking is enclosed and every residue reprocessed. Here a death lies where it happened until the strays and the dust are done with it, and then it stays longer. You will find, too, among the spoil, things that are not scrap and not bone: a hinged object with no evident purpose; panels bearing the same long serial numbers as the drainage channels a continent upstream, which no one reads here either, except you; and once — deep in the oldest terrace, and you will tell no one because there is no one — a door, lying flat, corroded open at last, giving onto solid conduit. The machinery rerouted around its own entrances long ago. The way down was answered before the question finished falling.

Because the machinery is here too. That is the final thing, and it changes the meaning of everything above it. At the base of the oldest terraces the deck's underside comes down in vast buttresses, and set into them are the intake mouths: slow, grinding, patient apertures that eat the ancient spoil and carry it back beneath the world — raw feed for the synthesizers, raw metal for the burnishers' eternal redeposition. The Remainder is not a grave. It is a queue. The whole landscape creeps, over centuries, toward the mouths; every terrace, every reef, every midden, every scratched plate and cached hoard and elder's worn shell is on its way back upstream, eventually, as syrup and shine and the crisp bright edge of a weld seam that will read as new forever. The Floor's youth is made of the Remainder's past, continuously. The animals here give the mouths a wide, specific berth — the one avoidance so uniform across every species that it reads less like fear than like knowledge. The single taboo of a place that otherwise permits everything.

So the two countries hold each other up, and neither could be itself alone. The Floor exports its entropy, its surplus, its dead time; the Remainder exports it back as raw matter and receives, in exchange, water, warmth, sugar, and children. One is a sentence that reached its period. The other is where the sentence was sent to keep happening — in scratch and hoard and pursuit and mud, written by the world's remainders, on the only pages the world keeps, until the mouths take the page.

You will stay longer than you intend. Here your footprints last, which after the Floor feels almost obscene, then feels like breathing. No stack retunes itself for you; you dig reef-mat and learn which strata of it your body accepts, you learn the tides of the fog, and one gray interval a gaunt follows you for eleven hours across the dunes before turning aside, and you discover that your own loan against the future was never called in — only suspended, upstream, in the warm water, where nothing needed it. Here it is needed. You will not be sure the feeling is worse.

And on still nights, when the wind lies down and the falls run quiet in their ropes, the calls come over the Sills — the two tones, faint, carried from some basin far up in the light of the false dawn. Then you will hear the answerer. There is at least one exiled caller in the Remainder, grown from a washed-down chick at the foot of the falls, throat-bell intact, first tone perfect — the carrier, it turns out, is in the body, not the ledger. It stands on the highest terrace facing the band of light and it answers every call that comes down, its second tone shifting through small precise values that no census ever requested: counting, if it is counting, the runners and the keepers and the strays and the bones and you — the whole population of the uncounted, reported nightly to a world that never asks.

Whether anything above receives it — whether, in some distant basin, the pavement's pause lasts one drip longer for the answer having come — you cannot know from below the rim. The animals of the Remainder do not seem to wonder. They have learned, unlike everything upstream, how to live inside an open question.

That may be all the difference there is. It is enough to build a country on.
