**The Quench**

The heat has to go somewhere.

That is the whole of the physics, and it is worth holding onto as you leave the plateau, because everything below the haze follows from it. The Anvil is warm the way a kiln is warm — the residual heat of formation still working its way out after however many centuries — and *out* has a direction. Out is down. Out runs along the pale lamellar veins, which do not stop at the rim-rock. Nothing about the veins ever suggested they would.

You descend the way you came, through the gap, and for the first two thousand feet the world is still the plateau's: virgin stone, knife-edged, dry as the inside of a bell. Then you cross a line. It is drawn across the entire forty-mile face of the escarpment at a single altitude, straight as a water level, because that is what it is — the height at which the weather has always broken and always will. Above the line, rock that has never been touched. Below it, rock that has been touched constantly, greedily, for centuries: black with wet, shagged with fern, streaked white where waterfalls thread down and gray where they used to. The escarpment has two ages at once. Above the line it is immortal. Below the line it is dying at a tremendous rate — the country rock sodden, frost-split, sloughing off in a rockfall a day somewhere along its length, so that the sound of distant stone hitting distant scree is simply part of the air here, arriving late and rounded, like artillery with the anger taken out.

And out of the rotting lower cliffs, the veins stand proud.

The pale not-quite-quartz does not weather. The stone around it rots away and the veins remain, emerging from the cliff face like the ribs of the world's reinforcement — buttresses of milky, metal-sheened mineral, some of them ten feet thick, running unbroken from up under the rim down through the wet zone and into the haze below. Lay your palm on one, even here, even miles beneath the nearest millwort, and you feel it: warm. Warmer than the rock, warmer than the air. Traffic.

Birds have found this. Of course birds have found this. The vein-ledges of the lower escarpment carry rookeries of a small gray-winged swift, thousands upon thousands of them, and the colonies on the warmest veins have given up brooding entirely — they lay their eggs directly on the mineral and let the Anvil's exhaust do the sitting. It works. It has worked for long enough to become instinct. Every wall-swift over the water below was incubated by the waste heat of a landscape that has never seen a bird, will never see a bird, and is at this moment faithfully transmitting, through the same warm stone, the correspondence of plants that do not know the eggs are there.

Then the haze takes you, and under the haze is the sea.

**The water**

They call it the Quench, and the name is older than any joke about anvils — it is simply what the water does. It is a terminal sea, forty miles across, brackish, without an outlet, fed by every river of the surrounding country, all of which reversed their courses within living memory and now run inward, downhill, to here. It is blood-warm in the shallows and warmer at depth, which is backwards, and the backwardness is the plateau's doing: the veins come down through the basin floor and open onto it, and the heat of the Anvil's formation — centuries of it, the whole thermal debt of a poured province — is paid out through those vents into the bottom of the sea.

So the Quench smokes. At dawn, before the wind sorts it out, the entire surface steams like a cup, and the haze that veils the plateau's forty-mile view — that soft floor the Anvil seems to float on — is nothing but this: the sea's breath, rising. The two places wear each other. The plateau stands on the Quench's exhalation; the Quench lives on the plateau's cooling. Neither has ever seen the other clearly. Perhaps twice a year the air dries out and the haze fails, and then the fishing towns get their view: the full escarpment, two and a half miles of it, the rain line ruled across its face, and above the line that impossible clean stone going up and up, fringed at each terrace lip with a thin green seam of something no one down here has ever touched.

The warmth explains the weather, and the weather explains the plateau's driest mystery. Storms build over the Quench constantly — the sea is a heat engine, the air above it always loaded — and every one of them climbs the escarpment and fails at the same altitude. The plateau's own warmth ponds over the heights in a permanent inversion, a lid of dry air the convection cannot punch through. The plateau has never been rained on because it stands above its own lid. And the rain it refuses does not vanish. It falls here instead — the plateau's ration and the basin's own, both, forever. The Quench is rained on for two.

**The Going-Down**

The sea is exactly as new as the plateau. They have the same birthday, spread across the same slow century, and everyone on this shore knows it, because the century is still within the reach of grandmothers.

When the melt rose — when whatever rose, rose — the country beside it paid. The basin foundered as the Anvil lifted: not in a night, nothing in this world happens in a night, but at a handspan a season during the worst of it, which is slow enough to outwalk and fast enough to watch. They call it the Going-Down. Fields tilted and would not drain. Rivers hesitated, stood still in their beds for a season, and turned around. The towns of the old lowland walked uphill ahead of the water, a street at a time, dismantling themselves and going — the houses here are still framed with pegs rather than nails, built to be taken apart, because that is what houses learned to be during the Going-Down and no one has seen a reason to unlearn it.

The old country is still down there. The Quench is warm but nearly airless at depth, and mineral-charged, and it preserves. Divers with weighted lines have come up talking about standing forests — whole drowned woodlands upright in the dark, leafless, patient — and about roads, walls, the straight lines of orchards. And they come up talking about the *sheen*. Because the vent water rises charged with the pale mineral in solution, and the drowned wood is taking it up, ring by ring, cell by cell. The old forests are glassing. Timber dredged from the deep water is half stone already: it takes a polish like horn, carries a faint lamellar shimmer, and will not burn. The sea is casting a copy of the old country in the plateau's own material, one molecule at a time. The Anvil was poured in a century. The copy will take a million years, and it will be more detailed.

The sinking never entirely stopped. It has slowed to the tempo of everything else: the basin settles a fraction of an inch a year, in step — surveyors on both ends of the escarpment could confirm this for each other, if either knew the other was measuring — with the rise of the Caldron. One ledger, two columns. The shore towns keep their own record of it in brass pins driven into the harbor stones, a new pin every generation, each one a finger-width above the last, marking the tide that never turns.

**The scribes**

Where the veins open onto the seafloor, the vents wear reefs.

The builders are sessile, colonial, unglamorous — polyp-things in shelly tubes, crusted over the warm mineral in mounds the size of barns. What distinguishes them is their appetite. They do not feed on the vent chemistry alone; they feed on its *schedule*. Their growth is keyed to warmth, and the warmth arriving at a vent-mouth is not steady — it is the traffic, the millwort correspondence, micro-degree pulses that have spent months and years descending through forty miles of stone — and the reef bands with it. Every pulse is a growth line. Sawn and polished, a slice of reef shows lamination fine as the grooves of an engraving, whorled, phrased, dense with what a patient eye would eventually have to admit is structure.

The dredge crews bring it up by the ton. They call it thinking-stone — for the pattern, which everyone agrees looks like it is *about* something — and it is sawn into slabs and sold along the whole shore. It takes a beautiful polish. There is a slice of it on nearly every mantel in every town above the brass pins: the plateau's mail, decades deep, transcribed by an animal, decorating the houses of people who do not suspect it is writing. Why would they? No one down here has heard a millwort tick. No one up there knows the sea is taking dictation.

One person is close. A naturalist in one of the middle towns — she catalogs the swift rookeries, mostly — has spent nine years aligning thinking-stone slices from different reefs by their storm-marks, and has found the same phrases arriving at different vents in sequence, days apart, always in the same geographic order, always spreading from the direction of the escarpment. She has concluded that the sea floor has a pulse and that the pulse travels. She is one inference from the truth, and it is a long inference, and she may not make it, and the plateau, as usual, is not going to help.

Meanwhile the fishing fleets, who have never read anything, know the map by heart. The fish of the Quench school along the warm lanes — the smoke roads, the fishermen call them, for the way the water above a shallow vein steams first at dawn — and so the lanterns of the night fleet lay themselves out along the buried vein network as faithfully as the millworts do on the heights. Put a child on a headland after dark and she is looking at the same map the plateau draws in green along its terrace lips: the same wiring, charted twice, by lamplight from below and by chlorophyll from above, neither chart knowing the other exists.

**The shore towns**

The census here is a fool's errand run in the opposite direction. On the Anvil, the honest first answer was that nothing lives there. Here, the honest first answer is that everything does, and the honest second answer is that the count is obsolete before you finish taking it. The Quench seethes. Swifts, gulls, eels, reed-cats, midges in pillars, fish in nations; the wrack line rewritten twice a day; nothing keeping its address, nothing holding its shape, everything eating, molting, rotting, replaced. The plateau's shutter needed to stay open for decades to catch its residents moving. Down here you could not build a shutter fast enough to catch them holding still.

And the towns are part of the seethe, with one exception, one habit of geology in them. They retreat. It is their oldest custom and their deepest one: the water rises a finger a year, and the towns move uphill ahead of it, a house this decade, a street this generation, everything pegged and portable, every family holding a high deed — land upslope, surveyed and paid for, that no one living will build on. A town here is a procession too slow to see. Measured over a century, the shore towns climb at two to four yards a year, which is — though there is no one on either end of the escarpment positioned to enjoy this — exactly a porter's stride. The world appears to have settled on a single walking pace for anything that carries its whole life on its back.

Their economy is the Anvil's exhaust, top to bottom. The potteries and glassworks cluster on the warm ground where veins outcrop along the shore, firing their kilns on heat that was banked into a plateau before anyone's grandmother was born. The fisheries follow the smoke roads. The dredgers sell the drowned country back to its descendants as unburnable furniture and thinking-stone. There is no winter — the sea will not allow one — so there is no lean season, no stored harvest, no hunger calendar; what they have instead of seasons is the shadow. Each evening the escarpment's shadow comes out over the water, and the distance it reaches before dusk swallows it swells and shrinks with the year. The far towns mark their festivals by which harbors go dark early. The thing that drowned the old country keeps the new one's calendar. Nobody down here finds this bitter, particularly. It is simply the arrangement.

And this shore is where the plateau's words come from. The botanists who will one day fight over whether the millworts are plants drink in these harbors now, between expeditions up the gap. *Millwort* itself will be coined at one of these tables, by someone eventually. Every name the Anvil will ever carry is being minted down here in a currency of breath, by the shortest-lived things in its economy, and will travel up to it the only way anything travels up: on foot, through the rim-rock, in a visitor.

---

**The ledger**

*Nothing here is lost,* the plateau says of itself, and it is telling the truth, and this is where the truth is kept. Everything the Anvil refuses — water, weather, rot, noise, birds, dying — pools two and a half miles below it, in the bath. The forge metaphor was never a metaphor. Anvil and quench: the two halves of one apparatus, both still in use, no smith in evidence. The blow was struck a few centuries ago. One half is still ringing — that single sustained aeolian note over the terraces, the ticking-over in the trunks. The other half is still taking the heat, and steaming, and will be taking it for ten thousand years.

Which returns us, at the end of the account, to you — because you came up through the gap in the rim-rock *from* somewhere, and this is the somewhere, and you left something on the heights. Your heartbeat went into the veins through your boot soles: seventy events a minute, the highest-frequency signal the network has ever carried, propagating outward and downward at the speed of warm stone. Transit time to the basin floor: years. But arrival is certain — the wiring runs in perfect tolerance — and somewhere below the smoke roads a vent will breathe your pulse into a reef, a flutter of warmth seventy to the minute for the eleven minutes you stood on that outcrop without knowing what it was, and the scribes will band it into shell the way they band everything, without comment.

Decades after that, a dredge will bring the reef up. It will be sawn, and polished, and sold, and set on a mantel in a pegged wooden house three streets uphill of the water, and some evening a child will take it down and turn it in the lamplight because one stretch of the banding shimmers strangely — too quick, too regular, like nothing else in the stone.

The Anvil will spend a very long time deciding what you were. The Quench decided the moment you crossed the rain line — warm, brief, downhill-bound, like everything it loves — and then wrote you into shell by accident, and sold you as a curio.

That is the other way a world has of keeping someone forever.
