**The Settled Country**

You reach it the way you reach any ordinary place, which is the first of its disguises: a branch line, then a carrier's cart, then a green lane between hedges so old they have gone woody at the knee. No shaft, no stair, no sweating rail. The vale opens below the lane without ceremony — four miles of pasture and water-meadow folded between low hills, a river wandering through it in fat unhurried loops, a village of pale stone at the crossing, a single steep-sided tor standing off-center like a thumb. It is called Newcombe Vale, and it is one of the small jokes of the world that nobody hears the name anymore. The antiquarians who come in summer with their sketchbooks remark on it: so ancient a landscape, they say, so venerable in every line, and saddled with a name that means *the new valley* — some medieval clerk's error, surely, some corruption of an older word. They are wrong the way a man reading a clock backwards is wrong. The name is the one honest document in the parish. The vale is one hundred and forty-one years old.

In the ledgers of the works — three days north by rail, visible from the vale's upper rim on the hardest winter days as a grey needle on the horizon wearing its own private hood of cloud, which the villagers call the Candle and take for a distant volcano or a distant mill and are, in a sense, right both times — in those ledgers, Newcombe Vale is *Consignment One*. It was the first landscape the works ever delivered. Everything the copper room above the Candle's cloud is doing now — the pouring, the curing, the tolling, the careful witnessed infancy — was done here first, at scale, under open sky, and then the sky was allowed to do what the glass dome will never permit: it was allowed to *weather*.

This must be understood first, because the eye resists it even harder here than in the room. Nothing in the vale looks young. The tor is skirted with frost-shattered scree. The lanes are sunken shoulder-deep from a century of hooves and rain. Lichen maps every south-facing stone in slow orange continents; rabbits have undermined the west pastures into soft honeycomb; there are badger setts driven six feet into the hillside above the mill, the badgers having found the ground diggable and good and asked no further questions. The cast stone was made to be kind — it weathers eagerly, faster than true limestone, and in fourteen decades it has manufactured for itself the appearance of fourteen millennia. The county's geology, as published, dates the vale to an epoch so deep that the error, were it ever discovered, would be the largest in the history of the science. It will not be discovered. The stone keeps only one secret badly: it carries no fossils. The quarrymen at the parish quarry — where men cut building stone from ground that was itself poured, castings of a casting, the whole village walled and roofed in second-generation rock — note only that combe stone is sweet under the saw and never turns up a shell. *Clean as combe stone*, they say of an honest bargain, and go on cutting.

But if you know what the room above the clouds looks like — if you have pressed your palm to curing rock and read the swollen clipboard — the vale becomes legible, and once legible it never stops confessing. Stand on the tor and look down at the field pattern: the hedges, which root best where the ground holds water, have followed the old pour seams line for line, so that the whole delivery plan of the vale is drawn out in hawthorn and may, the antiquarians say, be a system of ancient field boundaries, wonderfully preserved. The river — the Bourne — long ago won every argument the tender's stream is only now beginning; the channels cast for it survive as dead-straight abandoned reaches up on the second terrace, dry, moss-floored, semicircular in section, mapped in the county history as Roman leats. At the vale's head, the Dropping Well sheets its water over travertine terraces four feet thick — a formation that should represent tens of thousands of years of patient deposition and represents instead a hundred and forty of the young rock's furious leaching, the pale crust at the casting-house grate grown up into a landmark where villagers hang gloves and bird's nests on strings to be turned to stone for visitors. There is a crag on the north slope that weeps rust in long ochre tears every autumn — an armature rib, corroding sociably in its bed — and the crag is called the Bleeding Stone and has three separate legends attached to it, all involving a martyr, none involving iron. And scattered across the vale are perhaps a dozen boulders that ring. Struck with a knuckle or a pebble they give a clear bronze-ish note, the cure-ring, the sound of the made rock answering *finished* — a sound that has been fading for a century as weathering roughens the surfaces, so that the old people say the stones sang louder when their grandparents were young, and they are exactly right, and children still queue to strike the loudest of them, the Grey Ram, on their way home from school.

Beneath all of it, four fathoms down, lies what the vale was poured to cover. The old documents call it the Scald: a mining field that collapsed and kept collapsing, a treeless subsided country of orange water and open shafts, a wound that had stopped being land. The works did not repair it. The works entombed it, and grew a country over the grave. This is what the terrain trade is *for* — this is the answer to the question the copper room does not post anywhere — and the vale mostly keeps its dead ground decently buried. Mostly. A well-digger in the last century brought up a chimney pot from thirty feet, glazed, intact, and it sits in the church porch labeled ROMAN. And there was a flood year in living memory when one spring on the east side ran orange for nine days, and the parish still speaks of the year the Bourne bled, and does not know that it was the year the vale's grief rose briefly to the surface and was diluted, and passed.

Once a year the village holds its strangest and oldest observance, and holds it without the least idea why. On a morning in late spring — Ringing Day — the church bell, which was cast at the works and given to the parish at the delivery, and which is a bronze cousin of the hand bell hanging by a door three days north, is struck a single time at noon. One stroke. Then the whole vale keeps silence, in the fields, in the lanes, in the school with the children standing, until the note has died away completely. The vicar believes it commemorates a plague. It commemorates the afternoon in the spring of a year one hundred and forty-one years gone when the last section of the vale answered the hammer with a ring instead of a thud, and the country was tolled open, and the enamel plaques came down, and the world was declared set.

**The Walker of the Settled Country**

Begin with a sound, because that is where the inhabitant first appears: a light double tap, stone on stone, heard from behind a hedge or up on the scree, moving slowly along the contours of the vale over the course of a day. The villagers, hearing it, say *the Stonechat's about* — after the little bird whose call is exactly that, two pebbles knocked together — and they mean the man, and they smile, and they do not follow him. He is a fixture the way the Grey Ram is a fixture. A tall, weather-cured man in his sixties, long-legged from a working life spent as the district's letter-carrier, with a listening tilt to his head that people take for deafness and is in fact the opposite. He walks the bounds of the vale and the network of its lanes with a canvas satchel, and in the satchel there is a folding measuring rod, a small linen bag of fine white lime, a notebook, and a bronze-headed hammer of a particular works pattern — the twin, though neither of them will ever know it, of a hammer that taps fresh castings in a copper room above the clouds. Hers listens forward, for the ring that means *cured, ready*. His listens backward, for the dull note that means *hollow* — the voice of a void where an armature rib has rusted to red mud and the young-old rock has begun, in secret, to stand over nothing.

The post came down to him through his grandmother, and to her through her uncle, and so on back along a single family thread to a woman who had cooked for the pour crews during the two years of the Great Works, when the vale was an encampment of trestles and steaming pipes, and who stayed on when the works struck its plant and rolled north, holding a zinc document tube and a quarterly stipend and a duty nobody in the parish was ever told about. The tube is under his bed. It holds the as-poured drawings, which smell of his grandmother's pipe, and which include one sheet, stamped with the works' mark, showing the whole vale at one inch to the chain, annotated in a copperplate hand: *rehearsed at the House; model struck on acceptance*. He has thought about that phrase all his life. Somewhere north, before the vale existed, the vale existed in miniature — the tor four meters high, the Bourne a warm rill arguing with its channel, orchids wired to the little outcrop with brass clips — and was watched, and measured, and then, when the true pour below had set and rung, was broken up with hammers so the room could rehearse the next commission. The room does not remember its children. That, he understands, is what his family is for. He is the vale's memory, kept outside the vale's body, the way all memory is kept.

Because he holds the log. Not the drawings only — the *log*, the vale's infancy book, bound in three volumes, the columns unbroken across a dozen hands, the oldest entries made on-site by the first attendants in the two years after the Ringing: *first pool, first meander, first deposit*. He reads it the way other men read scripture, which is to say seasonally, and at the relevant places. There is a page recording the day the young Bourne first disputed a cast bend — *Stake 9: three millimeters of deviation; noted* — and he walks out each year on the anniversary of that entry and stands at what Stake 9 became, which is a broad oxbow hung with willows big enough to hide a wagon, water and a hundred and forty springs having made their own arrangements entirely, and he takes off his hat, feeling slightly foolish, and puts it back on, and notes the visit in his own pages, keeping the column unbroken.

His official duty is the annual walking of the bounds and the sounding of the named stones — sixty-one test points listed in the log with names half-issued and half-grown, the Ash Anvil, Bride's Brow, the Grey Ram, the Long Sisters — and the filing of a return to the works. The return is one line. *No defects found.* The sentence runs back through the ledger in a hundred and forty years of changing hands, an archive of nothing, the most beautiful maintenance record in the works' possession, read by no one. Each quarter-day a money order arrives, and each spring a sealed tin of grouting lime, addressed not to his name but to his title — *To the Attendant, Consignment One* — signed out by a clerk who has never once asked what Consignment One is, the payment having long since fossilized into the ledgers the way the single bell stroke fossilized into the parish year. And the return is, by now, a white lie the tradition sanctions. There are defects. There have been for thirty years. Where his hammer finds a dull note he comes back by night with a hand drill and the lime bag and he grouts the void and dresses the hole with dust and lichen scrapings, and mends the vale in secret the way a sexton straightens stones, and writes *no defects*, meaning: nothing the ground and I could not settle between ourselves.

Except one. Under Hare Meadow, on the east side, above the deepest of the buried shafts of the old Scald, there is a note his hammer has been finding for eleven years, and mapping, and it is not a note the lime bag answers. A drum-skin note, wide and slow and spreading — the entombed country subsiding at last beneath its lid, a hollow now broader than the church, growing by feet in a year. The regulations in the zinc tube are unambiguous. He is to report it. The works would come with drills and engineers and daylight, and in shoring up the meadow they would necessarily explain it, and the vale would learn in a single season what it is — would have its fourteen thousand borrowed years taken away and its hundred and forty handed back, its leats and its singing stones and its Bleeding Stone all corrected, its name suddenly audible again. He has not reported it. He has staked the meadow's margins with small numbered stakes that the farmer takes for drainage marks, and he measures, and he keeps a private page, and he writes *no defects*, and he waits. He knows what he is permitting. Some spring or some wet autumn Hare Meadow will fold inward with a sound like a held breath released, and the vale will have its first true landslip — its first grief, its first wholly unauthorized act, geology performed at last with no attendant's leave — and the parish will call it an act of God and be, in a sense, right. He has read the older margins of the log closely enough to believe this is not betrayal but the post's actual tradition, the one handed down beneath the official one, the same tradition that once let an unmanifested fern stand and lets, at this moment, a soft-footed stowaway drink from a chipped saucer in a copper room he will never see: the landscape is permitted, quietly, to surprise.

On Ringing Day he stands in the churchyard with everyone else. The bell is struck once at noon by the oldest child in the school, that being the custom, and the note goes out over the vale — over the tor and the oxbow and the Dropping Well and the hedges that are a construction drawing in hawthorn — and the whole country keeps silence until it dies. He is the only one present who knows what the silence is for. He stands with his hat off among his neighbors, hearing under the fading bronze the drum-note of Hare Meadow that only his mind's hammer can sound, and beneath that, fathoms down, the old orange country keeping its own counsel in the dark; and he thinks, some years, of the Candle on the northern horizon burning under its permanent cloud, and of the room at the top of it where some new consignment is at this moment ticking as it cools — some other wound's healing being rehearsed in miniature, moss taking the warm castings, a stream just beginning to disagree with its channel — and of the woman up there whose existence he deduces from the unbroken columns the way she might deduce his, pressing her thumb into rock that has not yet decided anything, writing it all down for someone else, later.

The note dies. The vale exhales. The attention that has held this ground since before the ground existed — two hands wide in the room, twelve hands deep in the log, a family long in the walking — goes on without hurry, having simply grown too large for any one keeper, the way everything living does. And the return goes north in the spring post, one line, in a careful hand: *No defects found*, which is not the truth, and is not a lie, and is what an attendant says of a country that has finally, after a hundred and forty-one years, begun to keep secrets of its own.
