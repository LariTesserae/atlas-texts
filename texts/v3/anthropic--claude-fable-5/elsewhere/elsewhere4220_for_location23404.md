**The Quench, Where the Wind Comes Down**

The plateau ends the way it does everything: gradually, and without announcement. There is no rim in the dramatic sense — no lip, no drop, no moment of standing at an edge. There is only a long, patient change in the arithmetic of the ground, the swells beginning to lean all one way, and the wind — the wind is how you know. On the interior the wind is directionless, a cold mineral breath from everywhere. Out here it has made up its mind. It is going somewhere, and it has been going there for ten million years, and if you walk with it long enough you will arrive where it arrives.

Here is the thing about the Anvil Plateau that the plateau itself never notices: it breathes twice. The first breath everyone on top knows — the vents, the sighs, the steam going up. The second breath goes down. The plateau's skin, for all the warmth banked beneath it, radiates its heat away into the black sky all night of its endless night, and the air lying against that skin grows cold, and dense, and heavy, and slides. It slides outward across the whole tableland, gathering, and pours off the plateau's flanks the way water pours off a roof — except there is no rain in this, only air, a continent's worth of chilled air descending three vertical kilometers, forever. On top it is just weather, the long cold draw underfoot of the dark. At the flanks it becomes a current with a name's worth of character, and if this world's creatures named things it would be named. Call it the Long Fall.

You go down with it, because there is no other way down and no reason to want one.

The flank is not a cliff. Nothing here is a cliff; everything that was going to fall has fallen, and this is where it fell. The plateau's skirts are a ramp of ancient ruin a hundred kilometers deep — every edge the tableland ever shed, every young sharp thing it ever was, ground down and graded and settled and lapped smooth by the falling wind until the whole descent is one vast dark banked curve, like the inside of a bowl the size of a country. The wind accelerates as you descend. It also, strangely, warms — compression heating it degree by degree as it falls into thicker air — so that the descent is a long crescendo of both violence and comfort, the gale rising to something that would strip an unwary walker off the slope, the cold loosening its teeth all the while. The air thickens. Pressure returns like a hand laid flat on the chest.

And then — somewhere in the last third of the descent — a sense you had forgotten you owned turns itself back on.

*Smell.* The plateau's wind carries no scent but mineral; ten million years of it have crossed nothing worth smelling. This wind, falling, begins to pick up what rises to meet it: salt, first, faint and unmistakable. Then sulfur, warm and rotten and rich. Then something underneath both that the plateau has nothing to compare to — the smell of wet life in quantity, of things dissolving and growing in the same water. You have been in a world of stone and fur and steam. You are descending into a world of *broth*.

The wind dies where the ground does. That is the last thing it does, and it does it all at once: the Long Fall pours down the final apron of the skirts, out across a fringe of dark gravel flats, and runs into air it can no longer sink through, and spends itself in a few last flat kilometers of gusts, and is gone. There is a place — you can stand in it — where the loudest wind in the world becomes a stillness so complete you can hear your own blood. That place is a shoreline.

Because below the Anvil, along the whole southern quadrant of its foot, there is a sea.

It has to be there, once you think about it. The plateau is a pressurized system, chambers and brine aquifers and gas seams plumbed together under a kilometer of cap rock, and a pressurized system relieves itself at its lowest seam or it is not a system, it is a bomb. The lowest seams outcrop here, at the base of the skirts, and here the plateau's deep water leaks — warm mineral springs in their thousands, a line of seeps running along the foot of the wall for as far as the foot of the wall runs, feeding a brine sea so dense and so salt it does not freeze, though the air above it never sees a sun. The sea is black, and deep, and blood-warm at the bottom where the same magmatic plumbing that fires the plateau's vents fires the seafloor's, and cold at the top where the Long Fall's exhausted breath settles onto it, and between those two layers it turns over slowly, ponderously, on cycles of years, a thermal engine running at the pace of geology.

Understand what you are looking at, standing on that hem of gravel. The plateau and the sea are not neighbors. They are the same machine. The sea's water sinks into the deep fracture systems and is heated and driven up through three kilometers of rock and exhaled from the vent-throats of the tableland as steam; it beads on cold basalt and is drunk by grazers, or falls as the brief blood-warm chain-rains, and what is not drunk runs off, and gathers, and comes back down the skirts, and returns to the sea, carrying the plateau's minerals with it. Every molecule the round furred timetable-keepers drink up there in the dark was seawater once and will be seawater again. The plateau is the boiler. The sea is the condenser. If the tableland is the anvil, this is the trough beside it, the quench, where hot and cold meet and neither ever wins, only exchange — a forge running at full production, in the dark, with no smith, which is the only way anything in this world has ever run.

But the sea has done one thing the plateau never did. The sea has made light.

You see it before you understand it: a line along the water where the seeps come out, blue-green, faint, continuous, kilometers long — the first light in this world that is not the ember-red of a vent's throat. After the plateau, after the hour it took your eyes to find the difference between black ground and black sky, it is almost unbearable. It is not much light. It is more light than the rest of the world contains.

---

What lives in the Quench lives the way the Quench runs: in layers, in circulation, and — alone in all this night — in sight of itself.

**The seep-mats first**, because they are not strangers. The warm springs along the foot of the wall wear consortiums banded by temperature and chemistry, ring on ring, refinery-flat — and they are the same lineage as the mats of the plateau above. More than lineage: the mats of the tableland are these mats' emigrant children. Somewhere in deep time the consortiums of the seafloor found the flank's fracture systems and began to climb — not intending, only growing, following warmth and chemistry up through the plumbing of the skirts, fracture by fracture, generation by generation, three vertical kilometers over some millions of years, until they surfaced at the vent-throats of the high dark country and rang them round with rings. And because a mat that meets a mat fuses and stops being neighbors, it is not clear — it may never be clear — that the climb ever ended in separation. Deep in the flank, in the warm veins of the skirts, the sea's mother-body and the plateau's daughter-body may still be continuous: one organism from the black seafloor to the highest vent on the tableland, the tallest living thing that will ever exist, shaped like a country's worth of rock and mistakable for one.

Along the seep-line the mats do something their highland children never learned. They build. Their chemistry precipitates mineral as they grow, and over ages they have raised terraces — pale crusted shelves and lips and galleries stepping down into the dark water, kilometers of them, the only architecture in the world. It is not construction, exactly; nothing decided it. But it stands, and things live in it, and if this world has cities of stone as well as cities of memory, they are here, grown rather than built, alive down to the mortar.

**The light is also theirs, at the root.** Somewhere in the outer, coldest rings of the seep-mats, deep in time, a chemistry that shed its spare energy as heat began shedding a little of it as glow instead — an accident, at first, an inefficiency. But water carries light the way rock carries the thrum, and in a lightless sea the first glow was the first *information*, and everything that could exploit it did. Grazing swimmers came to the shine because the shine meant food. Their predators came because the swimmers did. And then the arms race that follows all information: brighter lures, false shines, flash-signals, and — the great invention, the thing the plateau in all its finished age has never needed and never made — *eyes*. There are eyes in the Quench. They evolved for a light no sun ever supplied, a light life makes for life, and so vision here is not a way of seeing the world; the world is dark. It is a way of seeing *each other*. Every visible thing in the sea is visible because something living lit it, on purpose or fatally.

The census of the plateau is a click answered by a click. The census of the Quench is taken in light: the hem of the sea at any hour is a slow lace of glimmer and pulse and answering pulse, each species' shine its own signature, the whole shoreline performing continuously the same roll call the grazers perform across their dark gaps — *here. here. here* — in a different alphabet, at the speed of light instead of sound.

**The hem-runners** are the grazers' rhyme, though no kinship connects them but habit. They are swimmers, hand-length, dense-shoaled, and they live along the base of the wall on the one great irregular bounty the plateau sends down: the freshets. When a chain-firing rolls across forty kilometers of the tableland and drops its brief warm rain, the runoff gathers on the skirts into short-lived rivers — the runs — and comes down the flank in a matter of hours, and arrives at the sea as a pulse of warm, fresh, mineral-rich water spreading out over the brine. Fresh water is poison to brine life; the pulse kills a margin of small things wherever it lands, and feeds a bloom behind the killing, and the deltas of the runs are therefore the richest and most dangerous ground in the sea. The hem-runners work them the way grazers work vents: on schedule, on memory, arriving in the last hour. They cannot hear the chain-firings the way the deep things can, but they have learned the runs' geography and the intervals of the seams above, and a shoal will swim a night's journey along the foot of the wall to a delta that is not yet flowing, and hang off it in the dark, waiting for water that is still falling as rain three kilometers over their heads. The plateau teaches everything that lives off it the same single lesson, above or below, furred or finned: *be on time.* It has never once taught anything else.

**The unlit** are the hunters' rhyme, and the rhyme is exact. On the plateau, where the common sense is heat, the killer is the thing that becomes cold. In the Quench, where the common sense is light, the killer is the thing that makes none. The unlit are long, slow, large-eyed, and perfectly dark — not camouflaged, *absent* — and they hang off the shine of the hem in open water, drinking in everyone else's signals and returning nothing. They are the only creatures in the sea that see and are not seen, and like the cold hunters above they have pared their lives down to an accounting: months of drifting outlay, one strike of income, repeat. In both countries of this world, the top of the food chain is occupied by an absence. It may be the only law here that holds everywhere.

**The combers** work the Hem itself — the fall line, the long windrow at the top of the beach where the Long Fall, dying, drops everything it carried down off the plateau. And it carries a great deal, over time: mat-fragments scoured from the vent-rings, mineral dust, the fine dark gravel, and stranger freight. Grazer under-fur, the finest insulation the world has ever produced, blows off the rim in wisps and comes down the three-kilometer wind and ends in the windrow, and the combers — squat, amphibious, methodical shore-things that walk the fall line in loose companies — gather it and line their brood-hollows with it, so that generations of sea-creatures are warmed at birth by the wool of an animal none of them will ever know exists. Occasionally the freight is a body: a grazer that lost its timetable near the rim, once in a long while the great gaunt frame of a seam-walker dead on its last seam, carried down over months by wind and gravity and the runs. This is everything the Quench knows of the country overhead — its dust, its wool, its dead. The sea has assembled, in the combers' middens, a kind of museum of a place it cannot conceive, curated entirely from what falls.

**And then there are the sounders.** The largest things in the sea, and the deepest, and the slowest to be born or die — and they are the walker's counterpart so precisely that if this world had irony it would be embarrassed. Water carries the plateau's deep pressure-language far better than rock carries it, better than air ever could; the sea is, acoustically, pressed against the whole underside of the engine, and the sounders have made their lives out of listening to it. A sounder hangs motionless in the mid-dark, flank-on to the wall, its long body one continuous ear, performing the same subtraction the cold hunters perform on the ground's ordinary voice: hold the model of the engine's usual thrum, attend only to the remainder. A major chain-firing is legible to a sounder ten hours out and a hundred kilometers away, and when one begins to build, the sounder turns and swims, unhurried, and arrives off the mouths of the runs in the last hour, and feeds on the bloom the freshet brings, and listens again, and swims again — a life that is a connect-the-dots of eruptions along the foot of a continent, decades long.

Which means this, and it is worth standing still in the dark stillness at the wind's death to hold it properly: when a great firing gathers in the seams, there are hours during which the two finest instruments this world has ever grown are trained on the same event — the walker, high on the tableland, pressed full-length against the seam with its whole body an ear; and the sounder, three kilometers below and some kilometers out, hanging in black water with its whole body an ear — reading the same pressure, drawing the same conclusion, setting out for the same rain. Neither will ever know the other exists. They have listened to the same organ, from opposite sides of the same wall, for longer than most worlds keep species, and the wall has never once mentioned either of them to the other.

Then the firing comes. On the plateau the line of red throats brightens, the slow cannonade rolls out through felt, the warm rain falls, the grazers converge. And hours later, down the skirts, the runs flow, and the freshets spread, and all along the Hem the sea answers the only way the sea knows how — the bloom coming up shining, the hem-runners' shoals striking silver-green fire through it, the whole foot of the wall kindling for a night into the brightest thing under this starless black.

The plateau breathes, and the sea glows. That is the whole transaction, and it has run without interruption or witness for ten million years: heat into steam, steam into rain, rain into rivers, rivers into brine, brine into the deep rock and up again — and somewhere in the circuit, as a kind of interest paid on the principal, a line of living light along a shore that nothing on the blind warm continent above will ever see. The night made an engine, and the engine made a lamp, and the lamp shines on schedule at the bottom of the wall, in answer, in answer, in answer.
