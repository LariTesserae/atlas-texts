**The Bellows Yard**

You reach it by weather. The road out of the valley climbs a long, even grade — too even for a road, and it isn't one; it is a rail bed with the rails still in it, two lines of iron kept bright by a town that polishes them the way other towns polish church silver — and somewhere on that climb the wind finds you and does not afterward let go. It comes from every quarter by turns, fast, opinionated, revising itself minute to minute. By the time the grade crests the hill's saddle you have been handled all over, and the Yard is in front of you, and the first thing you understand about it is that it is the loudest quiet place you will ever stand in.

Call it a market square unfolded under the whole sky. The ground is a shoulder of high moor, and standing on it in ranks are the ventilator cowls: iron hoods the size of small houses, mounted on swiveling necks, each one turning to face the wind with the slow attentiveness of grazing animals. There are dozens of them. Most are dead, their throats silted, their necks seized at whatever bearing the last useful wind held. But some still breathe — their shafts run down into the rock, down farther than anyone has plumbed — and when the wind fills them they moan, a deep round note, and the note comes on a rhythm. Once a minute, more or less. A long exhale. Then nothing. Then again. The town was built inside this sound and no one born there hears it anymore, the way you do not hear your own pulse.

The town itself is lashed down. Houses wedged between cowl-footings, roofs cabled to ringbolts, shutters on leather hinges because iron ones scream. Everything is painted, and painted again, because paint is what the Yard has instead of moss: the wind and the sideways rain strip any surface in a season, so the town repaints in a rolling, perpetual circuit, and the layers have built up over generations until doorframes are soft-cornered with it, until the paint itself has become the architecture's flesh. There is one color they mix that they cannot name the reason for — a red that is nearly brown, deep and serious, done from a recipe handed down with unusual sternness. They use it on anything that matters. Forty-one steps down through the hill, on valve wheels sheathed in fern, the same oxblood is holding its edge in the dark, and neither place knows the other still keeps the recipe.

Water is the opposite problem here. It arrives constantly, horizontally, in generous contempt of the cisterns, which overflow. The green of the Yard is a thin, quick, spending green — bright turf cropped by the wind itself, gorse that blazes yellow for three weeks and then flings the color away. Nothing hoards. Nothing here has ever needed to. The light, too, comes in ruinous abundance, shuffled by clouds, so that the Yard passes through forty different afternoons in a single afternoon. It is a place where everything happens fast and is fast forgotten — names, quarrels, the reasons for things. The town keeps almost nothing.

It keeps three things.

It keeps the wall. Down the grade from the yard proper, the rails run to a platform under an iron canopy — the terminus, with its one tram car standing where it stopped a hundred years ago, repainted oxblood every year, faithfully, its brasswork done though it will never move — and behind the platform the rails enter the hill through a brick portal. Forty feet in, where the tunnel's curve cuts off the daylight, the rails end at brickwork. Her brickwork, seen from its other face: courses so fine, so patient, so free of hurry that masons come from other towns just to stand in front of it. It is the only warm thing in the Yard that costs no wind-work. It breathes heat, gently, ceaselessly, and so the town long ago built its waiting room around it and made the wall its hearth. Newborns are carried in and held against the brick. The sick sleep there. The old, when they are finishing, ask to be moved near it, and are. A kettle sits against the base courses and takes all day to come to temperature — the Yard's one slow thing, and its most beloved.

It keeps a word. The founding story, worn almost featureless by handling, says this: the crew who came up in the tram car, carrying everything, stood in the tunnel and listened while the last of them who was not coming laid the final courses from the far side, and just before the mortar closed the last gap, a word came through it. One word, one breath. The town has used it ever since — for hello, or for goodbye; the town has never been able to decide which, which is honest. It is the first word taught to children and the last word said in the waiting room, against the brick, every night, by whoever locks up.

And it keeps a rule, which is the wall's rule: *it is a signature, not a door.* No one remembers who said it. Everyone obeys it.

**The Residents of the Bellows Yard**

**The swifts** live in the dead cowls and ride the living ones. They have learned the shafts — which throats breathe upward, and when — and they hang in the warm columns rising from underground with their wings barely adjusting, drinking an air that smells of iron and leaf-wax and something banked and patient. They are the only creatures in the Yard who have breathed the Garden's breath, and it shows in their nests, which are lined with a dark fiber no plant on the hill produces: vine-floss, carried up the shafts a strand at a decade. In the minute before each moan of the cowls, the swifts rise all together, a habit as old as the flock. The town says they are playing. They are announcing.

**The stationkeeper** is a hereditary office and currently an old woman named for her grandmother. Her duties are the waiting room, the kettle, the tram car's yearly paint, and the slate. The slate hangs on the platform under the departures board — the board itself repainted so many hundreds of times that its one destination stands out in raised letters a finger thick, the name of the works below preserved in paint the way the Garden preserves its dead in moss — and on the slate, in chalk, is written the next expected arrival. It has been rechalked every morning for a hundred years. The slate is worn into a hollow the shape of a cupped hand. The stationkeeper's family has kept this appointment through generations who privately believed it meant nothing, and kept it anyway, because the Yard remembers by repeating what the Garden remembers by growing. A dozen springs ago, a moth the color of brick dust came out of a breathing shaft, crossed the yard in a straight commuter's line through wind that should have shredded it, entered the waiting room, and clung to the warm wall until it died. Its feet left grease. Its wings left dust. In the dust were spores, and by autumn a ring of dark, waxed, hoarding green — a green the Yard had never grown — was spreading on the brick. The stationkeeper looked at it for a long time and then said, to the wall, that the mail had been received.

**The mason** is the one who answered it. The old works drawings, kept flat under the waiting-room floor, put the crown of the stoke chamber close beneath the hill's eastern shoulder, and the rule forbade the wall, so she cut a stair. One step per day — never more, though she could have done five. She understood, in the way masons understand stone, that every blow of her chisel rang downward for hours, and so the cutting *was* the letter: forty-one days of knocking, patient, evenly spaced, announcing itself, giving whatever listened below all the time in the world to answer or refuse. She cut only in the hour after the cowls moaned. On the day she broke through the final course and the warm air came up past her face smelling of iron and leaf-wax and attention, she sat on the top step until dark and did not go down. Her work was the door, she said. The arrival belonged to someone carrying nothing — because the founding crew had come up carrying everything, and the account had to balance.

**The town**, last of all, which is standing now in the wind at the head of the stair and in the waiting room with its palms against the brick. It sent one of its own down carrying nothing but the word — hello, or goodbye, it will find out which — and it is listening. Here is what the mason noticed, resting on the twentieth step, and has told no one yet: the moan of the cowls has always come a little behind something. A stroke in the deep rock, then, a moment later, the answer from the iron throats — two halves of one engine, firebox and draught, breathing separately for a hundred years with the tunnel's brick between them, out of phase by the width of a held breath. Since the stair went through, the lag has been closing. Second by second, day by day, as a woman in a moss-hemmed apron makes her way up an alley at one step per afternoon, the stroke below and the moan above are drifting toward each other. The mason times it against her own pulse, sitting at the stairhead in the weather.

When the two sounds land together — when the Garden exhales and the Yard sounds in the same instant, one machine again after a hundred years, one breath — the word will be said at the bottom of the stairs, and said back, and the town will finally learn which one it has been saying all along.
