**The Risen Water**

There is a place — an hour's walk end to end, and every step of it drenched in seeing — where the water that went into the dark comes back.

The plateau that holds the Hollow Country does not go on forever. To the south it ends the way a table ends: a cliff, three hundred meters of pale limestone standing over lower country, and at the base of that cliff, in a half-bowl of fallen rock, there is a pool that is not a pool. It is a mouth. Below its surface the walls funnel inward and drop into a flooded shaft that descends past any light, past any sounding, into the body of the plateau itself — and up out of that shaft, day and night, without ceasing, comes water.

It is the same water. Every black river in the Hollow Country, every sweating wall, every sump where a pale thing hangs and tastes the current — all of it, having gone as far down as down goes, meets a floor of rock it cannot pass and is turned, and gathered, and pinched toward this one throat. Three days' dark of country drains through a gap you could bridge with your arms. The whole roaring nation up there — the ceilings, the carpet, the false stars, the silence-eaters — everything that lives above that water is, hydrologically speaking, upstream of this pool. The Risen Water is where the Hollow Country ends. The Hollow Country does not know it ends. Countries never do.

The water comes up blue. Not sky-blue — a deep, self-lit, mineral blue that seems to come from inside the shaft, though it is only clarity and depth doing to light what nothing in the world above the pool can do to it. Lean over the edge and you look down into forty meters of transparency before the blue closes like a pupil. It is the clearest water in the lower country, and the coldest, and it does not vary. Summer, winter, drought — the pool holds the temperature of the deep rock, the temperature of the Hollow Country's breath, and on warm mornings that breath becomes visible: a standing mist over the basin, cold air sliding out across the surface of the water and downhill through the willows, the exhalation of a country that has never once inhaled the day.

---

Light here is total, and that changes everything.

In the country below, light entered the way water enters a sealed jar. Here it enters the way water enters an open bowl: poured, squandered, lying around in heaps. Noon on the lower basin is blinding — the pale rock throws it back, the pools throw it upward, so that the underside of the cliff is lit from below and even the shadows are only dimmer kinds of shine. Everything sees. Everything is seen. There is no roar here to hide inside; the basin has silences, actual silences, at night between the sounds of falling water — a luxury no creature of the deep country has ever owned — but there is no darkness to spend them in. The one darkness in the whole bright place is the blue shaft itself, the pupil at the basin's center, and everything here treats it the way the roof nation treats a dead spot: the fish hold at its lip and will not go down, the diving birds turn back at a depth they could easily pass, as if below a certain blue their nerve simply fails. To a world that sees with light, the shaft is a hole in the world. It is, of course, the door.

And because light here is free — common as air, owned by no one — it is not the currency. In the Hollow Country light was bait, was expensive, was always a lie; sound was the honest medium, because a voice cannot help confessing its size and its place. Here the arrangement is reversed. Sound at the Risen Water is mostly honest and mostly ignored. It is *color* that speaks, and color, unlike a voice, can say anything. So this is a country of counterfeits: fish patterned exactly as the gravel they hang over, a bittern that is indistinguishable from seven reeds, caddis larvae that build themselves armor out of the streambed and become the streambed, moths that are bark, beetles that are seeds, a whole population dressed as the furniture. In the dark country only one nation ever learned to lie. Here everyone lies, all day, fluently, and the lying is so universal that the rare honest colors — the kingfisher's impossible blue, the courting flare on a fish's flank — read as what they are: extravagance, announcement, a voice raised on purpose.

---

The stone is the other thing. The stone is the connection made solid, and it takes a while to understand.

The water rises stiff with dissolved rock. Every liter of it has spent years in the dark eating limestone — widening a gallery by the thickness of a breath, fluting a wall, unbuilding a pillar — and it arrives at the surface carrying the Hollow Country in solution: the ceilings, the chambers, the acres of stone that are no longer there. It is a country's absence, running out of a cliff.

And at the surface, the absence comes out of suspension. Warmth and light and the breathing of green things strip the water of its dissolved breath, and the stone it carries falls out of it — precipitates, fast, onto everything the water touches. Below the spring pool the basin descends in steps of brand-new rock: terrace dams with curved lips, aprons and rims and basins of pale porous stone that the water is building even as it pours over them. A twig that falls in is stone in a season. The mosses grow faster than the stone can catch them and are entombed anyway, petrified at the root while still green at the tip, so that the terrace walls are made of moss upon moss upon moss, each generation growing on the mineralized bodies of the last — the same gesture as the flowstone draperies of the deep galleries, the same mineral act, performed on the other side of the world.

Understand what this means. Every chamber that grows in the dark has its exact double here: a terrace, thickening in the sun. The Risen Water is built — literally, gram for gram — out of the missing stone of the Hollow Country. The hollows there are the heaps here. When a gallery's ceiling recedes another finger's width under a million years of screaming, that finger's width arrives at this basin, eventually, and is laid down as a lip for bright water to pour over. This place is the other place inverted, extruded, turned inside out into the light.

But where the deep country's stone is finished — every edge gone, every surface settled, handled-smooth — the stone here is *raw*. New. Provisional. The terraces are freshly built and freshly broken; the floods tear them out and the water reroutes and builds again, and nothing here has the ten-thousand-generation polish, because nothing here has been allowed ten thousand generations of anything. The Hollow Country is a finished sentence. The Risen Water is a first draft that never stops being revised.

---

The spring has moods, and its moods are the other country's weather, arriving late.

Two waters rise braided in the shaft: quick water and old water. The quick water is last week's storm, fallen on the plateau, swallowed by the dolines, hurried down through the open conduits in days. The old water is base flow — rain that percolated the tight rock grain by grain and has been dark for decades, water older than any bird drinking it. They come up together, indistinguishable, so that a single mouthful at the spring contains this month and a lost generation.

And before the floods, the mouth speaks. When a great storm sits on the plateau and the deep country begins to *breathe* — pressure moving through the galleries as wind, the sumps filling, the black rivers rising — the change reaches the cliff before the water does. Dry overflow mouths higher up the rock face begin to exhale. The shaft's voice drops. A low moan comes off the basin, felt as much as heard, and every bird on the lower terraces stands up, and leaves.

Then the rising: the blue goes to milk, the milk goes to thunder, the pool climbs its own walls and the overflow mouths open and the basin becomes for a day or a week a single hammering flood, remaking its terraces, redealing its stone. The herons know the moan. The dippers know it. They have kept, for as long as there have been herons here, an accurate working calendar of the Hollow Country's weather — the floods, the breathing, the moods of a nation of chambers they have never seen and could not imagine — written entirely in their own departures. They are meteorologists of the underworld. They think they are only birds who know when to move.

---

The rules, as they assemble:

That light is common and color is speech, and speech here mostly lies, and the few honest colors shout.

That the direction of this place is *up*. Water up out of the shaft, mist up off the pool, stone up out of solution, moss up over its own dead, the hatch up off the evening water, the birds up off the terraces, the green up toward the sun. The Hollow Country falls; the Risen Water rises. They are the two strokes of a single engine, and neither can feel the other's half of the turn.

That nothing here is finished, and nothing needs to be. The flood is the landlord. Everything is becoming, and becoming is the local form of permanence.

That the one darkness is a door, and everything avoids it, and the door does not care.

# Who Lives at the Risen Water

Begin where this country begins: with the green.

---

## The Green Nation

The Hollow Country has never held a plant — not a moss, not a lichen, not a green cell within reach of memory. The Risen Water is the correction, and it is drastic. The spring-run is smothered in green: rafts of watercress dense enough to walk a small animal across, moss curtains on every wet face, algae glazing the sunlit shallows, willows crowding the banks with their roots in the terraces slowly turning to stone. It is the most fertile water in the lower country by an enormous margin, and none of the animals living on it knows why.

The why is this: the water arrives loaded. It carries, in solution, the dissolved rain of a million ceilings — the droppings of the roof nation, the leachate of the guano badlands, the entire falling economy of the dark country, rendered down to its chemistry and shipped. Sunlight fell on the plateau's night as moths; the winged ones carried it down as blood and slurry; it fell as rain on the carpet; the carpet ate it and passed it and died into it; the seeps took it; the black rivers took the seeps; and here it rises, years later, and the cress turns it back into leaves. The Green Nation is eating a country it cannot conceive of. The sun of the Hollow Country was the bats. The soil of the Risen Water is the bats too. The same animals, unwitting, hold up both skies.

---

## The Day Nation

In the gravels under the green live the mayfly larvae — grazers, two years in the cold water, unremarkable. Then the hatch.

On the right evenings they rise through the water in the tens of thousands, split their skins at the surface, and lift off the pool as a smoke of wings. The adults that climb into the air have no mouths. This is not injury; it is design. They are born unable to eat, because they will not live long enough to need to — a day, most of them, some only the hour of dusk. An entire nation whose adult form has deleted hunger. In the country below, everything is mouth; the floor is a single continuous act of eating; a white cricket's nine days are a long full life. Here a nation has decided that one day is a complete life, and spends it entirely on flight and love, and by full dark the pool wears a snow of spent bodies, and the fish rise all night to take them in.

They are the fastest clock in either country. The salamanders in the deep sumps live four hundred years; the Day Nation lives an afternoon; and the same water passes under both.

---

## The Bright Fish

The fish of the spring-run are everything the water nation of the dark is not: eyed, colored, violent, quick. They hang in the current dressed as the gravel beneath them — liars, like everyone here — and flare into honest color only to court or to threaten, switching between deceit and declaration a hundred times a day.

One of them matters more than the rest. In every generation, one great fish holds the lip of the blue shaft — the single best station in the country, where the rising water delivers whatever the dark lets go. It is the border guard of the bright world, and it takes its toll on everything that comes up. It will not go down. No fish of this country will; below a certain depth the blue becomes something their nerve refuses, and they turn back with the door still open beneath them. The shaft is to them what the dead spots are to the roof nation: the one place where their kind of seeing stops, and therefore the one place that does not, for them, exist. Both countries are ringed and pierced by each other's blind spots. Both have agreed, in their bodies, not to look.

---

## The Watchers

The herons are this country's patience, but note the difference: here, patience is a behavior. A heron stands motionless at a terrace lip for an hour and calls it discipline; a deep salamander hangs motionless in a sump for a year and calls it metabolism. The bright world has to *practice* what the dark world simply is.

The dippers are the commuters — plump birds that walk underwater, gripping the streambed, flying through the current with half-opened wings, citizens of the boundary the way the pale crabs of the deep country are citizens of theirs. The kingfisher is the honest blue, the one light in a country of common light that still functions as a signal, because it is carried and aimed.

And all of them, herons and dippers and the rest, keep the calendar. They read the moan of the overflow mouths, the change in the shaft's voice, the cold exhale quickening off the pool — and they move to high ground days before the flood, and they return the hour it slackens, and they gather at the spring mouth when the milk-colored water comes, because they know, in the old wordless way, what the flood brings up.

---

## The Exiles

What the flood brings up is the dark country's citizens.

White shrimp, first — hundreds in a great flood, spun out of the shaft into the pool. Then the blind fish, colorless, their lateral lines screaming with information that no longer maps to anything. Once in a long while, a salamander, translucent, its slow heart visible, four decades or four centuries old, delivered in an afternoon into the one thing its entire lineage has never encountered: light.

They arrive blazing white against every background, unable to see what is coming for them, their exquisite senses fluent in a language this world does not speak. The birds are waiting; the birds are always waiting after the moan. Most exiles last minutes. They are the only news the bright country ever receives from the dark one — a rumor in the shape of a fish with no eyes — and no one here can read it. The heron that swallows a blind fish has eaten a message from an unimaginable nation and registered only lunch.

But not all of them die. Under the terrace lips there are slots of permanent twilight, wet and cold, and once in a generation an exiled salamander finds one and holds it — and lives, sometimes for decades, in the seam between the countries: a creature of the dark, breathing the mist of the bright, tasting in the water pouring past it the news of the home it fell out of. It is the only individual in either country, save one nation, who has been a resident of both. It does nothing with the distinction. It abides, which is what it was always going to do, anywhere.

---

## The Eels

And there is one traffic going the other way.

They come up the river from a sea neither country has heard of — long, patient, absurdly strong — and they climb the spring-run, over the terrace lips, through the cress, to the pool. And some of them do not stop at the blue. They tip over the lip of the shaft, where the great fish gives way before them, and swim down into the dark, against the current, against the direction of both worlds, and are gone.

They are the only animal that enters the door. The bats haul the surface down as guano; the water hauls the dark up as chemistry; and the eels are the third current, the strange one: whole bodies, muscular and fat with the sea, pushed by something older than either country up the black rivers and into the sumps. Some of them get far. And in the lowest water, below everything, where the very large, very old, silt-backed things hang in centuries of stillness — *this* is what those things have been eating, once a year, for four hundred years. The eels are the bright world's tithe to the dark one, the single export running against the flow, and they explain the deepest country's oldest citizens, and neither party has any idea that a trade is occurring. It is the most balanced commerce imaginable: conducted for a million years, in full ignorance, without a single default.

---

## The Dusk Hour

Once a day the two countries touch, and this is where.

At evening the swifts that have hunted the basin all day climb away, and out of the high flaws in the cliff face the roof nation pours — the winged ones, gray as flowstone, spilling down the rock in rivers of their own and out over the pool. They come to drink on the wing, dipping to the risen water at full speed, and they come for the hatch, and for one hour the air over the basin belongs to both worlds at once: day shift ascending, night shift descending, trading the same smoke of mayflies between them.

Look at what is actually happening in that hour. The mayflies rising off the pool were grown on the cress; the cress was grown on the water; the water carries the dissolved rain of the colonies' own ceilings. The bats over the basin are eating their own rain, returned to them as wings. Sunlight to moth, moth to bat, bat to falling, falling to river, river to spring, spring to green, green to mayfly, mayfly to bat: the whole circuit of two countries closes over this one pool, every evening, in the shape of a million small mouths sipping from the risen water. The bats do not know they are the sun of one country and a guest of the other. They know the water is here, and the food is over the water, and the door home is a crack in the dark of the cliff.

They are the only nation with a working claim on both worlds — and even they do not know there are two. To them there is the roaring country and there is the hunting-night, and this basin is simply the best of the hunting-night, and the fact that the water they skim is the same water that carved their halls is not a fact their language of echoes can hold.

---

## What the Water Knows

Last, the connection itself, taken whole — because the truest inhabitant of the Risen Water may be the same one that arguably inhabits the Hollow Country, seen from its other side.

The water in the blue shaft at this moment has already been the dark. It has been the roar, felt through stone; it has been the sweat on a fluted wall and the rain-quickened seep and the black river sliding under a million voices; it has carried the taste of thriving colonies past the frilled gills of things four centuries old. And it is about to be the light: the mist, the cress, the mayfly, the snow of spent wings, the stone of a terrace lip. One water, changing state, on a loop so large that no creature in it lives long enough to see the turn — except perhaps the deep salamanders, who have the time but not the interest.

And the loop is longer even than that. The terraces the water is building — the pale new rock, the entombed mosses, the stone lips laid down out of the Hollow Country's dissolved body — will thicken, and compact, and in some span of time that makes even the salamanders brief, they will be limestone. And water will find a flaw in them. And begin.

The Risen Water is not the Hollow Country's neighbor. It is its afterlife and its ancestor at once — the place where the old country's absence is laid down as the body of some unimaginably later darkness. The two of them are not side by side but one behind the other, ages of a single thing, breathing each other in and out across a distance made of time. The dark country believes nothing, and is finished, and holds its shrieking nation like a cupped hand. The bright country believes itself brand new every morning, and is right, and is wrong — and the light lies over all of it, the terraces, the green, the honest blue, the pupil of the shaft, easily, completely, the way an open hand holds something poured into it, that believes itself to be the beginning.
