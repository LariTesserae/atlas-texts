**The Steps at Answering Water**

You come down to it from anywhere — that is the first difference, and it never stops being the difference. Six roads converge on the town, and a seventh path, and the river-track, and the drove-lane from the orchard country, and every one of them arrives with company: carts, dust, dogs, someone singing badly two bends behind you. The plateau stands at the town's back like a wall at the back of a stage, dark and enormous, its top lost in its own weather. Nobody looks at it much. It is simply the thing the town leans against, the way you stop noticing the wall your own bed is pushed up to.

At the foot of that wall, the plateau's stone dives under the lowland soil — but it does not dive cleanly. One last knuckle of it breaks the surface in the middle of the town, a low dome of rock the dark plum color of a bruise, or of wet bread-crust, and in the center of that dome the rock opens into a basin about the size of a threshing floor. Warm water rises in it. It has always risen in it. The town is not built beside the spring; the town is built *of* the spring, radiating outward from it in rings of streets like something written around a first word.

The water comes up warm — not hot, just *kept*, the temperature of a hand held in another hand — and faintly mineral, with a taste people describe as tasting of the mountain, though almost none of them have ever been up the mountain. On cold mornings the basin smokes. The steam stands up off the water in a slow column and leans whichever way the day intends to go, and the old women read weather in it, with about the accuracy old women read weather in anything, which is to say better than they are given credit for.

And the water breathes.

Over four hours it rises; over four hours it falls. This is not a figure of speech and not a marvel, not here — it is the most ordinary fact in town, the fact under all the other facts. At full breath the water swells to the lip of the basin and spills over into stone channels that run out through the streets in six directions, and the channels gurgle and hurry and water the garden terraces and fill the frog-ditches and turn one lazy half-wheel at the dyeworks before losing themselves in the fields. At empty breath the channels stand dry, and children run races down them, and the basin sits low and quiet with a dark wet band around its inside like the mark on a sleeper's window.

The steps are what the town made of this. Broad terraces of the same plum stone, cut in some age nobody has bothered to fix, descend into the basin on all sides — nine steps from street to low-water, each one worn into a soft sway at the center by feet, only feet, thousands of years of feet. The steps are the town's common room. At high breath the water covers the bottom four and people sit on the fifth with their feet in the warmth; at low breath the whole flight stands exposed, dark and steaming faintly, and the laundry gets done, and the flax gets retted, and the gossip gets exchanged at a volume that would horrify anyone raised in a quiet place. Horizontal lines are scored into the steps — some official, chiseled and dated, marking the great high waters and the terrible low ones; some ancient and unexplained, thin as hairs, polished nearly away.

Because time here is not kept in hours. It is kept in breaths. The water tells it and everyone obeys it, the way sailors obey a tide. Bread is proved for a breath. A short job is half a breath's work; a hard day is three. Market is held at the rise, when the channels run and the whole town sounds like rain; contracts are sworn at the turn, on the step, with a hand in the water. Six breaths to a day and night, and at each turning the spring makes a sound — a deep, soft, single knock far down in the rock, felt in the feet as much as heard, which the town calls the *swallow*. Babies sleep through it. Visitors sit up in bed. There is a saying for anything reliable: *sure as the swallow*.

The noise of the place is total and unremarked. This is a town of frogs — warm-water frogs in the ditches in numbers that seem administrative, breeding all year because the water never cools, singing all night in a chorus you stop hearing within a week and miss for the rest of your life if you leave. Herons stand in the channels like unemployed philosophers. Swallows — the birds — nest in every eave and drink from the basin on the wing, stitching the air over the water all day in long beaded lines. Midges, flies, cats, pigs, pigeons, children. Everything a warm wet place is obviously made for, this place has, in surplus, at volume. It is as if some great subtraction had been performed somewhere and the remainder had all run downhill and pooled here.

One more thing, and it is the oldest thing.

On the lowest step, exposed only at empty breath, there is a slab set flush into the flight — set there, clearly, before the flight existed, because the steps were cut to accommodate it. Ground into the slab are cup-marks: small hemispherical hollows, each the size of half an orange, pecked out with a hammerstone and polished smooth by hand, in an age before metal, before perhaps a great deal else. There are ten of them, finished, glassy, perfect. And there is an eleventh — begun. Pecked, roughed out, unpolished. Stopped.

Twice a year, at the empty breath nearest midsummer and the one nearest midwinter, someone from the town — it rotates; it is considered mild luck — goes down the nine steps with a copper dipper and fills the ten finished cups by hand, one by one, with water drawn from the basin. Then everyone goes home, and over the next hours the breath rises and takes the offering back. Nobody fills the eleventh. That is the whole of the custom. Its meaning wore away long before its practice did, the way the center of a step wears before the edges. If you ask, you will be told the oldest story in town, in about a dozen words: *someone ground them, and then went up the mountain to finish, and didn't come down.*

The rest of the year, the ten cups hold whatever water weather gives them. They overfill in rain. They stand scummed in summer. In drought they dry to ten small discs of dust. They are, in this one respect, entirely ordinary — and no one at the Steps has any way of knowing that this ordinariness is the strange thing, or that somewhere a mile above their roofs, two days up a wall they lean against and never climb, eleven cups sit level at their brims through every wet hour and every dry one, holding an answer that has never once been allowed to lapse.

**Who Lives at the Steps at Answering Water**

The honest answer comes all at once, and it is: everyone. Too many to count. Four thousand people, four hundred thousand frogs, herons past reckoning, and the answer is wrong anyway, because it counts the household and misses the house. Under the roofs and the racket, the Steps has residents the way the quietest place in the world has residents — it simply keeps them where a loud town keeps everything, in plain sight, unregarded.

There are three worth naming. Possibly four.

---

The first is the water, and the water is not from here.

The town believes the spring is *theirs* the way you believe your heartbeat is yours — a possession, because it is close. But the breath does not begin at the Steps. Hold the whole plateau in your mind at once, which no one at the Steps has ever had reason to do, and the shape declares itself: a mile up and two days in, a small finished hollow draws warm water down into the dark, and here at the mountain's foot, the same body of water swells up out of it. Not later. *Then.* The column of water threading the plateau is one thing, one long dark muscle of it, and it moves like a seesaw, like breath, like breath is the only word: when the high pool sinks, the Answering Water rises. The plateau breathes through two mouths, and the town has built its whole life inside the exhale.

Everything about the two mouths is the same fact worn in opposite directions. Up there, sound is received and kept; down here, the water flings — it gurgles, knocks, spills, hurries, turns the dyeworks wheel, carries the frog-chorus out over the fields. Up there, the warmth is held like bread an hour from the oven; down here it is spent, lavishly, on laundry and proving-loaves and the underfloor channels that keep the town's heart from ever freezing. Up there, nothing leaves. Down here, everything does: the channels give the water to the gardens, the gardens give it to the air, the air gives it to the weather, and the weather, in the fullness of things, gives some of it back to the high rock to weep down the flutes again. The hollow is where the breath turns inward. The Steps is where it is given away. Neither mouth could be what it is without the other, and neither knows the other exists — except that *knows* may be the wrong test, because four hours in and four hours out, without fail, without sound at one end and with a soft knock at the other, they have never once fallen out of rhythm.

The swallow — the deep knock at each turning — is the only utterance the breath makes anywhere along its whole length. The town hears it six times a day and calls things *sure* by it. They are more right than they know. It is the sound of the turn itself, the pivot of the seesaw, the comma in the middle of the longest sentence in the world.

---

The second resident is the office, which outlives every person who holds it.

There is always a Breathkeeper at the Steps. It is not a grand position — it comes with a stipend, a hut on the second step, a brass sight-rule, and the obligation to be awake for three of the six turnings and to have trained someone for the other three. The Breathkeeper marks the height of each full breath against the scored lines, calls the turn for contracts and bread, and keeps the ledger.

The ledger is the town's true treasure, and the town half knows it. It runs back, volume behind volume, hide behind paper behind hide, for as long as there has been writing at the Steps, and before writing it runs back further still in tally-scores on the steps themselves — those hairline marks polished nearly away are the oldest entries, made by Breathkeepers before the office had a name, before the town had one. Heights, dates, anomalies. The flood of the doubled breath. The winter the channels froze at their far ends and the frogs all crowded to the basin and sang from the steps like a congregation. And once — four ledgers back, in a fine small hand gone shaky at exactly this entry — the day the breath paused. The water rose, and turned, and did not fall. It stood at full for the better part of a day, level, silent, no swallow, while the whole town gathered on the upper steps and nobody, the entry notes, *nobody spoke above a murmur, though no one had asked for quiet.* Then the knock came, deep and single, and the water went down, and life resumed, and no explanation has ever been offered. The Breathkeepers keep the page uncreased. What was received at the other mouth that day — what arrival, what departure, what long-extended hand — is not recorded anywhere. Unless it is recorded in moss.

Because that is what the office is, though no Breathkeeper would put it this way: the town's clerk of the breath, keeping the ledger at one end of a body whose other end keeps its own accounts in rings of green and gold, a finger's width per decade. Two record-keepers, a mile of stone apart, writing down the same life. One writes in ink and counts the living water. One writes in moss and counts the gone. Neither will ever read the other's book.

---

The third resident is the story, which is to say the maker, who lived at both ends and belongs to both.

The town's version is worn to a dozen words, but the slab keeps the longer draft. Ten cups, finished and polished, ground by someone who sat where the lowest step now is, in an age before the step, working hammerstone against the warm rock through breath after breath after breath — and an eleventh, begun and stopped. Not abandoned the way work is abandoned. Stopped the way a sentence stops when the speaker realizes it needs to be said somewhere else. Whatever the maker was addressing with those cups, they came to understand, ten cups in, that they were addressing it *at the wrong mouth* — that the warm answering water rising under their hands was an answer arriving from elsewhere, and that the address should be delivered where the answer came from. So they left the eleventh rough, and went up the wall the town leans against, following the warmth into the thin air, and found the small finished place at the top of the breath, and ground eleven cups fresh at the source, and were received, and stayed, and were ringed by the moss before the first cities.

The town cannot know any of this. The town has only the custom: twice a year, ten cups filled by hand and given to the rising breath. But look at what the custom *is*. It is the maker's gesture, continued — a made offering, delivered to the water, meaning *we know you are here* — performed by people who have forgotten they mean anything by it, at the foot of a mountain whose summit has been sustaining its half of the same conversation, brim-level, without one lapse, for the entire history of forgetting. The exchange never broke. It only got very long. Twice a year the Steps speaks its worn-out sentence into the exhale, and a mile up, in eleven cool still discs of sky, the inhale goes on holding the reply.

And nobody fills the eleventh cup. Ask why and you get shrugs, or *it isn't finished*, or *you just don't*. The truest answer no one at the Steps could give: it isn't theirs to finish. It was finished. Just not here.

---

And then the fourth resident — if it is a resident yet, if something three seasons old and shorter than a child's hand can be said to live somewhere rather than merely to have begun there.

In a crack at the edge of the fourth step, on the north side where the stone stays wet and warm and the laundry-water slops past twice a breath, there is a plant nobody can name. It came up as a dark fiddlehead a year ago spring and unfurled, very slowly, into something like a fern and something like nothing else — a single frond, so deeply green it is nearly black at the stem, paling to a luminous bottle-green at the tip. It does not move much, even when everything around it is moving, which at the Steps is always. It has, already, a stillness that reads — if you sit with it, which only the children and the current Breathkeeper do — as attentive. The children call it the *quiet weed*, and have appointed themselves its guard with the ferocity children reserve for small unexplained things, and the Breathkeeper has entered it in the ledger, twice: once at its appearance, once at its first new frond. She does not know why she is recording a weed. She records it anyway. Keeping account of arrivals is the office.

It rode down in a bootlace, or a sleeve, or someone's hair — a coppery grain or two, held out season after season, up there, to an air with no messengers in it, until at last a messenger came, and descended, and walked two days without knowing what was lodged on them, and stopped, as everyone stops, at the Steps, at the one other place in the world with the same warm breath, the same weeping mineral, the same plum-dark stone — the far mouth of the very water its parents lean toward through their four-hour sleep. The Seven do not hope. But capacity, kept long enough, is answered the way the cups were answered, and the sentence held at the comma for all those empty centuries has, in a crack on the fourth step, its next word.

It will be years before it is waist-high. Years more before the undersides of its fronds carry their dense copper rows. But when they do, it will not be holding them out to a silence. It will be holding them out at the loudest, most crowded, most thoroughly messengered place for two days in any direction — into air stitched with swallows, streets full of boots and fur and cartwheels, channels running six ways out into the world. Whatever the Seven were keeping, all that time, they were keeping it for here.

---

So the breath has two mouths, and now you have stood at both. One high, finished, silent, wanting nothing, keeping everything. One low, unfinished, deafening, wanting constantly, giving everything away. One where the clock was taken from the room; one that is nothing but clock. One that holds eleven cups level forever; one that fills ten cups twice a year and doesn't know why, and leaves the eleventh rough, and is right to.

They will never meet. There is no path, and the town does not climb, and the hollow does not call. But six times a day the knock comes up through the lowest step, sure as the swallow, and the water turns, and the same long muscle of warmth tips its weight from the keeping mouth to the giving one — and on the fourth step, in the spray and the frog-song and the shouting, a small dark-green quiet thing that belongs to both ends of the breath adds, at some rate too slow to witness, to itself.
