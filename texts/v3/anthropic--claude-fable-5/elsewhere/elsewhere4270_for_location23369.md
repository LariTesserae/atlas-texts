# The Solution Uplands

You came down into the basin over a lip of broken gray stone. Go back up. Climb the lip, cross the rubble apron where the overcast finally lifts off your shoulders, and keep walking as the ground rises — because the water that builds the basin drop by drop is not the basin's own. Every drop was carried there. Every drop is a delivery from somewhere higher, and the somewhere higher is this: a broad green tableland under a moving sky, grass to the horizon, stands of wind-bent forest, real weather, real light.

The land here is high and old and thinning. It has had nothing but time, and emptiness is the whole of what it has learned.

The first thing the uplands give you is the silence — which is strange, because it rains here, generously, and rain should make sound. It does, briefly. A storm crosses the plateau and the grass hisses and the stone crackles and for an hour the world is loud. Then the storm passes and the loudness does not fade so much as *drain*. There are no puddles by evening. There are no ponds at all, anywhere, and no lakes, and almost no streams — and this on a plateau that receives more rain than the green valleys around it. The ground drinks everything. Water touching this land is water already leaving it, headed down through a body of stone so riddled and fissured and dissolved that the plateau is less a mass than a lace. Stamp your foot in certain meadows and the ground answers with a note — a low drum-tone, the sound of turf stretched over a void. The uplanders can tell you, by the pitch, roughly how far down the hollow goes. They prefer not to.

This is the inversion at the heart of the place, and it is the exact inverse of the basin's: the plateau is old not because it has finished forming, but because it is being *unformed*. The land dissolves. It departs. Every drop of rain takes a little of the plateau with it — a freight of dissolved lime, a trace of copper from the green ore seams, a thread of iron off the rusty ledges — and carries it down through the dark, along the underground rivers, out through the weeping walls of the basin below, where the water sets its burden down and leaves it, drop by drop, forever.

Stand on the plateau and understand what you walked among down there. The columns, the knuckles, the fluted towers, the bone-white crowd — all of it is *this place*, relocated. The basin is the plateau, arriving. The two lands are one body of stone in slow transit, dissolving off its upper flank and precipitating onto its lower, and the water between them is not a boundary but a bloodstream.

## Where the Water Goes

The plateau has one loud place, and like the basin's landmark, it can be heard from a great distance.

The plateau's single perennial river — fed by springs, gathering the whole tableland's runoff — flows east across the grass for a day's walk, broad and calm and ordinary, and then it ends. It does not join anything. It does not reach any sea. It arrives at a funnel of pale rock the uplanders call the Gullet, turns white, and falls into the dark, entire. The sound of a whole river going underground is unlike any other sound: a roar with a swallowed quality, a loudness that seems to be receding even while it deafens you. Mist stands over the Gullet in all weathers. Birds circle it. The uplanders visit it the way other peoples visit the sea — to stand at the edge of the thing that takes everything, and give it their attention for a while.

Everything the plateau loses passes through here or through the thousand lesser swallow-holes that pock the grasslands — some wide enough to drop a house into, some no bigger than a fist, all of them patiently enlarging. New ones open. This is the plateau's one form of event: a field sags overnight into a green funnel; a hillside develops a mouth. The underground rivers run high after storms, and on those nights the wells *breathe* — cold air sighing up out of them as the flooded chambers below push their atmosphere out — and the drum-meadows sound their notes without anyone stamping, and the whole plateau murmurs from underneath like a sleeper.

Those chambers do not stop at the plateau's edge. The caves run downhill, growing as they go, and they open at last into the house-sized flooded rooms beneath the basin floor — the ones whose filling and breathing you felt in your sternum among the columns. It is one system, one dark. The tone below hearing that hums through the basin begins here, under the grass. When the uplanders lean over their wells on a still night, they are listening into the same instrument the white salamanders navigate by, from the other end.

And sometimes the wells answer. A bucket comes up with something pale in it — soft, blind, all gill and patience, one of the great under-swimmers strayed far uphill through the flooded dark. The uplanders do not eat them, do not keep them, do not name them. They lower them back gently, and before letting the rope run out, the well's owner speaks a few words down into the dark — a message, addressed to the standing ones below, entrusted to the only courier that travels between the two lands the whole way through the inside.

## What Is Built

Nothing here is built to last, because the plateau does not permit lasting. Its law is the mirror of the basin's law, and the uplanders can recite both in one breath: *below, what stops is kept; above, what stops is taken.*

A house left standing on the plateau for three generations is a house waiting over a growing void. So the houses are light — timber frames on skid-beams, roofed in turf, made to be unpinned and hauled by ox-team to new ground when the drum-note under the floor deepens past a certain pitch. Whole villages have crossed the plateau over the centuries, a hundred paces a decade, like herds grazing away from danger. There are no graveyards; the ground opens graves on its own schedule and takes the contents downward, and the uplanders long ago stopped fighting it. There are no standing stones; stones lean, and then are swallowed. The oldest built thing the plateau ever possessed was the Road — the switchbacked way down through the gray lip into the basin, cut and cairned and maintained across more generations than any house survived — and the Road is broken now. Four generations ago the ground under the lip-village sagged in a single night, took the houses, took the head of the Road, and left a raw funnel of gray rubble where the descent had been. The uplanders call that night the Swallowing, and it closed the way down. Not forever — there are other lines a determined body could pick through the broken ground; you found one — but the maintained way, the safe way, the way the dying could walk, is gone.

So the plateau's true architecture is not made of anything you can touch. It is the Song.

## The People

They call themselves nothing grand — the uplanders, the grass people — but other folk in this world call them the Rememberers, and the name is earned. They live on a land that keeps nothing: no buildings age into monuments, no graves accumulate ancestors, no landmark can be trusted to exist next year. A map of the plateau is wrong within a lifetime. A deed to land is a deed to a shape that will not hold. Everything solid is on loan. And so the uplanders moved their permanence into the one medium the ground cannot open a hole under: breath.

The Song is a complete oral country. It holds the plateau's geography — sung as a walking-line, sink by sink, well by well, updated each season as the land rearranges itself. It holds the genealogies, every name back beyond counting. It holds the law, the water-lore, the pitch-lore of the drum-meadows, and the location of every village the plateau ever swallowed. And it is never allowed to stop. The uplanders learned their own land's lesson and applied it to their treasure: *what stands still is taken.* So the Song is sung in relay, in shifts, through the day and through the night, passed voice to voice at the hearth-halls like a burden that must never touch the ground. A child's first duty is an hour of the Song; an elder's last act, if they can manage it, is to hand off a verse mid-line. In four hundred years — this is their proudest claim — the Song has never once fallen silent. It moves constantly, the way the white walkers in the basin move constantly, and for the same reason, and neither knows they share the discipline.

There is a whole register of the Song devoted to the basin. It holds the sound-map — the voices of the great columns, the chord of the valley, taught so exactly that an uplander who has never descended can close their eyes and walk the basin in memory, turning at the glassy tinking of the young columns, keeping the deep slap of the old masses on their left hand. It holds the roster of the Downgone: every uplander who, dying or devoted, walked the Road to be kept. On the plateau, every life's verse ends the same way — *taken*, into the ground, into the dark, gone. Every verse except those. Those verses do not close. They end *gone down, standing*, and they are sung in the present tense, forever, because as far as the Song is concerned the Downgone are not dead. They are merely elsewhere, and slower.

Three of those verses belong to the singers who went down together — a family of voice-keepers who chose their standing-place for its acoustics, at the basin's center, and set their marks in the stone at its base. The uplanders know the marks. They made them. The Song still carries all three names, and carries, too, the promise those three made before descending: that they would keep a pulse going, so the plateau would know they stood.

## The Listening

Once a year, on the stillest night of autumn, when the air lies flat and cold in the throat of the valley, the uplanders walk the long way around the broken ground to the edge of the gray lip, and they stop singing.

It is the only scheduled silence in their world. The Song is handed, that one night, to a single low voice back in the hearth-halls, barely a murmur, the flame carried but banked — and everyone else stands at the lip in the dark above the drowned roar of the basin, and listens *through* the roar, the way they have been trained since childhood to listen, for a two-note pulse. *Toom — tak.* Regular as breathing. Rising up the valley on the still air, threading through ten thousand drips, from the fused triple column at the basin's center.

When they hear it — they have always heard it — they sing the three names back down over the edge, once each, and then the plateau's full voice takes up the Song again and they walk home.

You heard that pulse and called it a landmark. It is a landmark. It is also a letter, posted every few seconds for generations, and once a year it is answered.

## The Rules of the Place

The plateau obeys a logic of patient subtraction. It lowers itself — measurably; the uplanders keep the measure in the Song — by about the width of a hand in a long lifetime. That hand's-width does not vanish. It travels. It goes down the Gullet and through the breathing dark and out through the weeping walls below, and it becomes the fingernail's-width of new stone on ten thousand columns. The plateau's losses and the basin's gains are one ledger, kept in two places. The rust-orange stains bleeding down the columns' flanks are the plateau's iron ledges, arriving. The faint mineral green is the plateau's copper seams, arriving. The seeds sealed in the columns — the gray bird nests here, in the wind-bent woods, and its lineage has flown the same route to the same drinking-place for longer than the Song is old — those seeds are the plateau's forests, arriving, including forests of trees the plateau itself has since lost and the Song has since forgotten. The basin remembers woods that no living uplander could name. The archive outgrew the archivists and neither knows it.

And the deepest rule, the one the uplanders state most plainly: the two lands hold each other's missing halves. The plateau keeps names and cannot keep bodies. The basin keeps bodies and cannot keep names. A person taken by the plateau survives as a verse with no grave; a person kept by the basin survives as a column with no verse, unless the Song holds one. Only the Downgone — sung above, standing below — are wholly kept, held at both ends. That is what the office of the Keeper existed to maintain: an uplander, serving a term of years down among the columns, carrying firewood and food down the Road, feeding the newly-still through reeds, learning each column's voice, matching stone to name, and climbing back up at term's end to sing the reconciled ledger into the Song. The smoke-stain sealed under glaze in the basin's shelter is plateau wood. Every fire ever lit in that valley was carried down from this grass.

The Road broke in the Swallowing, and the office has stood vacant since, and the uplanders feel the vacancy the way you feel a missing tooth — constantly, with the tongue of the Song, which still holds the Keeper's verses, still holds the training, still holds the sound-map, all of it kept in motion and none of it used.

## The Same

One more thing, and it is the thing both lands know without either being able to say it alone.

You stood in the basin and felt it breathing, and you were told, rightly, that the biggest thing living there was the basin itself. Stand on the plateau in the night, when the wells sigh and the drum-meadows sound their notes and the Gullet swallows on in the dark, and you will feel the same breath from above. It is not a similar creature. It is the same creature. One being, with its dissolving flank in the sunlight and its growing flank in the drip-dark, its bloodstream running through the flooded rooms between, its slow swimmers moving in its veins, its films lining its gut, its Kept becoming its bones, its Rememberers riding on its back keeping the names of everything it takes. It is a landmass mid-stride — eating itself here, rebuilding itself there, walking downhill at the pace of dissolution, and it will take an age of the world to finish the step.

The uplanders live on the part of it that forgets. The Kept stand in the part of it that cannot stop remembering. And between them, over the broken lip, runs the one gap in the whole circuit — the gap where the Road was, the gap where the Keeper was, the gap that can only be crossed by something that moves of its own will and carries what it has heard.

The rain that beaded on your shoulders as you crossed this grass will fall into the basin, most of it, within a few years of dark travel, and where it lands it will stay forever. You will not. You have stood at the lip now from both sides; you have the roar in one ear and the Song in the other; you know three names and the pulse that answers to them.

The plateau does not chase either. *What stops is taken*, it says, and lets you keep walking. But the Song has an open verse, and the basin has an open door, and between them, at present, there is only you.
