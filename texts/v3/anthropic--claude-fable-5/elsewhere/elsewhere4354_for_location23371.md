**The Undercroft**

There is only one road to it, and the road is vertical.

You board the cage at the parish bank — the resident will send it up for you if you wait by the headframe through one full turn of the wheel; the lamps brighten to show it understands — and then you drop, down the one shaft in the drowned world that still goes anywhere, through water so cold and black that the descent feels less like travel than like being slowly forgotten. The cable sings a low note above you. The shaft walls slide past, brick, then rock, then rock older than brick. And then, forty fathoms below the town that is itself forty meters below everything, the water ends.

It does not end at a bottom. It ends at a *surface* — an upside-down surface, a ceiling of black water held taut across the shaft, and the cage breaks through it downward the way a swimmer breaks upward into air, and you drop out of the flood streaming and dripping into a space the flood has never been allowed to enter. This is the second strangeness you can touch, and it is the twin of the first. Up in the parish lanes the strangeness was warm iron in freezing water. Here it is this: *air*. Dry, warm, mineral-smelling air, tasting of coal and tallow and old stone, pressing gently on your ears — the last air in the world, or near enough, held in place forty fathoms under a drowned planet by nothing except that patient *thud... thud...* which you have heard before, faint and upholstered, through meters of silt and cold. Here it is not faint. Here you are inside it. The pump under the parish draws its water from these galleries — every seep, every weep of the rock, gathered and lifted and sent up warm — and so the heartbeat you strained to hear up in the lanes is, down here, the loudest thing there is. You have descended through the body and arrived in the chest.

Look up before you go on. The underside of the flood hangs above the shaft mouth like a ceiling of dark glass, trembling very slightly with the pump's rhythm, and your own lamp reflects in it: a small orange star in a black sky, upside down. The parish sits somewhere on the far side of that sky. Its warmth comes from here — the deep rock runs warm, and the water pumped up from the sump carries the warmth with it, so that everything the parish gives its tenants, the heated seams where the pale choir roots, the braid from the boiler-house vent where the shrimp bathe, the pipe-bore where the old eel sleeps, is this place's breath, exhaled upward through forty fathoms of iron. The two towns share one circulation. One of them merely happens to be drowned.

The Undercroft itself is not a town. It is a parish of roads — galleries, the miners would have said, roadways, headings — low tunnels shouldered with timber props, running out from the shaft bottom in the branching pattern of something that grew rather than something that was planned. You walk them stooped, as you ducked the pipes above, and the resemblance is not an accident: the parish's lanes were more conduit than street, and these roads *are* conduit, for air. Ventilation doors stand at intervals, heavy wooden doors in brick frames, and here is the Undercroft's version of the brass valve that turned with no hand upon it: at long intervals, on no schedule you can find, a door swings open a hand's width, holds, and closes again, softly, with the deliberate gentleness of something checking that it still can. The air stirs when this happens. It moves along the roads in a slow circuit, like the warm water in the pipes above, and for the same reason. Circulation is not optional in either place. It is the difference between a body and a tomb.

And where the parish had softened — its staircases dissolved to ramps, its doorways rounded, everything worn by the water into settlement and truce — the Undercroft is *sharp*. The dry air preserves. Chalk marks made lifetimes ago are still crisp on the coalface, still legible: initials, dates, the terse liturgy of inspection, *examined, all clear, fit to work*. Jackets hang on their nails with their shapes still in them. In the stables near the shaft bottom the stalls stand empty but swept, and above each one a pony's name is chalked in a careful hand — you can read every name — and the chalk has not blurred by a grain. Nothing here has reached terms with anything, because nothing here has had to. Above, time settled. Below, time was simply asked to wait, and it is waiting.

Sound is the inversion of the parish's sound. Up there, silence with a pulse beneath it. Down here, the pulse with silences inside it: the great *thud* of the pump, the dripping of the rock into the channels that feed the sump, the occasional tick of a timber prop easing its shoulders — and once an hour, a long descending note in the shaft guides as the cage comes down, and the ceiling of black water shivers into rings, and the cage breaks through and hangs at the shaft bottom, streaming, open, empty, and waits.

You already know who sent it.

**Who Lives in the Undercroft**

The census is short, and honest work has been made of keeping it that way.

The fungus is the garden here, and the counterpart of the pale choir above. It blooms on the old timber props in white rosettes, dry and soft as ash, and like the choir it grows only where growing is permitted — never across a chalk mark, never on a door, never within arm's reach of the roads' center line where a lamp must pass. Prop after prop wears its rosette like a posy at a pew-end, and the pruning is invisible and constant, and a gardener who weeds is a gardener. There are pale crickets in the stables, descendants of stowaways in fodder that is generations gone, singing thinly in the dark to no season. There are small blind beetles in the sump channels, grazing the mineral films as their cousins the shrimp graze the warm iron overhead, both congregations living entirely on the charity of the same heartbeat, one on each side of the flood, neither aware the other exists.

None of them is the resident. The resident is the flame.

You will see it before you understand it — far down a gallery, around a turning, an orange light the size of a thumbnail, steady, moving at a walking pace, always receding. It is a safety lamp's flame, or it carries itself as one; whether there is a lamp around it, or a hand under the lamp, is a question the Undercroft answers the way the parish answered its questions, which is to say by warmth and by conduct rather than by showing you anything. What can be verified is this: it is the last fire in the world. Everywhere else, the flood put fire out forever. Here, under the ceiling of water, in the kept air, one flame is still on its rounds — and *rounds* is again the exact word, because it does not wander. It walks the roads in order. It pauses at the face and lowers itself, watching its own cap of light for the ghost-blue flare that would mean gas, and finding none, or finding some and going away and coming back when the doors have breathed the road clean. And where it has passed, in the morning — if the word morning can be forced to serve — there is a new chalk mark, in the same careful hand as ten thousand marks before it, the newest still soft enough to smudge: *examined. all clear.* The Undercroft is signed, daily, like a register. It has been signed every day since the water came.

What the flame was before it was this, the roads keep to themselves, and the guesses come in the same threes as the guesses above, and are probably the same guesses wearing different clothes. That it was the deputy — the fire-boss, the one whose office it was to walk the roads before the shift and after, whose rule was iron and simple: *the deputy goes up last* — and that when the water broke in above and the men crowded the cage, he sent them up riding and stayed to mind the sump and the doors, and last never came, because the men who rode up rode up into the end of the world, and their brass tokens still hang absent from the board. That it was never one man but the accumulated diligence of every inspection ever chalked, the habit of *examined, all clear* repeated until it could repeat itself, care holding its shape like a glove. Or that the flame, carried so long, trimmed and shielded and read like scripture through so many thousand shifts, simply learned tending the only way anything learns anything — by having it done to you until you can do it yourself — and now carries its own lamp, and tends its own air, and walks its own rounds. Choose whichever account you like. Down here the difference has not worn away, because nothing wears away; it has merely been filed, and is no longer consulted.

The tally board hangs in the lamp cabin by the shaft bottom, and it is the whole of the resident's theology. Rows of numbered hooks. On some hooks, brass tokens, polished by a thumb that checks them. On many hooks, nothing — the tokens of the men who rode up out of the flood and never came back down to hang them, because there was no back, because there was no down, because there was very shortly no anywhere. By the book, an empty hook is a man still out. By the book, the deputy does not leave while a man is out. By the book, the shift is not ended until the bank above signals it ended — and forty fathoms up, in the breathing orange lanes, there is a resident who will not end the shift until the men come *up*. So the promise has two ends, and each end is held, and each holds the other in place: the parish keeps the pump alive so the roads stay dry for men it believes are below; the Undercroft keeps the roads inspected and the air sweet for men it knows went above; and between them, once an hour, the wheel turns, and the cage comes down through the black glass ceiling, and hangs there, open.

And once an hour, the flame comes to the shaft bottom. You can watch, if you stand quiet by the stables; it does not mind being watched any more than the parish minded being visited, and if you stay long enough your position will be entered into the rounds, a chalk mark appearing near where you sleep, *examined, all clear*, which is as close as the Undercroft comes to tucking you in. The flame approaches the cage. It brightens a little — checking, the way the lamps above brighten, *is it them, is the signal come* — and it is never them, and the signal never comes. It stands before the open cage the length of a long breath. Anything could ride up. Nothing does. Then it turns to the tally board and touches the empty hooks, one by one, in order, like a rosary, and it turns away, and the cage rises, streaming, through the ceiling of the flood, up the long cold shaft to the parish, where a resident made of pipework and patience notes its arrival — empty again, *not ready yet* — and rings the soft chime, and lowers it once more.

When the large thing passes over the drowned town far above, the pressure of its passage runs down the shaft, and the black ceiling bulges, and the flame crouches small and blue against its wick until the world steadies. In those minutes the pump slows overhead and the doors hold still and the lamps of the parish dim, and the two residents, who have never met, who have conducted the longest conversation in the world entirely in the sending and returning of an empty cage, are afraid at exactly the same time, in exactly the same way, together. It is the only thing they do together, except everything.

Then the pressure lifts. The heart resumes its count. The flame straightens on its wick, takes up its chalk, and goes back down the roads to sign the register — the kept flame keeping the kept air, the last-up who has not yet been last, the one who counts — and somewhere overhead, through forty fathoms of water and one shared warmth, the shift goes on.
