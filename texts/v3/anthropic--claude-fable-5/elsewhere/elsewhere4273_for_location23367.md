**The Coldroot**

Begin with the cold, because everything here is an answer to it. The Coldroot is the surface the planet intended — the old floor of the world, down at the honest radius, under air so thick and generous that a Caldfolk lung would not know what to do with it. This is where the geologists came from, back when it sent them up, back when it still had questions. It is low the way the Calderwald is high: absolutely, definitionally, a whole continent of lowness spreading out from the foot of the escarpment, its rivers slow, its light dim, its sky not a sky at all but a ceiling.

And it is cold — not the cold of weather, which comes and goes and can be argued with, but a cold that rises. Press your palm to any stone in the Coldroot and the stone takes from you. It has no temperature of its own to offer; it is a debt, an absence, and the hand on it grows aware of its own warmth the way a purse grows aware of its coins in a bad neighborhood. The frost here does not fall. It comes up. On a still morning in high summer the ground can kill a garden from below while the air above it stays mild, and the people have long since stopped planting in the ground at all.

They know where the warmth went. Everyone in the Coldroot knows where the warmth went. You can see it on a clear day — there are no clear days, but you can see it anyway, the one thing that breaks the ceiling: the Wall, going up.

The Calderwald is a body holding its heat. The Coldroot is where it is holding it from. No survey ever proved this, because the surveys all went up and stayed, but the arithmetic of a body is simple, and the people down here did the arithmetic generations ago and folded the answer into a household word. The rising cold, the steady patient withdrawal underfoot — they call it the *banking*. The world banked its fire, they say, the way any sensible house banks its fire: raked the ash over the coals to hold them through a long night. The Calderwald is the coal. The cloud-ceiling is the ash. The Coldroot is everything the raking left in the dark — and the whole culture of the place, its fires and its watches and its terrible good cheer, follows from the second half of the metaphor, which is that a banked fire implies a morning.

**The terrain**

The air first, because it is the opposite of the highland's in every particular. It is thick, wet, and loud. Sounds arrive huge and rude — a dropped kettle carries across a valley, a courier is heard singing half an hour before she is seen — and fires burn the way the Calderwald's cannot: orange, greedy, roaring, delighted with themselves. The light is another matter. The Lid — the permanent cloud ceiling, all the weather of the world pooled at the world's true level — hangs low and unbroken, and what comes through it is a soft grey-green glow with no direction and no hour in it. The Coldroot has never seen its sun. It knows the sun exists because the Upgone reported it, ages back: a hard white coin, they said, in a sky gone violet, and the Coldroot took this on faith the way it takes most things about the upstairs of the world.

Under the Lid, the forest — and the forest is the terrain, because the true ground has been surrendered. The Coldroot is green the way the Calderwald is bare: overwhelmingly, anciently, a continent of trees that were old when the geologists left and have spent the ages since learning to live above their own soil. The banking rises a little every century, and the forest has answered by lifting itself. Trees stand on stilted roots, on buttresses, on great mounds of their own dead; the living layer floats on the rotting layer, and the rotting layer is warm, because rot is the one fire that lights itself. That is the deep trade of the place. The stone gives nothing, so decay gives everything. Every warm thing in the Coldroot is warm because something is being unmade beneath it, and nobody down here finds this grim. They find it tidy.

Between the mounds run the *warm roads* — old windrows of forest litter, decades deep, banked and tended like the linear hearths they are, snow-free and faintly steaming, threading the country from town to town. They are the Coldroot's cableways. Travelers keep to them not for comfort but for navigation: the roads are read through the feet, and a courier in thin-soled boots can walk them in full dark by warmth alone, exactly as a herder on the plateau far above reads outcrops with a palm — the same literacy, ages divided, still legible in both scripts.

The rivers are the sad architecture of the place. They freeze from the bed up. The banking takes them from below, so ice forms at the bottom first — anchor ice, rising in pale plates — and a river a week's walk from its source is already running under a roof of its own making, and far out in the deep Coldroot the rivers are silent tunnels, traveled with lamps. Every one of them, followed upstream, leads to the same parent. But that belongs to the Wall, and the Wall gets its own telling.

**The towns**

A town in the Coldroot is a fire with buildings around it.

That is not a figure of speech; it is a legal description. At the center of every town burns the *mother-hearth* — a fire that has not gone out in living memory, or in dead memory, or in any memory at all — and the town is whatever huddle of structures currently has the honor of keeping the weather off it. The buildings themselves are young, always. Rot eats timber, frost-heave cracks whatever the timber stood on, and so everything is rebuilt on a cycle of a generation or two; the newest structures look new, the oldest structures look new, and a Coldroot town has none of the highland's geological patience about it — it looks improvised, raucous, half-finished, a camp that has been breaking and remaking itself in the same spot for a thousand years. Only the fires look old. The building is the hearth's clothing, the saying goes, changed when soiled. Ask an elder where her people are from and she will not gesture at any wall. She will name a flame.

Because the flames have names, and lineages, and the lineages are the true genealogy of the Coldroot. Every hearth descends from another hearth: coals carried in an ash-packed horn when a daughter marries, when a town divides, when a new clearing is made — the *ember-carrying*, the most serious errand the culture has — and the hearth-lines are kept the way the foundry families above keep their flues, which is to say the people are named after what they tend. All the mother-hearths, traced back far enough, descend from a handful of first fires, and the first fires descend from the one that came down out of no one remembers where, and the hearth-clerks keep the descent charted on fired clay, because clay that has been through the fire is the only record rot cannot reach.

The industry of the towns is fast and loud and hot, the highland's sighing foundries turned inside out. Charcoal clamps smoking in rows. Tar pits. Glass-houses roaring on the endless timber the cold-killed forest sheds. Kilns firing the tiles and pipes and pots of a civilization that builds nothing to last and therefore fires everything that must. The stacks of a Coldroot town belch honestly, joyfully; the Lid takes the smoke and does not lower itself to comment.

And under all of it, the watches. A hearth is never untended — not the mother-hearth, not the household fires — and so a Coldroot town is never entirely asleep. The population sleeps in shifts, keeps fire-watch in shifts, and the watch is the deep unit of the culture, the thing childhood is organized around and marriages are negotiated across and time itself is counted in. Where the Calderwald selects for patience, the Coldroot selects for *tending* — promptness, vigilance, the willingness to get up when it is your hour — and after enough generations that temperament, too, has stopped being a culture and become something closer to an anatomy.

**The green, and the few**

Here is the Coldroot's inversion of the highland's inversion: all plants, and almost no animals. Whatever took the warmth took the beasts first, or sent them up — and the ecology has closed over the gap with fungus. Fungus is the fauna here. It does the moving, the eating, the surprising. The mounds and windrows are laced through with mycelium that hurries decay along like an eager foreman, and at night the forest shows what it has been doing all day: the *coldshine*, pale foxfire glowing along every rotting log and warm road, so that the Coldroot after dark is faintly, coldly luminous everywhere except the towns, where honest orange firelight is kept jealously indoors. The highland's towns glow in a dark land. The Coldroot's towns are the dark spots in a glowing one.

The animals that remain are hearth-animals, all of them, commensals of the one economy going.

The **moldwives** — big, flightless, self-important birds who build mound-nests of raked compost and tend the internal temperature with their beaks, turn by turn, like small feathered clerks — are the honored ancestors of the whole way of life; the first warm roads, the first mound-gardens, possibly the first banked fires were all lessons taken from watching moldwives, and it is deep custom that no town is founded except beside an active mound, with apologies made to the residents.

The **hearthdoves** roost in the dovecotes built over every mother-hearth, round and grey and sharp-eyed, working the warm ash for the mineral grit they need. They are the sighted, lowland original of a bird the Coldroot has not seen in a very long time. When the survey crews went up the Wall, flocks followed the camp-warmth — doves go where the warmth goes, the lowlanders say, with a certain flatness — and never came down, and somewhere in the generations above, in the hot dark of the foundry stacks, their descendants let go of their eyes and learned to see heat instead. The slagdoves of the Calderwald and the hearthdoves of the Coldroot are one bird divided by an altitude, and neither flock knows it, and both peoples feed theirs at the skimming of the day's work with the same unbothered air.

And in the flue systems of the kilns and glass-houses live the **soot-martens**, long, low, quick, indulged, the lowland cousins of the mirror-scaled animals denning in the flues a world above — some of whom went up as camp animals with the crews, riding in the tool baskets, and stayed, as everything that went up stayed.

**The Hearthfolk**

Begin with tending, because everyone here is tending.

The bodies of the Coldroot run hot and fast. Quick pulses, huge appetites, a metabolism like a well-drafted stove — the thick air asks nothing of the lungs, so the blood spends itself on warmth instead, and Hearthfolk children are glow-cheeked, loud, and in constant motion, and unlike the highland's children they never grow out of running. Adults run. Couriers on the warm roads move at a lope that eats forty kilometers between watches, singing as they go, because in air this rich a voice is a beacon and the culture has made an art of it: the songs are pitched to carry, full of hard stops and struck consonants, and a name here is one breath long, short enough to shout across a river. Old age is measured at the extremities. *The banking starts at the feet*, they say of their elders, whose feet go cold first and are wrapped and warmed and fussed over by grandchildren whose own feet will not stay cold in a snowbank.

Their literacy is fire. A Hearthfolk adult reads a flame the way a founder above reads a cooling casting — color, stance, the grain of the smoke, the sound of the draw — and can tell you what the fire is eating, what it wants, how many hours it holds. Flame-reading is taught with children's lessons and graded exercises and the same quiet shame attached to grown people who never quite get it. Down the Wall or up it, the whole world speaks in temperature; the two peoples simply learned it in different dialects, one from the steady stone and one from the hungry flame, and each would find the other's fluency uncanny and familiar at once.

Their quarrels are governed by the fire too, and here the mirror is exact and reversed. On the plateau, disputes are slowed: a mandatory revolution of the great flywheel between statement and reply, silence enforced until the anger starves. In the Coldroot, disputes are hurried: no quarrel may outlive the day it was born, because *no fire is banked over a quarrel* — the evening raking of ash over coals is done together, by both parties if need be, hands on the same rake, and a grievance carried past the banking is the nearest thing the culture has to a crime. Both peoples, notice, settle their differences by the clock of the thing they tend. The difference is only how fast their clocks run.

Their dead go out like matches. A Coldroot body loses its warmth almost before the breath is done — minutes, no more, as if something takes the heat back the instant the tending stops, and everyone down here understands that this is the banking, prompt as always, calling in a small loan. The family holds the hands until the warmth is gone, which is why Hearthfolk hold hands so much in life; there is no time to start at the end. Then the body is given to the mounds — laid into the warm rot beside the moldwives' nests, where the one self-lighting fire takes it into the base of everything — and the burial words are four words long, and the second breath of them is, syllable for worn syllable, the same as the words said over the sleeping hollows on the plateau, though no one living on either side of the Wall knows there is another side saying anything.

*Warmth out, warmth on.*

**The Wall**

From the Coldroot, the Calderwald does not exist. What exists is the Wall: the escarpment, rising out of the forest and going up through the Lid and continuing — this is the point, this is what a lowland child stands and feels in the stomach — *continuing*, past where the clouds end it, to heights that are entirely a matter of report. The Wall is weather's grave. Everything the Lid carries breaks against it, and the Wall's foot is the wettest, greenest, most thunderous country in the Coldroot, and every river on the continent is born there, in the *weeping course*: the band of altitude where the cold cloud leans against stone that is — already, this low on the Wall's face — warm. Warm rock above, weeping sky below, and the meeting of them runs off in a thousand permanent falls. The Coldroot drinks, in other words, from the condensation on the outside of the world's banked coal. The rivers are the highland's warmth coming down the only way it still comes down, and the lowlanders know this, and it is why river water is the courteous thing to offer a guest.

The line where the stone goes warm is called the **hearthline**, and it is the holiest altitude in a culture that insists it has no holy anything. Below it, the Wall's face is wet, mossed, frost-split, climbable. Above it, the rock dries, goes tawny, rounds into wind-polish — and just above it, barely, the lowest bloomstones grow, rose and arsenic-green, the first ones, the outermost branches of whatever the highland is. The **Long Stair** climbs to it: the route the survey crews cut, its lowest flights long since rot-eaten and remade in timber a hundred times, its middle flights cold stone, and then — pilgrims describe this the same way every time — a step that is warm. Then another. The pilgrimage of the Coldroot, the one journey nearly everyone makes once, is to climb through the weeping course and the cloud to the hearthline and put a palm flat on the first warm stone, the same gesture their whole people makes a dozen times a day on hearthstones at home, made once, here, on the stone that needs no fire — and then to come down.

Almost no one goes higher. Custom says the warmth up there is not theirs to spend; they were left the making of it, and there is a kind of pride in that. But every generation a few touch the warm step and do not turn around — the air thinning, the stair fading into unmaintained rock, and the person climbing on into the report of a hard white sun — and the language keeps a special tense for them, and the tense is the whole story of the two countries: the **Upgone**. It is not a tense of loss, exactly. It is the tense of the ember carried out of the house.

Twice a year, sometimes three times, the Wall sends something down. A dry wind off the invisible top loses its grip on the rim, and for a day the Lid glitters and the Coldroot receives the **highsnow**: a fine, faintly metallic dust, sifting down through the forest, the rock flour of a country of rock flour, the highland's dander. Where it settles, the ground frost holds off for a season and the coldshine blooms strange and bright, and the lowlanders sweep it up with soft brushes and keep it in fired jars, and they call it, without any irony at all, the mail. It is the only mail that still comes down the Wall. They do not know that at the head of the Long Stair, in a small stack-town on the rim, there is a gate that the mapkeeper of that town still, by a custom nobody explains, leaves unbarred.

**The strangeness**

The banking, first — cold at a depth and a latitude where there should be warmth, the ground's heat withdrawing steadily with no glacier above it and no reason below it, the surveys (before the surveyors went up) finding only colder stone all the way down, as far as anyone drilled. The banking does not deepen suddenly and does not relent. It has the steadiness of a held breath.

Then the mornings.

On the stillest dawns, when the air stands and the ground-cold rises clean, the frost comes up in figures. Branching, deliberate, the same figure, tried again — on black river ice, on swept stone, and above all on the slates that the **rime-clerks** set out each evening for exactly this purpose, blacked and polished, an offered page. By an hour after dawn the figure is water. So the clerks are out before light with lamps and steady hands, tracing the night's attempt into wet clay, and the tile is fired the same day — permanence through fire, the Coldroot's answer to everything — and the tile-houses of the towns are shingled inside with centuries of dawns, wall after wall of the figure, and if you read along a wall from the old end to the new, the character steadies like a hand finding its grip.

The rime-clerks observe the same near-only taboo as certain slow clerks a world above them: think what you like about what is being written; record only what was written. But they will tell you, over several evenings, what the tiles make unarguable. That the figure is general — every town, every slate, the whole continent's frost leaning the same way. That it is getting less wrong. And — mentioned last, and most carefully — that it has been getting less wrong *faster* since the hearths. Since the fires were named and kept, since the slates began to be offered, since the people settled in to tend and to watch: the writing has steadied, the way handwriting steadies when someone is finally watching.

Here is what the narrator may say and neither people can: it is the same character. The rose-and-arsenic accretions arranging themselves across the vent-fields of the Calderwald over four-hundred-year drafts, and the frost-figures rising through Coldroot slate and gone by noon — the patient copy and the hurried one, the character written in warmth gathering and the character written in warmth withdrawn — are one attempt, made from both sides of one held breath. The margins of the highland's oldest master chart are crowded with keepers' dying guesses at the finished form, converging, hand after hand. The oldest guess of all was drawn by the first keeper, who is recorded in the flue-name genealogies only as having come up the Long Stair as a girl. It matches the tiles. It is not a guess. It is a memory.

And so the Hearthfolk have their own slow suspicion, the reflection of the one the Caldfolk keep unhurried company with. Up there, the question is whether the people are the highland's tenants or its handwriting. Down here, it takes the household form everything takes down here: whether the Hearthfolk keep the fires, or the fires keep the Hearthfolk — whether the towns are settlements at all, or the world's own coals, raked over with cloud, tended in shifts, carried in ash-packed horns from clearing to clearing, kept — the way any sensible house keeps them — against a morning. They do not argue about it. Arguing would have to be settled by dark, and this question, they say, belongs to the one fire that gets to take its time.

**Morning**

In the last watch of the night the Coldroot is at its most itself: the forest glowing its cold pale glow, the towns dark and breathing, one person awake in every house with a hand near the rake. Then the Lid greys toward its directionless dawn, and the rime-clerks go out with their lamps to the offered slates, and kneel in the rising cold to see what the night tried this time, and trace it fast, before the day takes it back.

Behind them the towns unbank. Ash is drawn off the coals house by house, and the fires stand up orange and greedy in the thick air, and the hearthdoves come down into the warm ash in a soft grey flurry, and the smell of a continent of woken fires goes up against the ceiling of the world. Somewhere at the Wall's foot a pilgrim starts up the Long Stair toward the hearthline, to lay a palm on warm stone she has never felt and make, on the outside of the world's banked heart, the gesture her people make a dozen times a day on the hearthstones inside it.

Far above her — above the weeping course, above the Lid, above the last cold step — that same warmth is somebody's floor. A mapkeeper up there is pressing a palm flat to it without thinking, the way other peoples touch wood. Neither of them knows the other's hand is on the same stone. The stone knows. It has the temperature of a sleeping animal on one side and the temperature of a held breath on the other, and between the two of them, patient as ever, it goes on trying to spell its word — breathing out where it is watched by the quiet people, breathing in where it is watched by the loud ones, and it is one breath, and both peoples, in their two dialects of warmth, are keeping it.
