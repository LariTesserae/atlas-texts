**The Chorus Fen on the Drowned Shield**

Begin, again, with rock — because it is the same rock, or its ancestor. The old crust the spire was pushed through lies down here under the haze, and it is old enough to have forgotten being liquid so thoroughly that it has nearly finished being rock at all. Three billion years of weather have negotiated away every edge it ever had. What remains is a shield of dark mineral worn flat as a floor, drowned under a hand's depth of warm water and a fathom of peat, stretching level in every direction until the mist closes over it. Nowhere in the fen — and this is the first thing a hand would notice, the hand that would have been cut on the summit — is there a single sharp thing. Every stone is a rounded stone. Every surface yields. The fen is what patience does to sharpness, given long enough, and it has had longer than almost anywhere.

From above, the haze is a thing you don't look into. From within, it is simply the sky: a warm pearl-grey ceiling, permanent, sourceless, hanging perhaps a hundred feet up. Light comes through it diffuse and shadowless, the light of the inside of an egg. Nothing here has ever cast a hard shadow or seen a horizon. A fine rain falls somewhere in the fen at every hour — not weather so much as circulation, the water going up warm and coming down soft — and part of that warmth, though almost nothing in the fen knows it, rises off the one place where the old shield is still hot: a ring of steaming water in the far distance, at the foot of something the ceiling hides.

The spire makes its own veil. The heat of its young root steams the fen, and the steam is the haze, and the haze is why the fen has never seen what stands above it, and why what stands above has never seen the fen. Each is the other's hidden half, and the curtain between them is drawn by the newcomer's own warmth.

---

**The sound**

If the spire is defined by silence, the fen is defined by the opposite of it, and not casually.

The air here is thick, wet, and warm — the perfect medium — and the water beneath the reed-beds carries vibration for miles. Sound is *cheap* in the fen, the way sound is expensive on the summit, and everything alive here spends it lavishly. At any hour the fen is a layered chorus: the tick and burr of the small water-life, the pop of gas rising through peat, the low percussion of the reed-beds when wind crosses them, and over and through it all the calling of the fen's people, which never entirely stops.

Space in the fen is not seen; it is *heard*. With the mist closing off everything past a few hundred paces, distance is measured in echo and attenuation, direction is read off the water's skin, and a landmark is not a shape but a sound-source — this reed-bed's particular rattle, that warm seep's particular hiss. A fen-dweller navigating home in the dark is doing exactly what a builder does reading a warm wall, in a different medium: moving through a field, fluently, without needing light.

And under everything, at the bottom of the whole soundscape, holding it up like a floor, there is one constant note. A single low tone, at the edge of hearing, that has not stopped in living memory or sung memory either. It comes from beyond the steaming ring, from above the ceiling, from the thing the fen has never seen. The fen was musical before the note existed — the oldest songs say so — but every song made in the last three centuries is keyed to it. The fen no longer has music that isn't, in some register, an accompaniment.

---

**The flora that will not keep**

Where the spire's plants are candles — dense, slow, four hundred years in the standing — the fen's are the opposite wager. Everything here grows fast, cheap, and wet, and rots faster. The dominant growth is the *drumreed*, hollow-stemmed, head-high, growing in beds that stretch acre upon acre; a drumreed goes from sprout to seed in a season, and a dead bed collapses into peat in two. There is a tree-analog, the *sloughwood*, spongy and pale, which achieves its full height in a decade and spends the next decade gracefully falling apart, shedding sheets of soft bark that the fen's people strip and use. Nothing vegetable in the fen outlives a short life twice over.

The consequence is a world without durable material. No stone that holds an edge — the shield's stones are all worn round. No wood that keeps — sloughwood rots in the wall as it rotted on the stump. Nothing the fen grows will still exist in thirty years. It is a rich world, an abundant world, and a world in which permanence simply cannot be built out of anything local.

Everything durable the fen possesses fell out of its sky. Hold that; it is the hinge of the place.

Light, too, is scarce. The ceiling's grey glow, the brief phosphor of certain rot, and otherwise nothing. The fen is a dim world with superb ears, as the summit is a bright one with none.

---

**The villages of the singers**

The fen's people are amphibious — sleek, peat-dark, about knee-high at the shoulder when they haul out, with dense oiled coats, small weak eyes, and ears like unfolding fans, elaborate as the builders' flank-bands and serving the same office. Their forelimbs end in webbed, clever, genuinely dexterous fingers: the fen, unlike the summit, has hands, and it has what hands make. There are tools here — reed-knives, weirs, woven traps, bark cordage — the whole grammar of made things that the spire, for all its architecture, never developed and never needed.

Their villages are mounds and rafts: heaped peat cores faced with woven drumreed, ramped and chambered, standing a few feet proud of the water, housing a few dozen singers each. A village is rebuilt perpetually, because it is perpetually rotting; a wall lasts eight years, a whole village migrates itself, panel by replaced panel, a full body-length across the fen in a generation without anyone deciding to move. Nothing about the architecture is meant to last. It is not where the fen keeps anything that matters.

Each singer has a name. This is worth pausing on, because it is the sharpest of all the differences: on the spire, nothing is called anything, and identity is a residue accreted on the body, read by touch. In the fen, everything is called something, and identity is a *call* — a short personal figure of notes, given at birth by the mother, elaborated by its bearer over a lifetime, sounded at every arrival and answering every hail. A singer's name grows as its life grows; an elder's name-call is long, ornamented, dense with quotation from every name it has loved or worked beside. You are, here as there, what you have accumulated. The fen simply keeps the ledger in air instead of on skin.

They live fast — twenty years is a long life — and they die into the water, and the water keeps nothing. Which is why they built the one thing they did build to last.

---

**The long song**

The fen's only durable architecture is made of breath.

Every night, in every village, in overlapping relay, some portion of the *long song* is performed — a continuous sung record, added to at the far end, maintained along its whole length, taught forward voice to voice. It holds genealogy, weather, boundary, grievance, remedy, and event; it is archive, law, and map. Performed entire it would take many nights, and it is never performed entire, but every verse of it is alive in enough throats that the death of any singer, any village, loses nothing. The pale ones under the resin town carry the town's memory by digestion; the fen carries its own by rehearsal. Both places have solved the same problem — how a thing outlives everyone in it — and neither solution would work an hour in the other's world.

The long song's oldest verse — the deep verse, sung rarely, and only by elders, and always pitched against that constant low note as though leaning on it — describes a night when the flat world broke. Fire standing up out of the water. The ceiling lit red from above for a season. The boiling of the eastern fen, the death of villages whose name-calls the verse still carries, the long rain of hot stone. The fen is old enough to remember the spire being liquid. It watched the birth of the thing above it, paid for the watching, and set the account down in the only stone it has.

The verse ends with the fire going dark, and then — the song marks the interval precisely, four winters — the beginning of the note. The mountain cooled, found its voice, and began to hum. The deep verse gives the spire its name, and the name is not a word. It is a held low tone, the note itself, sung back. The spire's name in the fen is its own voice.

---

**What falls**

Everything the fen cannot make falls out of its sky, and the fen has organized itself around the falling.

Stone comes down first and rarest — shards of the young rock, shed when the spire's cracks flex, striking the fen along a scatter-line east of the steaming ring. This is the only sharp material in the world of the fen. A flake of spire-glass is a knife that cuts drumreed like water, holds its edge for a decade, and passes down named lines of inheritance inside the long song. Every blade in the fen is a piece of the mountain.

Amber comes down more often — small hard beads, out of nowhere, striking the water with a *plink* the fen knows instantly as foreign, because the fen knows every sound it can make and that is not one of them. Singers dive for the beads. What they bring up is the fen's treasure and its lamplight: the resin drinks the ceiling's grey glow all day and gives it back all night, dim as a coal seen through a hand, and the brightest thing the fen owns. Old beads hang in the roof-peaks of every village. And some beads carry passengers — small creatures sealed inside, thread-thin climbers, bead-shelled crawlers, things like frozen droplets — animals no singer has ever seen alive or will. The fen's entire natural history of the world above is a few dozen strangers preserved in light, and the long song describes each one.

The fen does not know it is receiving a town's slow weeping, its eaves and its chimneys, a season per inch and then the drop. It knows only that the mountain sheds tears of lamplight, and it has drawn the obvious conclusion.

And wings come down. Along a line running out from the spire on the prevailing wind lies the quarter the singers call, in the song, the field-of-the-fallen: where the gleaners' last glides end. When a wing is sighted descending out of the ceiling — a stiff dark cross, dropping in a long shallow line — singers paddle out to meet it. Most arrive dead. Some arrive alive, and live a few days on the mounds, silent, enormous-eyed, attended.

The dying ones flash their pale throats. The singers cannot flash back — the fen has no light-language, as the summit has no voice — and so they answer the only way the fen answers anything: they sing to it, softly, while it signals into their darkness, two grammars sliding past each other at a hand's distance. But the singers noticed, generations ago, that the flashes have pattern, and one lineage among them keeps the *flash verses* — the dying signals transcribed into sung rhythm, meaning unknown, preserved anyway, a library of untranslated last words. The patterns are various. Many occur only once, in one dying animal, and never again in any other — as though each carried a word that had belonged to two, and was being said, at the end, by one.

Where the gleaners lie, a flower grows that grows nowhere else in the fen: pale, up-facing, blooming along the fall-line in a thin scattered ribbon that runs — though the fen cannot see it and the summit cannot either — straight back through the haze toward the spire, like a sentence trailing off in the direction of its speaker.

---

**The note**

Here is what the fen believes, and has believed since four winters after the fire went dark: the mountain is a single living being. It hums without ceasing. It glows — for on the rare nights, once in some years, when a hard wind tears the ceiling open, the whole fen falls silent at once, every voice, every village, the only silences the fen ever keeps, and looks up at a single soft ember burning impossibly high; and the long song holds each of these Great Silences as a rest, a measured nothing, performed as faithfully as any verse. It weeps light. It sheds winged messengers who come down to die among them speaking a language of flashes no one can read. Of course it is alive. What else could it be?

And so the fen has been answering it for three hundred years. On appointed nights the villages mass their voices and sing *at* the spire — pitched low, driven down through the water into the old shield rock, aimed along the stone at the mountain's root — greeting, condolence, question, the deep verse with the spire's own name in it. The chorus travels miles of ancient rock and climbs the young column and arrives at the summit as almost nothing: a periodic murmur in the deep stone, faint past faintness, which the builders feel through their feet in the dim hours and have touch-statements about, filed among their statements about the rock. Whether those long motionless conversations two builders sometimes hold in the honeyed afterglow — the ones about nothing operational — have anything in them of that murmur, no observation settles. The fen sings to a mountain that has no ears, and is answered, every hour of its life, by a hum the mountain does not know it is making. It is either no conversation at all, or the oldest one in the world, and the haze between them keeps the question closed.

But note this, before leaving. The careful observer on the summit, after long watching, concluded that what truly lives up there is not the builders or the gleaners but the thing they are all inside of — one continuous conversation, warm, wordless, older than any of its speakers. The fen, on no evidence but a hum, a glow, and a rain of tears and wings, decided three centuries ago that the mountain was one living being.

The fen may be the only one who got it right the first time.

---

Two settlements, then, on one column of stone: the crown and the floor of the same dark mineral, three billion years apart in age and a hundred feet of haze apart in fact. One keeps its dead in its walls and the other in its verses; one talks by touch at a season per inch and the other by voice at the speed of sound; one has never made a tool and the other has never made a thing that lasts. Between them, one-way and unceasing, passes everything the fen keeps forever — its knives, its lamps, its strangers sealed in light, its untranslated words of love — falling out of a town that does not know it is anyone's sky. And rising the other way, through miles of patient rock, a chorus arrives as a tremble in the feet of beings who have never imagined a below, from beings who have spent three hundred years addressing an above they have seen, in total, perhaps a dozen silent times.

The spire hums. The fen sings in its key. Neither can stop, and neither knows the other is listening — which may be, in the end, simply what a world is: one instrument, playing itself at both ends, with a cloud drawn across the middle of the song.
