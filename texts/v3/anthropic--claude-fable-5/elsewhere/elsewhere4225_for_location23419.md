**Lampfall**

The country under the cloud is old the way a settled argument is old. Everything down here has been worn to its conclusion: hills rounded past the point of asserting anything, rivers that finished negotiating with the land ten thousand years ago and now keep to their agreed meanders, soil so deep and so long-worked that a plow can go a lifetime without ringing on stone. The land is all late draft. Every slope has been forgiven by weather. And then, in the middle of all that patience, the wall.

It rises out of the orchard country without preamble — two kilometers of raw columnar basalt going straight up, hexagonal, unweathered, sharp as the day it broke — and it does not finish. It goes up into the Lid and is gone. Nobody in Lampfall has ever seen the top of it. The Lid is what they call the cloud: a ceiling of permanent grey-white that has never, in any record anyone trusts, fully opened. It is not overcast, which implies an alternative. It is the sky. By day it glows a dim even pearl — cloudlight, the town's brightest natural fact — and by night it is simply black, starless, a lid in earnest. The oldest songs of the low country are older than the wall is supposed to be, and a few of them, the very oldest, describe a flat horizon where the scarp now stands. Scholars fight about this politely and have for generations. The songs do not care. The wall does not care either.

What comes down the wall is not water. That is the first correction every child of Lampfall makes for every stranger: the falls do not reach the ground. Somewhere up there — everyone knows it, no one down here has seen it — whole rivers go over the rim, but two kilometers of falling unravels them. The white ropes fray into spray, the spray into mist, the mist into the Lid itself, and the great roar that is said to deafen the top country dissolves along with the water, spent long before it lands. The falls arrive in Lampfall as weather. A permanent fine rain rings the base of the scarp for a mile out — the Weep, they call it, a soaked grey territory of dripping fern and rotted fence posts where nothing dries and no one lives — and out of the Weep the water gathers itself back into rivers, seeping, threading, remembering how to be a current. The main one, the Gather, comes out of the Weep already cold and already strange: clear as glass, carrying no silt at all, because the young rock above has no soil to give it. The millers know this water. Hungry water, they call it. Water that comes down owning nothing cuts like it means to acquire; it eats weirs, undermines bridge footings, deepens its own bed year on year. The plateau exports two things, the saying goes, and only one of them comes through a wire. The other comes through the Gather, and it is appetite.

The town sits where the rail comes down. The cog line emerges from the Lid on the gentlest shoulder of the scarp, descends through the Weep under its thread of smoke, and ends in a yard of sidings and coal sheds at the edge of Lampfall proper, and from there the town runs downstream along the Gather in a long loose string — which is what it looks like from above, on the rare nights the Lid tears: a filament of light laid along black country, the only proof, from the rim, that anything lives down here at all. Because Lampfall burns light the way other towns burn nothing. The pylons come striding down out of the cloud like something wading ashore, and the current they carry is the town's entire premise: lamps on every street and over every door, lamps in the boat sheds and the fish market and the orchard rows, lamps lit at noon, because noon under the Lid is a kind of dusk and always will be. Other towns of the low country buy the plateau's current by the careful meter. Lampfall, the terminus, the company town, the landlord's doorstep, soaks in it. Strangers arriving by river at night see the glow on the underside of the Lid from twenty miles out and think the town is burning.

And it is loud. That is the second correction. The top country's people come down and call this the silent world, the missing organ, and the people of Lampfall find this hilarious, because Lampfall never stops talking. Voices carry here — that is the whole difference, the entire inheritance of living under a roof instead of a roar — and the town spends the privilege lavishly: market argument, tavern singing, bells for the rail and bells for the boats, gossip conducted across the full width of the Gather without either party raising a hand. Under all of it, though, if you stand still — under the talk, under the bells — there is a hum. It lives in the transformer yard by the rail terminus, in the lamp posts, in the walls of any building the wires enter, a deep steady sixty-cycle throb that is, quite literally, the spin of the turbines two kilometers up: the falls, translated, delivered, still falling. The town's clocks run on it. Lampfall keeps turbine time, its public clocks synchronized to the current itself, and when the load shifts up top the clocks of the whole town drift together, gently, and the stationmaster corrects them, and nobody thinks it strange that even their noon is a tributary of the plateau.

---

Begin, down here, with the crowd — because under the Lid, everything comes in crowds.

The Gather is full of fish: a small silver species, cold-loving, that thrives absurdly in the hungry water and masses in the deep pools below the Weep in shoals that turn the river white-bellied when they roll. The fishers of Lampfall live on them, and the fishers have partners they never chose. Out of the Lid, without warning, a dark heavy-bodied bird will fall — folded, plummeting, punching through the cloud ceiling like a stone through paper — open its wings at the last plausible instant, and take fish from the pools with a violence that leaves rings on the water for a minute after. The top country calls them anvil birds. Down here they are the finders, because a diving bird marks a shoal better than any local knowledge, and every fisher on the Gather knows to row hard for the splash. The birds tolerate this. They feed, they rest sometimes on the bridge pilings — silent, indifferent, faintly disreputable-looking, wet — and then they climb the air along the face of the wall and are absorbed back into the Lid, going home to the roar. They are the one citizen both countries share, the only commuters, and the fishers regard them the way the linemen above do, with something adjacent to respect: a creature that holds the two worlds in one life and seems to find nothing remarkable in it.

The foxes are Lampfall's. That mystery, at least, is no mystery from below. The town's margins teem with them — rail-yard foxes, fish-market foxes, sleek professional animals grown prosperous on a settlement that throws away more than most towns catch — and every so often an ambitious one follows the smell of the supply cars up the cog line and vanishes into the cloud. The rail crews keep a tally. The foxes that come back down, days later, riding the empty coal cars like disgraced princes, come back fat, and become briefly famous, and the children of the yard quarter feed them out of pure admiration: an animal that has seen the sun.

The people. Lampfall raises the plateau's people; that is the town's third trade, after fish and hospitality to the current. No child may live above the cloud, and so every keeper and lineman up there was a child down here first, raised under lamps, schooled in rooms whose windows face the wall. They grow up bilingual without meaning to. The hand-language of the crews leaks down the rail with every rotation and pools in the town's loudest rooms — the weaving mills, mostly, great lamp-lit halls where cheap current drives looms too thunderous for any voice, and where the weavers long ago adopted the linemen's signs and bent them to their own work, so that the plateau's language is now spoken fluently in exactly two places in the world: the top of the wall, and the loudest rooms beneath it. Children pick it up in the mill doorways the way children pick up anything forbidden and useful. By the time the company recruiters come through, half of every year's crop can already sign *valve*, *lightning*, *lunch*, and the one that means *the missing organ*, though down here they use it to mean homesickness.

The families who send people up learn to read letters the way sailors' families read weather. Mail comes down with every train, and the letters of first-rotation workers are long, wordy, relieved — voices clinging to themselves. Then, in some of them, the letters begin to shrink. The words go terse, then sparse; and there is a stage every Lampfall family knows and none discusses, when the letters stop being sentences and start being small careful drawings of hands. That is how a mother learns her daughter has stopped counting the days. The sign-names drift down the wall the same way the letters do, and take root: there is an eating-house by the terminus called the Two Taps, run by a heavyset old man who serves stew and says almost nothing and is loved without reservation, and everyone knows what the name means, and no one makes him explain what finally brought Heartbeat down the rail for good.

He is not the only one. Scattered through Lampfall — a few dozen at any time — are the downed long-stayers, the ones who spent decades above and then, for age or injury or reasons kept in their hands, descended for the last time. They do not thrive on the town's talk; you can find them, evenings, at the edge of the Weep, where the permanent rain drums the roofs in a small tame imitation of thunder, or standing in the transformer yard by the terminus, palms flat against the humming iron. The yardmaster lets them. They are reading. Ask one of them — sign it, they will not answer a voice — and the old lineman will tell you what the hum told his hands: Number Four is carrying heavy tonight; there is ice in a forebay somewhere east; Tilted-Walk still runs her station like a held breath. The whole town's light flickers faintly when the load shifts up top, and the long-stayers read the flicker the way other retirees read letters from home, because that is exactly what it is.

Twice or three times in a generation, the Lid tears. Nobody knows why; a dry wind from the far low country, the surveyors' letters suggest, the same rare wind that crisps the moss above. For an hour, sometimes less, a rent opens in the ceiling and a shaft of undiluted sun comes down onto the low country like something structural, and Lampfall stops — looms, market, boats, everything — and the whole town stands in the streets looking up through the gap at an impossible sight: green, two kilometers overhead, a rim of glowing gold-green with threads of white unraveling off it, and once in a very long while a single dark bird hanging motionless in all that light. Then the Lid heals and the lamps take over again. The company recruiters know to come through in the season after a tear-day. They always sign more children than usual.

And once a year, at the peak of the wet season, when the plateau is pouring everything it has and the hum in the walls climbs to its highest steady note, Lampfall holds the Brightening: one night when every lamp in town — street, house, boat, mill — is lit at once, every meter opened, the whole year's thrift spent in a single extravagance of light. The town says it is a festival for itself. The old long-stayers in the transformer yard know better, and so, in truth, does everyone: it is a letter. Because up on the rim, if the night is one of the rare clear ones, the crews walk out to the edge of the roar and look down through the torn cloud and see it — a thread of light, burning along the black — and they stand in the cold violent starlight and sign to each other the thread's name, which is also the sign for *child*, and also the sign for *where the current goes*.

Lampfall has a thousand voices and one of them is borrowed. Under the talk, under the bells, under the singing that carries across the water because down here it can — the hum. Touch a lamp post on any street in town and it is there in your palm, steady, structural, without rhythm: the turbines, which are the falls, which are the plateau breathing, two kilometers up and generations deep, speaking the only way it can reach this far. The top country's voice comes down the wall as light. The low country answers, once a year, in kind.

It is a landscape with a thousand voices and one hum beneath them all — the town, talking; and underneath it, in the wires, the falling.
