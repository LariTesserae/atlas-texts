# The Unfrozen Ground

The warm water at the low end of the steaming room does not run. It breathes. But breath goes somewhere. The pool wells up through its pale gravel, lifts, settles, and slides away again into the stone, and the stone takes it — down first, through the blood-warm dark where the pink blind fish turns over in its sleep, and then sideways, along a seam in the bedrock, under two days of walking that nobody walks anymore. It travels in the dark the whole way. It never cools. And at the end of the seam, where the red earth gives out and the land climbs into a high plain of frost-shattered stone, it comes up again into open air.

You come to it across the plateau, and you see it long before you reach it, because in winter it is the only thing on the plateau that is not white. From miles off it is a flaw on the horizon: a single standing plume of steam, upright in the enormous cold, leaning with the wind and reforming, leaning and reforming, like smoke from a fire no one is tending. The land around it is wind-stripped and silent in the way that only very cold, very open places are silent — a silence with distance in it, nothing like the close green hush underground. Snow lies over everything in long sculpted drifts. The stones wear rime. The sky is most of what there is.

And then the ground ahead of you turns green.

It happens across a boundary you could straddle. On one side, snow to the knee; on the other, moss — deep, wet, living moss, and winter grass gone bronze at the tips but green at the root, and dark unfrozen mud printed everywhere with the tracks of everything that has come here, which in the hard months is everything. The warm ground makes an island perhaps an acre across, rough-edged, following the heat in the earth rather than any shape the eye would choose. Snow falls on it and dies. All winter the storms come over the plateau and bury the world, and this one acre eats the snow as it lands, so that after a blizzard the plain lies white to the horizon in every direction with a single ragged green wound at the center of it, steaming.

The pool sits in the middle of the island. It is broader than the one in the room below — thirty feet across, maybe forty, open to the sky, blood-warm, floored with the same pale gravel. At its center the water lifts and settles, lifts and settles, with a slow internal pulse, and if you have knelt at the basin in the steaming room you will know the rhythm the moment you see it, because it is the same rhythm. It is not *like* the other pool's breathing. It is the other pool's breathing, arriving at the far end of the seam, the exhale of the same buried animal reaching its second mouth. Hold your hand flat in the shallows here and you can feel the pulse against your palm, and somewhere under two days of stone, in the green dusk of the chamber, the surface of the little basin is answering — lifting when this one settles, settling when this one lifts, the two waters trading one breath back and forth through the dark like sleepers sharing a bed.

Where the room's steam hangs and hazes, the spring's steam *escapes*. The cold takes it the instant it rises and turns it to visible cloud, and the wind combs it out across the acre, and where the warm fog touches the frozen stones at the island's rim it freezes onto them — so the green ground wears a fence of white feathers, every rock at its boundary furred with rime an inch deep, ice built up in delicate blades all pointing back toward the water that made them. The island crowns itself daily in its own frozen breath. On still mornings the plume stands a hundred feet into the pale sky, and the low sun comes through it, and the whole acre lies inside a slow golden smoke.

In summer the place almost disappears. The plateau greens over, the meltwater ponds scatter light everywhere, the plume thins to nothing in the warm air, and the spring becomes one pool among many — a little warmer than its neighbors, a little softer underfoot at its edges, unremarkable, anonymous, resting. The steaming room has no seasons; it holds its one long hot dusk forever. The unfrozen ground has exactly one season that matters, and spends the rest of the year waiting for it. Its whole self is winter. When the first hard frost comes down on the plateau and every other water seals itself under ice in a single night, the spring stays open, and the plume stands up, and the flag is raised, and within days the plain begins to move toward it from every direction.

Because everything comes. That is the spring's nature, and its burden. The room below is a secret; almost nothing has ever found it. The spring is the opposite of a secret — it is the most public place in a hundred miles, announced on the horizon by its own breath, and in the killing weeks of midwinter it is where the living of the whole plateau are gathered, jostling, calling, feeding, waiting out the cold on one warm acre. Where the room is crowded and silent, the spring is crowded and *loud*: geese muttering in the shallows all night, wind, the creak of ice out on the white plain, ravens shouting from the cairns, the stamp and steam-breath of deer standing to their hocks in the warm mud. The room's silence has teeth in it. The spring's noise has a truce in it. They are two answers to the same question, worked out at the two ends of one water.

People knew this place too. Of course they did — everything knew this place. Their marks here are the same modest kind the room absorbed, and made by the same hands, or hands of the same line: three steps cut into the low rock shelf on the pool's south side, short-strided, rounded by feet and frost, the exact gait of the steps at the bottom of the entrance throat two days away. A ring of hearth-stones in the moss, sunk to their shoulders in green. A cracked quern that is the twin of the cracked quern below, as if the same stone-worker made two and the same misfortune found both. Cairns at the island's rim, wind-worn, still standing, still used — the ravens sit them like an inherited office. And on the one boulder that the wind cannot reach, in the lee of the shelf, close enough to the water that the steam keeps it damp: hand-shapes. A row of short lines. Something that might be an animal. The same faded grammar as the marks above the pool in the room, worn here by weather instead of water, kept here by steam just the same.

They wintered here — the tracks in the old mud of the shoreline shelf, hardened now to stone in places, say so: hearths, herds, the worn steps down to the warm water. And in some season, for some reason, they walked the line between the two waters. The path is still in the land if you know to look for it: not a trail, just a long shallow dent running off the plateau toward the red-earth country, the ground very slightly more compact, the vegetation very slightly different, the way stone remembers hands. Two days of walking from the loud warm acre under the open sky to the slot in the ground and the honey-colored throat and the green dusk below. Perhaps they went down for births, or deaths, or sickness, or questions — the room has the shape of a place you go for something you cannot get in a crowd. Perhaps they carried water each way, a cupful of the spring poured into the basin, a cupful of the basin carried up blinking into the wind and poured into the spring, closing the circle their feet had walked. No one has walked it in long enough to ask. The dent in the land offers no answer. It only runs, faithfully, from one mouth of the buried animal to the other.

What lives at the unfrozen ground lives in the open, in numbers, and by treaty. Nothing here can hide and nothing here needs to whisper; the local genius is not silence but tolerance, worked out over ten thousand winters at the edge of one warm pool by creatures who would gladly eat each other and mostly, in the worst weeks, don't. To list them one at a time is slightly false here too — in January they are less a list than a congregation. But it is the only way in.

**The snakes** are the spring's oldest family, and the room's as well, though only one of them knows it. Slender, copper-dark, quick in summer and stone-still in winter, they den in the deep moss of the south bank where the warmth comes up strongest, dozens of them wound into a single slow knot a foot beneath the green, riding out the cold in the earth's exhale. They are the line the room's snake came from. Every few years one of the young ones goes walking in autumn, following warmth in the ground the way their kind follows anything — patiently, by degrees — and most of them find nothing and turn back or die, and once in a long while one of them follows the seam's faint heat all the way off the plateau, down into the red-earth country, to a crack in the ground no wider than a shoulder. The one that lives below now made that walk. It will not come back; the room has absorbed it into its weather. But its sisters are here, coiled in the warm bank, and in spring they pour out of the moss into the sunlight by the dozen, and the whole south shore glitters copper for a week.

**The fish** are the sighted cousins of the pink blind sleeper. They are small, quick, copper-flecked, entirely ordinary to look at, and they live their whole lives in the warm pool, generation on generation, sealed in one acre of open water the way their cousin is sealed in the dark. In the hardest frosts they gather over the vent at the pool's center, pressing down toward the rising warmth in a dense, slowly turning column, hundreds of them, all facing into the pulse — and at the bottom of that pulse, two days away through the stone, their eyeless kin hangs in the same water doing the same thing. Once in a rare while the seam gives something up: a blind white shrimp comes drifting out of the vent into daylight, pale as candle wax, stunned by the brightness it cannot see, riding the pulse in slow bewildered circles. The fish usually take it within the minute. Sometimes the current takes it back down first, and it returns to the constellation below carrying the smell of the sky on its shell, the only traveler ever to make the journey both ways.

**The ravens** are the announcers, and they are everything the frogs below are not. The frogs speak in position and silence; the ravens sit the cairns and *broadcast* — arrivals, deaths, weather, the location of anything edible, all of it shouted across the acre with total indiscretion. They are the spring's memory and its news service. They know every creature that winters here and the order in which the herds will arrive, and they greet the first geese of the freeze-up with a racket that carries for miles, the town crier announcing that the season of the truce has begun. They have sat the cairns since the cairns were built and very possibly supervised the building.

**The snipe** stay all winter, which almost nothing their size does on this plateau. They work the unfrozen mud at the pool's margins with their long bills, probing, silent, absorbed, the acre's quietest citizens — small brown monks of the shoreline, going about their sustenance in the middle of the January uproar as if the uproar were happening in some other country. **The voles** are quieter still: the year-round underclass, tunneling the warm turf, farming the grass roots, feeding the winter foxes and the rough-legged hawks, keeping the green mat trimmed from below the way the snails garden the moss columns underground. The acre is upholstered in their runways. They are the reason the winter grass never chokes itself, and no one has ever thanked them.

**The deer** come only when the cold turns lethal — filing in off the white plain in the blue evenings, breath standing over them, to bed on the warm ground and stand in the shallows and drink water that does not have to be broken. With them, at the acre's edge, come **the foxes and the lynx**, and here the spring's one law asserts itself: the truce. It is not kindness. It is arithmetic. In the killing weeks, everything at the spring needs the warmth more than it needs anything else, and a hunt that scatters the herd into the night costs more than it feeds, and so the predators drink beside the prey at the water's rim with a lowered, formal mutual disattention, and the acre holds. The centipedes are the pressure that authored the room's silence; the cold is the pressure that authored the spring's manners. What killing happens, happens out on the white plain, beyond the fence of rimed stones, in the other country. The green ground itself is spoken for.

**The moss** is close kin to the moss below — the same deep plush, the same animal warmth under the hand — but it does not glow. It has the sky; it never needed to remember light, having never lost it. Except in one place. Down in the vent, where the warm water rises out of the seam, in the throat of stone that daylight cannot reach even at noon, there is a thread of blue-green luminescence on the rock, faint, even, dimming when the fish brush it, recovering over long minutes. It is the same glow that banks the low end of the steaming room, surfacing here at the one point where the spring has a dark of its own — as if the light in the seam runs the whole way like the water does, and shows itself at both ends, keeping its hearths at either mouth of the animal.

**The wind** lives here the way the hush lives below: as the element everything else is phrased in. It is never absent. It combs the plume, builds the rime-feathers, edits the snow into its long drifts, carries the ravens' announcements out over the plain and the smell of open water back in. The geese sleep facing into it. The deer bed in its lee. The people's marks survive only on the one stone it cannot touch. Every shape at the spring — animal, vegetable, mineral, social — is a negotiation with it, and it has never once negotiated back.

**And the people** live here as they live below: as wear, held rather than erased. The steps with their short careful stride. The hearth-ring sunk in the moss, which the deer bed inside on the worst nights, folding themselves into the circle of stones as if the circle were still warm — and it is, faintly, being on the warm ground; whether that is why they choose it or whether the acre remembered the shape of a gathered circle here too, and kept making it, is the same question the room refuses to answer, refused now by two places at once, which begins to feel less like silence and more like policy.

Which leaves the largest fact, the one both places live inside: **they are one thing.** A single warm animal lies under two days of country with its body in the dark and a mouth at either end. Through one mouth it takes the sky in small — a ceiling-eye's worth of tired light, leaf-fall, bat-borne seeds — and dreams on it slowly in a hot green hush. Through the other it breathes out, hugely, publicly, a standing flag of steam on the horizon of a frozen plain, and everything living for a hundred miles gathers at the exhale to survive the winter inside its breath. The room is what the animal dreams. The spring is what it says. And the water goes between them forever, blood-warm in the seam, carrying the pulse from basin to pool and back — so that a hand laid flat in the bright wind-loud shallows of the unfrozen ground and a hand laid flat in the dim silent basin below would feel, two days of stone apart, the same slow lift and settle, the same long patient breath, and belong for that moment to the same body, as the frogs and the geese and the snakes and the blind pink sleeper and the people worn down to steps at both ends of the path have belonged to it all along.
