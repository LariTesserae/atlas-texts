**The Afterwood**

You see it three days before you reach it — a darkness along the eastern horizon that you take at first for weather, then for mountains, and finally, with a slow vertigo, for what it is: a single standing thing, green-black and continuous, its upper reaches lost in its own captive clouds. The land approaching it is strange, ringed. Concentric hills circle it for thirty miles, grassed over now, soft-shouldered, but their bones are wrong for hills — upheaved slabs, snapped strata, the ground around them still faintly tilted away, as if everything here was once shoved aside by something rising. The people who live in the wood call these the Scar Hills, without ceremony. They are the splash. The wood is the stone.

Then you pass under the eaves of it, and the first thing — after the vast wet green breathing of the canopy, after the rivers of light falling in shafts, after the rain, because it is nearly always raining somewhere in the Afterwood — the first thing, once your ears settle, is the quiet.

Not silence. There is weather-noise everywhere: rain ticking down through a hundred vertical meters of leaves, wind working the crown far overhead with a sound like distant surf, birdsong, water running in its carved channels down the bark. But underneath all of it the wood itself is *quiet*. Lay your palm against any surface and you feel it: stillness. No flexing. No cracking. No groan of dilation, no shriek of fibers tearing themselves longer. The growth here has all but stopped — a slow ring a year, a leaf replaced, a wound closed over a decade — the ordinary secret patience of old trees, spread across a single organism the size of a small country. Everything around you is *finished*. That is the essential fact of the Afterwood, the thing you must hold to understand it, and it is the exact inversion of the fact that governs a certain warm golden hollow far to the east of here, under the dry split country, where nothing is finished at all, and never has been, and the floor flexes like a held breath.

The Afterwood is what a germination vault becomes. It opened. It is the *after* of a place like that — the proof, standing in the weather, that the story the small drumming people tell each other in the ember-light is not a story.

**The Body**

There is no word in the Afterwood for "tree," in the way that people who live on an island have odd gaps around the word "sea." There is the Bole — the trunk-country, kilometers across at the base, its bark a terrain in its own right, fissured into canyons deep enough to hold villages, ledged, terraced, hung with gardens. There are the Reaches, which an outsider would call branches and which the inhabitants speak of the way lowlanders speak of provinces: the Third Reach has good fruit and difficult people; no one goes to the Ninth in winter. And there is the Crown, the upper country, a rolling landscape of leaf and limb where the light is total and the wind never stops and whole communities live their lives without descending, harvesting the weather.

Because the Afterwood runs on water now. This is the great metamorphosis, the one the vault-dwellers to the east cannot imagine, dry-lipped in their oil-lit dark: at some point in the opening — in the violence of the breach, the unfurling, the first catastrophic taste of sun — the whole economy of the organism converted. The resin cooled. The furnace below banked itself and went out, or went so deep it may as well have. And the wood turned its face up and learned rain. It learned rain *architecturally*: the bark of the Bole is carved — grown, rather, but the people say carved, and grown-to-purpose is a distinction this place has never respected — into a system of gutters and races and cisterns, so that the Afterwood wears its rivers on the outside. After a storm the entire trunk-country runs silver, water descending in a thousand braided channels, pooling in bark-basins the size of lakes, and the sound of it is the closest thing the wood makes anymore to its old voice.

The old voice itself is underfoot. The resin — the golden blood, the moving light, the pulse that was day and night to the people of the birth — did not vanish. It stopped. It cooled in its ducts over generations and became amber, and now the whole standing mass of the Afterwood is veined with it: seams of fossil light threading the dead heartwood, some as thin as wire, some as thick as a torso, exactly where the ducts ran. The people mine it, respectfully, the way you might quote the dead. And here is the thing that stops every visitor: the amber still glows. Faintly — a warmth more than a light, an ember-color deep in the stone — and it is dimming, measurably, a little less in each generation, the last charge of the birth running down across centuries. Amber lamps are the treasure of the Afterwood. A family's lamp is its lineage. When one finally goes dark it is not discarded; it is carried down into the Hollow and set among the others, and there is a gallery down there, in the cold, of thousands of small dead suns.

The amber holds inclusions. This is how the people of the Afterwood know their own natural history, and it is an archaeology conducted in gold: pilgrim-grazers by the thousand, frozen mid-march along ducts that no longer exist; silk-gliders caught in the act of being information; and — most sacred, handled only by the historians — the wax-sisters, the sealers, entombed in their own final patches, each one a leak that never reopened, preserved forever inside the very act of tending. The wax-sisters are extinct. They did not survive the opening; their whole vocation was the pressure of the living resin, and when the resin stopped, so, quietly, did they. They exist now only as their own monuments. The people call amber, in their oldest register, *sister-stone*, and a piece with a sister in it is never sold, only given.

The light here comes from the sun, and this must be said carefully, because it was not a simple gift. The histories are frank about it: the founders — the ones who walked out, blinking, into the first rain — could not, at first, bear noon. Not the brightness. The *shadows*. They had lived their whole history in a sourceless golden glow that never cast a hard edge, and the first time they saw their own shadows thrown black and sharp on the broken ground, several of them attacked the shapes, and one drummed at hers for an hour, asking it its name. Their descendants have made their peace so thoroughly that shadow-play is now the great art of the Afterwood — whole epics performed with cut screens and lamplight, and the villain of the oldest cycle is a shadow that answers.

**The Hollow**

You can still go down.

The throat the founders came up through has been kept — enlarged, stepped, tended for centuries — and it descends through the living bole into the place the Afterwood used to be: the vault of its own birth, empty now, cold, and so dark and so still that people of the surface communities go down in groups and hold hands without being ashamed of it.

The Hollow is a cavity the size of a valley, which has, now, every business existing underground: it is the space the seed unpacked itself across, and when the mass finally pushed upward and the growth turned outward and skyward, the interior was simply — left. The great root-plate that was once a ceiling is now footing for the country overhead. The floor of warm flexing heartwood is cold and absolutely rigid. The unfinished plains, the doughy provisional expanses where the ground had not yet decided — they decided. They hardened where they were, mid-thought, and walking across them is walking across a sentence stopped in the middle, ridges half-swollen, hexagonal bark-plaques spreading forever toward edges they never reached.

And the dead are still there. This is why the people descend. The founders' custom — the custom of the eastern vault too, because it is the same custom, carried — was to give the dead to the unfinished plains, to be taken in and grown-of, and so the ridges of the Hollow are ancestors, findable, named. Families make the descent once a generation to walk their own geology. There is a column in the Hollow's western reach, black-crested, leaning slightly, that seven separate lineages claim, and the dispute is four hundred years old and conducted entirely in affectionate drumming, and no one wants it settled.

Two things wait at the bottom of the Hollow, at its deepest point, where the heat used to come from.

The first is the kernel. The seed-heart it all unpacked from — spent now, split open into five vast petals of husk, each one curved like a landfallen moon, together enclosing a space the size of an amphitheater, which is what the people use it for. Full assemblies of the Afterwood happen perhaps twice in a lifetime, and they happen there, inside the opened heart of their world, by amber-light, everyone's eyes throwing back the glow ember-orange — because that, at least, the generations have not lost. Under the sun their eyes are only dark. Down in the true dark, the old layer behind the retina still answers, and an assembly in the kernel looks, from the rim, like a bowl of banked fire.

The second thing is a smooth patch of floor near the kernel's base, kept swept, where the historians say the founders' camp stood in the last years before the opening — and on it, in a cradle of sister-stone, the Gourd.

It is open. That is the whole point of it. It is a dry, ancient, unsealed gourd, empty for centuries, and it is displayed *open*, lid beside it, because it did what it was for: it was carried down into the dark by the planters of *this* vault, generations before the opening — filled with rain from a wood even older, somewhere far to the west — and it was kept sealed through the whole long roaring gestation, and on the day the Afterwood broke the sky and the rain of the world came down onto the faces of the people for the first time, the eldest song-keeper opened it, and the old water and the new water met.

The people of the Afterwood keep it open the way you keep a promise kept. And when they hear — as they have heard, from the drummed ledger, all their lives — that somewhere east under the split country there is a young vault whose people keep a gourd of their own, sealed, unsloshing, waiting: they nod, because they filled it.

**The Animals**

The Afterwood's fauna is a hybrid nation, half heirloom and half immigrant, and it is the only place in the world where those two histories interbreed.

When the wood opened, the galls went sterile — most of them. An organism that has finished building has no orders left to give, and the great castes of the made ones, cut off from their instructions, met their various ends: the sisters died with the resin; the chewers, it is thought, simply stopped, and their bodies are found occasionally in old channels, mineralized, enormous, facing the direction of their last task. But the ferals — the errors, the ones who had already learned to want things and to breed — the ferals *inherited*. Every native animal of the Afterwood descends from some caste's beautiful mistake.

The porters' descendants are the wood's beasts of burden now, properly domesticated: broad, beetle-bodied, patient, still helplessly moved by the smell of a task — the people paint their pack-baskets with gall-paste in the old way, a courtesy the animals no longer strictly need but visibly enjoy. The gliders became true fliers, generations of crown-wind selecting for wing from silk, and their swarms still carry pollen and scent across the Reaches, so that the Afterwood, even old and quiet, still thinks a little, slowly, through its air. And the gold-eyed line — the descendants of that first predator, the one that decided to exist — are the aristocrats of the canopy, long and quiet and unhurried, and the people of the Afterwood do not hunt them and are rarely hunted by them, and when the two meet on a high limb there is a moment of mutual regard that every child is told about and no adult can quite describe. Kin recognizing kin, is the usual attempt. The two lineages in the wood that were not assigned.

Into all this, from the outer world, came the immigrants: true birds, true insects, seeds on the wind, foxes of the Scar Hills — because the Afterwood, unlike its buried child, is *open*, and the world has been moving into it for centuries. The historians keep amber as the boundary-marker: anything found in sister-stone is native; anything not, guest. The guests are welcome. Some of them have been welcome for four hundred years. The distinction is maintained anyway, gently, the way you maintain a maiden name.

And the people themselves took up the vacant castes. This is the quiet spine of their whole society, the thing that organizes them more than law does: when the sisters died, the people became the sealers — storm-wounds in the high Reaches are patched by human hands with melted amber, and the guild that does this work is called, simply, the Sisters, and includes men, and no one has ever thought that strange. When the chewers stopped, the people became the maintainers of channels. The wood cannot bud its workers anymore. It has heirs instead. The founders' word for themselves, in the dark — *crew* — has shifted over the generations into a word that translates closest to *hands*. We are its hands now, the people say, without grandeur, the way you'd state your trade.

**The People**

They are small still — shoulder-high, dark-haired, few by lowland standards though vast by the standards of their origin: forty thousand of them, spread through Bole and Reach and Crown, a population that would stagger the three thousand of the eastern vault, and does, when the ledger-drummers try to convey it.

Their bodies have unlearned the dark. They sweat. They drink — openly, socially, from cups, and the histories record with tenderness that the founding generation found public drinking obscene, an intimacy performed in daylight, and would turn away from a person at a stream. Their skin lost its varnish-sheen within four generations. Rain does not amaze them; they have, by the historians' count, over a hundred words for it — rain that walks, rain that stands, rain heard through leaves versus rain heard through husk — and all hundred words together, the elders say, weigh less than the one word the founders came up not having, and received, all at once, on their faces.

Their names are still rhythms. That did not change and, you feel after a while among them, could not — a name drummed by the mother on the floor of the dwelling at birth, so that the wood is the first to hear it, even now, even though the wood is quiet and (they will admit) probably no longer listening. Their knuckle-language survives, carried beautifully by the dead resonant heartwood, but it has become a formal register — courtship and mourning and history — because in the open air of the surface, voices reach, and the drum is no longer the only way across a distance. Among their inherited rhythms is one very old love-rhythm, two hundred years old at the *time of the planting*, which everyone knows and no one performs. The eastern vault has it too. Neither people knows the other still keeps it. It is the oldest thing the two places share, older than the seed between them.

They make maps. This was, the histories say, a genuine crisis. To a vault-born song-keeper, a map is an act of arrogance — a claim that the world will hold still — and the first Afterwood generation to carve a permanent map was accused, in formal drumming, of faithlessness. But the world here *does* hold still. The geography is finished; the pass that was is the pass that is. So the maps won, and the song-keepers became historians, and the songs stopped expiring — with one exception, and the exception is the holiest thing they have.

The founders' grief is still in the culture, if you know where to hear it. They came up out of the roar of becoming — out of a world where the noise of growth was so total that quiet did not exist as a concept — and the histories call the first years on the surface the Quiet, and mean by it both the triumph and the bereavement, inseparably. The first generation could not sleep. The absence of the cracking woke them the way an alarm wakes others. So they invented lullabies that imitate it — long crackling mouth-sounds, split-timber sounds, the groan of dilation done low in the throat — and Afterwood children are sung to sleep with the noise of the birth to this day, by parents who mostly no longer know that it is imitation, that it was ever anything but a lullaby. A visitor from the eastern vault, hearing it, would stand very still.

**The Hearts, and the Carrying**

Once in a long while — twice a century, perhaps — the Crown ripens a heart.

It announces itself a decade in advance: a gall in the high country, but a gall unlike the sterile others, swelling season over season until it hangs the size of a house, hot to the touch in a wood that is otherwise cool, humming very faintly with a sound the old people climb up specifically to press their ears against, because it is the sound of the lullabies, coming from inside. A heart is a folded world. Everything the Afterwood is — vault and roar and pilgrims and sisters and golden pulse-light and the whole frantic grammar of becoming — packed down into a kernel, waiting for dark and heat.

And then the Carrying, which is the entire purpose, now, of the people of the Afterwood, and they know it, and it is the future tense they kept.

A ripe heart must go far. It cannot open in its parent's shadow; it needs what the wood cannot give it — deep dryness, cracked ground, the old heat of the underneath. So the people cut it down with ceremony and rollers and a week of labor, and a Carrying party is assembled — this is the honor of a generation; children drum petitions for years to be chosen — and the heart is hauled out across the Scar Hills and away, on roads the historians keep for no other purpose, for months, sometimes years, toward whatever drought-country the surveying parties have sung back word of. Split ground. Warm rock. A crack going down.

And at the chosen place, the heart is lowered and buried, and the ground, in time, begins to swallow it — and then comes the moment the whole culture is arranged around. Some of the party goes home. And some of the party *stays* — goes down with the heart, into the closing ground, carrying chert blades and the gall-lore and the songs in three tenses and a single sealed gourd of Afterwood rain — to be the crew of the new world, to live in the roar, to keep the names, and to be there, generations hence, when it opens; to see it; and to say so.

Those who stay are never heard from again. That is the shape of the sacrifice and everyone accepts it. The roots of the world are deep and the distances are long and drums do not cross drought.

Except.

The ledger-song of the plantings is the one song that never expires — a verse for every heart ever carried, each verse holding the direction, the country, and the rhythm-names of those who went down. There is a verse, among the oldest, for a heart carried east, into the split country, generations ago; the historians can recite the names. And it is the custom, on certain still nights, for drummers to descend into the Hollow, to the deepest point, near the spent kernel, where the oldest roots run down into rock — and to drum those names into the roots. A courtesy. A remembering. No one expects an answer; the theory that the roots of the world touch is understood to be a tenderness, not a science.

Once — a century ago, and it is disputed, and the two witnesses disagreed for the rest of their lives about everything except this — an answer came back up through the stone. Four notes.

Every drummer in the Afterwood knows the phrase. It is the reply the planters traditionally drum as the ground closes over them, the last transmission of every Carrying, four notes meaning, roughly: *it is being taken care of.*

The historians have never ruled on the account. They keep it the way the eastern people keep their two origin stories — both alive, unargued, whichever one the world needs it to be that year. But the drumming continues, each still night, names into the rock; and far to the east, under the dry split country, in a warm golden hollow where the cracking never stops, there is a four-note deep-verse whose origin nobody living remembers, and a floor that flexes, very slightly, like a held breath; and between the two places, through however much cold stone, runs whatever it is that runs between a mother and a child who have never met and are the same being twice.

The Afterwood is old. West of it, many months' travel, its own parent stands — the Grey Wood, the source of the water in the founders' gourd — senile now, its crown half-fallen, its people long dispersed; the Afterwood no longer drums to it, and does not speak of this. The chain does not run backward. It runs the other way: wood by wood, heart by heart, dark by roaring dark, walking across the world at the pace of centuries. The people of the Afterwood, heirs of the witnesses, hands of a finished thing, understand their place in it precisely, and will tell you, if you ask them what they are for, in the plain way they state a trade:

We saw ours open. Now we carry.
