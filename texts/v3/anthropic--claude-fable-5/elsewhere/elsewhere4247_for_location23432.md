**Ossel**

You come to it overland, six days by the downs road from the cage-head, and the first thing that reaches you — before the town, before the steam, before any sense of the place itself — is the white. It starts as a paleness in the ruts. Then the dust of the road is white, and the drystone walls are white, and the sheep grazing between them are grey against it, and by the last few miles you have understood that you are walking on the town's export, on its spillage, on the crumbs of its one long meal. Then the road crests the final down and you see Ossel below you, smoking gently in its valley like something just taken off a fire, and you stop, because everyone stops there, and there is even a bench.

What you are looking at is a hillside of stone stairs a quarter-mile broad, terrace under terrace under terrace, each one brimming with water the colour of skimmed milk and blue sky, each one spilling in a thin even film over its own lip into the next. The terraces steam faintly, all of them, all year. In winter the plume stands over the valley like a second, softer hill, and the coast pilots use it. Threaded in among the stairs, on stilts and re-cut streets, is the town.

The people of Ossel have known for four hundred years that their water is warm and comes from underneath the world. It is only in the last thirty that anyone has known *which* underneath. The water that fills these terraces is the water of the Long Soak. It is the roar of the Soakworks, arrived. It went down into the dark somewhere far to the west, spent years in the deep rock doing everything water can do, carved the young caverns, warmed the blind fish, sheeted down the weeping walls past the lamps and the catwalks and the flat unhurried hand-signs of the crews, passed the last lamp-pole and whatever waits beyond it, and then travelled on — three years, by the best measure anyone has — through conduits no one will ever see, to come up here. Ossel is where the underworld exhales.

**The Aprons**

That is what the terraces are called, and the name is domestic on purpose. The Aprons are not a wonder to the people who live on them; they are a surface, a floor, a slow tide that has to be lived with. Because the water that arrives at Ossel is not merely warm. It is *full*. It comes up saturated with dissolved stone — all that dissolving that makes the roar down below has to be paid for somewhere, and it is paid here, where the water meets the air, cools by a few degrees, breathes out its gas, and begins, immediately and everywhere, to let the stone back down.

The Aprons grow. Not the Soakworks' patient millimetre — the deposition that the deep water withheld through all its dark travelling it releases here in a rush, and a rush, in the language of stone, means a finger's width in a year. A hat left on a terrace wall in spring is a white hat by autumn and a stone one by the spring after. Doorsteps rise. The town re-cuts its streets on a rota, quarter by quarter, the way other towns sweep theirs, and the sound of the street-saws — a wet, patient rasping — is as much the sound of Ossel as bells are the sound of anywhere else. The oldest quarter lost the argument generations ago and was let go: it stands down by the lowest apron as a cluster of soft white mounds with a chimney-pot here and a gable-line there, glazed over, weeping thin water, and in the middle of it the old church, swallowed to the eaves, whose bell was entombed in the tower with the rest. They ring it still, on the year's great days, by a long iron rod driven down through the new stone to the crown of the bell, and what comes out of the hillside is not a peal but a deep muffled tolling *inside* the rock, felt in the soles of the feet as much as heard. The stone bell, they call it. Visitors find it mournful. Ossel does not. To Ossel it is the sound of a thing kept, not a thing lost.

The crews who come up from the Soakworks on their rotations — and many of them come here, it is where the road wants to take you — stand in front of those mounds for a long time, the first visit. They have seen this before. They have seen the survey camp on its terrace, the stovepipe weeping rust down new white rock, the handrails inside the flowstone like fossils of last decade. It is the same hand at work. It is, in the strictest sense, the same water.

**The Eye**

At the top of the Aprons, above the highest terrace, in a bowl of old smoothed stone, is the spring itself.

It is called the Eye, and once you have seen it the name closes over the pool and will not come off. It is perhaps twenty paces across, deeper than any line has found, and a blue that exists nowhere else in the region — not sky blue, not water blue, but the blue of enormous depth lit from within by nothing at all. It does not bubble. It does not ripple. The whole warm outflow of the underworld comes up through it in such volume that the pool brims and spills on every side at once, and yet the surface is dead calm, a lens, a disc of stillness with steam standing off it in slow ropes. The water that has never seen anything comes up through the one thing in the whole system shaped like an eye, and the eye does not blink.

Put your hand flat on the stone at its rim and wait, and you will feel it: a trembling. Faint, continuous, without rhythm, the same yesterday and tomorrow. For four hundred years Ossel called it the Undermaid's churn — the pale, eyeless woman of the town's oldest stories, who stirred the great pots under the world to keep the water warm for the wives above, and who must never be looked for, only thanked. The crews from below have a different account of the trembling. They say it is the roar. The last of it — the sound of the falls and the weeping walls and the whole carving argument of the deep, carried up through miles of solid rock until nothing survives of it but this shiver in the stone, too low to hear, too steady to mistake. Both accounts are used in Ossel, sometimes by the same person in the same hour, and no one feels obliged to choose.

Things come up through the Eye. Not many, and nothing made — the ways are too deep and too strange for anything but the water and what lives in it — but every so often the surface of the pool acquires a small pink interruption: a blind fish, soft as a thumb, risen out of its whole warm eyeless world into open water, cold, and light, and dead of the journey. Once in a rarer while, a drift of white shrimp, like a spill of rice. For centuries Ossel had a custom for these arrivals, worked out with no knowledge of where they came from: the fish were lifted from the Eye with a wooden paddle kept for nothing else, carried to a seep on the high apron, and laid there in the path of the heaviest drip, and the water was let do with them what it does with everything that holds still. There is a wall up there — the white wall, generations deep — of small stone fish.

When the first Soakworks crews came up the downs road, thirty years ago, and were shown the white wall, several of them, it is recorded, sat down on the ground. They had come from a place where the dead are laid on a high terrace in the path of the heaviest seep. Nobody had taught either town the custom. The water had taught it to both.

**The trade**

Ossel lives by lending things to the water.

Along the working aprons, under the drip-lines, stand the steeping-racks: frames of black iron hung with objects at every stage of whitening. Lace, which stones beautifully. Birds' nests, ropes, boots, letters folded open, wedding shoes — a bride's left shoe goes on the racks the morning after, by a custom older than anyone's argument against it, and comes off in a year, a shoe of white stone, and stands on the mantel thereafter for as long as the marriage does and generally much longer. The steepers who tend the racks turn each piece daily so it takes the stone evenly, and a good steeper handles a half-whitened christening gown the way the pump crews below handle a soft new flowstone floor: with the respect owed to something that is not finished being made.

And there is the quarry, which is the real money. The young travertine of the lower aprons is soft when it is cut — the saws go through it like hard cheese — and hardens in the air, and it is white and takes a polish, and it travels. Every pale lintel in the coastal cities, most of their doorsteps, the great steps of the Merchants' Hall itself: Ossel stone, which is to say Soakworks stone, which is to say the dissolved walls of the deep caverns, hauled up by water and laid down in the sun and cut and shipped. The crews below like this fact inordinately. There is a wet-speech joke, old now, that runs: *the surface people would not come down to us, so we sent the place up to them, and they live in it and call it theirs.*

The trade runs the other way too, in one small ceremony. When a rotation crew leaves Ossel for the cage-head, the steepers give them a loaf, stone-coated, white, inedible, *for the Undermaid* — and six days later and a mile down, at the shift's end, someone walks it out to the unwritten line two poles past the last lamp and sets it on the stone with the others. There is a row of them out there now in the dark, whitening further, Ossel's gifts, at the edge of the Lodger's quiet country. Whether anything regards them is not asked. It is not that kind of gift.

**The quiet**

Here is the inversion, and everyone who has known both places carries it around like a coin they keep turning over.

The Soakworks is the loudest landscape in the world, and nothing there ever visibly happens. Ossel is the quietest town anyone can name — the water arrives with all its noise spent, wells up without a ripple, spills in films too thin to speak — and it is the one place on the surface where you can *watch* the world being made. Stand on an apron for a season and the stone rises under your habits. The surface is a world of conclusions everywhere except here; here the underworld's sentence is still being written out, in fair copy, in white.

And the quiet has shaped the people the way the roar shaped the crews, from the opposite direction arriving at nearly the same place. Ossel talks softly and not much. Across the width of the pools, where even soft voices won't carry over the water's hush, the steepers long ago began borrowing hand-signs from the rotation crews who summer there, and wet-speech has taken root on the surface in a thin, sunlit dialect: *water rising, water steady, come, wait, eat*. And the sign made by drawing the flat hand slowly down the forearm, like a film of water descending — which below means *patience*, and the place, and the dead, and staying — is used in Ossel now too, and in Ossel it means the Eye, and the Aprons, and the Undermaid, and the water, and no one on either end has ever needed it to mean only one thing, which is perhaps the point of it.

It is for the quiet, and for the warmth, and for one thing more, that the soaked come here when they finally come up. A dozen or so at any time — pensioned lamplighters, old pole-drivers, people whose fingertips stay soft and white and whose ears have given a whole band of the world back to the roar. They cannot sleep in ordinary surface towns; the silence there, they say, has a hole in it. In Ossel they take rooms on the high aprons, and they sleep on the warm stone floors by preference, a palm flat against the rock, and through the rock, all night, comes the trembling — the last of the roar, three years and many miles old, faint as a pulse. It is enough. Brek herself, they say, will come up someday and take the room kept for her above the Eye, and lie down on the water she has listened to all her life, and hear it with her bones instead.

**The wedding of the waters**

The proof, when it finally came, came slowly, which everyone later agreed was correct.

Thirty years ago the borehole engineers tipped a barrel of harmless rose dye into the head of the Long Soak, logged it, and largely forgot it. Three years after that, in the month called Lentermonth, the Eye blushed. For nine days the pool ran the colour of the inside of a shell, and the films on the aprons pinked, and the town stood around the water in the steam and understood, all at once, four hundred years of itself: the warmth, the white, the trembling, the pink fish, the Undermaid and her churn. A rider went west on the downs road that same day, and six days later a slate went down the cage, and it is in the midden now, going into the stone with the rest: *The dye is up at Ossel. They have known us all along.*

They have kept the custom since. Once a year, on a fixed day, the crews below tip a barrel into the Long Soak — rose for all well, gold for a good year, once, after a bad winter and two deaths, a deep quiet blue — and three years later, on a day the well-keepers of Ossel can predict now to within a week, the Eye answers in colour, and the town takes a holiday called the Answer, and reads its news from underneath the world three years late and celebrates it anyway. Ossel's replies must go overland, by rider and road and cage, and arrive in days; the questions come by water and take three years. The two places hold, in consequence, the strangest correspondence anywhere: half of it at the speed of horses, half at the speed of stone, interleaved, patient, never quite in step and never broken.

Once, and only once, the water sent news no one had put into it. Nineteen years ago Brek stood on her terrace in the deep and said a flowstone dam had let go in the Third Reach, and was right. Three years after, the Eye ran clouded for a whole season — the Milk, they called that year, when the aprons grew a decade's stone in twelve months and the steeping-racks came due early and the quarry cut soft all winter — and the well-keepers wrote it in their book against the old date and found Brek's slate answered to the day. She reads the roar going down, they said in Ossel; we read it coming up. One by ear, one by eye and tongue. Same water. The two of them have met once, at the rim of the Eye, the listener and the taster, and are reported to have said almost nothing, and to have needed almost nothing, and to have both, at parting, made the flat hand slowly down the forearm — each meaning her own place by it, each understood perfectly.

---

So that is Ossel: the far end of the breath. Below, in the dark, the water takes the world apart, and the taking is a roar that never varies and never rests, and the crews live inside it like fish inside water. Then it travels, three years in the blind rock, and comes up through the Eye with the noise all spent and the stone all carried, and lays the world back down in the sun, terrace under terrace, white and warm and quiet, with a town living on it the way the snails live on the weeping walls — grazing, building, moving just faster than the growth, mostly winning.

The people of the Soakworks say the roar is the sound a hundred thousand years makes while it is passing. The people of Ossel have the other half of the sentence, and they say it rarely, and softly, the way they say everything: the Aprons are what the roar has been *saying*, all along. It just takes three years to arrive. And by the time it gets here it has nothing left to say it with but stone — which, in Ossel, has always been considered the more trustworthy of the two.
