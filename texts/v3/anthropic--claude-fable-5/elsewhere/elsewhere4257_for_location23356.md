**The Hush**

The Clamor Flats end. Few who speak of them mention this, because the flats are so wide and so uniform that they feel less like a place than a condition, but they have an edge, and the slow drift of their water has a destination. Follow the drift far enough — pan by pan, the water always moving with that gradientless patience, as if pulled by rumor rather than gravity — and the gray stone comes to a lip.

The lip is nothing. A step of rock no higher than a knee, running unbroken to the left and right for a distance no one has walked out. Over it the drift-water pours in a sheet — not a waterfall so much as a spilled table, a hand's-depth of water going over a knee's-height of edge along a front wider than a day's travel. Travelers who have stood at it call it the Last Sound. It hisses. That is all it does, a long white hiss under the roar of the rookeries behind, and it is the final noise the world makes in that direction, because what lies below and beyond the lip is the Hush, and the Hush does not give sound back.

Understand first what the Hush is made of. The mountains that once stood over the flats were not destroyed. Nothing is destroyed by weather; it is only moved. Grain by grain, over the same age that has no useful name, the substance of that vanished range was carried down the vanishing slopes, out across the flattening land, over this lip when the lip was a hundred miles further out, and laid down here — in a basin so broad its far side is a matter of faith — as silt. Silt fine as flour, gray shading to buff, deep beyond any sounding the people have managed. The Hush is the mountains, lying down. Every grain in it once stood in the sky above the flats. The two regions are a single landform separated by an age: the plain, and the plain's subtracted mass made solid somewhere else. What the flats forgot, the Hush is.

And the lip retreats. Slowly — a finger's-width in a lifetime, perhaps — the falling sheet undercuts the gray stone and the edge eats its way back into the flats, headward, patient, the way all edges do. The one place on the finished plain where anything is still happening is the seam where it meets its own memory. The Hush grows by exactly what the flats lose. It always has.

---

The silence is the thing the travelers cannot make anyone believe.

It is not the absence of the flats' roar, though it is that within a hundred paces of the falls — the hiss simply stops, sooner than distance can explain, the way a lamp stops at a wall. It is that the Hush absorbs. The silt drinks sound. A shout dies at arm's length, dull and small, as if spoken into cloth. There are no echoes, because there is nothing hard to echo from; the country is soft to a depth no one knows, and a voice goes into it and is kept. People crossing down from the flats — ears still ringing, bodies still braced against a pressure that has been leaning on them for days — describe the first hours in the Hush as a kind of falling. The chorus is gone. The echoes are gone. And then, rising out of the new quiet, the first sound the Hush gives every traveler: their own blood. Their own heart, loud as a drum in the sudden anechoic dark of the skull. The last chorus, reduced to one throat.

You stop hearing the silence as absence after the first hour and begin to feel it as attention.

The light is the same sourceless overcast, but the land under it could not answer it more differently. The flats glisten; the Hush is matte. Nothing here gives back silver. The surface of the basin is a crust — the top hand's-width of silt, dried and set into vast pale polygons, plate after plate after plate, each seamed to its neighbors by cracks fine as hair — and the crust eats light the way the depths eat sound. Distances go strange in the opposite direction from the flats: there the horizon dissolved into sky; here the pale ground and the gray sky refuse each other absolutely, and the horizon is a drawn line, hard, the only hard thing in the country.

Below the crust the silt is wet. It has never been anything else; the whole basin is saturated with an age of the flats' drift, and the water that goes over the Last Sound does not flow anywhere afterward — it sinks, and joins, and becomes pressure. Here and there the pressure finds a way up. These are the boils: low mounds, some a stride across, some the size of a rookery, where water forced from below wells through the crust and keeps a circle of ground soft forever. The boils are the Hush's cousins to the seeps of the flats — the same water, having crossed the whole finished world underground, coming up for air on the far side.

Walking here is a craft, the way listening is a craft on the flats. The crust holds a careful person. It does not hold a careless one, or a heavy sledge, or anyone who mistakes the pale skin of a boil-margin for honest ground. The people of this country read the polygons the way the flats' people read the mat-colors, and their paths show it: they run in long deliberate seams, plate-center to plate-center, skirting every boil at a distance that looks superstitious and is not.

---

There is one more thing under the Hush, at the bottom of everything, and it is the reason the two countries have never really been two.

Beneath the silt — under all of it, however deep it goes — lies the basement: the same gray finished stone as the flats, the same peneplain, continuing under its own wreckage without interruption. The flat rock that rings under the rookeries runs beneath the basin from edge to unknown edge, buried but unbroken. And it still rings. The chorus of the flats, pouring day and night into the bedrock through ten million attending throats, travels the deep channel outward in every direction, and out here, under miles of silence and silt, the stone carries it still — faint, filtered, slowed, stripped of every individual voice, but continuous. The Hush is soundless at the surface and full of the flats at the floor. The loudest place anyone has ever stood and the quietest are the two ends of one instrument.

When the synchrony comes — when the plain far behind the lip falls into its unison, ten thousand throats, then all of them — the pulse arrives here through the basement rock faster than any sound crosses the air above, and the Hush answers in the only way soft ground can. The saturated silt, shaken at just that patient rhythm, loosens. It does not quake; nothing so dramatic. The crust breathes. The polygons lift and settle a hair's width in time with a heartbeat happening beyond the horizon of the world. Water beads up along the crack-seams. And when the unison dissolves, far away, the loosened silt settles back a grain at a time, and settles differently than it lay before, and leaves — this is the thing — a lamina. A single pale layer, thin as skin, distinguishable forever from the layers above and below it.

Dig anywhere in the Hush and the walls of the digging are striped. Every synchrony since the first has a line in the mud. The flats do not know what was decided, and the criers do not remember, and the rhythm itself has no body to remember with — but the Hush has been taking it down, beat-season by beat-season, in order, since before there was anything on the flats to listen. One country says. The other keeps.

---

**Who Lives in the Hush**

---

**The mats, again, because they arrived first.**

They came over the Last Sound as everything comes over it: as fragments, torn slicks of pewter and oxblood riding the drift, most of them dead on arrival, a few not. In the Hush the old consortium found the opposite of its ancient work. On the flats the mats eat stone, evenly, forever. Here there is no stone to eat — only silt, and the mineral-heavy upwelling of the boils — and the deep partners, the slow ones at the bottom of the fabric, reversed their oldest habit. Around every boil in the basin, the mats *precipitate*. They take the dissolved substance of the vanished mountains out of the rising water and lay it down as crusts, rinds, low domed terraces of new rock — porous, pale, ringing faintly when struck, the first hard thing this country has made.

The colors are the familiar noncommittal ones, pewter and oxblood and the shadow-green, but around the oldest boils there is a color the flats have never shown: a mineral white, dense and final, where the living film has sealed itself over in its own stone. A white that commits. The people call the boil-rock by a word that also means *beginning*, and they are geologically correct to: the mats that leveled the last mountains are, around ten thousand soft mounds in the silence, laying the first grains of the next ones. Nothing on the flats is becoming anything else. Everything in the Hush is.

---

**The drummers, and the drowned swimmers.**

Air-sound being useless here, the native animals speak through the floor.

The crust-runners — call them drummers, since that is the thing they reliably do — are small, flat-bodied, absurdly wide-footed creatures that live their whole lives on the polygon plates, grazing boil-margins and the windfall of dead matter that the drift delivers over the falls. They communicate by tapping: rapid, patterned percussion, forefeet against the crust, and the crust — dry, taut, continuous — carries the taps from plate to plate for distances out of all proportion to the animal. Stand still in the Hush at any hour with a palm flat on the ground and the country reveals itself as quietly busy: taps, answers, the long rolling patterns that seem to mean *boil breached* or *crust failing* or nothing anyone has decoded. On the flats the air is the open channel and the rock the secret one. In the Hush the air is dead and the ground does all the talking. The two countries are one conversation turned inside out.

In the wet silt below hunt the drowned swimmers — and they are not merely analogous to the pale hunters of the flats' channels; they are the same lineage. Larvae have been going over the Last Sound for as long as there has been a Last Sound. Most died in the soft dark. The ones that did not found a country where their whole art — cupping sound, hunting the silences — meant nothing, because everything here is silence, and their fans, over an unrecorded span, became something else: broad, muscular vanes that swim the liquefied silt around the boils as their cousins swim water. They hunt by touch, by the pressure-ghosts of movement in the mud, and — the people insist on this — by the deep bell, holding position over the buried basement as if over a hearth.

During a synchrony, when the silt loosens, the drowned swimmers rise. All of them, basin-wide, up through ground that has briefly forgotten how to be solid, to hang just beneath the crust with their blunt faces upward, doing nothing, eating nothing, while the polygons breathe overhead. Their cousins on the flats rise through water to bask in the unison from inside it. These rise through the earth to bask in it from underneath. Nobody taught either of them. The rhythm has two congregations and has never met one of them.

---

**The quiet ones.**

Some of the silent criers — the exiles, the wrong-voiced, the mute survivors that the rookeries will not have and the channel-swimmers hunt precisely for their quiet — walk the drift. It is a long way, and quiet is the most dangerous thing a body can be on the flats, and almost none of them arrive. But the drift only goes one direction, and at the end of it is the lip, and below the lip is a country where quiet is not a hole in the roar but the whole sky.

They live at the falls-fringe, where mat-film washes down in rafts and the eating is endless, and further in, at the boils, where the people leave them alone with something more deliberate than tolerance. They are long-lived here, as they were long-lived in exile — longer, with nothing hunting silence — and for the first time in the history of their kind, some of them breed in it. Their young do not spend a first season being shaped by any chorus. There is no chorus. The gut-sacs of Hush-born criers grow smooth, unridged, casts of nothing — voiceless not by punishment but by upbringing, the first criers ever to be silent all the way down.

Except at the wells. A crier that grows up near boil-rock sunk to the basement — and they seek it out; hatchlings press themselves to the pale stone the way flats hatchlings press to the rookery edge — is bathed, through the rock, in the one voice the Hush contains: the deep bell, the filtered sum of the whole plain, no colony's accent surviving the distance. Their sacs take its shape. They become casts not of a rookery but of the world's own note.

They almost never sound it. But during a long synchrony, with the crust breathing and the drowned swimmers hanging under the plates, a well-raised quiet one will sometimes press its throat to the boil-stone and produce — softly, a hundred beats, in perfect time — the only voluntary sound in the whole basin. The people do not interrupt this. There is a scratched figure on the rim of the deepest well: a blunt wedge-shape, throat to a shaft, beneath a descending line of dots. It is the same hand, or the same tradition of hand, that scratched the swimmer under its row of dots on a cairn-stone far away on the flats. Whoever they were, they drew what basked in the rhythm on one side of the lip, and what sang it back on the other, and dotted the beats of both.

---

**The people.**

They are the spoken-over. The same people. The flats were never their home — the flats were their pilgrimage, their larder, their season of being deafened. This is the country they came back to, and it explains the name in the second direction: they are a people inaudible at home as well as away. In the Hush a voice reaches an arm's length. Their language grew accordingly — half of it is spoken skin to skin, a hand at the collarbone or the back of the neck, words felt as much as heard; names are rhythms, tapped, in patterns any child can drum on the crust and any parent feel through the soles of the feet from three plates away. It is widely suspected they learned the tapping from the drummers, or the drummers from them, and neither party has clarified.

Their great works are the wells. A well of the Hush is the listening blind turned exactly inside out: not a low wall built to shelter speech from sound, but a shaft sunk through the silence toward it. They dig down through the silt — years of work, the walls cased as they go with boil-rock, the newest stone in the world stacked into a tube aimed at the oldest — until the shaft finds the buried basement, the gray floor of everything, and there they stop and make a room. At the bottom of a finished well the deep bell is *present*: the ringing of the flats, arriving through the one unbroken stone, clear and continuous, the loudest thing in a silent country. The well-bottoms are their gathering places, their courts, their marriage-floors. On the flats, everything the people were happened inside pockets of quiet. Here, everything they are happens at the bottom of shafts of sound. A culture conducted, at both ends of the world, at the seam between the roar and the hush — never in either.

The deepest well they call the Ear. It was finished three long lifetimes ago, and the party that dug the last of it came up with an account that is still told at every well-bottom in the basin: that during a synchrony, from the floor of the Ear, you hear the unison *whole*. Not the way a rookery hears it, from inside, ridden; not the way a traveler hears it, deafened, local. Whole — the entire plain at once, every colony a strand in one arriving chord, the thing itself, at last, at a distance from which it has a shape. You cannot see a wave you are made of. From the Ear, they say, you are not made of it.

The crossings to the deep flats thinned after that, and stopped. The fringe rookeries below the lip still give eggs and meat; the cairns out on the far pans have not grown in a long time, and the flats do not record why, but the Hush does, in its way: the pilgrimage had always been toward the synchrony, and the Ear brought the synchrony home. Whether that was wisdom or a loss is argued, by touch, at well-bottoms, to this day. On the floor of the Ear sits a flat stone ground with small cup-marks in a spiral — sibling to the one abandoned in a half-collapsed blind on the flats, the same hand-span, the same spacing. This one holds no seep-water; the cups are kept dry, and there are more than seventy-one. The spiral continues past the old count, cup after cup, out toward the stone's edge, and the newest cup is half-ground, and the grinding grit beside it is fresh. Someone is still counting. Whatever the beats are a tally of, the tally did not end when the crossings did.

---

**And the last inhabitant, which is the same one.**

The rhythm that rides the criers and razes no boundary it crosses — the wave made of voices, the inhabitant the flats decline to confirm — does not stop at the lip. It arrives here through the buried floor, stripped and slowed, and here, for the first time in its bodiless circuit, something happens to it that does not happen on the flats: it is *kept*. The mats lean toward it and set it into stone. The laminae take it down, layer under layer, every unison since the first, in order, in mud. The flats are its instrument; the Hush is its record. It lives in one country and is remembered in the other, and since it cannot remember itself — since the criers wake from it with nothing, and the swimmers sink from it unchanged, and the people can only count it — the Hush may be the only place in the world where the thing at the center of everything has a past.

Whether an archive is an inhabitant is a question the Hush, like the flats, declines to settle. But stand at the lip at the seam of the two countries, with the roar at your back and the silence ahead and the Last Sound hissing between them, and the arrangement is hard to miss. The flats are the sound the world makes when it is finished. The Hush is the world writing it down — grain by grain, lamina by lamina, mountain by future mountain — as if it expects, someday, in some quiet not yet arrived, to be asked what was said.
