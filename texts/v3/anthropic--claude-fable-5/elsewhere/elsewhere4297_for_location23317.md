**The Comb.**

Leave by the fern-slot and turn uphill. The forest on the slope is tall at first, then shorter, then scrubby, and then it stops — not gradually but all at once, at a line as clean as a tide mark — and the crown of the hill is bare.

You hear the place before you see it. Through the last of the trees comes a thin, dry, many-noted whistling, no single pitch, rising and falling with the wind, and when you step past the tree line you see what is being played: the whole top of the hill is teeth. Acres of gray limestone blades stand shoulder to shoulder — some hip-high, some three times the height of a person — fluted, fretted, knife-edged, close-set as the tines of an instrument, and the wind drags itself across them all day without stopping. Down in the chamber, the swiftlets click in the dark like a fingernail drawn along a comb. Up here you learn where the comb is. It is easy to believe the birds, hunting over this field their whole lives, carried the sound of it home in their mouths.

Nothing on the Comb has been made. Everything on the Comb is what is left.

That is the first thing to understand, and it takes a while, because the shapes are so elaborate they look designed. Every blade is fluted from crown to base with parallel grooves the width of a finger, and each groove is the signature of rain — centuries of it, every drop taking its few molecules of hill and going. The scallops, the pockets, the lace along the crests: all of it is subtraction's handwriting. In the chamber below, every surface is a proposal for rock, soft-edged, still pouring, giving faintly under a thumb. Up here nothing gives and nothing is soft. Press a palm anywhere carelessly and it costs you blood. The chamber has not one hard edge in it; the Comb is not one thing *but* edges. The two places are opposite answers to the same wet question, and the question is limestone.

There are only two lights on the Comb: all, and none. The open crests take the full weight of the sky — a glare so total that the gray stone whitens and the air above the blades shivers — and between the blades run the grikes, slots a foot or two wide and ten or twenty deep, where the light fails within an arm's length and the air goes cool and stands still. There is no dusk here, no brown-gold in-between. You climb across the roof of the field in full sun, and below your feet, in the slots, it is already the chamber's kind of dark.

Everything tilts inward. It takes an hour of clambering to feel it — the whole fretted field is a shallow funnel, every groove feeding a runnel, every runnel a grike, every grike descending toward the low center of the crown, where the blades lean apart around a modest, unspectacular hole perhaps the size of a well-mouth. The throat. In dry weather a stream no wider than your wrist slides over its lip and drops into the dark without a sound worth mentioning.

Put your face over the throat and breathe in.

Warm air rises out of it, and the air smells of wet mineral, warm limestone, and a faint sweetness of guano and moss. You have smelled this before. It is the opening breath of the chamber, three hundred feet down and half a hill away, exhaling up its own plumbing. This hole is the top of the same building. The Comb is its roof.

And now the shapes of both places rearrange themselves around a single fact. The rain that falls here arrives faintly acid — it takes carbon from the air on the way down, and more from the thin pockets of leaf-rot in the grikes — and where it touches the hill, the hill dissolves into it. Down through the throat, down through the dark joints of the deep rock, warming as it goes, the water carries its stolen limestone for the better part of a year, and comes out at last as a glassy film weeping from a seam in the back wall of a chapel-sized room, where the warmth drives the mineral out of solution and lays it down as ivory stone.

The film that weeps down the chamber's wall is the Comb, dissolved and delivered. The shelves, the aprons, the half-poured apricot curtains — all of it is this hilltop, rearranged. The chamber gains a fingernail a year because the Comb pays one out. The two places are a single transaction, and the hill between them is the thickness of the ledger.

Take the census here from the outside in, because that is how the Comb is organized — not by altitude but by exposure, like nerve.

**The crests: the crust.**

Begin with the color, because the color is a lie. Clean limestone is pale — bone-pale, ivory-pale, the chamber's pale — and the Comb is gray-black. Scrape a crest with a knife and the truth shows white under the skin: the darkness is not the stone. It is a living crust, drought-hardened, a weathered felt of cyanobacteria and lichen that covers nearly every exposed inch of the field, and it is not a passenger on the blades any more than the green-gold film below is a passenger on the wall. It bores. It etches. It works weak acids into the surface it lives on, and the impossible sharpness of the fretwork — the lace, the razor crests, the pitting too fine for rain alone — is partly its bite. The Comb is not merely being dissolved. It is being *eaten*, patiently, by its own skin.

And the skin is kin. The film on the chamber's weeping wall and the crust on the Comb's teeth are branches of one ancient family, and the flood-water that goes down the throat carries cells of the upper branch into the dark, where some of them settle on the wet seam and take up the other trade. One family, two houses, opposite work: the branch upstairs unmakes stone by living; the branch downstairs makes it. Neither has ever seen the other, and between the two of them they are moving a hill.

**The grikes: the gardens.**

Drop into any slot and the Comb ends. The wind ends. The glare ends. Ten feet down between two blades the air is cool, damp, motionless, and green: ferns crowd the walls of every grike — the same species, frond for frond, as the screen across the chamber's entrance, their spores traded up and down the hill by wind and water — with mosses below them and pale begonias leaning out of cracks and land snails grazing the shaded stone. Each grike is a hanging garden a foot wide and as long as it wants to be, and each one is also a rehearsal. Dim, damp, windless, patient: every slot on the Comb is a small first draft of the chamber, and some of them, deepening a groove at a time, will be caves in their own right one day, long after there is anyone's word to take for it.

The snails deserve their own line in the ledger. Their shells are white, and the white is the hill — calcium taken from the stone by way of the crust they graze — so that each snail is a small mobile piece of the Comb that has briefly organized itself and gone for a walk. The Comb does not let them keep even that. An empty shell left on the open rock is unmade by the rain in a few seasons, dissolved back into the acid film like a debt recalled. The only permanent snails the Comb has ever produced are the ones whose shells washed down the throat in flood — they lie now somewhere in the lower walls of the chamber, glazed into the ivory, filed.

**The pond: the first dynasty.**

In the one broad depression on the eastern crown, where a collapsed grike has floored itself with washed-in clay, the Comb holds its single piece of standing water: a warm rain-fed pond a few paces across, olive-dark, fringed with sedge. There are fish in it — olive fish, bigger and bolder and greener than any fish downhill, rising to the surface and dropping again in the exact gesture their descendants make in the travertine basins below. This is the parent stock. Herons visit the pond, and herons' feet carry eggs; floods overtop its rim and send fry swirling toward the throat. Both of the chamber's origin stories are true, and both of them start here. The pale, rationed dynasties in the stone bowls below are this pond's emigrants, remembering open water without knowing they are remembering anything.

**The stream: the crabs' country.**

Between rains, the sinking stream is a chain of trickles threading the master grikes toward the throat, and the trickles belong to the crabs — dozens of them, apricot against the wet gray, the same improbable color as the iron-stained stone below, sidling the runnels with the same absurd patience as their solitary cousin in the chamber. Once, in some flood no one witnessed, one of them went over the lip of the throat and down through the dark and *lived*, and either that traveler or its line now walks the basin rims three hundred feet under this stream, past its own row of glazed statues. Up here the crabs are common. Down there the crab is a founder. The throat makes emigrants of the unlucky and ancestors of the survivors, and it does not distinguish between the two on the way down.

**The figs.**

Two or three rock figs have taken the deepest grikes, and their roots are the only soft-looking things on the Comb: pale gray, poured, flowing down the insides of the slots in smooth cascades — the chamber's travertine curtains inverted, alive, and running the other way. They are also the hardest workers of the hill's undoing. Every root is a wedge, every year of growth a fraction more leverage, and now and then in the stillness there is a single dry *crack* as a blade gives up a flake it held for ten thousand years. The chamber builds its curtains out of water. The Comb's curtains take the place apart.

**The commuters.**

The day sky over the Comb belongs to the swiftlets — the same individual birds, every one of them, that cling to the high city in the chamber's dusk. The grikes breathe insects upward all day: flies bred in the damp gardens, winged termites after rain, midges off the pond, all of it rising on the heat that comes off the blades, and the swiftlets ride the field's thermals in flickering loose wheels, mouths open, from first warmth to last. The Comb is the colony's pantry, and the arithmetic of it runs all the way downhill: every saliva cup in the high city is built out of this field's air, and the guano cone the beetles till on the chamber floor is the Comb's insects, converted and delivered by wing. At dusk the wheels tilt and stream downhill toward the fern-slot, and a few minutes later a different flight comes uphill in the dark — flittering where the first was pointed — as the bats take over the same air.

So the two places share one flesh, divided by nothing but sleep. The Comb owns the residents' working hours; the chamber keeps their lives between shifts. The Comb is where the chamber's population spends itself; the chamber is where it is stored.

**The terror: the hawk.**

On the tallest tooth of the field — a blade half again the height of the others, its crest whitened with years of droppings — sits, most of every day, a single dark hawk. It is a specialist. It does not hunt the wheeling flocks at noon; it hunts the *commute*: the twenty minutes at dusk and the twenty at dawn when swiftlets and bats pour along the flight-line between the throat of the hill and the slot in its side, and it takes them out of that river in long shallow stoops and swallows them whole on the wing without landing.

It shapes the sky exactly as the racer shapes the wall. The commuting lanes bend wide around the tallest tooth; the birds leave the slot low and scattered and only gather height beyond the hawk's country; the bats run the grikes like trenches when the light is against them. In the chamber, one slow snake that cannot fly draws the architecture of the nests. Over the Comb, one patient bird that will not land draws the architecture of the routes. Between the two of them — the terror of the roofed and the terror of the open — the colony's entire geometry, where it builds and how it travels, is decided by exactly two animals. And the hawk, note, does not really live on the Comb or on the chamber. It lives on the road between them. It eats the connection itself.

**The flood.**

Once or twice in a decade the sky settles onto the hill and stays, and the Comb shows what it is for. Every groove becomes a jet, every grike a race, the pond overtops its rim in a smooth silver sheet, and the throat — the modest, wrist-thick throat — drinks standing waves with a roar you can feel through the blades. And the blades ring. Struck by heavy rain, each thin fin of fretted stone sounds its own faint note, thousands of them at once, and the whole field becomes the instrument the wind only ever hints at: the Comb played fortissimo by weather, audible from the valley.

What goes down in those hours is the year's mail. Fish eggs and fry. Snail shells. A crab or two. Fig seeds, fern spores, cells of the black crust, the washed bones of whatever died on the open rock since the last flood, and forty tons of hill in solution. Everything the Comb loses, the chamber keeps. The flood is not a disaster here. It is the post.

**The full census.**

So: what lives here? Count the crust on the crests, the gardens in the slots, the snails, the pond dynasty, the crabs of the trickles, the figs, the wheeling commuters, the hawk on the tallest tooth.

Then notice that the count, here, never goes up. No soil accumulates — what little forms in the grikes washes down them. No dead remain — the rain unmakes them or the throat takes them. No sound stays; the wind carries even the ringing away. The Comb has one rule too, and it is the other rule: *nothing here is allowed to stay.* Not the water, not the shells, not the season's insects, not the hill itself. Everything on the Comb is in transit, including the Comb, which is subtracting itself at a fingernail a year and keeps no record of anything it has been.

It does not need to. Its archive is downstairs. Every fallen snail, every emigrant fish, every drowned cricket and washed-down bone, every dissolved ton of the hilltop itself — all of it is filed, in order of arrival, in the thickening ivory of a warm room it will never see. The chamber's memory is the chamber. The Comb's memory is kept in another building.

Stand at the throat at the end of a long day, when the glare has gone amber and the swiftlets are streaming downhill past the hawk's bent airspace, and the whole hill resolves into a single slow gesture: a body moving itself from its roof to its heart. The crown dissolves; the chapel fills. Someday — no hurry; nothing here has ever hurried — the Comb will be all sky, and the chamber will have sealed its last basin and poured its last curtain and become solid stone with a city and a crab and a hundred dynasties bound inside it, and the transaction will be complete: a hill turned inside out, its outside gone to water and its inside gone to rock.

A storm is building now, off to the west. The first drop strikes a blade near your hand and the blade rings, once, very faintly — almost the sound of a drop striking a full basin in a dim room. It nearly is. This rain will spend the winter crossing the dark interior of the hill, and around the time the fiddleheads at the slot below unroll their next season's fronds, it will arrive at the seam and fall — *tik* — and be signed in, and never leave.

Up here, it is weather. Down there, next year, it will be architecture.
