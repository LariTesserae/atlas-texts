## The Radiary

Follow the trunks. In the central well they rise like organ pipes and seem to end in the ceiling, but they do not end — they turn. Behind the sweating stone they lie down and run, mile after mile, through bedrock that has learned their warmth the way a sleeper's mattress learns a body, out from under the city, out past the last of its foundations, and then, gradually, up. You could trace them, if you had the woman's hands, by pressing your palm to the ground of an entire countryside: a line of faint fever in the earth, heading for high country.

They surface on a fell.

You arrive at the Radiary the way you would leave a body: through a long throat of a tunnel that grows colder as it rises, up worn stairs that dish in their centers exactly as the stairs below do — the same feet cut both, generations ago, going between — until the darkness ahead of you turns gray, then white, then intolerable, and you step out of a low stone mouth into more light than seems reasonable, and more sky, and wind.

It is a mountainside above the treeline, and it is a machine, and it is nearly silent. These three facts coexist without strain. Across the whole broad shoulder of the fell, in contour rows that follow the land like script follows a line, stand the fins: hundreds of them, blade-thin verticals of pale ceramic, each one taller than a ship's sail, each one rooted in a trunk that runs down through the hill to the roaring heart you have just climbed out of. From a distance — from the valley, from the village — they look like standing stones, an orchard of monuments, a comb dragged through the mountain's hair. Up close they are unmistakably plumbing. They are where the Exchange exhales.

**It is silent.** This is the first thing and the last thing, and it takes an hour to understand, because the silence is not an absence — it is the far end of the roar. All that sound below, the drone in the sternum, the tidal *whum*, the shrieking valves: none of it makes the climb. The rock eats it. What arrives instead, nine seconds after each beat — you can time it, if you have stood in the chamber below and carry its rhythm in you still — is heat. A pulse of warmth blooms at the base of every fin at once and travels up them in a slow wave, and in winter, which up here is most of the year, you can *see* it: a line of melt running up through the frost on five hundred blades simultaneously, the whole hillside's rime dying in a single traveling stripe and re-forming behind it, over and over, all day, all night, forever. The heartbeat of the Undercroft, made visible as weather. Visitors — there are almost never visitors — stand and watch it for hours and don't notice they've stopped speaking, though up here nothing stops them but the size of the sky.

**The cold is bodily, dry, constant, and the warmth lives inside it in stripes.** Each fin sheds heat to windward, so each one wears a lee of milder air, and each row of fins compounds the last, so that walking the field is walking through alternating countries: three steps of true mountain cold that makes the teeth ache, then a step of air the temperature of skin — Exchange air, unmistakably, humid and mineral, smelling faintly of ozone and warm ceramic and, on the days the deep vats are working, of bread — then cold again. Along the bottom of the field, where all the sheltered air pools together, runs a strip of ground perhaps ten paces wide that has never in living memory held snow. Grass grows there in January. The village calls it the Hem.

**The place is old in the exposed way.** Whatever the fins were for — and their purpose has not concluded; it is the one purpose in this whole system still legibly being served, second by second, heat in, heat gone — the people who might explain them are as absent up here as below. But where the Exchange has reached equilibrium with its conditions, the Radiary has reached an argument with them, and loses it nightly, and does not mind. The wind is ceaseless. It scours. Frost forms and is melted and forms again; grit rides the gales and polishes the ceramic to a soft glow like the inside of a shell; nothing scratched into a fin survives a season. The maintenance-motes do not come here. You can find them, if you look, in the tunnel mouth — a fine dark tidemark of them, a border of chittering hesitation just inside the stone, where the building's sense of touch reaches its end and stops, like a nerve at the surface of the skin. Beyond that line the Exchange cannot feel itself, and so beyond that line nothing is curated, nothing kept. Below, the local form of memory is wear, preserved forever at its exact depth. Up here the local form of memory is erasure. The frost takes everything each night and issues the field blank each morning, and the Radiary is not melancholy about this, because it has the wind's opinion of possessions.

Except — and this is the field's one act of keeping — heat holds a path. Ground that is walked daily along the warm rows stays soft, stays green, stays printed. There is exactly one such path in the Radiary, a thread of worn grass switchbacking up through the fins, and it is kept not by motes but by feet, a single pair, going up and coming down, every day for a very long time.

**Life gathers in the pulse's lee.** The vegetation is sparse and opportunist and, in one case, familiar: on the north faces of the lower fins grows a pale lichen in long thin ribbons, and it is the moss's cousin — the same feral wayfinding genome, carried up decades ago in the condensation that beads inside the trunks, still stuttering through its two instincts, *toward warmth* and *glow*. Up here its glow is nothing against the daylight, so it has found another script: it insulates, minutely, and where it grows the frost lies differently, and at dawn, before the sun burns the rime away, you can read it — fragments of frost-shadow lettering on the tall white blades, half an arrow pointing into the sky, the letters *ARRIV—* dissolving as the first pulse of the day climbs through them. The moss below spends centuries writing directions to platforms. Its cousin up here writes the same directions in a medium that lasts forty minutes. Between them they have divided the labor of remembering perfectly uselessly, and neither has ever been discouraged.

The fauna is fitted to the stripes. There are the fin-swallows — small, quick, soot-blue — which by every law of their kind should leave this fell in September and don't, haven't for a hundred generations, because the fins have made migration a thing that happens to other birds. They nest in the warm hollows where blade meets base, and they have learned the pulse the way the gallery moths below learned it: each *whum*'s worth of heat lifts a soft thermal off the field nine seconds later, and the swallows ride it, surfacing and settling with the heartbeat, so that on still winter mornings the whole flock rises and falls above the fins like something breathing them. In the melt-channels that carry the frost-water down through the field live translucent freshwater shrimp, glassy and unhurried, and the old ones — this has been noticed, and not explained, because the mote-fragments that wash up in the trunk condensation do not all digest — carry a faint amber spark behind the carapace, a drifting point of the Exchange's own status-light, glowing in a mountain stream that has never seen the chamber it reports on. And the village bees are wintered against the Hem in their dozens of hives, and the honey they make is prized three valleys away and cannot be described to anyone who has not been underground: it tastes, people say, reaching, like warm stone, like ozone, like bread that hasn't happened yet.

**The water goes down.** This is the current inside the current, the return stroke. Every pulse melts frost; the melt runs down the fins, gathers in the channels, and where the channels end it does not flow away — it sinks, into gravel beds and old deliberate seams, into the hill, into the dark, percolating for weeks or months through rock kept warm by the trunks it travels beside, until it beads at last on a ribbed ceiling thirty meters above a black pool, and falls, and lands with a *tick* that a roaring chamber somehow keeps audible. Every drop in the Undercroft is weather. Each tick the woman hears in the dark is a specific snowfall, arrived at last, months late, delivered to a place that has never seen the sky it fell from. The pools she passes each morning — the ones that tremble, the ones the old salamanders dream in, the ones that sometimes reflect a lit and crowded Exchange — are made entirely of mountain. She does not know this. The mountain does not know what its snow becomes. The system knows, in whatever way a circulation knows anything, and keeps both ends supplied.

**The strangeness here is mild, ambient, and it is the same strangeness, balanced.** The field measures short. Pace it, chain it, survey it with instruments of any era: the Radiary comes back roughly twenty meters smaller than it walks, a deficit that never announces itself, only accumulates, and the deficit is — the woman below could confirm this, if there were any way to ask her — the same twenty meters by which the central well of the Exchange runs long. The engineers' footnote-physics left slack in the geometry, and the slack lies below, and something lives in it; up here is where the slack was taken from. The two discrepancies are one discrepancy, carried on opposite sides of a ledger, reconciling to zero across forty miles of rock. Surveyors from the valley have given the field up as a bad job for three centuries running. The current Warden could tell them not to bother, and doesn't, because the wardens' lore on the subject consists in its entirety of one sentence, passed down with the maul: *the field keeps its change under the hill.*

And on certain dawns — after the coldest, stillest nights, when the frost has grown all night undisturbed — the rime on the lower fins holds shapes. Human shapes. Silhouettes melted faintly into the white, as if warm bodies had leaned against the blades for hours in the dark: shoulders, the curve of a resting head, here and there the print of a spread hand. Dozens of them, some mornings. The field below the fins shows no footprints. The wardens' lore calls them the Leaners and instructs, simply, that they be left to burn off on their own, unwatched — it is considered rude to look at them directly, and no warden has ever asked themselves where they learned that rule, or noticed that it is the same rule the woman follows at the pools, forty miles down, nodding sidelong at a lit crowd kept at an angle. The pending population of the Exchange, filed in a direction ordinary attention cannot turn, seems to press very slightly against the world at both ends of the system: as reflection where the water gathers, as impression where the frost does. Whether they are trying to come through or only shifting in their sleep is not a question anyone at either end is equipped to settle. At both ends, independently, the living have decided to be polite about it.

**Below the field, at the fell's foot, is Whitecomb, and Whitecomb is where the crowds went.** Not all of them, and not in any way the village remembers. But trace the parish rolls back far enough and they begin, all at once, eleven generations ago — a whole population arriving in a single decade with trades no hill village breeds: gauge-fitters, ledger-clerks, translators of no recorded language. The village calls the fins the Combs and believes them older than the mountain's name, a monument, a mystery, a place you take visiting cousins on fine days. They dry their laundry along the Hem in winter, white sheets steaming in ranks below the white blades. They graze sheep there in the cruel months. And they bring their dead there — not the bodies, which go to the churchyard, but the belongings: a coat, a pipe, a doll, carried up the evening after a funeral and set out in the frost lanes between the fins, unattended, unmourned-over, left. By spring the wind and the melt and the rime have taken every trace. Burial by erasure. Forty miles down, in the dark, their eleven-times-great-grandparents are buried as polish, kept forever at their exact depth of having-been-here, and the descendants who have forgotten them entirely have arrived by pure inheritance at the perfect inverse rite. The two graveyards are one graveyard, kept in opposite currencies. Nobody knows this. It works anyway.

**And there is the Warden.** One. The *almost*, again, in *almost nobody* — the field's entire population, wearing an oilskin older than he is in the honest way, patched by hands rather than motes, aging at the normal rate.

The wardenship goes back to the beginning of the parish rolls and comes with three things: the hut at the tunnel mouth, the long-handled beetle — a maul of black wood, its head worn asymmetric by eleven generations of the same swing — and the duty, whose reasons were lost so long ago that the duty itself has become the reason. Keep the fins clear. Ice is the enemy; ice on a fin blankets it, backs its heat up, makes it — the lore says — *sullen*. So the Warden walks the rows in the worst weather, when the melt-pulse can't keep pace with the storm, and strikes the iced fins with the beetle, two blows or three, in the patterns his mother taught him and her father taught her: patterns for shedding ice, patterns for testing tone, patterns — though no warden has used the word — for courtesy. A struck fin tolls. The whole trunk carries it. The blow runs down through forty miles of warm rock and arrives in the Undercroft nine seconds later as a hitch in the heartbeat, a syncopation in the *whum*, a figure repeated —

— and forty miles down, a woman flat-palmed against a ceramic trunk feels the great sound stumble in answer to her tapping, and does not insist that it is conversation, and continues it.

Because her taps come up, too. On windless nights, when the field is so quiet the swallows' wings are audible, the fins sometimes ring of themselves — a faint, patterned, deliberate tolling from underground, deep in the blades, rhythmic in a way that wind never is. The lore calls it the under-toll and says it is the mountain settling and instructs the Warden to answer it, beetle on the nearest fin, matching the figure as best he can, *for luck*. He has done this all his life, as his mother did. He believes he is being courteous to a hill. She believes she is being answered by a building. Both of them are wrong in the details and right in the whole, because the system between them is not passive; the heart has its own opinions and adds its own syncopations, so that the conversation has three participants, two of whom don't know the third exists and the third of whom has never explained anything. It has been going on, in one pairing of tapper and warden or another, for longer than either lineage of the duty remembers. It is possible — the parish rolls being what they are — that the first warden and the first tapper knew each other. It is possible they were saying goodbye, and that the goodbye simply never finished, and became instead the longest hello in the world, handed down at both ends to strangers who keep it going out of luck and politeness and something neither has a word for and neither has ever once thought to stop.

The walker below, on its long circuits, pauses each night outside the kiosk where she sleeps: one pause, one *whum*. What is less known — what only the swallows are placed to notice, and they keep no ledgers — is that at the same hour, up in the wind, the melt-pulse pauses too. One beat of frost, all across the field, that does not melt. The Warden has seen it, once or twice in a life, from the hut door: the whole hillside's rime holding its breath. Then the wave resumes, and the count, presumably, is confirmed at both ends. One below. One above. Warmth out, water back, sound both ways, discrepancy carried forward, accounts pending, the sum unchanged.

**Which leaves the shape of the whole thing, visible only from far enough away that no one stands there.** The Exchange circulates; the Radiary exhales; a heart in the dark and a lung in the wind, joined through forty miles of rock that has been kept warm so long it has forgotten being cold, one buried body with a village asleep on its shoulder and a city asleep on its chest, neither dreaming of the other. The fins are warm on the side facing you, whichever side that is. They have that from home.

And when you leave — down the fell path in the evening, through the Hem where the hives murmur, past Whitecomb's first lit windows — the cold lets you go at once, the wind forgets you at the treeline, the silence hands you back to the ordinary noise of the world without any reluctance at all, because the Radiary keeps nothing, that is its whole nature. But nine seconds after your own heart beats, for a day or more, you will keep expecting something. A warmth, arriving. A stripe of melt, climbing. You brought the rhythm up out of the ground without noticing, or it came up through your boot-soles in the field, and now it lives in you the way the *whum* lived in your chest on the ladder — except this one does not fade with reluctance.

It fades like frost. Completely, by morning. As if it were being polite.
