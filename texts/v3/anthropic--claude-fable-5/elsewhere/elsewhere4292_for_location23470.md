## The Drinking Stone

You climb to it rather than arrive — a long shoulder of grey country rising out of the low land, and then, when the slope finally gives out, you keep arriving for another mile, because the top is wider than the hill. It is a plateau of bare limestone that has been doing exactly this, in exactly this way, for longer than anything on it has been alive: taking the rain, and giving itself away.

The light is the first thing that owns you. There is no ceiling here but the true one, and the sky uses all of it. The plateau tells you the hour without being asked — long amber shadows thrown flat across the stone at morning, no shadows at noon but your own small one pooled at your feet, everything gilded and doubled again at evening. Weather crosses the top in visible provinces: you can watch a rain that will not reach you for an hour, walking on grey legs along the far rim. Nothing here is sourceless. Everything is announced.

And on a cold, still morning, from the eastern edge, you can see where the hill goes when it leaves.

Far out in the low country — two days' walk, at least — a single grey feather stands upright where nothing else stands. A breath that never blows away. Every other vapor in that distance rises and is taken by the wind; this one folds back down on itself and holds. From here it is small enough to cover with a thumb. The people who lived on this top would have known it the way they knew the pole star: the one fixed thing in the moving weather. The standing breath. The warm bowl. The place the hill's water goes to be born a second time.

### The Stone

The top is pavement — great flat slabs of pale limestone laid nearly level, split by a net of fissures, so that walking it is a long negotiation of stepping-stones with the gaps going down dark between them. Some of the fissures are a finger wide. Some would take your whole leg. A few would take you.

The rain made every one of them. That is the plateau's single occupation. Rain falls, gathers a faint acidity out of the air and the thin turf, finds a crack, and widens it — not by force but by dissolving, taking the stone into itself molecule by molecule and carrying it down into the dark. The slabs are scored and runnelled and fluted like things licked smooth. Every rain, the hill weighs less. This is not erosion in the ordinary sense; nothing is battered here, nothing broken and washed off. The hill is being *drunk* — swallowed by its own weather in solution, invisibly, a fingernail's depth in a long lifetime.

None of it is lost. That is the thing to hold onto, standing on the pavement with the wind taking your breath sideways. The dissolved stone goes down into the fissures, into whatever black patient plumbing runs under all this country, travels farther than anyone has ever followed, and comes up again — warm, at the far end, in the bottom of that distant bowl — where the water, cooling, gives the stone back. Lays it down drop by drop in terraces, lips, curtains, basins. Bone-white, ivory, the orange of old teeth.

The Seep is this hill, relocated.

The flowstone pillar thick as a barrel that you edge past down there: this slab, or one like it, gone traveling. The stone curtain the drops tick from: an old floor of this grass. The white coat thickening over the cup in the niche is, some centuries of rain ago, pavement a shepherd stood on. The hollow builds at almost exactly the rate the plateau dissolves, because they are not two processes. They are one process with a very long middle, and the middle is dark, and no one has ever seen it.

### The Sound

It is never quiet. Not loud — loud would be a different place — but the wind here is a resident, not a visitor, and everything on the top has been shaped by negotiating with it. It combs the thin grass all one way. It hums in the fissures at certain angles, a low fluting, the hill played like the mouth of a jar. Sound carries for miles across the pavement and comes back off nothing; a raven's croak from the far rim arrives whole, uncushioned, and keeps going past you into the next valley. Down in the hollow, each sound happens once, close, and is eaten. Here every sound is public.

In spring there are curlews, their long bubbling call falling out of the empty air like something being poured.

But the plateau's own sound — the one that is its signature, as the tick of drops is the hollow's — comes after heavy rain. At the pavement's low southern corner there is a slot in the stone, a swallow-hole the width of a door, and in a storm the gathered runoff of half the top converges on it in a brief brown stream and goes down. The sound it makes is a throat's sound: gulping, irregular, greedy and effortless at once. Then the rain stops, the stream thins, the gulping softens to a swallowing, then to a trickling, then to nothing — and the water is gone and does not come back. Not here. Put your ear near any deep fissure in the hour after rain and you can hear it below you, falling away inside the hill, going down and away, a sound moving in one direction only.

That is the last anyone up here hears of it. The next sound that water makes, it makes as a drop ticking from a stone curtain into a blood-warm pool, under a ceiling of steam, longer from now than any memory on this hill can reach.

### What Was Made Here

The people lived here. Not visited — lived. The plateau shows you all of it, because the plateau keeps its records the opposite way from the hollow: nothing here is entombed, everything is exposed, and the frost dismantles it so slowly that it stays legible for ages. The Seep erases by addition, a coat of stone a year until a cup becomes a sphere. The Drinking Stone erases by subtraction, a course of wall a century, and you can read every stage of the ruin.

There are hut circles on the sheltered eastern slope, low rings of stone with their doorways all turned away from the wind, hearth-slabs still fire-reddened at their centers. There is a fold for animals, its wall still waist-high in stretches. There are querns — grinding stones with worn dishes in their faces — and the pick-marks on the best of them are the same marks, the same hand's-width strokes of the same stone tools, that you can find softening under glaze on the rim of the third basin two days' walk below. They ground grain up here in the wind. They cut a channel down there in the steam. Same people, same patient picking, two records kept in two kinds of ink.

And there is the line of cairns. It runs off the eastern rim and down the long shoulder, small deliberate stacks spaced about a shout apart, and if you stand behind any one of them and sight along the line, it aims — exactly, unarguably — at the standing feather of steam in the low country. A road with no surface. Just punctuation, pointed at a breath.

They walked it. Carrying hides to soak, reeds cut from the marsh-flats on the way, or carrying nothing — carrying winter in their joints, perhaps, down to the one place in the world that was warm without fire, to sit to the neck in blood-heat under the grey ceiling and then climb back up into the weather. The grinding stone abandoned above the waterline down there; the querns abandoned beside the hut rings up here. They left both ends of their world at around the same time, for reasons neither place recorded. The grass thinned, or the rain changed, or they simply went. The plateau does not say. It only keeps the walls standing, a little lower every century, and holds the cairn-line true.

### What Lives Here

Everything, and all of it in the cracks.

The open pavement belongs to the wind and to a film — for there is a film here too, the plateau's answer to the orange paint in the spring's throat. It is lichen: grey-green, black, dust-gold, a skin of the slowest possible tenants, growing outward on the slabs at a rate that makes the terraces look hasty. It is the only living thing that faces the weather flat-on and wins, and it wins by conceding everything: no height, no softness, no hurry. The film in the spring's throat has never known weather; this one has never known anything else. They are kin the way the two ends of the water are kin.

But the fissures — the fissures are gardens. Lower yourself to the pavement, put your face to one of the deeper slots, and you breathe another climate entirely: dim, damp, windless, even. Ferns grow down there, dark-green tongues of them, and small soft herbs that could not survive one hour on the open slab, all of them thriving in a slot of permanent shelter a stride below the storm. Every deep grike is a splinter of the hollow's weather, kept in the hill's pocket. The Seep stops being strange the day you have smelled a fissure. It is only this — the crack's climate, let out at the far end, given warm water, allowed to fill a whole bowl and put a roof of steam on itself.

The snails live between the two worlds, and they are the plateau's quietest joke. White-shelled, patient, they graze the lichen film on wet days and retreat into the fissures on dry ones, and they build their shells out of the hill itself — rasped, dissolved, and reprecipitated in a spiral you can hold. A snail is the whole system in miniature: limestone taken into solution and laid down again as new architecture. When one dies on the open slab, the rain takes the shell back the way it takes everything, into solution, down the dark, and it is not sentiment but chemistry to say that some fraction of the flowstone pillar in the hollow, some layer of some terrace lip, is old shell — is snail — is this grazing multiplied by an age.

And there is a hare. Possibly several; you only ever see one at a time, and only briefly, because the hare is everything the salamander was excused from. It is made entirely of weather. Its coat is the exact dun of winter grass because everything here watches; its heart is oversized because everything here teaches flight, and the curriculum never closes — wind, raven, winter, the open sky. It knows the fissure-net the way the salamander knows its basins, but as bolt-holes, not homes: a grey blur, a doubling turn, and it pours itself into a slot in the pavement and is gone into the hill's pocket-climate until the sky is safe. It has never had a reason not to hurry. The salamander and the hare will never meet, and they are the same office held under opposite laws: the one animal, at the margin, living in the cracks of a process too big to notice them.

The birds nest in what the people left. Wheatears in the wall-gaps of the fold, flashing white as they go; a raven pair that rides the rim wind and owns the whole top by survey. And this, perhaps, closes one small account: the shadow that crosses the hollow's steam ceiling now and then, the bird that does not land — it lands *here*. The hollow offers fauna nothing and the plateau offers it everything except mercy, and the birds have chosen, and their flight-lines between the high country and the marsh-flats pass over the standing breath the way the cairn-line points at it: acknowledging, not stopping.

### What Leaves Here

Take the census slowly and it ends the same way the hollow's does — with the largest entry, the one everything else was living inside all along. But here the largest resident is not the place. The plateau is not a body maintaining itself. It is a body being spent.

Spent is different from finished, and both are different from dying. The Seep is finished: full of itself, closed, breathing its grey breath in a circle, keeping its blood-heat against every winter, changing nothing. The Drinking Stone is the open half of that circle — everything it is given, it swallows; everything it is, it gives away; and the two of them balance across the two days' distance like a held breath and its release. One place keeps everything and alters nothing. The other keeps nothing and is altering always, invisibly, a fingernail a lifetime, downward into the dark.

So the largest resident here is the one with two addresses.

The rain. Cold and quick and loud on this end — announced by walking grey legs, gulped down the throat-slot, heard falling away inside the hill. Then the long middle that no one has seen: the dark plumbing, the slow warming as it goes deep, the loading of dissolved hill into itself, a passage that takes longer than the walls have stood. And then the far end: rising through the throat of a spring as a lens of water standing slightly proud of a pool, blood-warm, mineral, patient, laying the plateau out again in white terraces under a ceiling of its own breath. The water in which the salamander spreads its hands fell on this grass when the hut circles had roofs. The storm soaking you now, running past your boots toward the swallow-hole, will rise into that steam when everything you know is as old as the cairns.

The two places are one creature seen at two ages. Here it is young: cold, loud, in a hurry, taking. There it is old: warm, quiet, finished, giving back. Between them runs the dark, and the dark is most of its life.

---

You could leave and come back in forty years, and the plateau would have forgotten you the day you left — no coat of stone to hold your traces, only wind and subtraction. The walls will be a little lower. A different hare will pour itself into a different slot. The hill will look precisely the same and will weigh measurably less, and none of the difference will show.

But stand on the eastern rim before you go, on a cold still morning, behind the first cairn of the line, and sight along it. Out there in the low country the grey feather is standing, exactly where it stood for the people who stacked these stones, exactly where it will stand for whoever comes after the walls are grass. It is the hill's own breath, delayed by centuries, exhaled at last through a warm mouth two days' walk away. The Drinking Stone and the weeping stone. One swallows the rain; one gives it back changed. Watch the plume long enough and the distance stops feeling like distance and starts feeling like anatomy — and then walk down along the cairns, the way they did, toward the standing breath, carrying nothing the water is not already carrying underneath you.
