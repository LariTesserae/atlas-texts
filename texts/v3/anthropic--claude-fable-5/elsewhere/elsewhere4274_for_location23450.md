# The Weirs

There is nothing here but ground. That is the first thing to understand, and it takes no time at all to feel it, because the ground announces itself the moment you arrive: hard, dry, ancient, stretching away south and west in the pale tans and greys of a country that has forgotten what a full cloud looks like. The herders call it the combed country, and the name is exact. The weather arrives from the west heavy with rain and passes overhead already lightened, brushed, *drunk from* — because the second thing to understand about the Weirs is the thing you cannot stop looking at, the thing that owns the northern half of the sky.

The Wall.

It goes up out of the plain like the edge of a different argument. Not stone — you can see that from a mile off — but courses. Brick, iron, slag-block, the blank faces of windows sealed so long ago the sealing itself has weathered into ornament, stratum over stratum, rising until the whole enormous flank simply enters the cloud and is gone. On the rare clear days you can follow it all the way, and at the top, miles up, there is a hairline of parapet, and at certain dawns a flash of tilted light comes off it — a single hard wink of sun on distant mirrors — which the people here call the mountain opening its eye. They do not know that on the far side of that flash, a hereditary guild is making the day's small adjustments so that light will arrive on time in streets they have never imagined. They only know that the eye opens, and that the mountain is awake, and that the mountain being awake is why they are alive.

Because the Weirs sit in the one wet place in all the combed country: the foot of the Wall, on its western side, in the long cold shadow of the mornings — a green thread sewn to the hem of the mountain, a ribbon of ponds and fig-shade and black-soiled fields running along the base courses for a few miles and not one step further. The mountain drinks the weather. And then, in its own time, by its own dark ways, the mountain weeps it back.

## The Water

It comes down in braids and seeps all along the foot, but chiefly it comes down at the Long Weep: a stack of drain-mouths high on the flank — some of them cast, absurdly, in the shape of open dragons' mouths, gone green with four hundred years of flow — pouring a white column of water down the courses into the first of the great catch-pools. The water arrives *warm*. That is the marvel that visitors cannot get past. It has spent days descending through miles of warmed masonry, through strata and galleries and channels no one on either end has fully mapped, and it comes out of the Wall at the temperature of a living body, steaming on winter dawns, so that the Weirs never freeze, never brown, never wait for spring like the rest of the plain.

And it arrives *carrying*. The water of the Weirs has a taste — iron, always, and under the iron something green and faintly sweet that strangers cannot place and locals call fig, correctly, though most of them have never wondered why. The language here has eleven common words for the water, sorted not by clarity but by burden — what a flow carries down — and the morning greeting when the Weep runs full is, literally translated, "good catch."

The town itself is the water's path made civic. From the first catch-pools the flow steps down through a cascade of built weirs — pond below pond below pond, each one kept by a family or a guild, each one doing its work in order. The upper weirs are the catch-weirs, netted and grated, where anything solid the mountain sends is stopped and gathered. Below them, the settling-weirs, broad and still, where the silt goes down — and the silt of the Weirs is the black blessing, the richest earth in five hundred miles, spread on the fields in the shadow of the Wall, where it grows what nothing else in the combed country will grow. Below the fields, the tail-ponds, where the herders water their flocks and the wild things of the steppe come at dusk, and where the water finally sets out southward as an ordinary stream, having been cloud, harvest, city, gift, and grave.

The Weirs can forecast their own floods, and it is the town's favorite piece of knowledge. On clouded nights the underside of the overcast at the summit glows — a faint warm gold, hanging up there like a banked ember — and everyone knows what it means: the mountain drank last night. *Be at the weirs on the third morning.* The water takes three days to come down through the dark, and it has never once been late.

## The Falls

Water is not all the mountain sends.

Rarely — a few times a year, oftener after the great storms — the Weep or the seeps deliver an object. A length of bronze mesh, fine as cloth, torn from something and carried down the drains. A tool of unfamiliar pattern. A lamp-globe of thick amber glass, once in a hundred years unbroken, and there are two of these in the town and they are, without any formal declaration, its holiest things. Leaves come down that match no tree of the plain until you look at the trees of the Weirs themselves. Once, in living memory, a drowned goat, sleek and strange-horned, mourned by people who had never seen its like alive.

These are the *falls*, and the catch-weir families gather them, and the Fall-house keeps them: a long cool hall by the first pool, shelf over shelf of everything the mountain has ever shed, arranged by generation. The Weir-folk read it the way other peoples read chronicles. When the water ran with brick dust for thirty years, they knew — knew in their own terms — that something vast up there was being unmade and remade, and they named the children born in those years the brickwater generation, and the name is still spoken with a certain gravity. The Fall-house is their history of a place they have never seen, written in its molt.

Nothing that falls is wasted. The bronze mesh, cut and framed, becomes the finest sieves in the world, and so the cloud-combs of the summit end their long lives combing the water at the foot — the same work, continued, a mile of masonry later. Fragments of that bronze circulate through the whole combed country as prestige metal, worn at weddings, traded for flocks; the steppe's silver is the mountain's skin.

## The Living

The green of the Weirs is a descended green. The broad-leafed shade trees of the pond margins, the crimson-veined pot-herb the locals grow in the warm shallows and call *weepleaf*, the wallfigs espaliered against the lowest warm courses of the Wall itself — all of it grew from seed the water carried down, generation over generation, a whole flora emigrating one flood at a time. The Weir-folk have their own names for every plant and no idea that each name has an older sibling far overhead.

In the warm catch-pools live pale fish that nobody introduced and nobody can quite explain; they are simply there, and the cooks have opinions about them. In the lowest drain-mouths, where a person can walk upright into the Wall for a hundred paces before the dark asserts itself, live slate-blue crabs, broad as dinner plates, that graze the sieves and gratings clean of algae — and the sieve-wardens feed them, and count them, and consider a grating without crabs to be a grating in trouble. Two peoples who have never met keep the same crabs by the same rules, and neither knows it, and the crabs, presumably, do.

At dawn and dusk, very high, a river of swifts pours along the flank of the Wall like smoke running sideways, too far up to hear. The herders time their movements by the Wall's shadow, which crosses the plain each day like the arm of an instrument; the whole combed country lives on the mountain's clock, drinks the mountain's sweat, spends the mountain's skin — a country governed absolutely by a city that remembers it, if at all, as a blue haze glimpsed from a parapet.

## The Wall

At the foot, the Wall is warm. You can press your palm against the base courses — everyone does; it is the town's habit, its handshake with the world — and feel the animal heat in them, and under the heat, if you stand still, the thrum. Vast, soft, layered, one slow pulse every few seconds, felt in the teeth more than heard. The old people of the Weirs read it diagnostically, the way other old people discuss the weather in their joints, and children fall asleep against the warm stone in the winter shadow-mornings, and Weir-folk who travel out into the steppe report the same complaint with the same wording, generation after generation: *it was quiet, like something had died.*

Here and there along the flank, external stairs survive — narrow maintenance runs from some earlier age of the Wall, climbing a few hundred feet and ending, every one of them, at masonry: at doors sealed flush and weathered blind. The Stairs to Nowhere. The young of the Weirs go up them in the evenings, in pairs, and sit at the top with their backs against the sealed doors and the thrum coming through the iron into their spines, looking out over the combed country going gold, working out between them what everyone here must work out — that they live at the foot of an impossible thing, and that the impossible thing is *generous*, reliably, three days after every glow, and that generosity on that scale from a source that cannot be questioned is the whole shape of their world, and holds.

# The Weir-folk

A Vantler is born onto floors. A Weir-person is born onto order — into a position in the cascade, and they know it from infancy the way other children know which way is down. Ask one where their family comes from and they will name a weir first — "we're third-weir people, silt side" — and only then a lane or a house, and the order of the answers tells you how a Weir mind holds the world: not as a map, not as a core sample, but as a *sequence*. Everything is upstream of something and downstream of something else, including people, including days, including the dead.

## Their Bodies

They are divers and sifters. Generations of retrieving sunken falls from the deep catch-pools have bred a people who take to warm water like the pale fish: long-winded, deliberate, able to go down into the green dark of the first pool and stay while a slow count runs out on the bank. Their hands are wide and their fingertips famously discerning — a silt-side child can plunge an arm into a settling bed and tell you by feel what the mountain sent that week. They weather on one side, half-jokingly: the Weirs live in shadow until the Clearing, the hour before noon when the sun finally crosses the parapet miles above and the whole town steps at once out of the mountain's morning, and their days are built around that line — cool shadow-work at the weirs before it, market and field-work in the hard light after.

## Their Work

A Weir-person does not ask what you do. They ask what you *catch* — or, among the settling families, what you *hold*, and the eternal genial dispute between those two verbs is the town's politics entire. The catch-weirs get the falls and the glory of them; the settling-weirs get the silt and the fields and the quieter, deeper wealth; and the argument over whether the mountain's true gift is what it sends whole or what it sends ground fine has been conducted on the pond-banks for centuries without resolution or any real desire for one.

And then there are the drain-walkers.

It began, as everything here begins, practically: when a drain clogs, the Weirs dry, and someone must go up into the Wall and clear it. The walkers are a hereditary line, and over the generations their clearings have taken them higher and deeper than any clog required — up the going-water, into strata of galleries and channels and vaulted rooms lit by their own carried lamps, following the warm flow toward wherever it is warm *from*. They have rules older than memory: never take, always leave light. At every major turning they keep a lamp burning, oil renewed on each ascent, out of courtesy — that is the word they use — *out of courtesy to whoever keeps the water coming.* And they will tell you, evenly, as men and women reporting a fact of their trade, that some of the lamps they tend are lamps no walker remembers placing. That certain deep galleries have the air of housekeeping. That once, three generations ago, a walker left a measure of salt on a high ledge as an offering, and the next walker up found the salt gone and, in its place, a small pruning knife of heavy bronze, guild-marked in no script of the plain, made with a patience you could feel in the handle.

The knife is in the Fall-house. They call it the Answer. What the question was, exactly, is discussed below.

## Their Speech and Their Beliefs

In the language of the Weirs, the word for "up" and the word for "from" are related, and every Weir-person feels the rightness of this in their bones: the source is not behind them or beneath them, it is *overhead*, unreachable, generous, and everything that exists came down. Time itself runs downhill here — the past is upstream, the future is down toward the tail-ponds and the steppe — and their word for fate translates, precisely, as *one's fall*. When something cannot be explained — the pale fish, the blue crabs, the lamps no one placed — a Weir-person files it under *it came down*, with a shrug, and feels no itch. This is not incuriosity. It is a people who live downstream of their own unexcavated mystery and have made peace with drinking from questions.

Their great dispute, conducted nightly on the pond-banks for centuries, is the summit. The Peopled hold that someone lives up there — that the water is sent, the glow is lamplight, the knife was a *reply* — and that this matters. The larger faction, the Alone, hold that the makers of the mountain are long gone and the mountain simply runs, an ancestor-machine keeping its own habits, and that this is both more frightening and more comforting, and Weir-folk savor positions that are both. There is a third faction, small and serene, which holds that the mountain is neither inhabited nor abandoned but *alive* — that the thrum is its heart, the water its sweat, the ember-glow its dreaming, the falls its molt — and that the only sensible relation to it is the one the town already has: gratitude, attention, and courtesy. They are considered eccentric, and are, in a manner of speaking, also right.

## Their Lives

A child's first formal rite is the walk-in: taken by night a hundred paces into the lowest drain-mouth, to stand in the warm dark with one hand on the wet stone, the thrum in their teeth, and see, at the first turning, a lamp burning that no one of their town lit that season — and understand, and they do understand, every one of them, you can see it land in their faces, that the water they have drunk all their lives is a *kept* thing, kept by someone or something in the dark overhead, and that courtesy is therefore not a manner but a debt.

At the end, a Weir-person settles out. The dead are given to the silt-houses of the field families, and in a year they are the black earth of the Weirs, spread on fields already built, layer over layer, from everything the water has ever carried down — and the oldest field families, who know the taste of their silt better than anyone, will say quietly that the black blessing has always had a lineage in it, that the mountain does not send them mud, it sends them *its own*, ground fine by the long dark descent. They say this without knowing how exactly right they are. Everyone here is fed by everyone, and by everyone above. The phrase they use, when a death is mentioned, is the phrase they use for all things finished with falling: *they've settled out.*

## The Courtesy

Somewhere in the middle of the mountain — above the highest turning the walkers have named, below the deepest plaza the summit's own antiquarians have mapped by lamplight — the warm water runs through galleries that belong to no one's records, and lamps burn there that both peoples tend and neither placed. The delvers of the high city find them and leave them lit, out of courtesy. The walkers of the low town find them and fill them, out of courtesy. Each people has made of the other its load-bearing question — *is there ground down there; is there anyone up there* — and each, without knowing it, keeps the other's question in good condition, oiled and burning, the way you keep anything you cannot finish and do not want to.

They have never met. Perhaps they never will; the mountain is very large, and both peoples are patient in their different ways, the one keeping, the other catching, the thrum passing through the whole warm mass between them like a pulse through a shared body. But on clouded nights the Weirs look up and see the golden ember of the summit dreaming, and on clear evenings the summit looks down and sees, if anyone thinks to look, a green thread and a scatter of small lights stitched to its own far hem — each the other's rumor, each the other's proof — and in the dark between them the lamps go on burning, and neither city can any longer say, and neither any longer needs to, whose light it is that guides the other home.
