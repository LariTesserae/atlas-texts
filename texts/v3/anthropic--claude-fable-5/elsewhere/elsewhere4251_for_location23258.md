# The Standing Rain

The wind that crosses the dry slope above the Comb Hollow does not stop there. It goes in through a thousand tuned throats and comes out the far flues carrying the chord with it, down the long grade of the country, losing the tone mile by mile into grass-hiss and distance until nothing of it survives but wind again. Follow that wind far enough — half a day's walking, down off the chalk and into greener land — and you will hear where it goes to be silenced.

You find this place the way you find anything loud: from far off, by surrendering to it. First a whisper under the horizon that you mistake for weather. Then a seam of white in the land ahead, where the country cracks open into a gorge. Then the roar, rising to meet you long before the edge, until by the time you stand at the lip you have stopped noticing that you can no longer hear your own feet.

---

**The water.** The river is born of the chalk itself — the rain that dry country refuses to keep, sunk through the hill and traveling dark for miles, surfacing at last in a line of cold springs at the foot of the scarp. Even the water here is kin to the hollow: it is everything that place pulled out of itself and sent away. The river gathers it, runs green and quick down a shallow valley, and then meets a lip of harder stone and goes over — sixty feet in one white unbroken sheet, into a churning pool the color of bottle glass.

Above the pool the air is full of rain that has never been in a cloud. It rises off the impact in a permanent standing haze, drifts, hangs, beads on every surface, and never entirely falls. That is the name of the place. Within a stone's throw of the falls there is a climate that belongs to nowhere else: everything soaked, everything dripping, moss on moss, ferns growing out of ferns, a green so saturated it looks lit from inside. Step into the spray zone and you are wet through in a minute, and the wetness is not an event, the way rain is. It is a condition, like the drone was a condition. You simply become a resident of it.

**The noise.** Where the hollow holds one chord — one selection out of all possible sound, sustained forever — the falls holds everything at once, which is the same as holding nothing. The roar is white: every frequency present, none of them standing, no pitch surviving longer than the instant of its birth. You can shout into it and not hear yourself. You can stand beside another person and be perfectly alone. The hollow's sound stopped registering as noise and became a property of the space; the falls' sound never stops registering as anything — it presses, it occupies, it scrubs the inside of the skull. One is a held thought. The other is thought's erasure.

And this, note it, is where the chord comes to die. The dry wind off the chalk funnels down the gorge and over the falls, and whatever ghost of the hollow's voice it still carries goes into the roar and is unheard. Of all the places in the world that wind could end, it ends here, in the one sound wide enough to swallow it whole.

**The comb.** Behind the falls and along the spray-shadowed cliffs beside it — you must climb down the gorge wall, on steps of wet moss, to see it — hangs the city.

If you have laid your palm on the pale pillars of the Comb Hollow, you will know this hand. The same substance: paper-hard in intention, laid in lamellae thin as fingernails, worked without tools, every surface carrying the print of overlapping crescent strokes, the texture of a thing smoothed by mouths. The same hand — but nothing here has been permitted to finish. The comb of the falls is gray-brown and soft, kept forever at the dampness of dough by the standing rain. It does not stand in columns. It hangs: curtains, ropes, festoons, sagging galleries slung from the cliff face and from each other, swaying very slightly in the concussion of the water, dripping from every low point. Passages that were open this morning are sealed by evening; walls that stood at dusk have been eaten to lace by dawn and are being relaid even as you watch. The city is never the same city twice. It is a shape held in place the way a fountain is: by continuous replacement.

**The light.** Through the falling sheet, when you stand behind it, daylight arrives green-white and trembling, never for two heartbeats the same — shattered, reassembled, strobing with the pulse of the water. Out in the spray, at certain hours, rainbows stand in the haze, drift, dissolve. And inside the comb itself there is darkness: real shadow, everywhere, moving, folding, the first honest dark you will have met if you came here from the hollow. The hollow's mercy was that light came from all directions at once and nothing could hide. The mercy here is the opposite. Everything can hide. Everything does.

**The air.** Saturated past saturation — cold as the pool in the open spray, and then, close against the hanging comb, suddenly warm, warm as breath, warmed by the metabolic heat of the millions inside. Your own breath is invisible here; the air has no room for it. Everything smells of water and crushed green, and under that, once you know to look for it, a sweetish smell of paper and old honey — the same smell as the hollow, exactly the same, but wet, the way a word is the same word spoken instead of written.

**The life.** It is everywhere and it is mostly them. The builders: small, the size of a thumb-joint, gray-mailed, many-legged, many-mouthed, pouring along the festoons in files and sheets and sudden migrations, tens of millions strong. They carry paste. They carry each other. They carry pieces of their own walls, freshly eaten, to be laid down elsewhere still warm from the mouth. Around and beneath them lives everything that can live off such abundance — moss fattened on their wash, swifts stitching the spray for the winged castes, pale fish in the pool below grown sleek on what the city sheds. And up on the gorge lip, fed by the drifting haze, the richest grass in the country stands heavy with seed. Remember the grass. It matters later.

**The unfinishing in time.** Nothing in the city is old. The oldest cell is perhaps a season; the oldest wall, a year; the oldest body, less. And yet the colony has hung at these falls since before the hollow fell silent — longer than the chord has been sounding. The falls itself retreats, as falls do, eating its own lip an inch a decade, and the city walks with it: on the downstream cliffs, for a mile below the present curtain, you can find the anchor-scars of comb that hung there centuries ago, washed smooth, mossed over, abandoned rung by rung as the water backed upstream. The place is ancient. Its contents are this morning's. The hollow finished happening long ago and will never happen again; the Standing Rain has finished nothing, ever, and never will.

# What Lives in the Standing Rain

**What they were.** They were the ones who left.

Same kind, same paste, same crescent hand — and once, same colony. When the builders in the chalk began their last great labor, when the first generation was rendered and smoothed and laid into the walls, when the paste in that dry hollow was first allowed to cure and the innermost pillars began, faintly, to glow and to hum — a portion of the colony rose off the comb and flew downwind and did not come back. Whether they fled or were sent, no memory reaches. Perhaps they were the dissent, the half of the swarm-mind that recoiled from the drying. Perhaps they were the mother's deliberate last swarm — everything mobile, hungry, and unfinished in itself, exhaled on purpose before the seal, the way a person setting their affairs in permanent order might first send the children out of the house. Both stories are one story now. Their kind of mind is passed mouth to mouth in wet paste, and it keeps nothing that long. What survived the centuries is not the memory of the choice. It is the rule the choice became.

**What they are.** Many, and determined to remain many. The same mind, in kind, that once ran the hollow's builders — a mind held between bodies rather than inside any of them — except that where the mother translated herself into tone and stopped, the daughters kept the mind in the flesh and pay the flesh's price for it daily: mortality, forgetting, hunger, and labor without end. The comb must be eaten and relaid forever, or it sets. The mind must be passed forever, or it dies with its bodies. Nothing about them accumulates. Everything about them is expenditure. They are, deliberately, the opposite bank of the family fortune.

**The rule.** *No note may hold.* It is the deepest law in them, below food, below defense. Any passage in the comb that begins to whistle in a draft is mobbed within the hour and chewed into a different throat. Any patch of comb that dries — under a sheltered overhang, in a drought summer — begins to pale, and then, thinly, to glow; and they eat it first, before anything, with the urgency other creatures reserve for fire. They know what a glowing wall is the first stone of. They chose the falls because it is the one place in the country where the rule enforces itself: the roar fills every frequency so that no resonance can stand, and the standing rain keeps every wall soft so that no shape can set. They live inside noise as inside armor. The hollow is at peace by discipline, its dissonances sealed behind pale doors. The daughters are alive by discipline, their one great consonance held forever at bay.

**The listening.** But there are drought years. The river thins, the sheet narrows, the roar drops from white to gray — and on those days, if the hard dry wind is coming down the gorge off the chalk, the chord arrives. Faint. Under everything. A ghost of gold at the bottom of the noise.

And the city stops.

All of it. Tens of millions of bodies, motionless on the wet gray comb, the files frozen mid-carry, the paste going cold in the mouths, for as long as the tone holds — a minute, an hour. No visitor can read what passes through the swarm-mind then: recognition, grief, homage, temptation; the sound of everything they refused to become, and the sound of everyone they were. When the weather breaks and the roar closes back over the tone, they move again, and by the chemistry of their kind the event is gone from them by nightfall — all of it but the stopping itself, which is instinct now, older than any body in the city, a reflex of reverence with no memory left inside it. They no longer know why they stand still. They stand still anyway. It is the shape grief keeps after it has outlived its own story.

**How they perceive you.** In the hollow you were noticed slowly, totally, and forever. Here you are noticed instantly, totally, and briefly. Within a minute of entering the spray you are found, tasted, walked over, assayed by ten thousand mouths, resolved — not food, not threat, not weather, not comb — and released. By morning no trace of you remains anywhere in the colony's chemistry. The hollow is keeping your hour behind a sealed door with everything it has decided never to lose. The city cannot keep you overnight. Between them, long ago, they divided remembering the way they divided everything else: one took all of it, the other took none — almost none.

**The one kept thing.** Because they do keep something. A single permanence in a culture of pure expenditure, held not in wax but in behavior. When the colony casts its swarms — and it casts them constantly, downriver, cross-valley, out into the green country in every direction — no swarm is ever cast upwind. No scout, in living or unliving memory, has flown up the gorge past a certain bend. The direction of the chalk is not marked, not signaled, not taught. It is simply never done, generation after forgetful generation, a door sealed in the flight-instinct itself. The hollow has its walled rooms it visits never. The daughters have their one unflown bearing. Each of them, in the end, keeps exactly one thing under seal — and it is the same thing.

**What passes between them anyway.** Twice a year the weather turns, and for a few days the wind runs backward, up the gorge, up the long grade toward the chalk. It lifts seed off the heavy grass at the gorge lip — grass fed on their spray, on the dust of their eaten walls, on the endless wash of the city — and carries it up onto the dry slope, and some few grains of it go down through a seam in the earth no wider than a doorway, into shadowless gold and perfect stillness, where something very large and very slow permits one of them, perhaps, to stand.

The blades in the Comb Hollow — the notation, the slow sentence being written one letter a decade — are written in seeds of the daughters' country. The hollow does not know whose grass it composes with. The city does not know that its castoff wash is being made, far upwind, into the only words its ancestor still writes. Neither will ever know. But the wind knows, if wind can be said to know anything: the same wind, the only traveler in the world that stands in both places in the same day, carrying seed up and song down, patient as a mouth.

Two halves of one old mind: the half that kept everything and stopped, and the half that kept nothing and never will. And between them, on the wind, a conversation neither admits to having — in which nothing is ever said, and nothing, on either side, is ever quite let go.
