**The Sough**

Follow the throb backward. It leaves the hollow through the bedrock the way a voice leaves a body — without diminishing the body, without asking — and it runs north-by-northeast under the heath for six miles, under the frost line, under the roots of the fog, through limestone that has carried it so long the stone would not know itself silent. Six miles of that, and then the flatlands stop being flat by accident and start being flat on purpose: ditched, drained, ruled into fields, a straight road, a straight leat, a town in the distance sitting an arm's length below where water used to stand. And at the center of all that dryness, in a shallow scoop of ground it has made for itself over the generations — soot-dark, hunched, nodding — the works.

It is called the Sough, after the tunnel and not the buildings, though the buildings are what a stranger would see first. The word is old and means two things, and the place has earned both: a drainage adit driven through rock, and the sound of a long exhalation. The miners who named it meant the first. The land around it has come to mean the second.

From the road it is a low black assembly of brick and iron: an engine house with a slotted wall through which the beam of the great pump nods, a longer hall behind it holding the electric sets, a chimney no longer used but never taken down, a scatter of sheds, a leat running away brimful and fast, and a stand of settling lagoons lying flat and pewter-colored under the same gray sky the hollow sees through its crack. Everything is filmed. That is the first strange thing about it, if you have come from the hollow's white fractures: nothing here is raw. The brick is black with a century of grease-breath, the iron is worn silver where hands go and thick with paint where they don't, the stone thresholds are dished by boots. The hollow is a young room in old rock. The Sough is an old room in the open air, and the two of them have their ages exactly crossed.

Under the engine house is the Eye — the shaft, twelve feet across, dropping past the reach of any lamp into the drowned galleries of a mine that has been dead for four generations. The ore gave out. The miners left. But the mine had been driven below the water table into ground that wanted to be wet, and the drained flats above it — the fields, the road, the town — had by then been built on the mine's dryness the way a house is built on a promise. So the pumping could not die with the digging. That is the whole biography of the place: the labor outliving its reason, the debt outliving the debtor. The works no longer takes anything out of the ground except water, and it takes that forever.

Now stand inside the engine house and put your hand on the wall.

You will not hear the works, not really — you are inside it, and being inside a sound is not the same as hearing it. What you will feel is the Bull. That is the great beam pump, the oldest set, a machine the size of a chapel, and it makes one stroke every four seconds: a lift, a knock, a fall, a rest. The knock is the hollow's piston-beat, six miles away and a decade running; here at its origin it is not a sound but a weather, arriving through the floor, the handrails, the teeth. Behind it, in the long hall, the electric sets stand in a row — the keepers call them the choir — and they come on and off with the demand of the water: more in thaw, more in rain, fewer in the small hours, and each time one starts or stops, a high harmonic shiver runs out through the bedrock like a change of key. At night the load falls and the works steps down into a lower register, and somewhere far to the south-southwest a cold green room, which no one here has ever seen, steps down with it.

Twice a day, at the turn of morning and the turn of evening, everything stops.

These are the soundings. The gauges that tell the truth about the water can only tell it in still water, so the Bull is eased off, the choir is shut down set by set, and for a handful of minutes the Sough does the one thing it is built never to do. The silence is not restful. It is a held breath with a cost accruing inside it. In the gauge shafts you can hear the water rising — actually hear it, a soft climbing applause of drips and slaps, the deep old water taking back its inches — and the keepers stand over the glass tubes and read the levels and write them down quickly, because every minute of stillness is a minute the works will spend the rest of the day repaying. Then the Bull takes up its stroke, the choir comes in, and the register returns.

Six miles away, in those same minutes, several hundred frogs lose their timing and stutter into ragged noise, and a stoat comes down a broken slope. Neither place knows this about the other. The hollow knows the Sough only as a sound with no source. The Sough knows the hollow only as a number with no cause — but that comes later, in the ledger. The two of them share a silence twice a day, and one of them fears it as a predator's hour and the other fears it as a debt, and the fear is the closest thing they have to correspondence.

**Who Lives in the Sough**

Start with the three who are counted on a payroll somewhere, because they would insist on being counted last.

---

**The wright, who hears by bone.**

He has been at the works longer than any machine but the Bull, and the works has taken his hearing the way the sea takes a coastline — not all of it, just the useful parts. Speech reaches him as weather now, a distant surf he answers with nods. But the frequencies of the works, the deep registers below and beneath the voice, those he receives with a fidelity no younger ear can match, because he receives them the way the hollow does: through rock and frame and sternum. He walks the halls with one hand trailing on the iron, and he can name a dry bearing two days before it cries out loud, feel a valve losing its seat, tell from the texture of the four-second rest whether the Bull's stroke is drawing true. He has become the works' fine sense of itself. Six miles away an old scar-flanked female frog sits by a weeping seam and hunts by the rock's voice, and the wright is her opposite number and exact kin: two elders, each grown into an instrument of the same engine, neither aware the other exists, both listening to the identical throb for the small tremors that mean life.

He sleeps in the cottage nearest the engine house, and he does not sleep through the soundings. None of them do. Whatever clock the body keeps, theirs are wound to the machine: at the turn of morning and the turn of evening, keepers off-shift wake in the silence with their hearts already going, and lie still, and count, and do not settle again until the Bull's first knock comes back through the floorboards. The hollow-born frogs were made of the rhythm before they hatched. The keepers were made of it more slowly, but no less thoroughly. They are the works' other chorus — the one that sings its relief silently, in pulse rate, when the rest ends.

---

**The gauger, who keeps the other memory.**

She has the ledgers. They run back a century in a cupboard that smells of paraffin — daily soundings, rainfall, sets run, coal then current, columns of water levels in a dozen observation bores scattered across the flats and up onto the heath, each bore a thin iron straw down into the dark, each a place where the works takes the land's pulse. She inherited the hand she writes in; the entries of four gaugers are distinguishable only by the slant of the fours.

And in the ledger for a year now a decade gone, in the column for the bores to the south-southwest, there is the anomaly. Overnight, between one sounding and the next, the level in the heathward bores stepped down — a small step, a foot and a half, but sudden, stepped, the signature not of weather but of *structure*: somewhere out that way the underground had rearranged itself, some sealed room of the rock had opened and taken its share of the water table into a new shape. No pump had changed. No rain explained it. The gauger of that day — her predecessor — wrote the numbers, wrote *unexplained*, and set a small penciled question mark in the margin, and the wright, who was younger then, stood over the page and said, "Ground's opened somewhere out there," and nobody walked the six miles to look, because the sounding pause was ending and the Bull needed steam.

That question mark is the hollow's birth certificate. It is the only document of the collapse in the world, the only witness besides the elder frogs, and it lives in a cupboard, unread, exactly as the memory of the sky lives in a handful of aging amphibians on the highest shelf of a pool margin. Two archives of one event, six miles apart, kept in ink and in flesh, and closing on the same fate: the ledgers will go to a records office and the elders will go into the stoat, and the opening of the ground will be remembered by no one, in two different ways.

---

**The greaser, who is young enough to still hear the loudness.**

The third keeper is a decade into the work and has not yet stopped hearing the works, which the older two regard as a phase, like homesickness. He does the rounds — the oil, the packing, the strainer baskets on the rising main. The baskets are where the deep water's passengers fetch up, and this is the second strange thing about the Sough: the works is inhabited all the way down, and it pumps its inhabitants.

The water the Bull lifts is old. It fell as rain before the fields existed, before the mine, some of it before the heath; it has spent ten thousand years in the flooded seams under everything, and it did not spend them empty. Blind white amphipods live in it — the same pale indigenous nation that keeps the deepest fold of the hollow's pool, the ones for whom the collapse was an invasion of sky. Their country is the whole dark aquifer, and the Sough has been driven into the middle of it like a well into a sea, and for a century the pumps have been lifting citizens of that country up through the impellers into daylight. Most do not survive the lift. They fetch up in the strainer baskets, pale commas in the mesh, and the greaser cleans the baskets on his rounds and tips them out for the hens by the cottage, and the hens are quick and grateful, and that is how the first people of the underworld mostly end: unwitnessed, in a chicken yard, six miles from their cousins who were revealed instead of raised.

Mostly. Not entirely. Which brings the census outdoors.

---

**The diaspora in the lagoons.**

The lifted water runs out through the sough tunnel proper — the old gravity adit, brick-arched, breathing its long cold exhalation into the leat — and across the settling lagoons before it is released to the river. The lagoons are open sky and death to anything blind and white. But their culverts are not. In the covered channels between the ponds, in the perpetual dark under the sluice decks, a remnant of the pumped-up nation has taken hold: amphipods that came through the lift alive, found the light, fled it, and founded a colony in the plumbing. They comb silt there exactly as their kin comb it in the hollow's pool and in the undrained seams below, three settlements of one people now — the deep country, the revealed pool, and this, the exile colony living in the works' own gutters, sustained by the very machine that deports them. They pay it no attention. They predate it and they will outlast it, and indifference on that scale is indistinguishable from patience.

Eels come up the leat against the current in autumn and lie all winter in the warm tailrace where the water leaves the pumps a degree kinder than the river. A heron owns the outfall, and she has done what the stoat did: learned the machine. When a set of the choir shuts down, the flow at the outfall slumps and the shallows over the weir go suddenly thin, and whatever was swimming there is presented. She stands through the shift changes like a gray official, taking her tax. She and the stoat have never met and never will — a heron of the flats, a stoat of the heath — but they keep the same calendar, issued by the same engine, and they are the only two creatures in the world who have read it for what it is: a schedule.

Pigeons hold the beam loft above the Bull, and their colony's low talk has settled, over pigeon generations, into the gaps of the four-second stroke — they mutter in the rests, exactly as the frogs sing in them, two congregations six miles apart filling the same silence without knowing it is shared. And in the long hall the spiders have mapped the standing wave in silk: webs survive only where the steel does not sing, so the roof trusses carry a punctuated script of web and bare iron, node and antinode, the same score the moss writes in emerald on the hollow's walls. The works publishes its one sentence in every medium offered to it. Moss, silk, birdsong, frogsong, ledger ink, the wright's bones. It has never once known it was being transcribed.

---

**And then the largest resident, which has no voice.**

In the hollow, the senior occupant is the sound. Here at the sound's own source, the sound is only labor — the noise of the real senior occupant being held at bay. The water is the oldest thing on the property and the only one with a claim. It was here before the sough was driven; it stood over these fields; it filled the heath's shallow pools, the ones the elder frogs remember, the ones that froze to the bottom — because the Sough drank those too, in the end, lowered them year by year until the heath went dry-eyed and its frogs went looking for wetness and some of them found a new slot in the ground where the fog drained downward. The works, in one decade's deep pull, opened the hollow and then stocked it. It made the room and supplied the tenants and has conducted their chorus ever since, and it has done all three in perfect ignorance, the way a heart ignorant of the body builds the body anyway.

The keepers do not say the water is winning, because it isn't, today. They say what the wright says, with his hand on the iron in the four-second rest, waiting for the knock that must come: *it's only ever borrowed.* Every stroke of the Bull borrows four more seconds of dry land. The interval between knocks — the same little room of quiet the frogs have crowded their whole song into, the same rest the pigeons mutter through and the keepers' hearts count through — is, from this side, the water's turn. Knock: ours. Rest: hers. Knock: ours. The two places live inside the identical rhythm and have divided it exactly oppositely, one filling the sound's absence with music, the other filling it with vigilance, and between them the beat goes out through six miles of limestone carrying both meanings at once and belonging to neither.

---

**Last: who lives here when it stops.**

Someday the debt is called. The town shrinks past keeping, or the money does, or the Bull cracks its great beam on a cold morning and the choir cannot hold the flow alone — the ending hardly matters, because every version ends the same way: the soundings' silence arrives one dusk and does not lift.

Then the water comes home at the pace of a tired man climbing stairs. It rises up the dead galleries level by level, finds the Eye, stands in it, tops it. The leat runs backward and then not at all. The lagoons join hands. The fields remember what they are, the ditches fill from below, and the flatlands go back to lying wet under the gray sky as they did before anyone owed anyone anything. The exile colony in the culverts is quietly reabsorbed into its own risen nation, the deportations over, the whole dark country one country again.

And six miles to the south-southwest, unwatched, the water table climbs back through the seams it left a decade before the frogs can remember, and the black pool at the bottom of a cold green room begins, drop by drop and then seam by seam, to deepen. The moss bands drown in order, the score erased line by line from the bottom up. The rowan gets its feet wet and then its knees. The swallows keep the high fractures longest. The hollow-born frogs, still calling the four-second cycle into a silence that no longer answers, climb the broken slope generation by generation ahead of the rising black, carrying the rhythm of a stopped engine up into the wind — and behind them the pool widens into a lake into a drowned room into a flooded seam, dark, lightless, trembling with nothing, and the blind white first people come drifting back through it into the reclaimed heart of their oldest province, combing the silt exactly as before.

The last resident of the Sough is the water, which was never a resident at all. It was the landlord, out of the house for a century, letting the rooms to pumps and pigeons and patient men. It forgets nothing when it returns, because it never learned anything — it only waited, four seconds at a time, in the rests of a song two choruses were singing, six miles apart, about it.
