The Works

Follow the roar out. Push back through the soft seam in the living wall — it closes behind you with its held-breath sound — and walk toward the noise, and the noise begins to take itself apart. What the moss had soaked into thunder regains its edges as you go: the geological roar resolves into a rotary drone, the drone into a churning, the slow concussion into something with a stroke and a return, and the periodic shriek stops being mysterious and becomes what it always was, which is the flanged wheels of coal wagons taking the curve of a rail siding. A quarter mile of unremarkable ground — bramble, cindered path, thin birches that belong to no one — and there it stands, and it is exactly one thing.

It is a pumping station.

It announces itself the way the hollow refuses to: a stack first, brick banded in two colors, visible for miles and streaming a soft grey pennant of smoke; then the boiler house crouched at its foot, breathing; then the yard, black with a hundred years of coal dust ground into cinders, where the siding comes in off the branch line and the wagons wait in a patient string. There is a crane. There is a water tower on iron legs. There is a wall around all of it, brick, with a gate, and a name and a date set into the gatepost in a stone lozenge, because everything here was built by people who could not make anything without signing it. The hollow keeps no records; the works is nothing but records. Every brick is a date. Every rivet is a decision someone made on a particular Tuesday.

And at the center of it, the hall.

It was built like a chapel because its builders could not help themselves — whoever put an engine in a room in that century could not stop the room becoming a church. Round-arched windows in tall ranks, a roof like a nave, doors you could process through. Inside, the inversion of the hollow is so exact it feels intentional, though nothing intended it: where the grove holds all its mass at the edges and keeps its center empty, the hall holds nothing at the edges — glazed white brick, wiped clean, a painted iron gallery, tall churchly panes of the same strained daylight — and puts everything in the middle. One enormous thing lives in the center of that room, and it is the engine.

It is the heart the hollow feels through its feet. Seen up close it is almost too slow to frighten: the great beam overhead rising and falling with the deliberation of something that has decided haste is beneath it, the flywheel — taller than three men — turning with a majesty that makes the eye keep checking whether it is turning at all, the piston sliding in its bath of oil, the whole assembly green-painted and brass-trimmed and polished the way the moss is deep: by accumulation, by decades of the same hand doing the same small thing. Eleven strokes a minute. It has done eleven strokes a minute since before anyone working here was born. Inside the hall the roar is not a roar; it is articulate — hiss, thump, sigh, the click of the valve gear like consonants — and it is warm, always, the warmth of a body, where the hollow is always cool. The two places have divided the temperatures of the world between them and each keeps its share.

What the engine does — what all of this is for, the stack and the coal and the chapel and the slow heart — is lift water. Behind the hall stands the well house, a smaller, darker building over a shaft that goes down through clay, through more clay, and into gravel. And the gravel is the thing, because the gravel is a riverbed.

The river did not go elsewhere. That is the works' secret, held in its boreholes, printed in the survey sections framed in the office where no one looks at them. Rivers that give up do not leave; they go down. Ten thousand years ago the river sank into its own gravels and has been flowing there ever since — the same water, the same direction, a river with no sky, sliding through the dark under the whole district on the buried bed it made when it still believed in daylight. The hollow sits in one abandoned reach of that bed; its permanent damp, the cellar-cool, the rain that never fully leaves, is the buried river sweating upward through ten thousand years of moss. And a quarter mile away the works has driven its wells down into the same gravel and hauls the buried river back into the light, eleven strokes a minute, tons at a time, and sends it away in iron mains to a town over the horizon that drinks it without ever having heard of any of this.

So the two places trade, and neither knows. Overland, the works sends the hollow its roar, and the grove takes it for wind. Underground, the land sends the works the hollow's water — the same water that beads on the underside of the crossing limb, gathered drop by patient drop, is here flung up the rising main by the thousand gallons. One river, two offices. At one end it waters a toad, twice a day, a drop at a time. At the other end it waters a town.

Begin with the engine, because everything here answers to it — but the engine, like the grove, has tenants.

Swallows own the roof trusses, generations of them, mudded nests in the same iron angles their great-grandparents used; they stitch through the tall windows all summer and the engine's rhythm does not trouble them because they, like the wrens, have never heard anything else. Pigeons hold the boiler-house eaves. Mice run the coal store the way voles run the moss-felt, corridors alongside the buried structure. And sometimes — not often — a wren appears on the yard fence, small and furious, one of the hollow's own dynasty come to the source of the weather, and detonates its foundry-built song at the works itself; and here, at close range, in the one place on earth its lineage was bred against, even that song fails, and the wren sings anyway, redundantly, defiantly, into the roar, because song is what you do to air.

But at the scale of eyes, in the whole thundering center of the works, the answer is one.

The keeper lives in the hall.

He is settled the way old toads are settled — not heavy but arrived at, as though gravity had been working on him personally through forty years of night shifts and he had decided to cooperate. He has a chair by the governor. He has a kettle, filled from a tap teed off the rising main, so that his tea is brewed from the buried river, cold-drawn and faintly mineral, tasting of somewhere old — he and the toad drink from the same water, one by the kettle, one by the drop, and neither knows the other exists. He has an oil-can with a long spout, and rounds he walks on a schedule you could set time by, if time mattered here; it does, it is the only place in the district where it does; the works runs on it. He lays the back of his hand on bearings. He wipes brass that does not need wiping. Mostly, he listens.

That is his entire office, distilled: he is the works' audience. The engine performs, ceaselessly, redundantly, eleven strokes a minute, and the keeper sits at the center of the noise the way the toad sits at the center of the silence, facing everything, and listens *through* the roar rather than to it. In an ordinary quiet room he is nearly deaf — conversation slides past him, he watches mouths — but he can hear a bearing beginning to run dry from across the hall, under the full voice of the engine, the way a mother hears her own child in a crowd. The roar is not sound to him. The roar is what air sounds like. Sound is the deviation.

Whether it is one keeper is not a question the works likes to settle. There are wage books in the office that could settle it; nobody consults them. There have been three keepers, or four, since anyone counted, each one trained by the last, each one taking the same chair, the same rounds, the same tea, until the town outside — which knows the works only as its water and its smoke — speaks of "the keeper" the way you would speak of the toad, as an office the place fills from within, a succession invisible because every member is identical in posture. Perhaps it is a dynasty. Perhaps it is, absurdly, patiently, the same man. The engine keeps no opinion. The strokes come on schedule either way.

Once a year, the engine stops.

Boiler inspection: the fires drawn, the steam bled off, the beam allowed to finish one last stroke and settle onto its blocks with a sound like a building sitting down. And then the silence arrives, and it arrives as violence — a pressure, a blow, a thing with weight; the keeper's ears ring with the ghost of the engine for hours, eleven phantom strokes a minute, his body refusing to believe the heart has stopped. He does what he does every year. He walks out into the yard, into the black cinders, and stands, and waits.

And out of the dark clot of trees beyond the siding — an irregular blank on the station's plans, unlabeled, the one shape on the survey the surveyors simply went around — the singing comes.

It has been coming all along; that is the thing he half understands, standing there. The wrens sing every hour of every day, pitched to clear a foundry, and for three hundred and sixty-four days the works drowns its own reply-mail unread. But on this one day the roar is gone and the songs arrive at full foundry pitch into dead air, and they are staggering — detonations, volleys, the loudest birds in the world making up, he thinks of it, for lost time — and under them the drum of something hammering on iron-dark wood, and pigeons grinding their five notes, a whole hidden arena in full voice a quarter mile away, answering a wind that has briefly stopped. It is the only sound the works ever receives in return for its roar. The keeper stands in the yard and takes delivery of it, once a year, on behalf of the whole establishment. He has never gone into the trees. The soft seam in the living wall is not on his side of anything, and he knows it without knowing how he knows.

Then the boilers pass, and the fires are laid, and the beam lifts off its blocks, and the roar closes back over the district like water over a stone.

It will sound exactly like this tomorrow. The works believes in tomorrow the way engines do — one stroke at a time, coal willing, iron willing, forever meaning until further notice. The hollow believes in it the way moss does, which is a different and longer belief, and the grove has heard winds before, and heard them stop. Between the two beliefs, underneath both of them, the river slides on through its dark gravels, drop to one and torrent to the other, a thing that already gave up once, ten thousand years ago, and found that giving up and continuing are not opposites. It carries the one place to the other and keeps its own counsel.

It was flowing in your grandmother's day. Same bed. Same direction. It will be flowing tomorrow — under the moss, under the cinders, under whichever of its two offices outlasts the other — the sky long since forgotten, the water not needing it.
