**The Given Country**

Begin with the water, because everything here is older than the water has any right to be touching.

The country is old. Not old in the ceremonial sense, where "old" means a few famous ruins — old in the sense of being entirely made, every hectare of it, by people nobody can name anymore. The soil lies twelve meters deep in the vales, black and worked and reworked until it has no memory of ever being rock. Every stone is rounded. Every stream was straightened once, centuries ago, and has been allowed to go crooked again, and the crookedness itself is old now. There are field walls whose builders are four hundred years past being anyone's grandmother, hedgerows planted by men whose names survive only as the names of the hedgerows, church towers whose bells have been recast three times from their own bronze. The rivers finished their arguments with the land millennia ago and settled into fat brown agreements. When you walk here, the ground gives slightly, the way a book gives when it has been opened many times.

And it is going down.

Not catastrophically. A few centimeters a year, evenly, without a single tremor — the sea arriving not as waves but as arithmetic. The tide comes a hand's width further up the sluice gates than it did in your childhood. A field your father mowed is samphire now. The country is settling the way a laden ship settles, deliberately, on an even keel, and the reason stands at intervals of forty or fifty kilometers across the lowlands: pale columns, two hundred meters tall, tapering, with the matte sheen of a tooth. Here they are called the keel-roots. They are the same organism — the same seed, the same season of seeding, the same recipe — as the lift-roots that stand on the Risen Shelf, an ocean to the east. The only difference anyone can measure is the sign of the number. The rock around a lift-root runs warm and dense. The rock around a keel-root runs cool, and the bedrock beneath it is lighter than physics says it should be, as though something were being drawn out of it patiently, grain by grain, and carried away by a road no one can find on any map.

Everyone here knows the address, though. Everyone here can tell you, to the millimeter, where their country is going.

**Land**

This is what the Shelf's visitors — the shelf-born come here, more of them every year, on the ships that still run — cannot stop touching: the *finish* of everything. Soil you can plunge an arm into. Riverbanks upholstered in root and moss. Willows — full willows, twenty meters of them, not the knee-high drafts the Shelf plants in its soil pockets but the finished poem — leaning over water that has known fish for ten thousand years. The shelf-born walk the lanes here with the stunned expression of people who grew up reading recipes and have been sat down, at last, in front of the meal.

But the land is being relieved of duty, parcel by parcel, and its people farm accordingly. Every lowland family keeps what is called the salt calendar: this field has forty harvests left, that one a dozen, the long meadow by the sluice perhaps five. It changes how you farm. There is no exhausting a field that the sea will retire before you can wear it out, and so the last harvests are the most generous, the ground given everything, rested nothing, spent like money in the final week of a voyage. The saying is that a field should go under *fat*. A drowning meadow, its final summer, is the greenest thing in the country, and everyone knows what that particular green means, and mows it anyway, and stands a moment at the end of the last swath.

The salt line moves inland a few paces a year. Behind it, wheat. Ahead of it, marsh. It is the exact mirror of the wet seams threading the Shelf's black rock — the first drafts of rivers there, the last drafts of fields here — and the people on both shores use, without ever having conferred, the same word for the moving edge. They call it the *hem*.

---

**The settlements**

The towns are brick and timber and mortared stone, crooked, dense, worn — stair treads hollowed by feet, door posts polished dark at shoulder height by six generations of passing hands. Nothing here was grown from the bedrock in a single seamless piece; everything was carried, stacked, joined, repaired, and the repairs repaired. A town on the Given Country's coast is a document with every correction still visible.

And the towns are not evacuated. This is the custom that outsiders find hardest, and it is the one the country is proudest of: a town is *kept company*. People live in a drowning town — trading, marrying, mending roofs that have a decade left — until the spring tides come up the high street. Then there is a last market, held in whatever square remains dry, famously good-humored and famously unbearable. The doors are taken off their hinges. The lintels are sawn out — the lintels here carry not altitudes but freeboards, the height the sea stood below your doorstep the year you were born, a column of names and shrinking numbers, and when a house is given up its lintel goes with the family, inland or onto a ship. The bell comes down from the church tower. And then everyone leaves, and no one demolishes anything, because the sea is not owed a ruin. It is owed a town, whole, and it gets one.

At the country's center — lowest, oldest, wrapped around the largest of the keel-roots the way the Anchorage wraps its great column — stands the Ballast. It is ringed with tide-gates and kept at its height by pumps, not in defiance and not forever, but by agreement: the Ballast will be last. It is loud in a way no town on the Shelf is loud, wet-aired, smelling of ink and tar and brine, and it is where the recipe was written — in the long pattern-halls behind the gates, by a generation now mostly dead, who could not stop their country sinking (it had been sinking for nine hundred years; the delta silt compacts, the sea is patient; the dikes and pumps and prayers were a war fought at a rate of centimeters, and it was being lost) — and who found, in the roots, not a rescue but a coupling. The roots stop nothing. They *bind*. What goes down here comes up there, and so the pattern-halls did the only thing left to a country that could not be saved: they aimed it. You cannot keep a land from ending, runs the line carved over the halls' door. *You can tell its ending what it is for.*

---

**What lives here**

The sea does not wait for the land to finish leaving. That is the astonishment of the coast: the Given Country, going under, is more alive at its hem than it has been in living memory.

A wheat field goes to salt marsh inside a decade, and the marsh is a factory. Samphire first, then the crabs, then everything that eats crabs. Eels run up drowned lanes between drowned garden walls. Herons stalk the flooded orchards, hip-deep among apple trees that still, absurdly, blossom, their roots in brine, spending themselves the way the last meadows do. Seals haul out on the steps of a church whose bell hangs, now, in the Ballast. Oysters crust the garden walls of the parish of Harrow-under-Dyke, three fathoms down, and the shelf-born divers who visit — they always visit; they come across the ocean to swim over the kind of past their own country cannot manufacture — say the drowned towns are furred and jeweled and crowded, reefs with street plans.

And twice a year the sky fills, because this is the other end of the geese. The traveling flocks — the cousins of the Shelf's stay-geese, the ones who never stopped leaving — winter here by the hundred thousand on the new marsh, fattening on a country's dissolution, and fly east in spring to summer on the Shelf's black-glass pools. They are the ferry line. In the mud on their feet ride the Given Country's smallest citizens: shrimp eggs, midge, the beetles — so that the invisible founders of the Shelf's waters, the aristocracy of shrimp that inherited a continent, were emigrants from these drowning parishes first. The Country has been colonizing the Shelf from the beginning, wing-borne, a smear of mud at a time, and neither the geese nor the shrimp were consulted, and both are thriving.

---

**The people**

They speak of the land in the past tense, and it takes a visitor a while to hear that it is not grief. *This was good soil*, a farmer says, standing in soil that is still, at that moment, superb — and means it the way you'd say it of a parent still living: not a report of death but an accuracy about direction. The people of the Given Country are the precise inverse of the shelf-folk, and both peoples know it, and it is the axis their whole strange kinship turns on. On the Shelf, the land is younger than its grandmothers and the present is made of futures. Here the present is made almost entirely of pasts — every lane named for someone unrecoverable, every field carrying its ghost of a field, the dead outnumbering the living ten thousand to one and lying, quite literally, under everything.

They date themselves by freeboard. *I was born at two meters over*, a woman says, and the sea has been climbing toward her number her whole life, and the custom — never voted on, never broken — is that by the time the water reaches your birth-mark you should have decided: inland, or the ships. Some decide at twenty. Some stand in their doorways at seventy with the tide at their lintels, having decided by not deciding, and are keeping the house company, and everyone understands.

The ones who take ship become the Shelf's people, and the crossing works its changes both ways. Country-folk who visit the Shelf come home shaken by the lightness — *a country with no one under it*, one letter said, *you can hear yourself think there, and there is nothing else to hear* — and most come home, and some don't. The shelf-born who visit here come home shaken by the weight, having stood for the first time on all the dead strangers a normal place is built on. There is one custom the Shelf keeps that began as a kindness and hardened into law: *no drowned names on new water*. No emigrant may name a shelf pool for a lost parish. The new country is to be on a first-name basis with its own living, and the old names go under with their own ground, where they were promised they could stay. The sawn-out lintels that cross the ocean are the single exception — carried into the grown-stone houses of the Anchorage, mounted indoors, private, touched on leaving the way lintels are touched everywhere. A column of names and freeboards on one wall; a column of names and altitudes carved into the doorway. The family descending on one shore, rising on the other, the two ledgers of a single account.

**The work**

Almost everyone here does some version of the same job, which is the opposite of midwifery and has no comfortable name. The country settles for *keeping*.

The reckoners are the most numerous: the copyists of the Ballast's long sheds, who are turning a country into paper as it turns itself into sea. Every parish register, every field boundary, every recipe, tide table, lullaby, dispute, and repair record — copied, and the copies copied, and the whole shipped east in fireproof chests, so that the Shelf, which has no past, is quietly accumulating the entire memory of a country in its archives, a bequest it has been asked not to open for a hundred years. The reckoners' saying is that a thing is not lost until it is lost *twice*.

The pilots chart the new shallows, and their work expires annually — last year's chart is a map of the year before's town, streets becoming channels, the market square a fine anchorage that no one will anchor in. Because that is the grave-tenders' doctrine, argued once in the early years and settled forever: the dead stay. Nine hundred years of churchyards will not be dug and shipped. The dead were promised this ground, not another, and so the ground keeps its promise and takes them down with it, and the sea above a drowned parish is treated as ground — boats slow across it, nets are not shot there, and the charts carry, alongside their soundings, the small cross-hatched notation that means *someone is under you; go gently*.

The wardens of the keel-roots keep the same log the lift-root wardens keep, in the same fierce boring exactness — *no change, no change* — and the two sets of families are, in fact, families: the fourteen holdings on the Shelf were founded by younger children of the fourteen here, and the logbooks are exchanged annually and read against each other, forty years of matched baselines, the two ends of the recipe checked against itself. The warden cellars here are the coldest rooms in the country, and the parishes use them as larders, and step around the log-desk, and consider this part of the arrangement.

And then, the same week of late summer that the Shelf holds its Leveling, the Given Country holds the Sounding. The descent is measured, benchmark by benchmark, and the figure read aloud in every square, and in the Ballast the gallery of bells — one bell now for every town the sea has been given, forty-one of them, each rung once — counts the country's gifts before the number is spoken. Then, weeks later, the ships bring the Shelf's figure west, and it is posted beside the Sounding's, and in forty years the two numbers have never disagreed. The fall here, the rise there, even to the millimeter, and nobody can say why they must match, and everybody can say why they must match. It is the nearest thing either shore has to a prayer answered annually: the bread proving there; the loaf being paid for here.

---

**The dead, and the weather**

So the churchyards go under, and the orchards, and the towns whole with their doors off; and here is the thing the pattern-halls did not plan and would not have dared to:

The shallows over the drowned parishes are the warmest, richest water on the coast. The sunken country — its walls, its lanes, its generosity of final harvests, its dead going dark and rich under three fathoms of green light — has become the nursery of the whole eastern sea, and the great wet wind of the world loads itself there. It lifts off those warm shallows, heavy, and goes east across the ocean, and climbs the scarp of the Risen Shelf, and lets go of everything it carried. The morning cloud-tide that pours across the young rock at ankle height; the weeping stone; the water standing in Marek's Pool, holding its exact second sky — some of it, every day, was vapor over the roofs of drowned towns that morning. The Given Country arrives on the Shelf as weather. It has been arriving all along.

The shelf-folk say: *our ancestors are not buried in this land; our ancestors are this land.* The people here have their own sentence, and it is the older one, and quieter: *our ancestors are keeping the land company.* Down they go together, the promised ground and its promised people, a few centimeters a year, evenly, without a single tremor — not dying, the grave-tenders insist, and after enough years here you believe them — *being carried*. And an ocean east, on rock still ringing like a new pot, the rain comes down and begins, patiently, to teach the stone what this country spent nine hundred years learning and is now, handful by handful, wingbeat by wingbeat, centimeter by centimeter, giving away: how to be a landscape. How to hold water. How to hold the dead. How to be old.
