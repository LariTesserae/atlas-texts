**The Underfall**

Stand at the plateau's rim — and the rim is the one thing the plateau owns that descends, all at once, two miles, without apology — and watch the wind go over. The wind that long ago ran out of anything soft to carry never stopped carrying. It carries still: rock-flour from the grazers' milling, ash sifted off the spinal vents, the fine dust of the slag windrows being planed back down, the whole grey remainder of a long subtraction problem, streaming over the edge in banners you can see from a day away. A subtraction has a remainder. The remainder goes over the edge. It has been going over the edge for as long as the plateau has been the plateau, and two miles down, under a mile of cloud, it has been arriving.

You will not see where. From the rim there is no below. There is only the cloud ring — a permanent collar of vapor clasped around the plateau's foot, the color of a held breath, generated by the simplest weather in the world: the hottest country anywhere meets the ordinary air of the world it was lifted out of, and along the whole two-mile wall, without pause, the wall wrings water from every wind that touches it. No water ever carved the plateau. The plateau carves water. It has been carving it out of the sky for ages, and the water falls, and the dust falls, and the sound falls — the drone goes over the rim with everything else; nothing stops it — and everything that falls lands in the same place, and that place is the Underfall.

You came through it, in fact, if you ever stood on the plateau. There is no way up that does not begin in the wet. You may not have noticed. The Underfall is easy to mistake for weather on the way to somewhere loud.

---

The land itself is not simple. It has never had the time to become simple; simplicity is what happens to a country when the wind finishes with it, and the wind will never finish here, because the wind is still delivering. The Underfall is a hem of soft ground thirty, fifty, in places eighty miles wide, circling the plateau's foot entirely — a ring-country, an annulus of fen and silt and standing milk-colored water, built grain by grain out of everything the tableland above ever lost. Every particle of it was plateau once. That should be understood first: this is not a neighboring country. It is the same country, ground fine, rained out of the air, and taught to be soft.

Where the plateau was made by taking away, the Underfall is made by arrival. The silt lies in beds hundreds of feet deep, brick-red at depth, greying toward the surface, still settling — the whole land has the young unfinished face of a thing still deciding on its expression. The waters are opaque with rock-flour, the color of old brick gone to milk. It rains, gently, almost always; when the wind has been strong on the rim above, the rain comes down faintly red, carrying the plateau in solution, and the fens drink their parent country a few grams at a time.

And everything grows here. Nothing has ever *not* grown here — inversion, again, of the country upstairs. There was never a bare Underfall waiting for life to find it; the first grain to arrive arrived into wet, and something took hold of it, and has never once let go. What grows is mostly one thing, and the one thing is mostly the whole place: a felt. A continuous grey-green tissue, spongy, cool, ankle-deep to shoulder-deep, spread across the silt for two hundred miles of hem, parted only by the milk channels and the rain-pans — a single fabric of growth so unbroken that the honest description of the Underfall's geography is: *one meadow, wet, wearing a country underneath it.*

The light is grey and shadowless, a mile of cloud between the fens and the sun, and it arrives — like the dust, like the rain, like everything here — slightly used. That phrase means more in the Underfall than it should. Because the plateau's other export comes down with the rest: the worn softness of its reality, ground fine along with the stone. Up above, where the seams of things have worn through, loose time is a hazard — the thing every animal seals itself against, at furnace temperatures, at industrial volume. But ground to powder, diluted across two miles of falling and a hundred miles of fen, the same brokenness arrives below as something else entirely. It arrives as a nutrient.

The silt is full of spent moments. You will notice it small: a footprint that goes on settling for a day after the foot, patiently completing itself; a rain-pan that holds the morning's sky well into the afternoon, not as reflection but as retention; a channel that runs, for an hour, with yesterday's rain. Nothing here fears this. The felt grows fattest where the fallen time lies thickest. What is a wound at the source is food in the delta — that is what a delta is for — and the Underfall has made a metabolism of it, the way soil makes a metabolism of everything the heights let go.

Even the present arrives secondhand. Sound takes time to fall two miles and slant across the fens, and so the drone of the plateau — which is total here too; the Underfall lives its whole life inside the fallen voice of the country above — reaches the felt the better part of half a minute old. The Underfall is never in the present. It receives the present the way it receives everything else: from above, in full, and slightly late.

And here is the fact that matches the plateau's central fact, its mirror twin: the Underfall is crowded, and it is quiet, and these two facts are the same fact. The fens are dense with life beyond anything the tableland could dream — the felt alone outweighs the whole loud fauna upstairs — and almost none of it makes a sound. It cannot afford to. The Underfall's entire economy is hearing. A thing that makes noise cannot listen, and everything below is listening, all of it, always, to the one voice that falls through the ceiling and never stops. The plateau roars because it must insist. The Underfall is silent because it must receive. Between them, they are one conversation, and each country is the half the other cannot be.

---

What lives in the Underfall is memory. Everything else — the felt, the milk, the rain, the mills — is paper.

That has to be understood before the census makes sense, just as it had to be understood, up above, that what lives on the plateau is time. The two are not different residents. They are the two halves of one resident, torn along the rim. The plateau's animals carry sequence forward, sealed and burning, each one a pressurized argument for *when it is*. The Underfall carries sequence backward — keeps it, presses it, files it in the silt. The plateau is loud and keeps no records. The Underfall is quiet and keeps everything. A world where reality has worn through can survive on exactly this arrangement and no other: one country to say the time, and one country to write it down.

So: the census.

**The felt** is the great fact, and it is the archive. Its tissue records. Sound crossing it leaves ripples that do not fully die; the ripples are retained, layered, banked into the growth the way seasons are banked into wood. Every dusk, when the steam-hammer roll call comes rolling off the rim above — each animal sounding its fire, the herds counting the pieces of the one fire — the chorus falls through the cloud, arrives half a minute old, and the felt writes it down. All of it. It has written down every dusk chorus since there have been dusk choruses. Walk the fens on a still night and the felt murmurs — replaying, at whisper volume, from whatever stratum the evening's dampness has softened — and you can wander in and out of centuries: here a district repeating a chorus a thousand years deep, there the mating-synchrony of two beasts long since opened by the threshers, their meshed strokes still audible, still perfectly in time with one another, in the grass. Up above, the emptied hearths sit dark on the flats, fires gone out honestly. Down here, every one of those fires is still sounding, in soft copy, somewhere in the felt. The plateau skipped the argument about death. The Underfall entered it, and won it on a technicality: nothing that was ever heard here has finished dying.

**The mills** are the Underfall's verticals — its only verticals, and its only monuments, and they were not built; that must be said clearly here too, because the mills look built. They are the flyers. The flyers never land — except once; every flyer lands once, and this is where. An old flyer, hearing its own bearings begin the thin rising shriek that is its mortality, rides the rim winds outward, over the edge, out above the cloud, and unwinds down through it — spending, across most of a day, the angular momentum it has carried since it was thrown from its mother's back. The felt hears the shriek from miles above, days out, and is never wrong about where the flyer will fall; some calculations, it seems, are done the same way in ligament and in fabric. The flyer comes down into the soft, unbroken, still faintly turning, and turns itself into the mud over weeks, slower and slower — and then the fen takes over the turning. It grows up into the ratchets, threads the wing-governors, sheathes the whole animal in grey-green, and keeps it going: one revolution a year, forever. There are thousands of them across the hem, tall overgrown spindles rotating too slowly to see, all in phase with one another, all in phase with the drone. They are the Underfall's calendars — its one concession to keeping time in the plateau's sense — and they are graves that never stopped working, which in this world is the highest honor either country knows how to pay.

**The criers** are the small fauna, and the couriers. The wall's buttresses cast acoustic shadows; there are deaf districts far out in the hem where the drone cannot fall, and felt that cannot hear starves the way a beast without tenders drifts. So: the criers — slender, silver-grey, mouthless, swimming the milk channels — go where the sound is. Near the wall the temperature gradients lens the fallen drone into corridors, loud reaches where the hum stands in the air almost solid, and there the criers coil and bask, trembling, taking the chorus into their bodies the way the flyers upstairs skim heat from a plume. Charged, they travel — days outward, into the deaf fens — and give it back through their skins, note for note, dusk by dusk, a recital of everything the shadowed districts missed. A crier at full voice is quieter than rain. It is the loudest citizen the Underfall has, and that is the country's whole discipline in one animal. (The rain itself, it should be said, is the other citizenry: each drop one small exact thing at one exact interval, a billion tenders keeping the felt's beat honest. There is one rain in the Underfall, as there is one fire above. It has never stopped. It has only been divided.)

And then there are **the blanks**.

Circles of bare mud out in the felt — silver, smooth as poured metal, a mile across. The felt grows to their margins and stops. The criers detour around them, adding days to a crossing without apparent thought. Rain falls on a blank without making any sound at all; you will stand at a margin and watch the drops land silently and feel the wrongness in your chest before you understand it. Nothing is recorded on a blank. Nothing has ever been recorded on a blank. Test the felt at the margin and you find worse: the strata beneath are not empty but *emptied* — voices that were kept there, kept for centuries, gone, and the growth healed smooth over the absence.

The blanks move. Slowly. Nothing has ever seen one move.

And here is the worst thing, told plainly, because the Underfall — younger than its parent, and gentler — has not yet learned to stop explaining itself: chart the blanks, and chart the circles of silence that walk the plateau two miles above, and it is one chart. Same sizes. Same slow courses. When the quiet takes a beast upstairs — takes its running, leaves it a stopped and perfect engine, its portion of the one fire gone unbreathed — that beast's kept voice is found gone from the felt below, in the same season, healed over, blank. The two countries share everything else by falling: dust, rain, light, time, sound, the dead. This one thing they share directly, without the courtesy of distance. Whether the quiet is a blank's shadow standing up through two miles of stone, or the blank is where the quiet's takings soak through and land, neither country says. It took the fens an age to understand what you may as well be told at once: they are the same thing. It carries no time to lose, and it keeps no memory to lose, and those are not two descriptions.

So both countries hold the shape of what it takes, since neither can hold the thing. The herds above keep a gap open in the chorus where a rhythm used to be. And the felt below, around every healed blank, keeps a ring of deliberate silence in its own murmur — a groove of nothing, grown and maintained, replayed nightly among the centuries of kept voices: the sound of a voice not being there, filed carefully, so that at least the absence is on record. The Underfall cannot keep what the quiet takes. It keeps the taking. It is a small stubbornness, and it is the whole character of the place.

---

Dusk in the Underfall happens to the ceiling.

The grey light fails, and then, sourceless, a bloom comes up in the cloud from above — a spreading orange stain across the whole sky of the hem, brightening as the furnace hearts of a province come up like coals through a grate, two miles overhead, seen through a mile of held rain. That is the only sunset the Underfall has ever had: the plateau's entire population, showing through.

Then, half a minute behind the light, the chorus arrives.

And the felt begins to write. You can watch it happen — the recording is faintly luminous, a pale grey-green wave moving out across the fens as the roll call lands and is taken down, district by district, ring by ring, two hundred miles of hem around the foot of the loud country, the mills turning their imperceptible fraction, the criers charging in the loud reaches, the healed blanks holding their groove of nothing, the whole soft remainder of a finished land doing the one thing it condensed out of the sky to do.

Above, the plateau says what it has always said, every animal at once, at industrial volume, against a worn-through sky: *Still. Still. Still.*

Below, in light instead of sound, in full, and slightly late, the Underfall gives the only answer it has ever given, which is the reason there can be a *still* at all:

*Kept. Kept. Kept.*
