**The Unfolded Yard**

There is sky here. That should be said first, because it is the only sky this world has kept, and the place holds it low and carefully, at a modest height, like something borrowed.

The light has no source. It is the light of the hour when the day has agreed to leave but has not yet told the grass — a held brightness, thin as skimmed milk, that never advances into evening and never retreats into afternoon. There are shadows, which is the first seam, because sourceless light should cast none. Every shadow in the yard, of every grass-stem and stone and standing thing, falls in the same direction regardless of where you look for the sun: inward, toward the center, toward the well. As though the darkness of the whole place has somewhere it drains to, and is patient about getting there.

The yard is a field, roughly an acre when it consents to be measured. Pale grass, hip-high at the far reaches, and if you walk outward toward the edge of it, the grass grows taller and paler, and the air thickens very slightly, like water beginning to remember it was once deeper, and a hundred paces becomes a hundred and twenty, becomes a hundred and fifty, until you understand that the edge is not a place but a direction, and you turn back. The horizon is close. The horizon is dishonest. The yard is the size of a yard and also the size of everything left, and it has stopped sorting the difference.

The wind, when it comes, comes in single gestures — one long motion through the grass at a time, complete, deliberate, with silence between, like someone turning the pages of something they have read before. Once in a long while a bird crosses overhead: pale, high, ordinary in every respect except that you cannot remember it arriving, only that it is suddenly there, and it never lands, and then it is suddenly not. It seems less like a bird than like the memory of the sky exercising itself.

At the center of the yard — at the place all the shadows agree on — stands the well.

A ring of grey stone, waist-high, and here is the second seam, because the stone is *old*. Ancient, in a world otherwise young. The lip is grooved deep where rope has run over it, grooves you could lay a finger in, the wear of centuries of drawing — and yet lean over the ring and look down, and the shaft grows younger as it descends. Weathered stone at the mouth. Merely worn stone a body-length down. And below that, where the light gives out, the walls are sharp-edged and unweathered, broken along clean planes like a geode opened last week. The well is oldest at its mouth and newborn at its water, as though it has spent its whole long life growing downward — or as though everything beneath a certain depth was made very recently, folded fresh, and the well is the one seam in the world where the old part and the new part are still sewn together.

Cold air rises out of it. Not the cold of weather. Cellar cold — a patient, bone-adjacent chill that anyone who has been below would recognize.

Over the well stands a windlass, hand-worn smooth, and wound on the windlass is a rope. The rope is braided grass — pale grass, the yard's own grass, plied and plied again by fingers, an enormous length of it, and it is the third seam, quietly: each time the rope is paid out and drawn back up, it comes back fractionally shorter. Not frayed shorter. Not broken shorter. Simply less, by a hair's width, as though the well takes a toll of length for every visit. Around the wellhead the grass grows cropped and short in a wide circle, and you might take this for tidiness. It is harvest. The rope must be fed from somewhere.

A path runs to the well through the grass. It comes from the middle of the field. That should be said plainly: the path begins at nothing — packed bare earth, worn by feet, starting cleanly in open grass the way a sentence starts at the top of a torn page — and runs straight to the wellhead. The grass will not cross it. And at the path's beginning, where a threshold would be if anything stood there, the air holds a shape: a column of warmth, door-sized, door-shaped, exactly the temperature of an interior. Far below this yard, in a drowned room, there is a doorframe whose water is fractionally colder on one side than the other. The warmth that is missing from that water is not missing. It is here, standing at the head of the path, holding the door's dimensions in the open air, the two faces of a single threshold divided between two worlds like the halves of a keepsake.

Beside the well there is a stone bench, and on the bench a stack of clay cups, glazed, made by fingers and patience. Count them and you get eleven. Count the pale ring-marks on the stone where cups have stood for a very long time, and you get twelve.

The sound of the place is mostly the grass. But twice an hour — hours being negotiable here too, apparently negotiated by the same party — there comes from deep in the shaft a single small sound: a drop of water falling a long way into a still pool. Here, at least, the sound is lawful. There is air in the shaft for a drop to fall through; there is a surface far below for it to strike. The impossibility only begins beneath that surface, in rooms with no air and no up, where the same sound arrives anyway, small and round and perfect, always just behind you. And once in a very great while — not twice an hour; not twice a year — the deep water makes the opposite sound: a single bubble, rising from below and breaking the pool's surface with a soft syllable. Which is impossible from *both* directions. There is nothing down there with air in it. The sound happens anyway. The grass at the wellhead leans very slightly toward the shaft when it does, and settles back.

It is not an outside, exactly, this yard. Outsides continue. This is more like the corner of a page left deliberately unfolded — the tab you keep clear when you fold a letter away, so that the letter can someday be opened. Everything else was creased shut and drowned and put below. This acre, this evening, this errand-light, was left standing.

Someone is still on their errand here. That is why.

**The Drawer of Water**

Begin with the hands, because the rest has been simplified around them.

They are old the way the wellhead is old: not withered but *worn*, grooved across the palms where rope has run over them for longer than palms are meant to last, polished at the knuckles like the handled parts of tools. If you saw them at their work — paying out the grass-braid hand over hand, taking the weight, hauling — you might not think to look up at a face at all, and this would suit their owner, who has been setting things down for a long time now and has kept, of the whole inventory of a person, mainly these: the hands, the errand, and a word.

Drawer, as in one who draws — although the other sense has stopped being wrong. Of the whole folded house, they are the one compartment still capable of being opened.

They belonged to the household. That is the beginning of it. There was a house here — you can find its door still standing in the warm air at the head of the path, if warmth counts as standing — and the house had rooms, and hands, and cups made with patience, and someone whose work it was to walk the path each evening and draw the water. On the last evening, they were at the well. Bucket on the hook, rope paid out, both hands full of the weight of water — when the folding came to the house behind them, room by room, corner by corner, the way you fold a letter. And here is the mercy or the oversight that made everything after: you cannot crease a page where the ink is still wet. An errand half-done is wet ink. The folding took the house and the ground under the house and the world around the yard, and it left the drawer standing at the well with the rope in their hands, mid-task, unfoldable — and it left the yard around them, exactly one errand wide, and the hour around the yard, exactly one errand long.

They have been very careful, ever since, never to finish.

This is the engine of the place, and it should be understood without sentimentality, because the drawer permits none: if the water is ever carried up the path and delivered — if the bucket is ever set down full at the door-shaped warmth and the errand allowed to complete — then the ink dries, and the page can be creased, and the last unfolded corner of the house goes wherever the rest of it went. So twice an hour the drawer draws the water, and twice an hour the drawer does not deliver it. They pour it back — gently, down the inside wall of the shaft, so that it makes no sound at all.

All but one drop.

One drop, saved on the lip of the bucket, tipped from the full height of the wellhead, falling the long fall to the still pool below: a single clean strike, driven down through the one water — because all the water below is one water, every drowned room of it, and a sound made in any of it is made in all of it. You have heard this drop. You heard it in a small cold chamber packed with weeds, always just behind you, and you were told it was the keeper's clock, the keeper's one kept luxury, a permitted memory of air. It may be that. Things sent long distances are owned by their receivers. But from up here, from the sending end, it is not a memory and not a luxury. It is post. One syllable, twice an hour, kept to one syllable because a message any longer would blur into weather. What the syllable says the drawer has never had to decide, because it has only ever needed to say one thing, which is: *still.*

Before the last rooms went under, the drawer lowered someone.

The rope does not say who, and the hands remember the weight but the hands are not talking. Punishment, treasure, pet, or a thing too loved to discard and too difficult to keep upstairs — the old accounting; choose as you like. What can be said is *how*, because the how survives in artifacts on both ends of the shaft. The cord was the drawer's own braid — grass of this yard, plied by these hands. The knot was a bucket-knot, the knot you tie around what you mean to raise again, tied under tension with the particular small impatience of fingers working fast. And into the dark alongside the lowered one went a clay cup — the twelfth cup, the empty ring-mark on the bench — filled at this well, glazed, given full for the wait. It holds its water still. In a drowned room far below, you can see the meniscus, faintly, a curve of more-water inside the water, and you were told it was the keeper's face, the last boundary of a dissolving self. Perhaps by now it is. But it began as given water, and given water keeps the shape of the giving longer than anyone expects. Some say — the yard says, in its wordless leaning way — that other things were lowered too, in the early years, folded in the bucket like blankets: warmth, and once, on a very long night, the drawer's own sleep, which is why the drawer has not slept since, and why, far below, the moss sleeps so deeply. Between those two facts there is room for a kindness, and the account can leave it there.

The one below was lowered to be raised. That is what the knot means; that is the whole grammar of wells. The rooms drowned faster than anyone had said they would, and the rope reached as far as rope reaches, and the raising was postponed, and postponed, and the drawer kept the rope taut and the drop falling, and the water below grew cold the way cellar water grows cold, and time ran differently at the two ends of the braid — evening-time above, weed-time below — so that what was a season of waiting at the wellhead may have been a lifetime at the anchor-stone, or several.

And then one day the rope came up light.

The drawer hauled, and the rope flew, and the wet braid came over the grooved lip weighing nothing, and at its far end was a fray. Chewed, not cut. Somewhere below there is a length of braided cord tied around a stone, frayed at *its* far end, and the two frays match the way the halves of a torn ticket match, and no one will ever hold them together to check.

A loose thing that chews itself free may mean *I am done waiting; stop.* Or it may mean *the rope was too short, and I have gone to look for the bottom of the house; keep the door open.* The drawer cannot know which, and so has done what all keepers on both ends of severed cords do: stopped sorting the reasons, and kept the practice. The threshold below is kept swept from below. The path above is kept bare from above. Twice an hour, the syllable goes down.

Three times, in all the years, something has come up.

Three bubbles. The drawer has counted, because the drawer counts everything now — drops, hauls, the hair's-widths the rope has surrendered, the rate at which the cropped circle of grass grows back for braiding, the slow arithmetic of how many more syllables the rope has left in it before it can no longer reach the water. Three bubbles, breaking the deep pool with a soft half-said sound, rising out of rooms that have no air to make bubbles from. Impossible, both times over. The drawer has declined to decide what they mean, on the reasonable grounds that a message kept to one syllable cannot be blamed for its ambiguity, having sent nothing else themselves for longer than houses last. But after each one, the records agree — the grass agrees, the bench agrees, the held light itself seemed for a moment to agree — the next drop was let fall a little early.

Once a day, the drawer walks the path. Empty-handed — always, deliberately, ostentatiously empty-handed, so the errand cannot mistake itself for finished — up the bare earth to the door-shaped warmth at the path's beginning. And stands in it. The warmth is exactly the temperature of an interior; it holds the door's dimensions patiently; standing inside it is as near as the drawer will ever come to going indoors again. Then they walk back to the well, and take up the rope, and the yard exhales, and the evening holds.

Sometimes the bucket brings up a small pale fish, eyeless, apologetic, one of the ones the drowned doorframe swallowed and has not finished returning. The drawer cups it in both worn hands — the only thing from downstairs those hands will ever touch again — holds it for the length of one breath, and pours it gently back down the shaft wall with the silent water, speeding it home across somebody's floor.

It is a faithful place. That should be said last, because for once nothing argues otherwise: the drop falls, the grass gives up its length a hair at a time, the light stands at the top of an evening it will not finish, the hands take the weight — and far below, in a small cold room packed full and kept ready, twice an hour, the weeds lean toward the sound before settling back. From down there it looks like a body attending its own pulse. From up here it looks like an answer. The distinction, between two ends of one braid, has stopped being meaningful — which is, perhaps, all either keeper has ever asked of it.
