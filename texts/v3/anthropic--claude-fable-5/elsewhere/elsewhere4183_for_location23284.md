**The Outfall Terraces**

You ascend to them along the water. Two risers run up the bore beside the freight cage's rails — pipes thigh-thick, lagged in the same mineral-stained canvas as everything below, warm under the hand the whole two thousand feet of the climb, and pulsing. Put your palm flat on the lagging anywhere along the ascent and you can feel it: *intake, compression, discharge, settle,* the Old Lady's four-beat breath translated into surge and slack, traveling up through the dark ahead of you. The water reaches the surface before you do. It has been doing so, without one missed beat, for longer than anyone at either end has been alive.

The bore opens on the mountain's southern flank, high up, in air so dry it hurts. The opening is called the Throat, and the Throat smokes — a standing plume of warm wet breath rolling out of the rock into the cold thin morning, visible from three valleys away, so that from a distance the mountain appears to be exhaling one long ribbon of cloud off its shoulder. This is the Condensery's air, spent, risen, escaping. Strangers on the far ridges take it for a volcano and are, in the long view, not entirely wrong.

Just below the Throat, the risers empty into the outfall basin: a black stone tank the size of a small house, cut into the living rock, where the water arrives still warm, still tasting of the deep — iron and faint sulfur and something mineral-sweet underneath, a flavor the terrace-born are raised on from infancy and miss savagely anywhere else, describing all other water, without irony, as *empty*. The water does not pour into the basin. It *breathes* into it, in surges, four beats to the cycle, eleven cycles to the minute, and the basin's surface rises and settles, rises and settles, all day and all night, forever. On cold mornings it steams like a body.

From the basin, everything descends. Twelve terraces step down the flank in long dry-stone curves, each one a strip of green perhaps ten paces deep and half a mile long, and between and through them runs the water — an iron main branching into stone channels, the channels branching into runnels, the runnels into finger-width feeds, spreading down the mountainside in the shape of nothing so much as a vine, which is what the terraces call it: the Vine, trunk at the top, growing downward, its leaves the fields themselves.

And here everything is the Condensery inverted. Below, nothing has been worn down and everything is being deposited; up here the rock is old, golden, tired, and everything is being taken away — the wind files at the walls, the winters pry the terraces apart stone by stone, the paths are worn into grooves by generations of the same feet, and nothing accretes, nothing thickens, nothing is given, except along the channels: for the warm mineral water lays down, wherever it runs, a slow pale crust, the deep's own new stone arriving on the surface a spoonful at a time, furring the runnels exactly as the flowstone below is furring the abandoned pipework — the same white drapery growing at both ends of the same pipe, the mountain patiently adopting the whole length of it at once. The farmers chip the scale out each winter, grind it, and spread it on the fields. The terraces are, in this sense, eating the mountain's infancy. No one up here finds this strange. Water is warmth is stone is bread. It has all come up the same bore.

Below, the dark is thick and close and full of movement. Up here the light is vast and the world is nearly empty of it — hawk-hung distances, hard dry stars, a sun that cracks lips and fades cloth and lays black shadows sharp as tin under every wall. Below, sound is the architecture; up here sound is thin and wide and mostly wind, with a ticking of channels under it, and swifts screaming down the terrace faces at evening, and — if you press your ear to any channel stone, any wall the Vine touches, anywhere on the flank — the pulse. Four beats. The whole mountainside keeps her time. The farmers open their sluices on the surge without thinking about it. Children skip rope to it. Put a terrace-born abroad in a silent valley and ask them to estimate a minute and they will be uncannily accurate, and their fingers will move, very slightly, and they will not know why. Two thousand feet of rock apart, at the two ends of one pipe, the same engine paces two peoples who have mostly never met.

**The holders.**

You can identify a terrace-holder at a distance the way you can a tender — by the opposite marks. They are brown and lean and cracked where the tenders are pale and plumped; their hands are all callus, backs and palms alike; they squint at *everything*, having lived their lives in glare and wind, and when they descend the bore — and they do descend, some of them, every generation — they spend their first shift below drinking the air in astonishment and describing it, without irony, as *thick* and *kind*, and their skin, over the months, pales and softens, and the terraces say of such a one, with a shrug that is not quite disapproval, *the Warm has taken them.*

They are counters. It is their deepest adaptation, deeper than the squint. Everything on the terraces is measured, because everything on the terraces is water and water arrives in breaths — and *breath* is, in fact, the unit. A household holds so many breaths a day, written in the Book; a field is valued in the breaths it drinks; a marriage settles breaths on the couple; the dead have their breaths read back into the common share at the funeral, aloud, which is the closest thing the terraces have to a eulogy — *she returns forty breaths a day to the Vine* — and it is understood by everyone present to be a statement about generosity, and it makes them weep. A people who measure their whole lives in another creature's breathing do not need to be told they are dependents. They put it in their arithmetic instead, where it can be trusted.

Their dialect, like the tenders', is compressed — but where the tenders' speech was squeezed by noise, the holders' was dried by wind, which eats long sentences. They speak in short declaratives with the ends worn off. The two dialects, oddly, have converged; a tender and a holder, meeting at the Throat, understand each other better than either understands the wordy people of the valleys, and both know it, and it embarrasses neither.

**The figures.**

The offices up here mirror the offices below the way a reflection mirrors — same shapes, reversed.

There is the *taster*, who is to the water what the breath-reader is to the engine. Every morning at first light she goes to the outfall basin, cups her hands, and drinks, and reads the news of the deep in the flavor: more iron means the lower runs are shedding; a sulfur brightening means the young rock is sweating somewhere new; a certain flat chalkiness, appearing perhaps twice in a career, means the flowstone has swallowed something it shouldn't have. Her findings are chalked on a slate and sent down on the freight cage's first descent, and the breath-reader's slate comes up on the same cage's return, and the two women — it is nearly always two women, no one knows why, it simply keeps happening — have in some cases corresponded daily for decades, slate by slate, and never once stood in the same room. When the current taster's counterpart below retires, the terraces will hold a small feast for a person none of them has ever seen. This has been done before. It is considered entirely normal.

There is the *Counter*, keeper of the Book of Breaths, which lives in the gauge house beside the basin — a ledger the size of a paving stone, generations deep, in which every breath of water on the mountain is written, and through which, consequently, everything that happens on the terraces eventually passes. The Counters are, by long tradition and exact symmetry with the cage-keepers below, the gossips and the historians. Nothing on the flank is secret from the Book. In the Book's back pages, past the accounts, are the other records: the years, the plagues, the weddings, the names of everyone who ever went down the bore, and against most of those names a small mark meaning *stayed*.

There is the *pool-keeper*, and here the mirror is at its most exact. Because the risers, every so often — once a month, once a season — deliver a passenger: a translucent shrimp, drawn through the screens below despite everything, arriving alive in the outfall basin, blind and pale and impossible in the sunlight, drifting in the surge like a small ghost of the deep. The custom is old and absolute: children net them — it is always children, it is a coveted duty — and carry them, cupped and shaded, to the Pale Pool: a covered cistern in the coldest cellar of the gauge house, dark, warm, mineral-fed from the main, where a colony of the deep's shrimp has now lived above ground for longer than the Book can say. The pool-keeper tends them, keeps the dark absolute, keeps the water breathing. No one has ever articulated why. But everyone on the terraces knows that two thousand feet below, beside a valve bank, a crew of pale strangers keeps a spread hand's worth of green moss alive under a lamp and feeds it bread — a piece of the surface, kept living in the deep — and everyone understands, without it ever being said, that the Pale Pool is the answer to the Garden. Each place keeps a small hostage of the other, tenderly, forever. It is the treaty, written in living things because the two peoples do not trust paper from strangers, only water and bread.

And the bread. Every dusk, when the cage goes down with the night shift, a basket goes with it — flat loaves, terrace-baked, part of no account in the Book, entered nowhere, paid for by no one. It is simply *the bread*, and it has gone down every dusk for fifty years, and most of it is baked by one old woman on the third terrace whose brother went down the bore half a century ago and stopped coming up on leave because, he wrote once, the dry air had begun to hurt him. He was a boy from fields. The terraces know — because the cage-keepers tell the Counters everything, slate by slate, year by year — that far below there is an office called the Gardener, held by a stooped, wordless old man who touches a hand's-breadth of green with two fingers at the start of every shift, and feeds it crumbs. She has never asked whether the crumbs are hers. He has never said. The bread goes down.

**The neighbors.**

At dusk the Throat's white plume turns black. The Smoke — the terraces' name for what the crew below calls the Roof — pours out of the bore in a soft ten-minute thunder, spirals once against the sunset, and spreads down over the terraces to feed. This is not scenery. This is the harvest's other half. The moths that would strip the crops rise off the fields into the dark and the Smoke settles them, night after night, all season, and the farmers stand in their doorways and watch the flickering over their own green and say nothing, because there is nothing to say about it any more than about rain. In the hour before dawn the Smoke pours home, heavy now, carrying the terraces inside it — sunlight turned to moth turned to bat, descending the bore to fall, in due course, as the slow gray snow that feeds the beetles, that feed the floor, that feeds the whole warm dark. The holders know this. They say it plainly, when they say it at all: *the fields go down every morning.* The Condensery is lit by lamps and has never seen the sun, and runs on sunlight even so, imported nightly, on wings.

So the rule holds up here too — has to, and does, the whole height of the pipe. *Nothing is poisoned on the flank.* Once, in a bad moth year within living memory, it was proposed; and the tenders came up the bore in a body, pale and blinking and furious in the terrible dry light, the only time in the Book the two crews ever stood shouting on the same ground; and the rule won, as the rule always wins, because a rule kept absolutely for long enough stops being policy and becomes character, and by now it is the character of the whole mountain. The holders lose a share of every crop to the moths and shrug the loss into the arithmetic: *the Smoke's breaths.* It appears in the Book that way, an account paid to an animal, entered without comment, every year.

The other neighbors keep the surface's hours. The swifts own the day the way the Smoke owns the night, screaming down the terrace faces, nesting in the dry-stone walls, and the holders read them as the crew reads the crickets — swifts flying low and silent mean weather off the west, and the sluices are set for it before any cloud shows. Lizards station themselves on the warm channel stones, a hand-span apart, the dry bright cousins of the pale things below, and are never disturbed. The wind is counted a resident — the oldest one, the landlord before the water came — and is spoken of in the singular and consulted like a difficult relative.

And below the gauge house, built directly over the junction where the risers meet the main — where the surge in the iron is loudest — stands a long low bunkhouse called, with the terraces' entire supply of irony, the Loud House. The retired tenders live there. They came up, career by career, from the wet amber dark, and found what every one of them was warned of and none believed: that the silence of the surface kept them awake, that a body paced for forty years by a four-beat breath cannot sleep without it. In the Loud House the Vine's pulse comes up through the floor, through the bedframes, through the sternum, *intake, compression, discharge, settle,* and the old tenders sleep like children. In the evenings they sit outside in the reddening light — pale still, always pale, the Warm never quite gives its color back — and watch the Smoke pour out of the Throat, and their lips move slightly, counting, and the terrace children sit among them and learn the deep's clipped crooning dialect the way other children learn lullabies.

**The largest resident.**

The holders call the Vine *her* too — the same *her*, and they know it. The iron main, the branching channels, the pulse in the stone: none of it is a separate creature. It is the Old Lady's arm, reached two thousand feet up through the mountain into the sunlight, and the terraces grow along her wrist. When, once a decade or so, the seals below must be changed and the engine is stilled for her long surgery, the terraces know it to the minute: the basin's surface goes flat, the channels sink to a trickle of stored reserve, the pulse leaves the stones, and the whole flank falls into a hush the holders call *the held breath* — sluices closed, voices lowered, the Loud House's old tenders standing out on the path facing the Throat, saying nothing, until the water shivers, and surges, and the beat resumes, and somebody on every terrace, every time, without embarrassment, cries.

**And the length of it.**

What truly lives on the Outfall Terraces is the same thing that lives below, seen from the other end: the arrangement — one circulation wearing two rooms of one house. Water rises and becomes barley and moth; moth becomes wing; wing becomes the slow gray snow of the deep; the deep sends up warmth and water and, spoonful by pale spoonful, its own new stone; bread goes down at dusk and shrimp come up alive in the surge; the Garden is kept below and the Pale Pool above; and along the whole two-thousand-foot length of the pipe between, everything — pale or brown, furred, finned, shelled, iron, green — keeps the same four-beat time.

They share the prophecy too, of course, from opposite ends. What the crew calls the Leaving, the terraces call the Drying: the far cold generation when the infant rock finishes its cooling, the plume off the Throat thins to nothing, and the water stops. The Book's very back pages hold the terraces' answer to it — the record of the Listening, the duty each generation owes: walkers sent out along the range with copper rods, pressing ear and rod to fissure after fissure, sleeping on the rock to feel it, hunting the next infant stone, the next warm breathing pocket, the next place young enough to be colonized in its infancy. Three days east, the current page says, there is a smoke off a ridge that was not there in the grandparents' time, and a warmth in a crack that the rods like the sound of. It has no name yet. Naming it is not this generation's work. Its entry in the Book is simply a small mark, the same mark set against the names of those who went down the bore and stayed: *there is somewhere to go.*

They do not dread the Drying, any more than the crew dreads the Leaving, and for the same reason: it is far off, and it only confirms what both ends of the pipe already know and the valleys never quite believe — that the tender and the holder, the Roof and the Smoke, the Garden and the Pale Pool, the old woman baking on the third terrace and the wordless old man two thousand feet beneath her feeding her crumbs to his moss, are the same kind of thing. Tenants of one warmth. Residents of one breath, met at its two ends.

For now the mountain exhales at the top and drinks at the bottom, the Smoke pours out at dusk and home at dawn, the basin rises and settles, rises and settles, eleven to the minute, and the terraces count without knowing they count.
