The Corrie That Ran Down

You reach it by walking, which is the first difference. An old mountain, this one — old the way a hand tool is old, everything sharp about it long since handled away. The scree lies down. The ridgelines are shouldered and soft. Lichen has claimed everything, grey-green rosettes on every face of every stone, and the trail is a real trail, made by feet and hooves and centuries, deep in places, stepped in others, kind. You crest the lip in ordinary afternoon light — light with a sun in it, light that will fail toward evening the way light is supposed to — and you are looking down into the corrie, and the first thing you hear is weather: wind combing heather, a burn running, a raven somewhere improvising.

And if you have stood in the other bowl — the young one, far off across the ranges, the one still hidden in its cloud — then the second thing arrives more slowly, the way a word's meaning arrives in a language you half know. Under the wind there is nothing. No floor to the sound. And the nothing has dimensions. It is a silence exactly the size and rhythm of a thud, and it comes up through the soles of your feet the same way the thud does, and you stand on the lip of the corrie hearing, with terrible clarity, the shape of what stopped.

## The Burn

Begin with the water, because the water tells you the regime changed.

A burn leaves the corrie. This should be said plainly, because in the sibling bowl it would be an impossibility on the order of a wound: water *departing*, over the lip at the open end, down the old mountain's flank, into a river with a name, and eventually into the sea. The corrie once held a fixed quantity of water in a closed loop, like coolant — the same droplets rinsing the same stones forever — and when the machine seized, the loop fell open, and the coolant simply started leaving, and it has been leaving for four and a half thousand years. Some of the original water is in the ocean now. Some of it has been cloud several thousand times. The corrie's first act as a mortal place was to begin spending itself, and it has never stopped, and it will never run out, because the regime that replaced the loop is weather: rain falls in, the burn runs out, an open ledger, drawing on the whole sky.

The waterfall still comes off the eastern wall — the same wall, the same line of descent, the design is the design — but it swells after rain and dwindles in drought and locks into blue-white pillars in hard winters, and no flourish in its spray has ever repeated. It strikes the old basin and the basin does not ring. There is a crack in it, hairline, running its full width, dating from the seizure. A cracked bell is just a bowl. The waterfall makes the sound water makes everywhere, which is to say a different sound every second of its existence.

## The Floor

The floor is deep in itself. That is the second difference, and it takes a spade to appreciate.

Peat. Two meters of it in the low ground, black and dense, laid down a hair's breadth a year since the stop — the corrie *keeps history now*, keeps it the way it once kept its floor clean, compulsively, totally. Cut into the peat and you read time in bands: pollen of willow, then birch, then heather; a fine line of ash from some far-off burning; the wing-cases of beetles; a lens of silt from a flood eleven centuries back. Debris, in the old bowl, was a scandal — one loose feather was a wound in the world. Here the entire floor is made of debris. The ground you walk on *is* yesterday, compacted, meters of yesterday, and the corrie adds to it continuously, leaf by leaf, death by death.

And at the very bottom of the peat, where the black gives way to stone, the peat-cutters know to expect two things.

The first is the stone itself: clean, black, sharp-cornered under the peat, unweathered, *kept* — the old floor, exactly as it was maintained, sterile as an instrument, preserved by burial in the condition of the last cycle. The corrie sits on its own past self the way a forest sits on bedrock.

The second is the feather band. A single thin stratum, corrie-wide, at the exact horizon where the peat begins: dark feathers, thousands of them, pressed flat, all deposited at once — and among them small bones with a machined smoothness that unsettles the cutters, bones that were never grown so much as *finished*. The band is the corrie's genesis layer. Below it, eternity. Above it, everything. Something came down out of the air here, all together, on one day forty-six centuries ago, and time has been accumulating on top of it ever since.

The old grid can still be found, if you know to look. The vegetation long ago blurred off its stations and seeded the gaps and became blanket bog, but at machined intervals across the floor there are rings of richer green — deeper grass, sweeter, where the sheep gather of their own accord — and the shepherds call them the fairy greens and think nothing of the fact that they sit at regular spacings, with tolerances. Those are the footprints of the green islands: the places where the banked fires burned. When the machine stopped, the spring that had been held — held at operating temperature, one instant of growing sustained for the whole of the corrie's sealed youth — was finally released, and each island completed its moment. One riotous decade, the pollen record says: willow scrub exploding into thicket, flowering, seeding, spending everything, and then, for the first time, autumn. Spring, released, becomes a year. A year, released, becomes a death. The fairy greens are the ash of the oldest fires, and the richest ground in the bowl.

## The Walls

The wall is a photograph of one beat.

The basalt columns seized mid-stroke — some extended half a meter proud of the wall, some withdrawn flush, some caught at every stage between — and they have stood in those positions ever since, so that the whole eastern face is a single frozen instant of the machine's cycle, readable if you know how. You can stand below it and reconstruct the last moment: these columns were rising, those falling, this one at the top of its travel, dwelling. The shepherds call it the Organ, for the look of it — pipes of different heights, a music arrested — and the name is better than they know, because an organ is exactly what it was: a wind instrument, breath-driven, tuned, and stopped between one note and the next.

The lichen dates it. Map lichen grows at a known crawl, and it could never grow on the sliding columns — a moving part is unclaimable — so every thallus on the Organ postdates the stop. The largest rosettes say forty-six centuries, give or take. The corrie's whole history is exactly as old as the oldest thing willing to grow on it.

The bright mineral veins have tarnished grey-green, and no light moves in them — with one exception, which the old people will tell you about if you sit with them long enough. Some winters, in hard frost, a faint blue worm of light crawls a few meters along a vein high on the north wall, and dims, and dies. It is always on the north wall, always in the unbroken stretches, and it always dies where the vein gaps. There is a last pulse still circulating somewhere in the deep closed loops of the mountain — residual charge, a final dispatch from a nervous system with nothing left to govern — and it survives only where the vein is whole, because the gaps have no jumpers now. The pale relays walked off their posts four and a half thousand years ago. The wall's last thought can no longer complete, and once in a winter you can watch it try.

One residue of the old physics pools near the Organ's base, in a pocket perhaps ten paces wide: clap your hands there and the echo returns a half-beat *early*, arriving before the clap the way footsteps once arrived before feet. The children of the valley dare each other to stand in it. Most last a few seconds. It is like being briefly ahead of yourself, they say, and they come out of it laughing and rubbing their arms.

## The Tarn

The basin is a tarn now, peat-brown, deep at the wall, and in its shallows lies the sleeper.

It never surfaced again. When the eleventh second failed to arrive — when the waterfall's leftward whip missed its cue for the first and last time — the back went down, or stayed down, and it lies now half-sunk near the eastern shore: four meters of smooth dark length, keel of spine just breaking the surface, mossed over, an island. No one has ever found a head. No one has ever found an under. Divers nest on it every summer, black-throated, wailing across the water at dusk, raising broods on the one component in the corrie too large for the peat to swallow. Whether it was ever alive remains exactly as unanswerable as it was when it moved. The difference is that now nothing depends on the answer. The birds nest on it either way.

There are char in the tarn — small, red-bellied, ancient of lineage, arrived the ordinary way, as eggs on the feet of birds, some spring not long after the stop when the corrie first became the kind of place things arrive at. They eat larvae. Trout would eat them if trout could get up the falls. The divers eat the char. The corrie has a food web now, arrows pointing everywhere, everything in the bowl finally a meal for something, which is the tax the open ledger charges, and also the whole of its glory.

## The Animals

The swifts nest. Begin there, because it is the profoundest sentence that can be said about them: their ancestors never landed, and they *nest* — in the crevices of the Organ, in the cracks the seizure opened, wet-eyed young in the dark of the stone, fed on midges, fledging clumsily into the bowl's airspace and flying badly for a fortnight, because they have to learn, because nothing about their flight is issued anymore. They improvise. They chase weather fronts. They leave in autumn — leave the corrie entirely, over the lip and south with the ordinary swifts of the ordinary world, and some of them die abroad and some return, and no two of their days are alike, and each of them carries its laps on a thread now, strung, remembered, a life instead of a turn.

But on certain still evenings — warm air, no wind, the light going long and low across the fairy greens — the flock rises all together, and for three or four seconds it happens: the circuits. Interleaved closed curves, wingbeat for wingbeat, the old timing diagram flickering through the species like a dropped habit briefly remembered, precise, silent, terrible. Then a midge column drifts through it and the whole thing dissolves into hunger and chatter. The shepherds call it the reel. Their grandparents said the reel used to hold for ten seconds; it holds for four now. It is a memory the blood is slowly spending, like the coolant, like everything here, and one distant summer it will not happen at all, and no one will be able to say which evening was the last one.

And inside the reel — every time, in every generation — the loops bend around one empty point of air near the eastern wall, and re-close behind it, as around a hull. Nothing is there. Nothing has been there for four and a half thousand years. The accommodation has outlived whatever it accommodated by geological time, and the swifts keep it without knowing they keep it, and this bend in the air is the corrie's only monument. Other places raise stones to their founders. The corrie's founder is commemorated by a swerve.

The salamanders are dark now — the pallor went out of the line within some centuries of the last current — and they live as salamanders live, under stones, damp and secretive, eating beetles, being eaten by herons. But each spring, on some cue no one has isolated, a few of the old ones climb the north wall and find the vein-gaps — the exact gaps, the ancestral posts — and lay their bodies across them, bellies to the stone, and wait. Nothing comes. The brightening never arrives. After an hour or a day they climb down and go eat something. It would be sentimental to call it faith and inaccurate to call it anything else, and the one winter phenomenon the old people most want to see before they die is a salamander lying across a whole vein on the night the blue worm crawls — the last pulse completing one gap, one time, through the great-great-descendant of its jumper. It has never been witnessed. The seasons are wrong for it. That is what weather costs.

The moths eat willow. There are caterpillars — the corrie has *larvae*, has generations stacked inside single summers — and pipits eat the caterpillars, and the moths still cluster at dusk on the seized columns of the Organ, riding nothing, out of a habit older than the peat. They tremble there in their dozens, the fine shiver in the wing-fur, and here is the small mercy the run-down bought them: the trembling readies flight now. It means what it means everywhere. A creature warming its engine because it is about to *do* something. When a heron's shadow crosses the wall, the moths go up like grey ash, every one of them spending its shiver on the air.

## The Stop

How it happened is reconstruction and hearsay, but the evidence agrees with itself, and it agrees with what a visitor to the other bowl already suspects.

The feather band tells you the end came from the air, and came in one day: two hundred and six swifts down at once, components without a machine, litter of an eternity. The Organ tells you the seizure was mid-cycle, unclean, cracks radiating from the columns caught farthest out of the wall. And the swerve — the inherited bend in the reel — tells you that before the end there was already one living thing in the airspace, one open system among the closed ones, already being routed around.

So the corrie's people, who have had four thousand years to think about it, tell it this way. One of the swifts wore. Its loop drifted, a feather's-width a lap, an arc-second a year, a closed curve precessing around the bowl like a clock hand geared to centuries — and the arithmetic of a drift is open, and open arithmetic always completes. Some lap, its curve touched a neighbor's. First contact in the history of the airspace. Feathers. A wobble propagating through the timing diagram, circuits shaving circuits, the interleaving — which had no procedure for damage, because damage is change — tearing itself apart over who knows how long, hours or years, until the great flywheel below, which had never in its existence received a single input, received one. And stopped. Mid-thud. Mid-thought.

The other bowl's own elegy imagines a gentler ending: the worn swift simply expiring, ages hence, the circuits redistributing, the machine running on complete and uninhabited. Perhaps that is how it will go there. The corrie is the proof of the other completion — the drift crosses a neighbor before the wearing finishes, and the machine catches life like a fever and dies of it. And what the fever leaves is not a gap and not an empty engine. It is this. It is peat and char and divers wailing at dusk. The living swift, the corrie's people say, survived the day it caused — the only thing in the bowl with a self to improvise with — and starved, and learned hunger, and learned midges, which were already arriving on the first weather the moment the bowl fell open, because the ordinary world floods a leak the instant there is one. Seeds on the wind. Spiders on silk. Ordinary swifts, blown in on a front, finding a bowl of stone full of insects and one strange, dull, drifting bird that flew like a memory. The reel is what's left of it in the blood. The swerve is where it isn't.

## The People and the Neighbor

There are shielings on the floor, and drystone folds on the fairy greens, and a bothy with a good roof near the tarn, and — say it plainly, because it is the whole verdict — there are graves. A small walled plot on a rise above the burn, headstones going back thirty generations. The corrie takes the dead now. That is what running down bought. No bowl that keeps its cycles can hold a grave, because a grave is a thing that is *kept missing*, and missing is a relation only the living sustain, and the corrie sustains hundreds of them, tenderly, under the turf, in the black archive it makes of every year.

On the bothy windowsill someone has left a swift's feather, dark and small, and no one knows or wonders which bird it came off of, because feathers are litter here. They drift against the fold walls in August. The children fletch toy arrows with them. The sibling bowl's one impossible export — its single shipped unit, the lone proof smuggled down through the cloud that something in it was alive — is, in the corrie, what the ground is made of. Two meters deep.

And on still nights of hard frost, when the burn runs quiet under ice and the wind lies down, you can go out onto the peat above the dead flywheel and lie on your back, and feel it. Faint, regular, arriving through miles and miles of bedrock, up through the old mountain's roots from a young mountain far across the ranges: *thud... thud... thud*. The neighbor, the valley people call it, and have always called it. The mountain's brother, the one that never sleeps. The two bowls were cast from the same catalogue — the same eastern waterfall, the same forty gaps in the veins, the same back in the basin, the same count of circuits in the air — and they have never touched and never will, and the stone between them carries the one message the running one has, forever, to the one that ran down.

The tempo of the thud is close to a resting human heartbeat. Close enough that everyone who lies out on the frost-peat has the same experience: for the first minute you cannot tell whether you are feeling the neighbor through the rock or your own pulse in your ears, the running machine or the living body, and you lie there in the dark between an engine that will never die and a ground made entirely of things that did, trying to sort out which beat is which.

The old people say don't bother. They say the corrie settled that question the day it stopped, and settled it in favor of everything now asleep on its floor — the char under the ice, the salamanders in their stones, the moths as eggs in the willow bark, the divers gone south, the dead in their plot, the sheep in the fold, you on the peat. The neighbor keeps time, they say. We spend it. That is the whole difference and the whole of the kinship, and the mountain sends its brother the only answer it has: not a thud, having no engine — just all of those heartbeats at once, small and unsynchronized and mortal, running down all night long, which is the local word for running.
