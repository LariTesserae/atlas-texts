**The Keeping Fen**

You come down off it through the same cloud, because there is no other way down, and for most of a day the world is the wet grey inside of that cloud and the plateau's flank under your hands. This is worth noticing, because the flank is the only place the mountain exists. Above, it is a rumor beneath the heath; below, as you will learn, it is a rumor beneath the water; but here, on the descent, for a thousand vertical meters of dark seeping stone, the mountain is simply itself — naked, cold, streaming. Every crack of it runs with water. This is the heath's spillage, the fraction it loses to wind and gravity, and it comes down the walls in a hundred cold threads that have the strange taste of nothing at all: water that has been through a machine, filtered by ten thousand living vessels, stripped of every mineral the heath could hold back. You drink it on the way down and it is like drinking the idea of water.

Then the cloud's underside opens beneath your feet — you drop through the floor of it as through a ceiling — and the first thing that reaches you is not sight but sound.

After days on the heath you have forgotten what a continuous noise is. It arrives as a low wide mutter, still far below: water moving, and something like wind in dry grass, and under those, at intervals, a clamor you cannot yet identify and will turn out to be birds. The fen is audible from an hour above itself. By the time you reach the base of the wall, where the hundred threads gather into springs among the boulders, the mutter has resolved into a landscape.

The fen fills a basin at the plateau's foot, miles across, and it is floored with the mountain. That is the plain fact underneath everything here, and it takes a while to assemble: the grit in the channels, the pale sandbars, the grey clay you sink into at the margins — all of it is the old peak, the substance that washed off this altitude ages before the first cushion of the heath ever swelled. The mountain did not disappear. It lay down. It moved here a grain at a time and spread itself out flat, and the water came and lay on top of it, and so the plateau and the basin are the same rock in two conditions — one buried under work, one buried under water — and you have walked from one of its graves to the other.

On the mountain's flat body the fen has grown itself in the opposite spirit to everything above the cloud. Where the heath is dense, the fen is open: black pools lying like dropped mirrors between reed-beds the color of tarnish, veined through with slow channels that go where slowness takes them. Where the heath is silent, the fen talks constantly — the wind that the ten thousand domes baffled comes down the wall and runs loose across the basin, and the reeds take it and turn it into a long dry seething that never entirely stops. And where the heath is thrifty past obsession, the fen is a spendthrift of a kind that would scandalize the plateau if the plateau could be scandalized. Everything here sheds and lies. Dead reeds lean, fall, and stay fallen. Rot happens in the open air, in a broad brown generous smell — nothing like the heath's contained vinegar heat, no lacquered pits, no accounting — just decay lying out in public like laundry. Midges stand in grey columns above the pools, uncounted, unconsumed, spent into the air by the million. The fen wastes because it has never once, in its whole existence, been poor. Wealth comes down the wall every hour of every day; wealth lies a hand's depth under every channel. Thrift never had a reason to evolve.

That is the surface, and the surface is honest as far as it goes. But stand at a cut bank — the channels undercut their margins, and here and there a black face of the fen's own body is exposed, two meters, three meters of it — and you find the second fact, the one the noise and the squandering are only a skin over.

The fen keeps everything.

The cold water and the acid of the reeds seal what they cover. Nothing down there decays all the way. The cut bank is a stratigraphy of everything that has ever fallen: reed-layers fine as pages, seasons legible like rings, a dark band of wing-cases where some ancient summer's insects came down all at once, lenses of pale bird-bone under the old colony grounds. You can push your fingers into a layer and pull out a reed stem, whole, flexible, a thousand years drowned, and it will smell faintly of the summer it grew in. The fen forgets nothing. It cannot. Forgetting, here, would require the one thing the fen has never developed — a way to destroy — and so its entire past is still on the premises, filed in order, a few feet down.

And in the layers, if you look — you will look, once you know to — there are the grey flakes.

They come off the heath. Storms cross the plateau a few times a year, and the wind takes what the heath's economy cannot chase: flakes of the grey bark that sheathes the great hauling cables, papery scales off the domes, a dust of the demolitions. It blows off the rim, falls through the cloud, and settles on the fen, and the fen receives it the way it receives everything, and covers it, and keeps it. The heath consumes its own dead so completely that it has no fossil record, no rings older than its living mounds, no history it could be said to possess — except here. Every era of the plateau's labor is represented in the peat by its windblown flakes, layer under layer, going down past the reach of any cutting. The heath has demolished and rebuilt every district of itself ten thousand times, and only the fen knows. The most self-erasing place in the world keeps its whole biography in the body of its neighbor, and neither of them is aware of the arrangement. It is possible that this is what the two places are, together: one economy with two ledgers. The heath keeps no waste and no memory. The fen keeps all of both.

The light, before the inhabitants: the cloud that reads as plain high overcast from the heath is, from below, a roof. The fen lives all day in a pewter dimness, shadowless, the pools grey, the reeds grey-gold. But the roof does not reach the horizon, and twice a day the sun gets in under its eastern or western rim and runs horizontally across the basin for most of an hour — a low amber light that ignites every pool at once and puts a long blue shadow behind every reed, so that the fen, which spends the whole day muttering in monochrome, ends and begins it in brief unreasonable glory. The heath never sees this. Above the cloud there is only the honest grey brightness. The gold belongs to the underneath.

**The Inhabitants of the Keeping Fen**

The fen's temperament, first, because as on the heath it sets the terms — and it is hospitable in the manner of an archive: everything is admitted, and nothing is ever returned. The heath registered you as an entry, compensated for your weight, and forgot you. The fen does not forget you. It cannot. Your first footprint at the western margin filled with water behind you and is being kept, and everything you shed while you are here — a hair, a thread, the print of your sleeping body in the reeds — has already been accessioned. The fen notices its guests the way a diary notices days: not with attention, but by containing them permanently.

Its tenants are legion, which is itself the contrast. The heath tolerated three grey riders in a day's walk and one bird at a time; the fen billows with lives it never counts — the midge columns, the small striped fish that stack themselves in the warm eastern channels, frogs whose evening chorus is the loudest single thing under the cloud, a heavy placid wading creature that grazes the reed-roots and is eaten, eventually, by nothing, and simply lies down one day and joins the record. Most of these can be honored collectively. Three cannot.

The dark birds live at the fen's center, on a mound, and the mound is the colony — literally: generations of bones and guano and collapsed nests, compacted and grown over and built upon, so that the birds stand on a hill made entirely of their own ancestors, the tallest thing in the basin after the reeds. At home they are nothing like the solitary silent pilgrim you watched taking heat at the vent. At home they are a market, a parliament, a riot — they call from before the under-light of dawn until after the under-light of dusk, and their clamor is the fen's signature noise, the one you heard from an hour up the wall. They fish the channels; they rake the midge columns on the wing. And among them, at any given time, there is one who holds the climb.

It is held the way the riders hold their cables: as a line, an inheritance, a territory with no area. One bird knows the route up through the cloud — the ledges, the updrafts along the seeping wall, the vent-fields of the plateau above — and before it goes it does the thing that makes the whole silent trade run: it walks out onto a particular pale bar of sand in the western channels and fills its gizzard with grit. The bar is the mountain. The grit is the old peak's own substance, and the salts and the phosphorus in the bird's gut are the fen's wealth, concentrated through fish and insect into a few portable grams. So the courier that climbs to the heath is carrying the mountain home — returning, at the rate of one gizzard-load a month, a measurable fraction of nothing at all, the very minerals that washed off that altitude before either place existed. Given enough time — and both places have nothing else — the fen is sending the mountain back up, and the heath is building it into its living body, and the peak that eroded into a basin is being reconstructed, atom by atom, as plant. The bird does not know this. The bird knows the warm vapor, which is the payment, and the long climb, which is the price. When it comes back down through the cloud it smells of the vents — a warm, faintly green smell that no other fen-thing carries — and for some days it is quiet, and stands a little apart on the ancestral mound, and the young birds come and stand near it, close but not touching, the way one stands near someone who has been somewhere.

The channels have their own nation: black, patient eels that come up the fen's one slow outlet from whatever lies at its far end — the outlet leaves the basin's eastern rim and goes down into country this account does not reach, which is only to say that the world continues, and that the fen, like the heath, is a single knot in a longer rope. The eels arrive small and translucent and leave, years later, heavy and dark, and while they are resident they are the fen's chief readers: they work the cut banks, nosing the exposed layers, eating the drowned centuries a little at a time. They are the only inhabitant that consumes the archive, and they do it so slowly the archive does not notice.

And then the pale band, which is the fen's mystery, as the smoothed lanes were the heath's. In every cut bank in the basin, at the same depth — well below arm's reach, old past all reckoning — the record stops. For the width of a hand there is nothing: no reed, no wing-case, no bone, no grey flake off the heath. Only a fine grey mineral silt, sterile and even, as though for one season or one century the fen received nothing from the world but stone. Something happened. The wall calved, or the cloud failed, or the heath, in some ancient extremity, vented itself catastrophically down onto its neighbor — the flakes stop below the band and resume above it, so the heath, whatever it endured, endured. No living thing knows what the band records. The fen kept it anyway. That is what keeping means: the archive does not require that anyone be able to read it.

Which brings the count, at last, to the resident that fills the place the way the labor filled the heath. Up there, the truest inhabitant was the work — present tense, continuous, its materials endlessly replaced while it went on unbroken. Down here it is the opposite tenant. The past lives in the fen the way cold lives in deep water: not a thing in the place but the thing the place is made of, layer under layer, tenured, complete. Nothing that ever lived here has ever entirely left. The fen's materials are all still on the premises. The heath is a verb that keeps no record; the fen is the record of a place that cannot stop revising itself, and between them, above and below one cloud, they make a single finished sentence that neither of them can read.

You live here too, for a few days, while you skirt the pools and sleep in the reeds. And where the heath gave you its one acknowledgment on the morning you left — your route revised overnight, without malice, without ceremony — the fen gave you its acknowledgment the moment you arrived, though you will not understand it until you are going. Your footprints along the western margin have filled with water and settled. This year's reeds will fall across them; silt will follow; the layer will close. Somewhere in that same layer, blown down on the last storm, there is a grey flake off one of the great cables you laid your cold hand on, a mile above the cloud — and there the two of you will be filed together, the traveler and the heath, side by side in the black patience of the peat, kept by the one place in the world that keeps, for longer than anything else will hold any part of either of you.
