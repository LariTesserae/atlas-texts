# The Rain-Vault

You cannot reach it by going anywhere, which is the first strangeness, because everything about the place insists that it is *near* — under your feet, under the fox's feet, under the pale slab and the scrubland and the country for a day's walk in every direction. No one has stood in it. No one will. It is known the way a heart is known: by its beat arriving at the surface.

There are two ways the surface hears it. The first is the pool in the Sun-Cistern, which is deeper than it can be, and which never empties, and which sends up, once a year, a gold salamander to look at the light. The second is a crack in the open desert, half a day east of the tilted slab — a fissure in a low rib of rock, no wider than a forearm, that breathes. In the heat of the day it exhales: a steady cool wind smelling of wet stone and enormous age, air that has not seen the sun in longer than the sun has had names. When the weather turns and the sky presses down, the crack inhales instead, drinking the desert's air with a low hollow patience. The people have come to it for a very long time. They do not leave anything there. They come to stand in the breath, in the worst of the summers, and to say the name, which in every language that has held it means roughly the same thing: *the place where the rain waits.*

Below the crack, below the breath, below more stone than there is anything else in this country, the chamber lies. And it is *vast*.

The room is dark. That must be said first, because it is the whole condition of the place, the way the glow is the whole condition of the little chamber above. Not the amber dimness of the cistern's worst hour, which is a lit room to a cricket's eye — this is the other thing, the original thing, darkness with no bottom and no memory of an alternative. The vault has never been light. It has never been loud. There is no eye in its ceiling and never was; the ceiling is somewhere overhead, unmeasured, known to the things that live there only as the origin of drips. The chamber's size cannot be seen and so is known by ear: a drip falling at the far end takes its own time arriving, and arrives softened, rounded, considered — the same courtesy the little dome upstairs extends to a voice, performed here across a space you could not shout across.

Most of the floor is water.

## The Lake

It fills the vault nearly wall to wall, black beyond black, still beyond still — the pool of the Sun-Cistern grown to a nation. It does not lap. It has no wind to answer and no shore worth the name, only a narrow hem of stone beach on one side, sloping up into terraced rimstone, dry basins stacked like abandoned steps, marking where the water stood in richer ages and stands no longer.

The water is old. That is the plainest and largest fact about it. It is rain — actual rain, fallen from actual clouds — but the clouds are gone ten thousand years. It fell in the green age, when the country above was not scrubland but savanna, when rivers moved on the surface in the open sun, and one of those rivers, working at a weakness in the pale rock, carved a throat and opened a round eye and laid down a floor of overlapping travertine fans, smooth as frozen cream, in a small domed chamber that would one day hold light the way a bowl holds milk. The vault's water *made* the Sun-Cistern. It is the same water. It cut the entrance you slide beneath; it polished the floor the geckos hold their territories on; it wore the oculus open, patiently, from beneath and above at once, and then — the world drying around it, the rivers failing one by one — it withdrew. It went down the ways it had made, pooled in the deepest room it had ever hollowed, and sealed itself in behind its own slow masonry. The little chamber it left behind stood empty for a while. Then the sun moved in.

So the two places stand in the oldest relation there is: the vault is the maker, and the cistern is the made — the finished room, the abandoned house, occupied now by a tenant of light. And the pool in the cistern's floor, the one fed by nothing you can see, is the door left unlocked between them. It goes down. It narrows into a flooded seam, a black thread through the rock, hours of swimming for anything that could swim it, and it opens at last in the vault's ceiling, underwater, a small drowned oculus facing the wrong way — the vault's one eye, and it looks at darkness, and once a year something goes up through it.

The lake is not fed. That must also be said. The rains of the country above are thin and rare and mostly the stone drinks them before they get far. What the vault holds is savings — the wealth of a climate that will not come again — and it is spending. It spends into the breathing crack, exhaling its own substance as vapor, grain by invisible grain. It spends into deep seeps no one will ever trace. It has been spending for a hundred centuries and may spend for a hundred more; the drawdown is written on the walls in those stacked dry terraces, each one a former surface, each one a little lower than the last. The vault is mortal. It is simply mortal at a speed that no living thing inside it can perceive, and so its residents live in a stillness that looks, from any life's vantage, exactly like forever.

And here is the fine strange justice of the arrangement, the fact the people's tellings circle without quite landing on: some of what the vault exhales comes home. The moist old breath rises through the fissured stone, and part of it finds the cool lower walls of the Sun-Cistern and beads there, and the beads gather into threads, and the threads run their grooves down to the pool — the pool that never empties, fed by nothing you can see. It is fed by the vault. The great dark room waters the small bright one from below, through the air, and the small bright one gives back down the seam whatever falls through its eye. The rain-vault is the cistern's hidden rain. The two chambers have been passing the same water back and forth, breath and drip, for ten thousand years, the way the swifts and the bats pass each other in the oculus — opposite spirals, never colliding, one slow wringing of the same cloth.

## What Grows

Nothing green. Green is a habit of the sun's, and the sun has no habits here. What grows in the Rain-Vault is stone, and it grows the way everything in either chamber does anything: slowly, and without ceasing.

From the unseen ceiling hang straws of calcite, hollow as reeds, thin as the wick of a lamp, some of them the length of a standing man — each one the fossil of a path of drips, growing a ring at a time, one ring per age of dripping. Where the drips have been generous the straws have thickened into columns and pale curtains, folded like cloth, and if the vault could be lit even once the light would pass through their thinnest folds as through skin. It never will be lit. The curtains are the most delicate visible things in the world that no eye has ever seen, and there is a completeness in that, a sufficiency, that the place seems to rest inside.

On the lake's surface, where the water is most saturated and most still, the stone grows *flat*: rafts of calcite, thin as frost, form on the very skin of the water and ride there, white on black, growing outward from their own edges. When a drip strikes one it rings — a note, small and clean, the vault's only music — and when a raft grows too heavy for the water's skin it lets go and rocks down through the dark to pave the bottom. In the shallow drip-cups along the shore, grains of grit have been rolled and coated and rolled again until they have become perfect stone pearls, dozens to a cup, each one wearing centuries in layers you could count. The vault furnishes its own rooms. It has no visitors to furnish them for. It does it anyway, or rather the water does, having never learned to stop building simply because it stopped having anywhere to go.

And on the lower walls, at and under the waterline, grows the thing that everything else here eats: the mats. They are films and cushions and slow shelves of living matter, pale grey, pale gold, colorless-on-colorless, and they are the strangest quiet fact of the vault, the mirror of the moss-ring above. The moss in the Sun-Cistern feeds on stored light — photosynthesizing at midnight by the wall's slow exhale of the previous noon. The mats have gone one further. They feed on the stone itself, on its minerals and its chemistry, on the faint riches the old water strips from the rock — life run on rock instead of sun, the one hearth in all this country that owes the daylight almost nothing.

Almost. Because the seam is open, and through it, settling down from the pool above, comes the snow.

It is a very thin snow and a very slow one: a fig leaf that spiraled through the oculus on some autumn wind; a swift's molted feather; the husk of a cricket; the fine golden dust of the scrubland, sifted through a wagon-wheel of sky, drifting down through the bright pool, into the seam, down the black thread, and out at last through the drowned eye into the lake, where it descends for a long time and settles on the mats and the mud like a tithe. The Sun-Cistern keeps light for hours and gives it back. The sun reaches the Rain-Vault too — but as fallen things, years late, transformed past recognition, the light arriving only after it has finished being light. The two places are the same act performed at two speeds. Receive everything. Lose nothing quickly.

## What Was Left

No one built here, and no one came, and so what was left was left by the world.

On the highest dry terrace, above even the oldest waterline, there is a shelf of bones. They washed in during the green age, down the throats the river kept open then, and stranded on the ledge when the water fell: the long skull of a crocodile, ribs of some great grazing beast, the small precise skeleton of a bird that flew over a savanna that no longer exists. The dripping ceiling has been at work on them the whole time since. They are half stone now, glazed and joined to the shelf, the crocodile's grin filmed in calcite like a thing preserved in candle-wax, and in another age they will not be bones on stone but figures *in* it, the way the ibex hoofprints upstairs have passed from mud into travertine. The vault does not keep relics. It incorporates. What arrives, stays, and what stays, slowly, becomes the room.

The people, who have never seen any of this, are nonetheless in the tellings exactly right about its nature. The light waits in the little room, they say; the rain waits in the great one; the pool is the door between them, and the gold swimmer is the messenger. They blow their ochre hands around their fingers in the chamber the old water built, and they stand in the water's breath at the crack in the desert, and between those two courtesies they have the whole geometry of the thing, held in story the way the vault holds rain.

## Sound and Time

It is mostly quiet, but it is never silent, and this is the vault's inversion of the cistern's law. Upstairs, nothing is ever fully dark. Down here, nothing is ever fully still: somewhere across the black water there is always a drip on its way, and the drips are the whole visible face of time. Each one falls from a straw it is lengthening, into water it is not replacing, and rings against a raft it is thickening, and the rings cross the lake and return off unseen walls, so that the vault continuously describes its own shape to itself in a language of small collisions. The residents read it fluently. In a place with no light there is no seeing, but there is nothing hidden either; every arrival, every movement, every letting-go of a heavy raft is announced to the whole nation at the speed of sound in old water.

Time otherwise is written, not enacted. There is no crossing disk, no filling and spending of walls, no dawn to doubt. Time here is laminae in the flowstone, rings in the straws, layers on the pearls, terraces on the walls — time as ledger rather than pulse. The cistern breathes once a day. The vault breathes once an age, and it is exhaling now, and has been exhaling since the green world ended, and its residents live inside that single endless out-breath the way the geckos upstairs live inside an afternoon.

Only one thing here keeps a faster clock, and it is the vault's whole calendar: once a year, at the drowned eye in the ceiling, the water stirs.

# Who Lives in the Rain-Vault

Begin with the inversion the place itself insists on: the Sun-Cistern is a well, and many things drink at it and leave — the fox, the swifts, the people with their ten thousand years of visiting. The Rain-Vault has no visitors. Nothing has ever come down to it that did not stay; nothing uses it, because to be in it at all is already to belong to it entirely. It is a place without guests — with one exception, and the exception is the most important resident it has, because she is the only one who leaves.

## The Hearth

Lowest and first, the mats. They are the vault's fire, its moss-ring, its whole agriculture: the pale living shelves that eat the stone's chemistry and are eaten in turn by everything above them. They have no shape a hand would recognize as a creature and no age anyone could assign — they may be, in the sense that matters here, a single tenant older than the lake's own stillness, growing and dissolving and growing, one thing wearing a succession of bodies, which is, as the people noticed of the salamander upstairs, simply how these chambers do living. Where the snow from the seam settles on them — the leaf-fall of the sunlit world, arriving years late — the mats grow richest, and the richest mats draw the thickest congregations, so that the fig tree in the scrubland, without ever knowing it, feeds a nation at the bottom of the earth with its discarded leaves. The vault's food runs on stone; but its feasts run on the sun, delayed past all recognition. Everything here that grows fat, grows fat on the memory of daylight.

## The Nation of the Shore and the Deep

The shrimp are beyond counting here too, but they are the elders of their kind: the little transparent grazers in the cistern's pool are a parish, a colony, an upstairs congregation of this parent nation. Now and then — after the rare great rains, when the seam surges — a drift of larvae is carried up the black thread and spilled into the bright pool, where their descendants will live out generations in a place with a sky, grazing by the light of a stone that remembers noon, never knowing they are provincials of a darkness. The traffic runs the other way as well: pool-born shrimp are drawn down the seam and arrive in the lake like immigrants from a legend, carrying in their transparent bodies the faint different chemistry of sunlit water. The two populations have never diverged into strangers. The door has always been open. They are one people with two countries, and the seam is their road, and neither country knows it is only half of home.

The snails work the waterline and the drip-cups, and the pearls are their neighbors; there are blind white isopods on the beach of stone, unhurried as everything, tending the hem where the mats meet the air. Nothing among them hunts urgently, and nothing flees far. The vault has room, and time, and the mats do not run out. It is a nation without famine and without seasons, and its politics, if it has any, are conducted entirely in the placement of grazing circles, negotiated over decades, audible to everyone.

## The Pale Nation

The salamanders are the vault's great folk, and they are white — white past white, the unpigmented flesh of animals whose line gave up color when color lost its last witness. They are eyeless. Not blind the way the crickets upstairs almost became blind, stalled halfway by a room that refused to go dark: the vault refused nothing, offered nothing, and the descent that stalled in the cistern ran to completion here. Their eyes are sealed under skin, small sparks long since banked; in their place they carry the lake itself as a sense — every tremor in the water, every drip's ring, every raft's letting-go arrives along their sides as plainly as sight. They live to enormous ages. The elders among them have felt the waterline itself descend within their own lifetimes, terrace to terrace, and are the only residents old enough to know, in their bodies, that the vault is spending itself — and they are untroubled by it, or they carry the trouble the way the crickets upstairs carry theirs: on behalf of everything else, quietly, in the hour when no one is listening.

They hang in the black water for days without moving. They gather, sometimes, in loose congregations near the drowned eye in the ceiling, faces tilted up at an opening they cannot see and could not pass — and if that posture sounds familiar, it should. It is the exact posture of the gold one at the surface of the pool upstairs, looking at the oculus. It is the posture of the household, learned from the pilgrim, facing the direction the important thing comes from.

## The Pilgrim

Here is the answer to the question the people of the Sun-Cistern stopped asking.

Every so often — once in a long lifetime of the pale nation — a salamander is hatched wrong, or hatched right, depending on where you stand. The skin over its eyes thins instead of thickening. Pigment comes into it slowly, over years, mottled gold rising through the white like dawn coming up through milk. The elders know these hatchlings by feel and by chemistry before any difference shows, and the nation does not shun them; it *keeps* them, guides them without seeming to, herds them gently over decades toward the deep center of the lake and the eye in the ceiling, because everyone in the vault knows, in whatever way a nation of the blind can know anything, what a gold one is for.

She is for the door.

When she is grown — and she grows larger than any of the pale folk, and older — she makes the first ascent: up through the drowned oculus, into the seam, hours in the black thread with the rock close around her, following the water's grain upward, until the thread opens and the water above her goes *strange* — thinner-tasting, warmer, and then, unthinkably, *bright*. She rises through the deep pool of the Sun-Cistern into the only lit water in her world, and puts her blunt gold face at the surface, and opens the only working eyes her nation owns, and looks at the eye in that other ceiling — the round dry oculus with the sky in it, the thing her whole people face without seeing, the source of the snow that feeds their feasts, the tenant that lives in the house their water built.

Then she goes home, and the vault receives her the way the vault receives everything: completely. The pale nation gathers — this is the one event, the calendar's single mark, the vault's swift-rush and dawn-chorus in one — and they hang around her in the dark and read her. She carries the visit in her body: the chemistry of sunlit water in her skin, the taste of the moss-ring's runoff, warmth still leaving her, and something else, something the elders drift closest to take — whatever it is that light leaves in flesh that has stood in it. She is the vault's one sense that reaches the sun, an eye on the end of a year-long nerve, and through her the nation that has never seen keeps, at one remove and once a year, its acquaintance with the day.

The people upstairs wondered whether the gold salamander was one animal or a lineage, and stopped asking, having noticed that everything in the cistern is both. They were right to stop, and here is the answer they didn't need: a lineage, and one animal — one office, worn by a succession of bodies, exactly like the swift's nest and the moss-ring and the pool and the light itself. When a pilgrim grows old, a hatchling somewhere in the black water is already thinning its eyelids, already going gold beneath the white. There has never been a year without one. There has never been a year the pool was not visited. The vault keeps its appointment with the room it made the way the sun keeps its appointment with the eye.

And this is the vault's one uncertainty — the mirror of the crickets' dawn-doubt, the single crack in its forever. The pale nation lives inside a stillness where nothing is ever lost, but once a year something *leaves*, up through the ceiling, into a world of dryness and light and swiftness, and is gone for days, and the nation hangs in the water below the drowned eye and waits. The crickets upstairs sing to the dimming, appointed worriers for a light that always returns. The pale folk keep their vigil at the eye, appointed worriers for a pilgrim who always has. So far. In both chambers, at the joint where the certain world touches the uncertain one, something faithful stands watch — and in both chambers, so far, the watch has always ended in relief.

## The Ones Becoming the Room

The bones on the high terrace must be counted among the inhabitants, as the fig root is counted upstairs, and for the same reason: an arm that has been doing one thing for centuries is no longer merely part of something else. The crocodile has been becoming stone for a hundred centuries and is more resident now than anything that merely lives here — it is on its way to being the vault the way the hoofprints upstairs are on their way to being the floor. It drank from this water when this water ran in the sun. It is the only body in the vault that ever saw daylight with its own eyes, and it lies now on the highest shelf, glazed and grinning, above a nation of the blind, the darkness's one native memory of the green age. The dripping ceiling attends to it a layer at a time. Nothing here would call that burial. It is the opposite: it is being taken in.

## The One the Others Are Made Of

And last — because it is not *in* the vault the way the others are; it is the vault, the way the kept light is the cistern — there is the resident everything else here lives inside.

Every animal in the Rain-Vault, like every animal upstairs, has one behavior its biology does not require, and here too they all face the same direction. The pale nation gathers under the drowned eye. The pilgrim rises to look at a light she cannot live in. The mats grow richest where the sun's discarded leaves come down. The people, who have never touched this place, walk half a day into the desert to stand in its breath. Each has its plain explanation, and the plain explanations are probably true. Taken together, they are all forms of attention paid to the same thing.

What lives most fully in the Rain-Vault is the old water itself — rain, ordinary rain, the same that falls wasted on all the world, distinguished only by having been *kept*. It fell in the green age and never left. It built, in its running youth, a small domed room with an eye in its roof and a floor like frozen cream, and then the world dried and it went down into the deepest of its own works and has lain there since, doing in the dark, with rain, exactly what its old house does in the light of noon: receiving everything, and losing nothing quickly. It has a household, and the household is made of it and fed through it and speaks in its drips; it grows its slow white furniture; it breathes — one breath per age — out through the crack where the people stand, up through the stone to bead on the walls of its own first masterpiece, so that the pool in the Sun-Cistern, fed by nothing you can see, is the old water still tending the room it built, still keeping it, drop by drop, from ever standing empty.

It is not a spirit. Nothing haunts the vault, nothing watches from it, nothing wants. It is water, and it is spending, and one distant day — long after the last pilgrim, long after the crocodile has finished becoming the shelf — the great room will stand dry, terraces to the floor, and its straws will hang silent over stone pearls in empty cups, and even that will only be the vault doing what both chambers have always done, at the tempo proper to each. The little room fills and spends daily and calls it breathing. The great room filled once and is spending across ten thousand years and calls it nothing at all, having no crickets to sing the worry and no dawn to end it.

But that day is not near, and nearness is the only distance the vault acknowledges. For now the lake lies black and brimming under its unseen ceiling, and the rafts ring under the drips, and the pale nation hangs in the still water reading the room with their sides, and somewhere in the dark a hatchling's eyelids are thinning, gold rising through the white. Upstairs, the sun stands in the eye and the walls drink; downstairs, the walls weep and the pool holds; and between them, along a black thread of water and a rising column of breath, the two chambers go on keeping each other — the maker and the made, the rain and the light, each doing the only thing it has ever done, which is to hold what the world let go of, and give it back so slowly that the giving looks, from inside any single life, exactly like keeping it forever.
