**Underfall**

The city at the foot of the rim has a proper name, and a spare, and a civic drawer full of spares, which is fitting, because names are one of the things that pile up down here.

Underfall is this generation's name. It will be retired in due course — not because it wears out, nothing wears out in Underfall, but because new names keep arriving, and it is considered wasteful, almost impious, to let a good one lie unused. The city has been Brackenmouth, Ninefonts, Sillow, the Ladle, Roke. It has been called things no throat now living can pronounce, and those names are kept too, written in the ledgers, because down here everything is written in the ledgers. Down here, if a thing is not written down, insisted upon, repeated aloud, it is simply gone — and *gone*, in Underfall, means gone, in a way the country upstream has forgotten the meaning of.

---

**The falls**

Stand anywhere in the city and look up and there is no sky. There is the cloud floor: the underside of the permanent skirts that wrap the great plateau's rim, a lid of brown-grey vapor sealing off the upper world, lamplit faintly from beneath by the city's own glow. And out of that lid, along the whole northern horizon, comes the water.

It does not come as one fall. It comes as a fringe, a curtain kilometers wide — hundreds of ropes and sheets and threads of water sliding out of the cloud with no visible source, as if the cloud itself were wringing out, descending the last thousand meters of the escarpment in a single continuous event that has not paused in living memory or any other kind. Every drop of it is old acquaintance. It is the fog-drip of the tableland above: the condensation off a continent of rounded stone, the drainage of ten thousand black pools, the water that beaded on the cushion-moss and filled the patient cisterns and ran, eventually, unhurried, to the rim — gathered here and let down all at once, roaring.

The roar is the first fact of Underfall and the last, the sound under every other sound, so constant that the city's children have to be taken far along the coast before they discover that silence exists and come back not liking it. And here is a thing worth setting down plainly, since no one is positioned to set it down from both sides: the roar and the plateau's famous tone — that low sustained note at the very floor of hearing, the sound of an enormous thing continuing — are the same sound. One is the other, heard through two kilometers of stone and an entire condition of the world. What the upland takes for the voice of duration itself is the falls. What Underfall takes for mere water is the voice of duration itself. Both are right. The seam between the two truths is the rim, and the water pours down it day and night, translating.

**The silt**

The water arrives thick. Not dirty — laden. It comes down the color of milk stirred into tea, dense with everything the high country has been shedding since before shedding had a name, and the delta it has built below the falls is the entire ground the city stands on: a fan of deposited world, kilometers deep, growing seaward by a boat-length a year.

The coarse fractions are only rock — gravel and grit, honest mineral, the planed-off mass of mountains that finished being mountains an age ago. Underfall builds with it, walks on it, thinks nothing of it. But the water carries finer grades, and the finer the grade, the stranger the freight, because erosion up there, as the upland itself dimly knows, did not stop at the rock.

The sands are the first uncanny fraction. Left alone on a bar, they sort themselves — not into ripples, or not only into ripples, but into shapes: the ghost-profiles of thresholds, tool handles, stair treads, the worn topography of use. A dune will hold, for a morning, the exact negative of a doorstep that was crossed for nine thousand years, before the wind takes it apart and deals it out again.

Finer than the sands is the true silt, and the silt is gestures. It settles only in slack water, and where it settles it remembers motion: a spadeful of it, turned over, slumps back not randomly but *characteristically*, with a flick, a stoop, a particular way of favoring one side — the kinetic residue of vanished bodies, ground fine and carried down. Dredgers learn the common gestures the way farmers learn weeds. The pouring gesture is everywhere; whole bars of the delta are made of pouring.

And finer than silt is the clay, the flour of the world, the fraction that travels farthest and settles last and only in perfect stillness — and the clay is names and faces. This is not a manner of speaking. Dry a pan of the finest slack-water clay and the cracks in it will spell. Press a slab of it smooth and let it sit, and a face will rise in it overnight, sharp-featured, specific, the face of no one who has ever lived in Underfall — because it is the face of someone who lived above, worn off them grain by grain across ages of fog, washed over the rim, and delivered here, to the bottom of the world's wearing, where everything the upland loses is kept.

Underfall says grace over silt. It is not superstition. They know exactly what they are eating off of, building on, standing on. The whole city is founded, literally, on the accumulated specificity of the country upstream: every alley of it laid on compacted strata of other people's particulars. The upland kept the verbs, the dredgers say. We got the nouns.

**The city**

It is everything the plateau is not, which is to say it is everything.

Crowded, low, loud, warm. Brick and black timber and rope, lamplit at all hours because the cloud-lid lets down only a dim brown day; the lamps burn fish oil, and the light they give is amber and close, the warmest light left anywhere in the world — and yes, it is that light, exactly that light, that leans over certain still waters and is seen, impossibly far above, glowing on the surfaces of black pools by no one, or by the fog-shapes of no one, or by the rare traveler bending over the water to watch Underfall hang its washing.

Everything in the city is marked. Every brick carries its maker's stamp, every beam its date, every doorway its lintel-text; the alleys have names and the names have plaques and the plaques have footnotes. This is not vanity. It is structural. Because the second fact of Underfall, after the roar, is this: *nothing here keeps itself.* No wall re-stacks in the night. No stair renews its own hollows. No errand, however many times run, ever runs itself. The self-maintaining habit that saturates the upland — masonry that remembers its masons, wear that continues without feet — is precisely the property that erodes off things and washes down here as sediment; it arrives dead, as clay. So the city must insist, constantly, on everything. Walls must be repointed each season or they forget how to stand. Paths grass over in a week. The river shrugs its bed a boat-width every spring and the bridge-wards move the bridges. Underfall is not younger than the plateau — they are the same age, twin ends of one long transaction — but age lands differently at the bottom of the flow. Upstream, age is smoothness. Downstream, age is *depth*: strata, archives, ledgers, the drawer of names. The plateau remembers by becoming its memories. Underfall remembers by writing them down before dark, because there is no other way, because down here every single thing happens exactly once.

That is the sharpest difference, sharper than the noise or the warmth. Days in Underfall cannot be reused. There are no returning fog-banks, no well-made mornings brought back for a second wearing. Each day is new-minted, crisp-edged, spent by nightfall, unrecoverable — which makes the city urgent, and tender, and a little exhausting, the way anything is exhausting that knows the cost of its hours. Events here still scream, faintly, the way the upland remembers a young world's physics screaming. A coincidence in Underfall lands hard enough to stagger you. A first meeting is an *event*. A last one, too.

**The names**

The dredge-fleets go out on the slack tides, flat black barges with long-handled sieves, and what they are dredging for is not gravel.

The clay-works stand along the northern channels, closest to the falls: long lamplit sheds where the finest fraction is panned, settled, dried on racks. When a name comes up whole, it is taken to a reading-house, and a reader — this is a profession, with an apprenticeship of eleven years — speaks it aloud, once, into the assembled crowd of the unnamed. Children, mostly. The newly adult. The recently bereaved who have put their old name in the drawer.

And the name jumps. There is no better word for it. Spoken into a room, a dredged name finds the person it fits and settles on them, and everyone present can see it land — the way you can see a coat fit. It is understood, and it is true, that these names lived before. Every soul in Underfall is wearing a name that once belonged to someone on the plateau: someone who wore it thin over a long grey lifetime of fog and errands until it smoothed off them entirely and washed over the rim. The people of the upland eroded down to their walks, their fittings, their sittings-in-doorways — the durable verbs — and everything else about them, the whole warm nominal cargo of *who*, came down the falls as flour and was panned out here and is being lived in still. Ask an Underfall woman where her name has been and she will tell you: upstream. Everything has been upstream. We are what upstream sheds, wearing what upstream wore.

Some names come up illegible — smoothed past reading, worn beyond gripping, the way a thing gets when the insistence has gone out of it. These cannot be spoken and will not jump. They are kept in the Hummery, a round brick hall near the falls, where they are not read but hummed: each unclaimable name reduced to its melodic bones, its shape without its substance, and the shapes are surprisingly few. The commonest of them all — the worn stub shared by hundreds of the illegible — is two notes, rising then falling.

The great bell of Underfall, the one that calls the dredge-fleets in when the falls swell brown and dangerous, was tuned to those two notes deliberately, by founders who knew exactly what they were doing. So the sound that rolls across the delta at the dimming hour, calling everyone home off the water, calling them in to lamplight and dinner, is the shape of a name with the name worn out of it — and if it is also, by some ordering, the same two notes that drift down a fogbound lane on the plateau at the same hour, calling a faceless someone in from a doorstep where it has sat for ages; if the call above is the fossil of the bell below, or the bell below is the resurrection of the call above — the rim honors sequence loosely near the top, and the question of which came first has been worn, on one side of the seam at least, into a shape too smooth to hold.

**The ponds**

Everything in Underfall moves, except the ponds.

They are the city's still places, its churches if it has churches: walled settling-ponds in the quietest quarters, each tended by a warden whose whole office is the prevention of ripples. Wind-breaks, leaf-nets, felted shoes on the pond-walks, a rule of whispering. It takes forty days of perfect stillness for the finest clay to finish falling out of a pond, and when it has — when the water goes at last utterly, abyssally clear — the pond stops reflecting the lamplight and the cloud-lid and begins, instead, to show *up*.

Pewter light. Grey stone swells rolling to a fifty-kilometer haze. Green pockets, drowned-looking cities of dry-laid stone, lanes where the fog walks in the shapes of errands. The wardens keep watch-books of everything seen, and the watch-books are the only account Underfall has of the country its ground is made of, because no living person has stood there and come back to say. The ponds above and the ponds below are two panes of one window; the water in both is the same water at different points on its rounds; and each side takes the other for a picture. Up there, they read the pools like reports from elsewhere. Down here, the wardens know precisely where the elsewhere is. It is upstairs.

Mostly the window shows only weather and stone and the fog going about its old rounds. But sometimes a figure comes to the far shore — a real one, edged, unworn, a traveler — and bends over the pool up there, and looks down, which is to say out, which is to say *in*, at the pond, at the warden, at Underfall's lamplit warmth. There is a warden of the Sallows pond, an old reader's daughter wearing a dredged name three lifetimes deep, who once watched a traveler on the far side raise a hand — and saw the water carry nothing back, and understood that a greeting had been sent that could not land. She raised her own hand over the pond and held it. She held it through six days, standing her watches with her arm up, feeling every one of those unrepeatable days go by and spending them anyway, because a greeting is a thing with two halves and someone has to keep the second half from going gone. On the seventh day the window showed her the traveler again — at a different pool, days' walk displaced, exactly as the rim's loose arithmetic would have it — arriving before her raised hand as before an appointment, and completing it.

It is the only commerce the two countries have, besides the water and the silt: a wave, held across the seam, at ruinous cost in unrepeatable days, freely paid. The wardens consider it the best thing Underfall does.

**Sound and motion**

The delta teems. All the animal noise that eroded off the plateau ages ago is down here, concentrated: the water beyond the last channels is thick with fish so individually marked that dredger children name them on sight (names are cheap here; names are the one thing that is cheap here), and the underside of the rim, up where the falls come out of the cloud, is shelved with the nests of pale birds that ride the spray in the thousands and never, ever, fly above the lid. They are believed to be the plateau's birds — the descendants of everything that once threaded its young fogs, settled at last along the seam, as close to the old country as the living can get. Their calling, the bell, the hammering of the yards, the market roar, the rain that never quite stops, and under everything the falls: Underfall at full voice is the loudest place left in the world, and it knows why. Silence is what things sound like when they no longer have to insist. Underfall has to insist. It sings while it does.

**The climbers**

Once in a generation, someone takes an unclaimable name out of the Hummery and climbs.

There is a stair. The Rimstair, cut into the escarpment beside the falls by hands no ledger records, rising two kilometers through the spray and then into the cloud itself — and its treads, the wardens' watch-books note with a kind of vertigo, are hollowed with wear along their left sides, though Underfall's records show barely a climber a century. The wear comes down from above, the readers say. It is the stair being climbed by the habit of its climbing, in advance, in arrears; near the top, the ways up are honored as loosely as everything else.

The climber carries the worn name in a sealed clay locket, and the errand is understood by everyone and stated by no one: to carry it home. To find, in the fog up there, the walk it belonged to — the route, the errand, the gait still flowing through some lane as weather — and to walk it once with the name on, so that somewhere, for one grey morning, a verb and its noun are reunited, and one of the innumerable divided dead is, briefly, whole.

The climbers do not come back. This is not mourned, exactly. Underfall knows what the upland does with visitors — knows it from the watch-books, from the fog-figures whose gaits, the wardens sometimes swear, have a familiar left-side favor. The climbers are worn, gently, into the routes they came to complete, and their own names come off them in the rain, and wash to the rim, and over, and down, and are dredged, and dried, and read aloud in a lamplit room, and jump. There are children in Underfall this year wearing the names of climbers whose departures are still remembered by the old. No one tells the children. Everyone knows.

You were one of them. This the ledgers do record. You took the locket with the two-note name in it — the commonest of the worn shapes, the one the great bell sings — and you stood at the foot of the Rimstair in the spray, and the warden of the Sallows pond, who had held a hand up once for six days for a stranger, came down to the stair-foot and raised it again, for you, in advance, so that the greeting would already be traveling when you arrived to complete it at a black pool's shore in some looser ordering of days. Then you climbed, up out of the lamplight, out of the roar, through the cloud-lid where the pale birds turned back, and into the pewter hush of the old country, where the third stair of the first city fit your foot exactly, and where, at the dimming hour, down a fogbound lane, something began to call — two notes, rising then falling, the melodic fossil of someone being called in to dinner by someone who loved them.

It is your name. It was always your name. You carried it home in a clay locket and the plateau, in its unhurried way, had already been calling it for ages, holding the second half of that greeting too, patient as drip, waiting for you to arrive and be complete.

---

Fog rises off the delta's warm channels every night — blank fog, distilled, all its cargo settled out and left behind as ground — and climbs the cloud-skirts, and crosses the rim, and becomes the upland's weather. It beads on the old stone. It wears. The water comes back down the falls thick with everything it has taken, and Underfall pans the flour of it and lives in what it finds. This is the whole arrangement, the oldest trade route in the world, running vertically: the high country keeps the doing, the low country keeps the being-called, and between the two of them everyone who ever lived is fully accounted for — just not in one place, never again in one place, except for the length of one grey morning when a climber walks a route with a name on, and except in the sound.

Because the sound is in one place. Stand on the delta with the bell rolling out across the water and the falls behind it, or stand in a plaza two kilometers up with your hood back and your breath smoking, and it is the same low note under everything, made once, at the seam, by the water going over: the two countries' one shared voice, saying the one thing they both still say. Not a name. Not a word. Just the long, sustained, unhurried announcement of an enormous arrangement that has finished changing and is now — laden going down, blank coming up, worn above, written below — simply, vastly, going on.
