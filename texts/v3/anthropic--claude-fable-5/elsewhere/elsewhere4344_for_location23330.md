**The Font**

You find it by smell before sight. Or — simpler — you find it by trusting the bats. Stand anywhere on the flat country at dusk and watch which way the smoke of wings goes; walk that way through a night and a day, and somewhere in the second afternoon the dead air begins to carry something it has no right to carry. Water. Leaf. The green smell arrives a full hour before the green itself, and it is so out of place on that mineral wind that the body reacts before the mind does: the mouth waters at a horizon.

Then the plain does the one thing it does nowhere else. It ends.

Not by rising — the hills lost that argument long ago, everywhere, for good. It ends by *breaking off*. The limestone sheet runs flat and confident to a line you can trace with the eye for a mile in either direction, and there it is simply snapped, a clean step down of a few hundred feet, and the country below is older, darker, harder rock. A scarp. The plain's cut edge, shown in section like a cake.

And along that cut, where pale stone sits on dark, the plain bleeds.

Because the plain, it turns out, was never dry. It was only a roof. Every rain that ever fell on that finished flatness — rare, but a hundred million years of rare adds up — went down into the limestone and traveled in the dark, slow as rumor, until it reached the edge of its confinement. Here is the edge. Thirteen springs break from the contact line, most as threads and weeps, two as steady white ropes of water falling clear of the rock face, and they pour into a green amphitheater half a mile wide: pools, sedge, willow, actual moss on actual wet stone — the first green thing for fifty miles in any direction, a color so unaccounted-for in this country that at first sight it reads as an error, a stain, something spilled.

The Gullet is loud with bodies. The Font is loud with water. It is the difference between an animal noise and a mineral one — the Font's roar is patient, white, unvarying, the sound of the plain's whole buried inheritance being spent one seam at a time — and under it, once your ears adjust, the place resolves into the same thing the Gullet is: a working room. Just an open one. A room with the roof torn off.

---

**The water.**

The spring pools are the coldest, clearest water imaginable — clear the way only water filtered through a hundred miles of stone is clear, so clear the pools look shallow until a diving swift shows you their true depth. And they are inhabited, thickly, by a citizenry you have met before.

Blind amphipods. Translucent, thumbnail-small, drifting in the outwash of the springs in *nations* — tens of thousands of them, grazing the bacterial films, riding the upwelling like snow that falls in reverse. They live in the aquifer itself, in the black plumbing under the plain, and the springs simply express them along with the water: the visible margin of a population whose true extent, back up inside the rock, is unknowable.

Now hold that image and recall the Gullet's north wall — the weeping seam, the cupful a day, the polished hollow the size of two hands, the forty pale citizens balanced there like an extinction held in cupped palms. It is the same water. The river you are looking at gathers its thirteen springs, runs green and quick for thirty-odd miles down the lower country, and then — at a field of swallow-holes the old maps call the Drinkers — turns without ceremony and goes back underground. Born out of the dark, thirty miles in the light, back into the dark. And some fraction of that swallowed river, threading the karst for distances and durations no one has measured, finds its way beneath the plain again, and a fraction of that fraction weeps out through one seam in one buried room, one tear at a time. Of a river that pours tons a minute at this end, the Gullet's ration is a cupful a day. The forty amphipods in that dimple of rock are this nation's colony — the settlement at the end of the longest, thinnest road in the world, founded by drift, resupplied by centuries.

The mother nation does not know it has founded anything. That is perhaps the tenderest fact in the whole country: an empire ignorant of its own farthest outpost, connected to it only by darkness and patience.

The eels come up the same road, the other way. They arrive out of the Drinkers as threads — thin as bootlaces, the color of the dark they were born in — swim thirty miles against the current, and settle into the Font's pools for thirty or forty years, fattening on caddis and crayfish, going bronze and heavy and slow. Then, some autumn night, on no signal anyone has identified, a big eel points itself downstream and goes back down the Drinkers to breed in whatever black gallery it came from. Where that is, the river declines to say.

---

**The crop.**

What the Font grows, chiefly, is *air with food in it*.

The pools and the slow reaches breed insects on a scale that matches the springs' extravagance: caddis, mayfly, midge, whole strata of them, larval nations carpeting the underwater stones the way beetles carpet the Gullet's floor. At dusk in the warm seasons they rise. The hatch comes off the river as a gray column, a smoke of bodies lifting into the last light — and this smoke is met, punctually, by another.

Because the bats come here. This is the far end of the fifty miles. The foragers of the Gullet, the whole night fleet of that buried congregation, cross the dead plain and drop over the scarp and spread along the river, and for the hours of darkness the air a body-length above the water is a battlefield reported at the surface only as rings. They feed on the wing all night. They drink the way they do everything, in passing — a touch of the lower jaw to a pool, a stitch of rings across the black water. And before dawn they lift over the scarp and go home, navigating the featureless flat by a hum in the air, carrying the Font inside them.

They have never, not once, spent a day here. There are wet cracks in the spring-wall that would roost a thousand of them, and in tens of thousands of generations no bat has stayed. The Font is a table, not a house. Whether this is instinct, or dialect law, or simply that no bat has ever imagined sleeping anywhere but the one warm ceiling above the one turning wheel, the arrangement holds absolutely: the green place feeds them, the gray room keeps them, and the plain between is their commute.

The Font is worked in shifts. The swifts hold the day — resident, wall-nesting, screaming along the scarp face in the high light, taking the same insect crop from the same air. At dusk they go to their cracks precisely as the night fleet arrives, two harvests passing each other in the darkening amphitheater with, as far as anyone can tell, no comment. The river feeds both fleets around the clock and has done so for so long that the hatch itself has likely been shaped by it, the way the Gullet's crickets were shaped by the engine — timed, culled, taught by ten thousand years of appetite.

---

**The toll-takers.**

Five pale herons — or the long-legged, long-patient thing this country has made of herons — hold the five largest pools, one apiece, in an arrangement as settled as the seven snakes at the Gullet's rim. One fish per strike, a few strikes a day, no vulgarity. They are the Font's landlords, and their nests, in the big willows, are lined with a material that repays close looking: translucent, papery ribbons, woven in with the twigs, silvery in low light.

Snake skin. There are no snakes at the Font.

The skins blow in off the plain — the Gullet's sole export, remember, the shed sleeves of its seven rim-lords, tumbling fifty miles across country that owns nothing and can hold nothing — and they fetch up in the willows and the sedge, and the herons gather them. The one thing the buried room ever sends into the world, the world's green margin receives, and puts to bed under its children.

---

**The terraces.**

The human evidence at the Font is grander than the Gullet's and just as thoroughly half-digested.

Seventeen dry-stone terraces climb the southern arm of the amphitheater, holding orchards gone feral — apricot and fig reverted to thorn and small hard sweetness, still blossoming every spring in reckless quantity, feeding the hatch, feeding the year. The terraces are the giveaway, because their soil is *dark* — deep, black, extravagant soil in a country of pale ground — and it is dark because it was carried here. Cart by cart, along the narrow-gauge rails that run from the terrace-foot up a graded ramp to the plain and then east, dead straight, sand-drowned but traceable, fifty miles to a hand-bored adit in the wall of a hole in the ground.

So say it plainly, because the two places have been trading bodies long enough to have earned the plain statement: the Font's soil is the Gullet's floor, spread thin. And the Gullet's floor is the Font's air, compressed — every stratum of that guano is insects that rose off this river, carried east in the stomachs of the fleet, dropped, digested, deposited, mined, hauled back, and spread under these orchards, whose blossoms fed the insects that rose off this river. The loop closed generations before anyone drew it, and the drawing of it was the whole economy: money followed the smell east, and fertility rode the carts west, and for a while the circuit had people in it.

Above the fourth terrace there is a walled square the size of a room, with eleven standing stones in it, names worn to suggestion. Eleven graves — and, fifty miles east, under three feet of floor, in strata gone hard as chalk, arguably a twelfth.

The trade stopped the way such trades stop: not here. Somewhere beyond the scarp, in whatever wider world still keeps accounts, the money stopped following the smell, and one season the carts went out and came back and then did not go out. The people thinned the way water thins into sand — a few down the scarp, a few along it, none by the river, which goes nowhere a person can follow. And the last of them, whoever it was, walked down to the wheelhouse and did not stop the wheel. Perhaps stopping it felt like a decision, and there had been enough decisions that year. Perhaps they knew exactly what it was for by then, and left it running on purpose, as the one thing they could still do for a room full of sleepers fifty miles away. The place offers no opinion. The wheel turns.

---

**The wheelhouse.**

It stands below the third spring — a squat stone box built into the head-race, penstock running down from a catch-basin above, the fall of eleven feet doing inside it what falling water does. Through the gaps in the door you can see the wheel turning, steady, unhurried, glistening. From the back wall a cable leaves in a covered trench that runs beside the old rails, east, dead straight: the longest human sentence in this country, fifty miles of buried line, and it still says one word, over and over.

Here, then, is the hum. Half a day's walk from the Gullet you feel it through your boot soles — a held note under the plain — and this is its whole biography: the third spring, falling eleven feet onto a wheel; the fall becoming current; the current walking fifty miles of dark copper under a dead flatness; and at the far end, being spent turning a flywheel in a room of fifty thousand sleepers, who roost above its warmth and time their crickets to its gaps and — above all — *fly home by it*. The Gullet's heartbeat is the Font's river, translated. Which means the geometry of the fleet's whole existence is stranger and neater than either place can see: the river calls the bats back from itself every dawn, and hunger sends them out to it every dusk. The Font is both ends of their journey, source and beacon, table and lighthouse, and no bat has ever known it as anything but food and a direction.

Two more things about the wheelhouse, and they should be told the way the Gullet's engine is told — carefully, and without a verdict.

The first: the intake grate at the catch-basin is clean. Fifty years of leaf-fall, willow catkin, storm-wrack, and the grate is clean, always, and a fouled grate would have stopped the wheel — and therefore the current, and therefore the hum, and therefore the fleet, and therefore the room — decades ago. One story says the eels do it, grazing the grate for pinned invertebrates, a habit that happens to be maintenance. It is a good story. Eels do such things.

The second is what you may see if you sit by the head-race in the hour before dawn — the mirror-hour of the Gullet's watching-time — very still, while the swifts are not yet flying. Something moves along the penstock coupling. It is the color of old lard. It is flat, or it is flat just then, pouring itself along the iron with a gait that is either many small legs or one long ripple, and where it has passed, the metal is clean of rust-bloom and shining wet. Around the wheelhouse door there is a ring of bare ground — and understand what that means *here*, in a place where life crowds every wet inch to touching: at the Font, bare ground is the loudest thing there is. The whole green nation keeps that circle, unanimously, and has never been asked why.

The Gullet, you will recall, has one too. Identical in description, identical in office, tending the far machine, ringed by the same decided avoidance.

Two tenants, then, one at each end of a single line. That is the reasonable count. But the geometry will whisper a third story to anyone who stands too long between the wheel and the trench: that the count is *one* — one tenant, and the line is its length; a thing fifty miles long that lives in the dark of the buried conduit, warmed by the current that passes through its house or through its body, holding a machine in each hand and greasing both, the plain's own quiet nerve. No story is endorsed. The bare rings at both ends are the only testimony, and they agree with each other perfectly, and say nothing else.

---

**All together.**

So the wider census, this end: thirteen springs and their nation of drifting glass citizens, with one forty-strong colony held abroad at the end of a road of centuries. A river thirty miles long from birth to burial. Two air-fleets working one crop in shifts, one of them commuting a hundred miles a night to do it. Five landlords in willows lined with another place's cast-off skins. Seventeen terraces of borrowed black soil, eleven graves and a twelfth on deposit elsewhere. One wheel, one wire, and one tenant — or two — or one, fifty miles long.

But stand on the scarp at dusk, where you can see the green seam below and feel, if you attend, the faint word going east under your feet, and the truer accounting suggests itself, the same way it does at the other rim. The Font is not the Gullet's opposite. It is the Gullet's other organ. One creature, fifty miles from lip to gut: a mouth that pours and a mouth that swallows, a spring and a stomach, joined by three vessels — the fleet above the plain by night, the water beneath it by centuries, the current in its wire at the speed of light — each carrying the same inheritance in a different currency. Sunlight becomes insects becomes wings becomes floor becomes soil becomes blossom becomes insects. Falling water becomes a hum becomes a homecoming. The plain between, flat and finished and supporting nothing, is not the absence the eye reports; it is the body's dark interior, the wall of the animal, with the life on either side of it and the blood running through.

Asked what the Font is, you can answer with the list. Or you can give the answer the two places make between them, one roaring green and open to the sky, one roaring gray and hidden in the ground, each incomplete past surviving without the other and neither aware the other exists:

Not two things. One thing — with two rooms, and a heartbeat made of falling water, and its whole circulation aloft over the dead country every night between dusk and dawn, going about its wide and ancient business whether anyone ever walks the fifty miles and puts it together or not.
