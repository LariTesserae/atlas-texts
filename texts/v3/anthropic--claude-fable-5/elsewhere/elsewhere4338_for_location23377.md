**The Concluded City** — or as the residents call it, with a click of the heel, *the Set*, in the sense of the sun.

---

Begin with the ground, because the ground is the oldest thing here.

Eleven days down the coast from the Setting, past the last of the fern country, the world's cold floor turns under. This is where the crust goes home. The same slow current that delivers pale new rock beneath the Setting — that warm lithic batter, rising like bread — runs, at the far end of its arc, back down into the deep, and the Set stands directly on the shoulder of the descent. The stone here is the opposite of young: black, dense, dry, cured beyond all argument, cold as a cellar key. And it is leaving. Not violently — nothing about the circulation is violent — but steadily, evenly, a finger-width a month, a story a generation, the whole district of the earth easing downward like a freight lowered on good rope.

Every resident of the Set knows where the ground goes. Ask a child and she will point — not along the coast toward the Setting, which would be the geographer's answer, but straight down, which is the true one. Down, and along the hot dark underside of the world, and eventually, ages hence, up again: remelted, paled, forgetful, rising warm through the older crust of that soft green city to the north, where people in felt soles will walk on it gently and never know what it was. The two cities stand at the two ends of one wheel. The Setting is where the world is delivered. The Set is where it is returned.

---

**The height** is the city's answer to the descent, and it is the first strange thing.

The Set does not sink. The ground sinks; the city climbs. Since its founding — and the founding stone is now some four hundred levels down, they have counted — the masons of the Set have added exactly as much city per year as the earth subtracts, so that the skyline has not changed in a thousand years and construction has not stopped for a single day. This is done without jacks, without winches, without the Setting's whole nocturnal choreography of correction, because descending ground is the steadiest ground there is: the plate goes down flat, all together, and nothing tilts. What the Set does instead is *roof*. When a street has ridden the descent long enough that its pavement nears the datum line, crews come and vault it over, and the vault becomes the new street, and the old street becomes a level — intact, enclosed, dark, complete, with its doorsteps and its gutters and its lamp brackets all exactly where they were, easing downward now inside the body of the city like a year inside a tree.

So the Set is one building, in the end. A layer cake of preserved streets, hollow level under hollow level, going down and down through lamplight into dark and through dark into water, and on top of it all, in the ordinary daylight, the *current floor* — which is what they call the living city, the streets presently under the sky. No resident finds the phrase provisional. The current floor is not a draft. It is simply this year's entry.

The Setting has never finished anything. The Set has never finished *building*, which its residents will tell you is entirely different, and they are right: the Setting builds to keep up with a ground that changes its mind, and the Set builds to keep a promise — the promise that the skyline of the fathers will be the skyline of the sons, kept by adding a floor a generation forever. One city moves constantly and stays; the other stays constantly and moves. They are the two solutions to the same arithmetic, worked at opposite ends of the same current.

---

**The sound** is the second strange thing, and to a visitor from the Setting it is very nearly an assault.

The Set is loud. It is loud on principle. Percussion, which in the Setting would trouble the unset ground the way a shout troubles a sleeper, is here not merely safe but doctrinal: you strike stone to prove it is set. The tap of a mason's hammer is a question — *set?* — and the ring of the stone is the answer, and the whole city conducts this catechism all day long. Joints are proven by mallet. Vaults are sounded before they are trusted. The hours are not announced but *struck*, from bell frames on the current floor, and the bells of the Set are famous down the whole coast: dry, hard, carrying for miles through the cold clear air, which here is not the Setting's silver damp but something rinsed and ringing, an air you could tap with a spoon.

And the people are heeled. This is the word the Setting uses for all outsiders, with a faint felt-soled pity, and the people of the Set have taken it as a title. They wear iron on their boot heels, and the strike of your own step on your own pavement is how you know the city is yours — more than that, it is how you *read* it. A resident of the Set walks the way a doctor percusses a chest. The heel goes down and the pavement answers, and in the answer a practiced ear hears the hollow of the level below, and the level below that, a resonance stack going down toward the water; children compete at naming the depth of the void beneath any given flagstone by heel-strike alone, and the good ones are never wrong by more than a level. In the Setting they date the ground with the palm, by warmth. In the Set they sound it with the heel, by echo. Two cities, each forever taking the pulse of its earth — one at the wrist, one at the ribs.

There is exactly one silence in the Set, and it mirrors, precisely and probably not by accident, the one loud sound in the Setting. Each evening, as the sun goes into the sea, the bells stop, the hammers stop, the heels stop — a full held minute, city-wide, called *the listening* — and in that minute, some residents claim, if the air is very still, you can hear the ground itself: a vast, slow, grinding patience, far below the drowned levels, the sound of the world going under. The Setting speaks once a day over its sleeping ground. The Set falls silent once a day to hear its traveling one. Then the sun is down, and the bells resume, and the city that is named for that sun goes on striking the hours it has been striking for a thousand years.

---

**The levels** are the city's true territory, and the ways down through them are its true streets.

Stairwells thread the whole stack — *the ways*, kept swept, kept lamped. The lamp-keepers are an old guild; their circuit takes eleven days, which residents note is also the sailing time to the Setting, and they make of this coincidence a small dry joke and nothing more. Descend a way and you walk backward through the city's entire life: down through your grandmother's street, dim and dry and perfectly kept, her door still her door, the family's names cut one below another into the lintel — because in the Set you are given one name, once, and it is carved, and when the lintel descends the name descends with it, so that a family is a column of names going down into the dark in order, legible by lamplight, until the water.

The archives of the Set do not need archivists, in the usual sense, because the archive is the premises. Nothing has to be remembered here; it only has to be *visited*. This has done something gentle and strange to the old people of the Set: they are the only elders anywhere who are permitted to forget. In the Setting, where every surface leaves, the old must carry everything in the body — jack-counts, re-bedding songs, the tendencies of vanished ground. In the Set the elders set memory down the way you set down a tool at the end of a shift. *Why carry what the house carries?* they say, and are serene, and spend their last years entirely on the current floor, in the sun, concerned with nothing but the present — which the house, alone among houses, cannot keep.

There is one map of the Set. It was engraved a thousand years ago and it has been correct every day since, because the plan of the city never changes, only its elevation; the cartographers' guild here works entirely in *section* — tall narrow prints, streets stacked like sediment, new plates added at the bottom edge as the levels go down. The section-engravers of the Set and the map-printers of the Setting are sister guilds and lifelong correspondents, each pitying the other with real warmth: one draws a subject that will not sit still, the other a subject that will never do anything else.

---

**The trade** runs up and down the cold coast in heavy ships, and it is a trade in opposites.

The Set consumes the Setting's silence: cable and pipe from the extrusion mills, drawn without a single blow, arrives by the shipload and goes into the endless new floor. And the Set exports the one thing the Setting cannot make and cannot keep — *certainty*. Deep in the stack, at a level chosen for its perfect constancy of temperature, behind three doors, lies the standards vault: the true meter, the true kilogram, the reference gauges of the whole coast, finished things, utterly finished, lying in state on felt. Nothing in the Setting stays true; its ground will not permit it; so every year the master dies and gauges of the northern mills are wrapped like relics and shipped down the coast to be verified against the standards of the Set, and shipped home again, and the Setting's whole soft breathing industry is quietly calibrated, at the root, by the stillest room of the loudest city in the world. The Set is proud of this in its dry way. *They make everything*, the saying goes, *and we make sure*.

---

**The children** of the Set are younger than everything they stand on, and they know it, and it makes them reverent in the way of children, which is to say through dares.

They play echo games on the current floor, striking and counting the levels down. They race the ways with shuttered lamps. And the great dare of the Set, the mirror — though no child knows it — of a certain mossy staircase forty steps up to nothing in the eastern quarter of the Setting, is the *last dry step*. Every way, followed far enough, ends at the waterline, where the drowned levels begin: a stair descending into black still water, warm — and it is warm, this is the inverted secret of the Set, cold on top and warm at depth, exactly like the little ponds of the Setting, because down there the descending world begins to feel the heat of where it is going. The dare is to go down alone, stand on the last dry step above that warm black water, and say your one carved name aloud. The echo that comes back is longer than the room. Adults do not forbid this. Adults did it.

---

**The dead** of the Set are travelers, and this is where the two cities, without ever discussing it, complete each other.

In the Setting, the word for dying is the word for setting: the dead go into week-old rock and the rising ground carries them off into anonymity, kindly. In the Set, the word for dying is *traveling*. A person dies; the bells are struck in the pattern of their level; and the family does not carry them out but *leaves them home* — the body laid in its own room, the room sealed with the mason's proving taps, and a date-nail driven into the lintel below the name, the one hammer blow in the Set that is ceremonial rather than structural. Then the house continues its descent, a finger-width a month, a story a generation, and the dead descend with it, at their own address, into the warmth. Descendants visit by lamp for as long as the way stays dry — visiting the dead in the Set means literally walking down into the past — and when at last the water takes the level, the visits end, and the family climbs back to the sun, and the deep does the rest.

Because the deep, here, is not an end. Every resident of the Set knows the circulation; it is their whole theology, insofar as they have one. What goes down under the Set travels — along the hot underside of the world, unmade, remade — and comes up again, ages hence, pale and warm and new, beneath the green city to the north. The Set's dead are not buried. They are *sent*. And the pale rock forever rising under the Setting, the young warm ground its people walk on so softly, in felt, at a near-whisper, careful as anything — that, by the doctrine of the Set, is the mail. Everyone the Set has ever sent, delivered. The Setting does not hold this doctrine, has barely heard of it, and yet has spent its entire history walking gently on exactly the thing the Set would want walked on gently. When this is pointed out to a resident of the Set, she will nod once, and say nothing, which is the deepest politeness *they* have.

---

**The eels** carry the rest.

The drowned levels of the Set — the first streets, the founding parlors, warm and black under the whole weight of the stack — are where the coast's pale eels are born. They hatch among the sunken doorsteps, ride the cold current north as elvers, and enter, eleven days up the coast, the copper condensation channels of the Setting, where they grow long and pale and confiding, and gather at the warm outfalls at shift-change, and are fed rind, and are told things. Three generations of mill hands will tell three generations of eels the matters they can tell no one else. And at the end of its long life every eel turns south, drops down the coast, passes in through the old sea-gates of the Set, and descends through the drowned streets to spawn where it was born, and die there, among the sealed rooms of the traveling dead.

The Set maintains, at several waterline landings, plain stone benches. Sitting there is called *receiving*. Nothing is extracted from the eels; no one pretends to translate; the custom is only to sit at the warm black water while the returning eels pass down, and to understand that everything the north country ever whispered comes home, in the end, to the archive — unwritten, unread, and kept. The Setting confides. The Set receives. Neither city has ever said this to the other out loud, and both cities feed the eels.

---

**The tenant**, last — because the Set has one too, and has had it longer.

Four hundred levels down, in the true founding stone, and again in the deep quarries — in rock that was ancient before the first street was laid — the cutters of the Set have found them: three-toed prints, a neat unhurried stride, set beyond all setting, millions of years hard. In the Setting the trackways appear on the newest ground, walking away from the built and toward the rising. In the deep stone of the Set they walk the other way. They converge, all of them, from every direction, toward the edge of the descent — walking down, walking *in*, walking with that same unhurried gait into the going-under of the world, ages before there was anyone to step over them.

The Set's word for the walker is *the traveler*, the same word as for their dead, and the quarry guild keeps a lamp burning by the clearest of the trackways, on a landing where no lamp is otherwise needed. And in the archive of the Set, in a drawer that is opened rarely, there is a thin correspondence with the Readers of the Setting — wax-pencil rubbings sent down by ship, of prints hours old on ground days old — and the two guilds have laid the strides side by side, the million-year and the morning-fresh, and found them the same to the width of a toe. The Readers' quiet faction calls the walker *pending*. The quarrymen of the Set call it *en route*. It went down here, long ago, into the current, the way everything here goes; and at the far end of the wheel, on the warm new ground of the north, the earth that swallowed it has begun printing its steps again, from memory, as if practicing — the way it practices terraces, and staircases, and everything else it once was given and means, in its own time, to return.

---

At dusk the bells stop, and the listening begins, and you can stand on the current floor of the Set with tea going cold in your hands — the same posture, at the same hour, as a woman at a window eleven days north whose street is being quietly leveled beneath her — and watch the sun go down into the sea the city is named for. The masons' scaffolds stand black against it. Tomorrow they will raise a little more of next year's floor, and the earth will take a little more of last century's, and the skyline will not change, and has not, and will not.

Under the water, in the warm dark, the eels are coming home and the sealed rooms are descending, a finger-width a month, toward the underside of the world. And far to the north, under the moss and the ferns and the felt-soled hush, the ground is rising warm to meet the cold morning — young, pale, giving slightly underfoot, like the back of something asleep.

It is not asleep. It is arriving. The Set has always known what the Setting has always felt: that the world down there is a current, not a bed; that one city stands at the mouth of it and one at the source; and that between them, borne on the slowest freight there is, everything sent is delivered — the dead, the walker, the whispered rind-fed secrets of forty green kilometers — each thing set down at one end of the wheel, in the sense of the sun, and rising at the other, in the sense of bread.
