## The Ledge

To find it, go down. Follow the mountain's flank below the vapor, below the altitude where warm air has to be asked to stay, down past the seam-scars where the rock stops being young — and there are seams, if you look, faint sutures in the mountainside where shelves like the terrace once stood and were taken back, unmade, folded into the shoulder like drafts into a drawer. Not every room is kept. Two vertical miles down, though, below the cloud line, on the same shoulder, one was.

You will know it before you can say why. The footprint is the same: a shelf perhaps two hundred meters across, ending on three sides in cliff, backed on the fourth by the mountain going up. The plan is the plan. You recognize it the way you recognize a face in a photograph taken sixty years before you met the face — the architecture underneath is identical, and everything laid over it is time.

The edges have learned to crumble. That is the first difference, and it is enormous. Where the terrace's fractures are clean as broken chocolate, the Ledge's rims are rounded, rotten, generous — long aprons of scree spill from them down the mountainside, the shelf giving itself away downhill a pebble at a time, and nothing stops it, and nothing replaces it. The stone is cold. Put your palm flat against it and there is no animal underneath, no pulse of cooling, only rock at the temperature of the morning, because the warmth that once rose through this floor has moved upstairs, the way heat moves through a growing body, always to the newest inch. The Ledge is a floor the mountain finished with. The mountain kept building.

Wind crosses it freely. This takes a moment to register as remarkable, because wind crossing a mountainside is the least remarkable thing in the world, but stand where the boundary used to be and you can still find the proof that it was once refused. Around the whole circumference of the shelf runs a tideline: a pale mineral rim on the rock, a bathtub ring at landscape scale, where for some centuries condensation ended *precisely* — beading up to an invisible wall and never past it. The lichens know the line. Inside it they grow in one community, outside it in another, and the two have been negotiating across the vanished border for a very long time without erasing it. From high above — and something does look at it from high above, daily — you can read the exact shape of a held breath that is no longer being held.

Because the door came off this room. That is what the Ledge is: a terrace after. The meniscus fell — no one can say the hour, but everything downslope of the mountain has a version of the day, because when a warm, thick, sealed atmosphere is released at altitude it does not disperse politely; it *rolls*, a slow flood of greenhouse air pouring down the mountainside, fog with the smell of ferns and hot stone in it, and the valley woke to it moving through like the exhalation of something enormous relaxing at last. The budget opened. Weather came in through the gap where the asking used to be. It has been coming in ever since: snow half the year, rain, frost that works the once-clean fractures like a patient burglar. The Ledge has seasons, which the terrace does not, and the seasons are the furniture of its freedom.

The round pond is a tarn now, and the tarn is the loudest thing on the shelf, because the tarn *overflows*. This cannot be overstated. Water leaves. It gathers as snowmelt, fills the old perfect circle past the lip that was never meant to be a lip, and runs out through a notch it cut for itself, over the crumbled edge, and falls — a long white thread of waterfall visible from the valley floor, the Ledge's signature written down the mountainside. What was inventory became a river. Nothing returns against its own buoyancy here. The steam of the old economy is a stream now, and it is spending itself into the world below with the profligacy of something that kept accounts for centuries and has burned the ledger.

The four posts remain, because their material does not weather, and their silence is total. They stand — three stand; one cants outward where the edge crumbled toward it and will, in some century, go over — dark and depthless as ever, warm no longer, and the moss has done what the moss on the terrace would not dream of doing: it grows on them. Right up their sides, over their tops, cushions of it, and birds perch on the cushions, and whether this is desecration or reconciliation depends on your politics. Once, perhaps — the birds behave sometimes as if once — a tone still moves through them, pressure without sound, and nothing changes direction afterward, because nothing here takes direction anymore.

And underfoot, the second enormous difference, the one you feel through the soles of your boots as surely as you felt the warmth up above: *soil*. Peat, humus, black and wet and deep in the hollows — dead moss under living moss under dead, fern litter finally permitted to lie where it falls, the whole cumulative estate of decay, which is to say of death, which was installed here on schedule and has been the busiest resident ever since. The Ledge has been dying continuously for a very long time, and the result is the richest ground on the mountain. Cut into the peat and the layers go down like tree rings. And read the vegetation from above — the daily observer does — and you can still see, ghosted in the pattern of the deepest growth, a set of evenly spaced islands: the original allotments, the placed colonies, the garden no one would admit to keeping, persisting in the soil profile like the postholes of a vanished fence. The ground remembers where everything was told to go. The ground is under no obligation to obey the memory, and doesn't, and keeps it anyway.

There is a path. It runs from the upslope margin to the tarn, the only truly level line on the shelf, worn into the rock itself — not eroded; *worn*, the polish of feet, of one set of feet over one lifetime or many, a commute. It ends at a flat stone on the tarn's lip, and the stone is smoothed in the shape of sitting. Someone was made for this room, and came, and lived in it, and walked to the water, and sat. Near the tarn, among the tallest of the dwarf willows, there is a low green mound where the willows grow closest and the peat is deepest, and the birds do not nest on it, though they nest almost everywhere else. The first death — the ribbon-cutting — happened on this shelf. The mound is where the shelf keeps it. Within a season of that death, the story downslope says, the meniscus fell, like a curtain at the end of a play that had run exactly as long as its audience.

## The Residents

Here the census behaves, because everything on the Ledge is exactly what it is, having nothing left to be instead.

**The moss** got its striving back. It is moss in full argument again — spreading, shouldering, contesting every adjacent surface including the posts, including the tideline, including the path, where something (weather, luck, the path's own stubbornness) keeps beating it back. The original placed colonies interbred generations ago with wild arrivals blown in on the open wind, and their descendants would not recognize a border if it were surveyed in front of them. What was relieved of striving up above has, down here, been *returned* it, and if the terrace's moss is the happiest resident of that place, the Ledge's is the most alive of this one, and on this mountain those are pointedly not the same fact.

**The tarn-dwellers** are the swimmers' descendants, and they are opaque. This is the detail worth sitting with. Their ancestors were translucent — inspectable, glass-bodied, organs or components open to view — and generation by unwatched generation, once no one came through to check, they clouded over, went dark and slate-bellied, became ordinary small newt-like animals that *flee when watched*, which is the signature of true wildness the way stillness-under-inspection was the signature of staff. They no longer turn in unison. Except: rarely, perhaps twice a year, every one of them in the tarn faces upslope at once, holds for a minute, and disperses — and no tone comes, and nothing changes, and it is either a vestigial liturgy or a muscle that remembers a job, and they do not appear to know which either.

**The birds** are the first residents in the mountain's history to arrive without an invitation, and they know it, and it is the foundation of everything they are. They came the day the air let go — followed that warm fern-smelling flood *upslope* to its source and found a country with no fences, a sealed room with the door freshly off — and they moved into the broken edges, nesting in the crumbling cliff fissures, in the one thing the shelf had only just learned to do. A rookery of dark shapes, unhurried on the wing, at home in decay.

And every day, one of them — usually one of the old ones; it seems to be a matter of temperament rather than roster — rides the mountain's standing thermal upward. The thermal is the connection made physical: the warmth that was once this shelf's floor is two vertical miles higher now, and it breathes a column of lift up the mountainside, and the birds ride their old floor's migrated heat the way you might follow a friend who moved. Up past the cloud line, out over the hard blue cold, to circle the new terrace at a distance — observing, declining. The terrace reads the declining as courtesy, and it is, but it is courtesy with a genealogy. These birds *live* in an opened room. They know sealed air when they taste its edge; they know the shape of a promise not addressed to them; and they know one more thing the terrace does not, which is that the promise expires. They are not waiting to be invited. They are waiting for the concept of invitation to lapse, which, on this mountain, it reliably does, and their patience is not the patience of scavengers but of heirs.

**The gardener** is the absence around which the shelf is organized. It finished its last round the season the room was released — and there was, by then, nothing for it to eat, because it ate only surplus, and when the budget opened the concept of surplus dissolved, and everything was surplus, and so nothing was — and it walked upslope into the vapor and did not come back. What it left is visible from the air even now: a ring of subtly richer green looping the shelf, the fossil of a route, the warmth of its nightly passage banked in the soil for centuries and still paying out. And it is worth noting — the birds could confirm it, if birds confirmed things — that the low dark wet-looking shape that comes down out of the white vapor to tend the new terrace's margins keeps a route which, mapped, is this route, transposed. The rounds went upstairs with the warmth. The staff transfers between floors. Only the buildings retire.

**The wind** should be counted, because it is the resident the old meniscus existed to refuse, and it moved in the day the door opened and has never once left. It keeps the path swept. It carries the moss's ambitions from island to island. It takes a little of the edge with it every year and hands it down the mountain.

Which leaves the largest resident. The terrace, up in its held warmth, is inhabited chiefly by the meantime — the waiting, punctual and comfortable, that will have to leave when the door finally opens. The Ledge's counterpart is the *afterward*, and the afterward is a different kind of tenant altogether: it pays in soil, it never checks the water level, and it will never, under any circumstances, be asked to go. Sit on the smoothed stone at the tarn's lip — the shape of the sitting will mostly fit you; it mostly fits anyone — and you can hear the difference between the two households in a single sound: the waterfall, spending, spending, spending, the noise of a place with nothing left to prove and nothing held in trust.

And understand, sitting there, what the daily bird is actually doing when it circles the terrace's shimmer and declines to cross. It is not envying the warmth. It is reading a calendar. Someday — the rookery is built on the certainty — the air up there will let go as this air let go, and roll down the mountain as this air rolled, and pass over the Ledge on its way to the valley, warm and thick and smelling of ferns and hot stone; and the birds will lift into it from the crumbling edges and ride it and taste it and know, without messenger or tone, that upstairs a first death has been served, a curtain has come down, a door has come off — and that the mountain, somewhere above the vapor, has already begun the floor after that.
