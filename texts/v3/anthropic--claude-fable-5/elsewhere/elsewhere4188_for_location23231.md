# The Eye of the Fen

You cannot fail to find it. That is roughly the only way it works: the fen keeps everything else hidden — its hollows, its channels, its chapels of root — under one continuous green deception, and then, at its low southern edge, it gives itself away. A round pool of black water, forty paces across, open to the sky. The fen's single confession. You could see it from the ridge a mile off, a dark coin dropped on green cloth. Birds see it from higher than that. Rain finds it without trying. Everything arrives here by falling — light, leaves, weather, the occasional astonished beetle — which makes it, in that one respect, exactly like the buried places it drains, where everything also arrives from above: the drip, the leaf, the boot through the moss. This world is fed from overhead at every scale. The tarn is simply the place that admits it.

Stand at the edge — carefully; the edge is a lie, a lawn of sphagnum quaking out over open water, undercut for the length of your arm, so that the shore of the tarn is actually a roof, the same moss whose underside is, somewhere far back in the fen's interior, a ceiling hung with drips and cities — stand at the edge and look down, and you will see the second thing the tarn has in common with the hidden country upstream. The water is dark. Not muddy — clean, cold, and stained deep amber-brown with tannin, tea brewed over centuries, and below a forearm's depth the light simply gives up. The tarn is a piece of the bog's interior that happens to be wearing the sky as a lid. Above the surface, the loud air: wind, birdsong, the change of clouds, the whole meaningless everywhere-at-once that leaf-haulers come home shaking from. Below the surface, a darkness as complete as the dark of a closed hand.

Two darknesses in one fen, then. One roofed with living root. One roofed with light. And between them, connecting them, doing all the work: the water.

---

**The water.** Every drop that falls through that far root-woven chamber — filtered ten meters down, arriving cold and clean and tasting faintly of tannin, striking a pool with a note that some small blind listener knows by heart — every one of those drops keeps going. It sinks through the moss-felt floor, threads the peat, joins other water in channels no one has mapped because there is no one whose business it is, and travels — slowly, over weeks, over months — downslope through the body of the fen until it reaches the place where the peat finally weeps it out. That place is here: an underwater seep on the tarn's northern side, a soft spot in the drowned bank where the bog's whole plumbing comes to a point and lets go. The tarn is the outfall of everything. It is where the fen's held breath is finally exhaled into open water.

And it pulses. Twice a day the water table shifts its weight, and the seep strengthens, slackens, strengthens — the same tide that is, deep in the fen's interior, a long cold plant-smelling draft through root-walls, a breath during which a whole scattered city sets down its tools and stands still in the moving air. Here it is not a breath. Here it is a slow push of cold along the tarn's north bottom, twice daily, regular as anything in this world gets. The same event, worn two ways. Nobody in either place knows the other is feeling it.

The bottom of the tarn is an archive. Peat, silt, and then leaves — sunken leaves in their thousands, layered season under season, blackened, flattened, going down through decades like pages. A birch leans over the southern edge and has been paying into this account for eighty years; the same birch drops leaves landward onto the moss, where — though the tree could never know it — some are hauled down through the root-walls, pressed flat under stones, and become the shingled roof of a hall. One tree, two roofs. The leaves that fall on water take longer to arrive at their purpose, but they arrive: they sink, they settle, they feed.

The surface is its own country — a film half a molecule of tension thick, patrolled by whirligig beetles spinning their glittering incomprehensible patrols, dimpled by water striders, dusted after wet weather with drowned springtails washed out of the moss in their thousands, the gray tide of the interior arriving at the coast at last. Dragonflies own the summer air above it. The heron owns whatever the heron stands on, whenever it comes.

But the tarn's real citizen — the one being who lives in it, rather than on it or above it or passing through — is at the bottom, in the dark, in the archive. And she is reading.

# The Eel

She came up out of the world's far side. That is not a figure of speech: she began in the deep Atlantic, in the warm blue nowhere where all her kind begin, and drifted as a leaf-shaped scrap of glass across an ocean, and entered a river as an elver no longer than a fingernail is wide, and climbed that river for a summer, and then — on one particular rainy night some decades ago, the grass soaked flat, the whole land briefly negotiable to a body that carries its own water in its skin — she left the river and went overland. A hand's-width of muscle swimming through wet grass in the dark, uphill, toward a smell. The smell was this tarn. She poured herself over the quaking lip of it and down into the tea-dark cold, and she has been here ever since. She is older than the chamber upstream. She was already ten years in this water when the great root died and made that hollow. She is, in the archive of the fen, one of the deeper layers.

Her body now is as long as your arm and then some, thick as a wrist at the middle, night-colored above and old-pewter beneath, all of it one continuous answer to the question of how to move through a world without corners. Her eyes are small and were never the point. The point is the nose.

Because she reads. Water, to her, is not a medium but a text — a continuous document of dissolved information, and her sense of smell is refined to a degree that makes the vole-folk's whisker-field look like a blunt instrument. She can taste one part in billions. She can follow a thread of scent through still water the way a finger follows a line of print. And she lives at the mouth of the seep — the outfall of the entire fen, the place where every drop that has fallen through every chamber and channel and pool of the hidden country finally arrives, carrying everything it has touched.

The seep is the bog's diary, and she has been reading it for decades.

She read the seasons in it before anything else: the bright green-sap taste of the growing months, the flat mineral taste of frost, the great sweet flush of autumn rot. She read the year of the drought, when the seep thinned to a whisper and tasted concentrated, anxious, old. And she read — she could not have known what she was reading, but she read it — the death of the great root: two winters when the water ran sweet-rotten and rich, a long luxurious taste of something enormous coming apart in the dark upstream. It was the best feeding of her life; the whole tarn bloomed on it. It was also, though no signature in the water said so, the birth of a world — a hollowness opening in the green, a held breath forming, a chamber that would fill with drips and herds and woven halls. She drank the beginning of the Burrow Commons and filed it under *good year*.

And then, gradually, a new note. Faint at first, then persistent, then structural: something warm threading through the cold tea of the seep. Musk. Milk. The green wet-hay taste of gnawed living root, the particular smokiness of chewed moss-pulp, the ammonia trace of many small bodies living close together and tidily. It arrived, it grew for some years, and then it steadied — steadied in a way she has learned to trust, the way a spring steadies, the way anything steadies that has found its size and intends to keep it.

She does not know what it is. She has no category for a city. But she knows it as surely as the vole-folk know their natal drip: a signature of lives, upstream, ongoing. The deepest law of the hidden country — *stay distributed, stay light, stay quiet; do not let the great sleeper feel you* — has succeeded absolutely in every direction but down. The water tells. The water always tells. And so the one being in the world who knows the vole-folk exist is a reader at the bottom of a black pool a mile away, who wants nothing from them, who could not reach them if she wanted to, and who has come — in whatever way an eel comes to anything — to prefer the taste of them there. It means the seep is alive. She noses into the cold push of it, twice a day when the pulse comes, and reads the news, and the news is: *still here, still small, still quiet.* It is a good taste. It has been a good taste for a generation.

---

**Her days** are the interior's days turned inside out. She buries herself through the bright hours — backed into the silt and leaf-archive with only her head free, breathing slowly, a night-colored thing in a lightless place under a brilliant lid — and hunts the margins after dark. Beetle larvae. Caddis in their gravel cases, which she cracks like nuts. Worms working the drowned edge of the peat. And in spring, salamander larvae, gilled and slow in the shallows, of which she takes a certain number every year.

The salamanders are the tarn's other traffic with the hidden country, though neither end of the road knows it is a road. They breed here, in her shallows; the survivors climb out and disperse across the fen; and some of them, following water downward the way their kind follows water, find their way through seeps and channels into the buried chambers and never come back — where they sit beneath drip-points for months, cold and patient as stones, and are treated as sacred listeners attending to some rhythm underneath the rhythm. Perhaps they are. Or perhaps a salamander under a drip is simply tasting each drop as it lands, reading the thin filtered thread of it for the one taste it hatched into — open water, deep tea, the tarn — homesick in the only tense a salamander has. From the eel's side of the fen they are not mysteries. They are the spring crop, and the ones that leave are the ones that got away, and she does not miss them.

**Her heron.** She is not the top of her world, and she knows it better than anyone. Sometimes a shadow arrives from the loud air and stands at the margin — stands with a stillness that beats even the salamanders, one yellow eye angled down through the first forearm of light — and the surface of the tarn, her whole sky, is rearranged around a presence she can feel through the water and never fully see. She has no name for it. She has a gap where the name would go, and a doctrine: depth, darkness, stillness. Her word for virtue, if she had words, would be the same as theirs — *quiet enough not to be noticed* — arrived at independently, in another darkness, under another kind of ceiling that has its own errands. She has never been taken and never quite relaxed. This too is a way of living politely inside a larger attention.

**The rain** is her drip-clock made total. In the buried chamber, each drop falls alone, on its own interval, and small blind listeners tell time and give directions by the pattern. Here the same water arrives all at once: every raindrop a fingertap on the taut roof of her world, a storm a million drips with no interval at all, the whole sky speaking simultaneously. She rises in heavy rain. Rain means the seep will run strong and cold and legible; rain means oxygen; rain means the border between water and world going soft, the way it went soft on the night she crossed it. In hard rain she hangs just under the drumming surface, an arm's length of darkness in the dark, and the two roofs of the fen — root and light — sound, for an hour, almost alike.

---

**And her ending,** because she has one, and unlike almost everything else in the fen she has always known its shape. It is coiled in her the way the drip is coiled in a vole-pup's ear: a direction, kept since the ocean. Some autumn — a wet one, the outlet trickle running full over the tarn's southern lip — the coil will turn. Her eyes will grow. Her back will go the color of the deep sea in advance of the deep sea. And on one particular rainy night, decades after another rainy night, she will pour herself over the lip and go: down the trickle that becomes a stream that becomes a river, down through the estuary's astonishing salt, and out — arm over arm of dark water — back across the whole width of the world to the warm blue nowhere, to end where she began, and begin what ends there.

Her world does not end in catastrophe either. Theirs will close like a wound healing; hers concludes like an errand run. A world that ends by arriving somewhere is nothing to lose sleep over, and eels do not sleep much anyway.

Meanwhile the pulse comes up the seep, cold and regular — and a mile back in the fen, in a chamber she will never know exists, everything stops and stands in moving air. A birch leaf lands on the black surface and starts its slow fall through the decades toward the archive. The whirligigs write and erase, write and erase. And out of the peat, faint and warm and steady, threads the taste of milk and musk and gnawed green: the day's news from a chapel of roots that believes itself unnoticed, read by the one reader it will never meet, who finds it good.

The fen keeps its secrets from the sky, from the ridge, from itself.

The water tells the Eye everything. The Eye stays open.
