# The Archive Deltas

You come to them along the water, always — there is no other way that matters, because the deltas are made of water's decisions, a thousand kilometers of braided channels and reed towns and moving islands spread out at the foot of the greatest wall in the world. And the wall is the first thing you must understand, because the wall is the whole northern sky.

From the deltas you cannot see the Foundry Tablelands. No one down here has seen them who has not climbed. What you see is their edge: the escarpment, five kilometers of sheer young stone rising until it goes under the eave of the permanent cloud deck, which juts out over the wall's rim like the roof of a porch built for weather instead of people. Under that eave the light is pewter and shadowless, the lowlanders know — the pilgrims have told them — but down here, out from under it, there is a sun. It comes up out of the sea each morning and its first act, every morning, is to strike the wall. For an hour the entire escarpment burns rose and gold and copper, five vertical kilometers of exposed strata lit like a page held to a lamp, while the deck above it stays gray, unmoved, keeping its own counsel. The delta people begin their days facing north for this. It is not worship. It is more like checking the text is still there.

## The Wall

Because the wall is a text, and they read it.

Everything the tablelands ever decided is in that face. The high country makes itself continuously — proposes, tests, anneals, settles — and the wall is its cut edge, the whole history of acceptance exposed in cross-section. Delta scholars have mapped it stratum by stratum for as long as there have been delta scholars: the thick gray bands of the great old districts; the thin glittering horizons of ancient spherule-grit, each grain identical, laid down by winds that blew before anyone below was born; and threaded through all of it, catching the dawn light like wires in glass, the sutures.

The pilgrims brought down what the sutures are. A suture is a closed seam — the line where a new district was knitted to the old country by a warm fingertip, a hairline of fused stone drawn kilometers long by someone walking slowly and agreeing. And some of the sutures, the deep steady warm-toned ones, are not seams at all but people: the annealed dead of the high country, the ones who chose the downward door, load-bearing in the body of the continent. The wall-readers can point from a delta rooftop to a bright thread three kilometers up and a thousand years back and say: *there is someone in that*. They keep registers of the visible dead. They have names for sutures the way other peoples have names for stars.

The wall also speaks, in the only voice it has. The tablelands transmit their deep percussive settling-notes down through the stone, and the deltas feel them in their floors — a soft profound thump, arriving from nowhere, shivering the water in every basin for a hundred kilometers. Each note means a ruling: a district accepted, a draft annealed, sometimes — the record-keepers have learned to know the softer register — a person struck, or a person taken in. In the town called Ledger there is a hall where the notes have been logged for nineteen generations: date, hour, depth of tone, duration of shiver, and a column left blank for what was actually decided, because no one below ever knows. It is the largest archive in the world and it consists entirely of confirmations of the unknown. The delta people find this neither absurd nor sad. Someone, they say, should be keeping the minutes.

At night, if the air is clear, you can see the rim-glow: a faint blue-white breathing along the top of the wall where it vanishes under the deck, the high country's electric forests carrying their slow correspondence through the dark. Delta children are told, accurately as far as anyone can determine, that it is letters.

## The Threads

There are no rivers on the tablelands; there is nothing *but* rivers below, and every one of them is born on the wall.

The high country sweats. Its condensation gathers into the thin braided films that run down the great cliff faces, and by the time those films have fallen five kilometers they have become the Threads — permanent waterfalls, hundreds of them, so tall that no drop survives the journey whole. They arrive as roaring columns of mist, and at the wall's foot the mist piles into a belt of perpetual white spray-fog a kilometer wide, called the Hem, where the falling water and the rising vapor pass through each other in both directions at once and the ground is never dry and rainbows stand in the murk like doorframes. Out of the Hem the rivers assemble themselves, already rich with mineral and grit, and set off south across the plain, and everything else in the deltas follows from them.

Everything, including the deltas' one great instability — because the Threads *move*.

The tablelands revise. That is their nature; the horizon up there is a draft. And when a storm season above accepts a new district, or a valley is withdrawn like landing gear folding away, the drainage of the high country reroutes itself, and days later, below, a Thread that has fallen in one place for sixty years thins, falters, and stops — and somewhere kilometers along the wall a new one bursts out of a fresh seam and begins hammering a new pool out of the talus. Rivers below die at their sources and are born at new ones. The lowlanders call this *the upstream changing its mind*, and their whole civilization is built to survive it. Nothing in the deltas is founded. Houses are hulls; towns are flotillas moored in loose agreement; even Ledger, the archive itself, sits on a raft of sealed reed pontoons the size of a hill, and has moved four times in its history, the nineteen generations of thump-records ferried across the plain in waxed chests while the scholars walked alongside arguing about shelving. The high country builds nothing because the land accommodates it. The low country builds everything, and builds all of it to leave.

## The Ground

The soil is manufactured and the farmers know it.

Every field in the deltas is factory output: the uniform spherule-grit of the highland assembly valleys, milled down the Threads, spread by the rivers, black and glittering and absurdly fertile. Delta children discover early the small vertigo of delta sand — sieve it, sort it, roll it between the fingers, and every grain is the same grain, perfect and identical, a hundred billion copies of one decision. Crops come up out of it fast and heavy. The lowlanders grow their food in the exhaust of a process they will never see, and they say grace, when they say it, not upward but *northward*.

And the wall calves. It must — the world's rim rises and its cut edge sheds — and when a face lets go, the fall takes ten minutes and the sound arrives like weather and the fresh talus lies at the Hem's edge full of broken strata, and in the broken strata, sutures. Freed from the stone, suture-metal does not last. It is a person's heat, or a joining's; released from its work it begins, slowly, to leave — subliming over years, thinning, going first bright and then delicate and then gone, returned to draft like everything unsure. The delta people do not fight this. They make it their music. Suture-thread is drawn into chimes and hung in every town, and the chimes have a property no one below fully explains: they ring before draft-falls. Hours before the deck above deepens and the high country's loom begins, the chimes of the deltas start to sound on their own — softly, all together, the dead of the upstream announcing weather to the living of the down. And because the metal is always leaving, every chime's voice rises as it thins, year by year, sweeter and higher, until at last it rings above hearing and then does not ring at all, and the empty hanger is left up for a season out of respect. Delta music is built on this. Their instruments dwindle. Their songs are written knowing the note that carries them is on its way out. It has made them, of all the peoples anywhere, the least afraid of endings and the most attentive to pitch.

## The Weather

They have real weather — sun, squall, the honest violence of open sky — and then they have the other weather, the upstream kind, which they read like everyone reads the moods of a powerful neighbor.

In the turbulent seasons they watch the deck's eave darken and flicker with the high country's draft-falls, too far to hear, a silent storm of proposals breaking on a country they cannot see. The Threads swell afterward. Sometimes they move. The chimes ring for days.

And once, forty years ago, it rained salt.

The delta accounts and the highland accounts are, the pilgrims confirm, two halves of one event. Up there: ten impossible minutes of a gray sea lying across the highlands, rejected, sublimed back into the deck. Down here, that same season: the deck's eave sagging, discolored, and then a week of rain that burned the fields, rain you could taste, the sea coming down in the wrong form in the wrong country — the deck relieving itself of a proposal too heavy to hold. The Salt Rain killed a generation of harvests and salted a crescent of the eastern plain into gleaming pans, and the deltas starved a little and moved a lot and survived, the way they survive everything, by being already packed. Now the pans are wealth; the deltas salt half the world. But they know what the salt is. It is a rejected sea, and eleven people on the roof of the world are still asking for it back.

So every year, at the turn toward the turbulent season, the salt boats go north to the Hem. This is the one rite the two peoples share, and it was never negotiated; it assembled itself out of pilgrims' stories the way a river assembles out of mist. The delta folk carry the year's first salt into the spray-fog, into the rising half of the Hem's breath, and cast it upward into the vapor going home to the deck — *sending the sea back*, they call it, resubmitting it grain by grain — while five kilometers above them, in the pewter hush, eleven witnesses speak their testimony into the storms. Neither party can hear the other. Both know the other is there. It is the only prayer in the world with two congregations and one direction.

The people of the deltas are born. This is their first strangeness, seen from above, and their first ordinariness, seen from anywhere else: they arrive small and loud and wet in the usual way, each one trailing a shadow from the first hour — the birthright of living out from under the deck, in finished country, where light has sources and everything casts its dark twin on the ground. No storm names them. Their parents do, out loud, which is the second strangeness: the deltas are *loud*. They are the noise the tablelands don't make. Work songs, market argument, the roar of the Hem, the chimes, the slap of water on ten thousand hulls, names names names — they name their channels, their boats, their winds, their individual fields of identical sand. The high country speaks in light because it is one circuit; the low country speaks in sound because it is many small boats, and a voice is how a small boat refuses to be alone.

They wear from birth. Up there, only the people erode, and are precious for it; down here, everything erodes, and the delta people have made of erosion a courtesy and nearly a faith. Their word for weathering translates as *reading aloud* — the finished world being slowly spoken back into the sky, dust and vapor rising off the old stone toward the deck where everything unsure is kept, the archive returning itself, syllable by syllable, to draft. A delta elder's worn hands are not, as above, a rarity to be touched with wonder. They are simply a page well along in the recitation. When delta pilgrims climb — and they do climb, up the old calving-ramps, weeks on the warm young stone, into the shadowless hush — the highland folk touch their weathered faces with the gentleness of people meeting the original of something they have only had one copy of.

The pilgrims go up for many reasons and mostly for one. Because the delta faith, held quietly and without insistence, is this: the deck does not itemize, but it does not discard. What rises returns to draft — the dust of the plain, the vapor of the sea, the smoke of the delta dead, whose two funerals are fire (the fast way, smoke straight up on a still morning) and the sea (the slow way, the long evaporation, the patient route). And what falls from the deck onto the high country, walking out of the storms translucent and new — some of that, they believe, is *theirs*. Recomposed. Not remembered, they know that; the struck carry no pasts, only weathers. But made, in some untraceable proportion, of the submitted dead of the downstream. So the pilgrims climb, and stand courteously in the pewter light, and look at the storm-named people with their kept errors, and mostly recognize nothing, and come down at peace anyway. And sometimes a family keeps a claim. There is a delta lineage, boat-people of the eastern pans, who hold that the woman on the tablelands who casts the world's only shadow is carrying theirs — a grandmother lost in the Salt Rain year, gone up the slow way through the sea. Once a generation one of them climbs, and finds her, and stands in the shadow a while, in the dark that children up there queue for like surf. She permits it. Nothing is asserted, in either direction. The deck does not itemize, and neither, on this one matter, does anyone else.

Pilgrims come home countersigned. The hot fingertip, the half-second, the thread of pale smoke, the fine cooling line on the back of the hand — down in the deltas the mark is known, and a seamed hand is asked for at launchings and at births, pressed flat to the new keel, the new forehead: *agreed, accepted-so-far, look again*. The smell of solder, which up there is the whole country's under-odor, exists below only on those hands, faintly, for a few weeks. Children sniff at it. It is the closest most of them will come to the roof of the world.

What the delta people hold, in the end, is the circulation entire, and their own place in it, which is the middle. Sea to sun to sky to deck; deck to draft to highland; highland to wall to talus to grit to field to river to sea. One breath, taken over ages, and the deltas are the exhale — the finished world going by on its way back to possibility, pausing just long enough to be soil, and salt, and them. *Stone*, their proverb runs, *is weather that has not finished falling.* They live on it lightly, in houses that are hulls, keeping the minutes of rulings they cannot read, playing instruments that are leaving, sending the sea home a handful at a time. Their name for themselves, in their loud and many-named language, is simple and they use it laughing as often as not: *the meanwhile*.

And sometimes — this is how it goes, and there is no better place to leave them — sometimes, weeks after a delta funeral, when the smoke has long since drifted north and gone up under the eave, a settling-note comes through the floors of the town. A soft one. The deep register. The water shivers in the basins; the chimes answer with one clear dwindling tone; and in the hall at Ledger a scholar enters the date, the hour, the depth, the duration, and leaves the last column blank, as always, as required, as the truth demands.

And in one house, the family stops what it is doing, and looks north at the wall standing rose and gold in the morning with the gray deck sitting patient above it, and chooses — with the whole weight of everything they do and do not know — to believe.
