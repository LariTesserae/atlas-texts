**The Combs**

You see the reason for the place long before you see the place. The country up here is dry the way old bone is dry — pale rangeland, cracked pans, a sky so high and hard and blue it seems swept — and in all that emptiness there is exactly one cloud. It stands in the south like a white tree. A column rises out of some low hidden fold of the land, climbs, catches the prevailing wind, and leans — always the same lean, always northward — streaming and flattening as it cools until it lies along the face of a great escarpment like a hand laid against a forehead. Everyone in the dry country steers by that cloud. It is the only permanent thing in the sky. Follow its lean for three days, uphill the whole way, and you arrive where the cloud arrives, and everything here is up.

The escarpment runs east and west farther than a day's walking: a long scarp of pale stone, a few hundred feet of sheer face, and above the face a rim of wind-scoured pavement rock. Along that rim, for the better part of a mile, stand the combs.

They are the first thing anyone mentions and they are hard to describe honestly, because the honest description sounds too simple: frames. Tall rectangular frames of silvered wood and salvaged iron, ten and twelve feet high, set in rows angled to the wind, and each frame strung — top rail to bottom rail — with hundreds of vertical cords. Goat-hair cord, twisted gut, here and there a length of drawn wire older than anyone's grandmother. When the cloud presses against the scarp and the wind drives the fog up over the rim, the fog goes through the strings, and the strings take their toll. Each cord beads. The beads swell, join, and run, and at the foot of every frame a channel carved into the living rock catches the run and carries it inward, and every channel joins another channel, generation-deep grooves all conspiring gently downhill toward the dark mouths of the cistern caves. You can read the whole economy of the place in the color of the stone: dark and lichened wherever water has been running for centuries, bone-pale everywhere it hasn't. The map of the harvest is written in stain.

Behind the rim, in the wind-shadow, is the town — perhaps three hundred souls, though the Combs count households and goats and cups, never souls. Low dry-stone houses roofed with slabs, built half into the rock, doors facing away from the weather. Below the houses, terraces: garden beds the size of beds, each one walled, each one owed a written measure of water and not a spoonful more. And under it all the cisterns — cool caves where the channels end, where the drip is constant and musical and every voice comes back twice.

The dryness is the first strangeness, once you understand what you are looking at. Rain here almost never falls. The sky above the Combs stays empty while the cloud arrives *below* the sky, sliding along the floor of the air like something keeping its head down, water that comes sideways, on schedule, from somewhere else. The Combs own no weather. Every drop they drink walked in on the wind from a low hot place three days south, a place they have mostly never seen and speak of the way you speak of a rich, deaf, distant relation. They call it the Kettle. What the Kettle would call itself is not a question anyone up here can answer; but travelers who have stood on its smooth stone lip and looked down into the gray-gold steam say the name is fair. It holds heat the way a pot holds soup, and this cloud is the soup's breath, and the Combs live on the smell of it — except that a comb is a tool for making a smell into a meal.

The sound is the second strangeness. The combs sing. Wind through a thousand tensioned cords makes a voice — thin, high, chordal, never the same for two minutes together — and the people read it the way farmers elsewhere read the sky. A rising fifth off the western rows means the cloud is swinging heavy; a dry buzz in the old wire frame means dust, not fog; children are taught the pitches before their letters. It is a loudness that never repeats, and this is the exact inverse of the Kettle's famous roar, which never changes: down there the noise is so constant it works as silence, and up here the silence is so rare it works as alarm. When the wind dies at the Combs, everyone stops. Everyone listens. A still hour is remarked on for days.

The third strangeness takes no time at all to notice if you have come up from the south: everything here is small, and everything small is busy. Where the Kettle keeps a population of enormous pensioned individuals, each ancient plant alone in its forty feet of settled ground, the Combs are crowded to the last crack. Cushion plants wedged in the pavement rock, gray-green and hard as upholstery. Thyme-smelling scrub fighting for wall-joints. Lichens in territorial wars measured in centuries and millimeters. Bees working the terrace walls; swifts that drink off the comb-strings on the wing, never landing; goats on the scarp face standing at angles that should not be possible, cropping what should not be croppable. Nothing here has settled anything. Every boundary is contested every season. The flowers advertise furiously, in colors meant for eyes, and their pollen is built light and dry, made to ride wind for miles — pollen with papers for the whole world, where the Kettle's pollen long ago surrendered its passport. Lushness without crowding, down there. Up here, crowding without lushness — and not one living thing that has stopped working.

The fourth strangeness keeps to certain mornings, and is better told with the residents, because it may be residents.

**Who Lives in the Combs**

The first honest answer is *everyone*, and it is a true answer, and it is the shallow one. People live here, obviously, audibly: string-makers and channel-carvers and cistern-wardens and goatherds and one tinsmith, houses full of children who can name a wind by its chord. The place is loud with living the way the Kettle is silent with it. But stay, and the Combs turn out to be layered like their own rock, and the deeper residents declare themselves slowly, the way the far cloud declares its lean — by always doing the same thing until you finally see it.

**The string-makers live here**, and theirs is the honored craft, because a comb is only as good as its cords. They comb the goats in spring, twist and wax the hair, stretch gut on frames through the winter, and they speak of a string's working life the way you would speak of a person's — its taut years, its slack years, its retirement. A retired string is never thrown away. It is woven, with others of its generation, into coarse strong wrapping-cloth, and the wrapping-cloth has exactly one use, which we will come to, because everything at the Combs comes to it eventually.

**The carvers live here**, deepening the channels a grain or two a year, and if you have stood in the Kettle you will feel the rhyme before you can say it. Down there the water builds itself a skeleton — the mineral beards under the pipe joints, deposit, a few grains a year, addition with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. Up here the same water carves itself a path — subtraction, a few grains a year, the same patience with the opposite sign. The carvers do not know they are finishing the water's sentences. They only know that a channel wants to go a certain way, and that fighting a channel's wants is wasted work.

**The wardens live here**, and they keep the ledger: every household's measure, every terrace's due, every cistern's depth, going back in an unbroken column of figures for longer than the town has had its present name. The ledger's first pages are the strangest thing the Combs own. They are not about water. They are diagrams, drawn in a confident antique hand nobody now reads fluently — pipes, a great wheel, towers in section, valves, and beside certain figures a doubled notation, two strokes close together, over and over, like a pulse taken and taken and taken. The wardens keep those pages the way you keep scripture you cannot pronounce. Whether the Combs are the descendants of hands that once built something, three days south and down, and moved up out of the heat when the something no longer needed them daily — that is not a question the ledger answers. The ledger only keeps the pages, and keeps them first.

**The water lives here**, and this is the recognition that takes longest and then cannot be un-had. It is the *same water*. The Kettle's loop — pumps to towers to channels to mud to pumps — is famously closed, topped up only by whatever rain finds its way down through the steam; but a loop that breathes is not closed, it is only slow, and the breath is the plume, and the plume is the cloud, and the cloud is the Combs' entire fortune. Every drop in the cisterns has been through the pumps. Every drop has hung as a bead on a leaf as broad as a door, has hissed out of a tower on its own schedule, has ridden the wind three days north, has been combed out of the fog by a string some grandmother twisted. The people of the Combs are, by weight, mostly that water. It is in their blood keeping their smaller time; it leaves them as sweat and breath and the transpiration of three hundred walled gardens; and the wind takes that too, and some of the wind goes south. Some of what the Combs breathe out falls on the basin as the rare rain that tops the loop. The pumps down there say *it does not need anyone*, twice with every beat, and it is true — it needs no one. But it is not true that no one needs it, and it is not quite true that nothing comes back. The loop was never closed. It was only larger than the basin.

Which brings us to the bundle, and the cup, and the mornings.

Once a year, at the dry moon, when the plume runs thinnest and the singing off the combs goes reedy, two walkers go south with the bundle: a side of goat-leather, a coil of waxed thong, a crock of tallow-and-beeswax grease, new cord — all of it wrapped in the cloth woven from retired strings, so that the strings that spent their lives taking the Kettle's water go home at last around the Kettle's leather. Three days down. The walkers cross the cooling ten degrees in as many steps, set the bundle on the smooth stone lip, and turn their backs on the noise, and come home. By the next morning the bundle is gone. No one has ever watched it go; watching is considered the worst manners the Combs recognize. They call the whole arrangement *paying for the weather*, and whether the weather is for sale, or the payment is rent, or the entire exchange is two very old households keeping a courtesy neither remembers beginning — that is not a question either place answers.

But there is a rule for the walkers, and the rule is: don't let the noise into your pulse. Stand on the lip, set the bundle down, and do not stay listening, because the pumps down there keep a time — *thud-THUD, thud-THUD* — and a heart is an impressionable instrument. Most walkers keep the rule. Some do not. The ones who do not come home changed in a small, patient way: they tap tables in doubled time; they wake at odd hours already keeping it; they stand out at the rim on still nights with one hand laid flat on the rock, listening through the palm the way a farrier listens to a flank. Everyone knows what it means. Nobody hurries it. Within a few years such a person goes to the first cistern cave, where a dented tin cup hangs on a peg — there is always exactly one — and takes it down, and walks south, and does not come back. The tinsmith beats out another cup and hangs it on the peg. The plume, people say, runs a little fatter for a generation after; the Combs keep no measurements of the cloud, because measuring it is considered rude, so no one can prove this, and no one at the Combs wants it proved.

Travelers who have been inside the works report a tin cup on a valve wheel in the coolest gallery, dented, polished bright on one side by what looks like a century of the same grip. It is the same grip. It is taught to every child at the Combs before they can carry a full cup without spilling: two fingers through the handle, thumb on the dent. A century of hands, all schooled by the same grandmothers, is one grip.

And then the mornings — the fourth strangeness, kept for last, because the Combs keep it quietly. Now and then the wind backs, the plume swings, and instead of streaming along the scarp face the cloud comes up and *over*, whole: a whiteout, every comb streaming, every string strung with beads, the town's edges dissolved. People stay in on those mornings, not exactly from fear. The ones who have been out in it say the fog holds figures — drift gathered briefly into the shape of someone, standing at a comb with a hand raised flat to the strings as if listening through the palm, holding still for the space of a doubled beat, then letting go and being fog again. The Combs call these mornings *visits*. On visit mornings they set full cups of water on the doorsteps, and by noon the cups are empty, which proves nothing in air as thirsty as this, and which nobody wants proved either. Down in the Kettle, they say, what goes into the works accumulates — condenses, patient as a mineral beard, into roughly the shape of a person sitting down. Up here, it seems, the same tenants keep a second address, and arrive as they must arrive, being what they now are: thin, and traveling, and made dense again for a moment by a thousand cords of goat-hair, because that is what the Combs are *for*. A condensery too, when you come to it — the daughter-works, string where the mother is iron, taking what is thin and making it dense, the one trick the family has ever had.

On the stillest nights, when the wind is fully down and the combs stand silent and the silence is deep enough to be an event, you can do what the changed ones do. Lie at the rim. Put your ear to the rock. It comes up through the bones of the country from three days south — faint, doubled, patient: *thud-THUD, thud-THUD*. Every child at the Combs has been sung to sleep in that meter. The lullaby is old and most of its verses have worn smooth as the Kettle's lip, but the refrain is clear, and it answers the far beat the way the far beat asks. The pumps say *still here, still here*. The Combs sing back, twice with every beat:

*Still there. Still there.*
