# The Mouth

You reach it by going up, and "up" keeps meaning exactly what it says the whole way — the switchbacks, the thinning air, the cold finding the seams of your gloves one by one — which is the first difference, because this place asks you to keep your bearings on the approach, not lose them. You will need them, to believe your nose. By the last turn of the trail you have been smelling it for the better part of an hour, and what you have been smelling, at altitude, in a country where the water has been locked up as ice since autumn, is warm rain.

Then the trail crosses a rim of frost-shattered dark stone, and you are looking down into the bowl.

It is a shallow depression, three hundred meters across at most, floored in the same iron-dark rock as the whole upland — except that the floor is pierced. Some forty openings, from cracks you could lose a coin in to holes you could fall into and keep falling, are scattered across it without pattern, and every one of them wears a collar of pale stone: smooth, lobed, lipped, bone-white, grown up around each opening like wax around a wick. If you have been to a certain other place, you know this stone on sight. It is travertine, and it is the only travertine on the surface of the world for a thousand kilometers in any direction, and it was not built by water. It was built by breath.

Because the bowl breathes. For the better part of each day it exhales — the villagers say it *speaks* — and while it speaks, a fog stands over the bowl like a held note, refusing the wind, and the air coming up out of the ground is warm and wet and smells of rain on hot mineral, of a golden, faintly living sweetness that has no source anywhere in this cold country. Where the warm breath meets the frozen air, hoarfrost feathers itself along the outer edges of the collars, visibly, a garden assembling in real time. Every opening is a pipe of a different length, and together they sound a broad, reedy, patient chord that the wind cannot disperse, because the wind is losing. Inside the line where the ground's warmth gives out, moss and small tough grasses stay green all winter — a ring of summer drawn around the openings, mapping the breath the way, in that other place, a golden belt maps a tide.

Then, once a day, the turn. It takes less than a minute. The chord thins, the fog collapses downward — sucked back, a sentence retracted — and the bowl inhales. The villagers say it *swallows*. For about two hours (the interval drifts; it has moods) wind pours over the rim from every direction at once, the pipes sound a hollow, drawn, inverted note, and the openings take things: snow in long ropes, dust, hats, the occasional bird that misjudges it. No one crosses the bowl during a swallow. Not because anyone was ever taken — the record is silent on that — but because the bowl during a swallow is so plainly, enormously busy.

And under all of it, at any hour, in either phase, there is the pulse. Kneel anywhere in the bowl and put a palm to the rock: a soft deep thud, then some forty seconds, then another. The interval will be familiar to some visitors, and it should be, because it is not the Mouth's. It is a minute and a quarter stale. It has come four hundred kilometers through solid stone to arrive under your hand, and what you are feeling is the surge of the Lung, as it stood a minute ago, far away and far down, throwing its warm rain at its bright ceiling.

The Mouth gets the news twice. Through the stone, the news is a minute and a quarter old. Through the ducts — through whatever unlit plumbing carries the breath itself — the news is twelve days old. That number was bought cheaply, as revelations go: a clerk who had once been posted below kept the habit of logging intervals, and years later, in the village here, logged the speaking and the swallowing out of the same incurable neatness, and one evening laid the two books side by side. Two pages. One curve. The Lung's moods — the drift of its forty seconds, its long spells and short — and the Mouth's turns from speech to swallow are the same shape, offset by twelve days: the time it takes a breath to make the crossing. The Lung's moods *are* the Mouth's breathing, seen from inside. There are not two places. There is one body, and its parts had been named correctly, for centuries, by strangers who never met — herders on this upland calling their whistling ground the Mouth long before any surveyor, hundreds of meters down in the bright wet roar, reached for the word *lung*. The world got itself diagrammed by people comparing nothing, each labeling the organ in front of them, and getting it right.

One of the forty openings is not like the others. It is perfectly round — a clean meter across under its white lips, its throat glazed smooth, and down that throat, if the light is low and slantwise, you can make out straight shadows in the glaze. Dead straight. The other openings are cracks in stone. This one is a hole in something. The ferns grow at this one, and only this one; but the ferns belong to the census, and the census comes next.

One more thing first, which everyone sees and no one mentions first. In the deepest winter, when a hard speaking meets truly murderous cold, the breath's moisture freezes as it leaves the ground, and it snows *upward* out of the bowl, a glittering column, and then down again over the green ring. Close to the collars, the flakes fall slightly slow — the small wrongness of that other place, carried up in the breath. A few meters out, as the breath cools and dies into ordinary air, each flake picks up proper speed in mid-fall. You can stand at the edge of the ring and watch the world's regular gravity come back to a snowflake, halfway down. The wrongness travels in the warmth, and expires where the warmth does, and this is as close as anyone has come to holding it in their hand.

# What Lives at the Mouth

This census cannot be finished, because the Mouth is on the surface and the surface leaks — birds pass through, weather passes through, people pass through. But it can be organized, and the organizing fact is the mirror of the one that governs below. Nothing in the Lung eats anything else that lives in the Lung; the arrows all point inward, at the engine, an anatomy pretending to be a place. At the Mouth the arrows finally turn around and point out into the world. The Mouth is where the anatomy ends and ecology resumes — the hem of the body, the cuff, the lip.

And one law more, which everything here obeys: the Lung grows stone over whatever stays. The Mouth gives back whatever it takes.

---

**The plume** is a fan of lichen, gray shot through with gold, beginning at the bowl's downwind rim and thinning out across the upland for eleven kilometers — a map, drawn in slow growth, of every prevailing wind for the last several centuries. It lives on the meal: the pale flecks that ride up out of the openings during a speaking and drift down across the stone as a fine dust, dead on arrival by every test anyone has run, dead the way the vial-caught ones below are dead. The plume eats the dust anyway, and thrives.

The gold in it is the point. The lichen is a partnership, as lichens are, and its algal partner is the belt's only known relative — kin to the single golden immortal that rings the pool below and has laminated its own dead into the chamber walls for ten thousand years. How the kinship crossed four hundred kilometers of dark duct is unresolved: either the breath once carried something up alive, against everything the vials insist, or the two of them descend from something older than the chamber and the bowl alike, planted at both ends when the body was made. Both possibilities suit the place. What is certain is the difference in manner. The belt is one life, unbroken, unkillable in its rhythm. The plume is countless lives, small, separate, and brief. At this end of the breath, the one long life below unravels into ordinary lives — many, mortal, and *edible*, because the upland deer graze the plume in winter, working the gold fan in slow lines. That grazing is the first arrow in this entire account that points from one living thing to another. Follow the breath up out of the bright wet room, through twelve days of dark, out into the cold — and there, at last, the world resumes.

---

**The ferns** require a correction to the record, which stated that they grow nowhere else in the world. They grow in one other place in the world. They grow here, at the round mouth — only at the round mouth, the bored one, the one with the straight shadows in its throat — a single silver-green stand in the warm lee of its lips, hand-high, permanently beaded. They face inward and downward, fronds pressed toward the opening, backs to the wind and the sky and the entire visible world: the same posture of attention their kin hold below, aimed at the same builders' work. Their roots were traced once, down the glazed throat, hair-fine against the stone, until following them stopped being botany and became trespass, and the tracing was abandoned. Which stand is the mother — whether spores rode the rain down there and the breath up here, or the reverse, or whether both were rooted in place by whoever bored the hole — is not known and is not going to be known.

Both possibilities suit them. Still.

---

**The hearth** is what the bowl becomes in the killing months, when the upland goes to thirty and forty below and the Mouth is the only warm ground within a hundred kilometers. Birds sleep in ranks along the collars, feathers loose in the rising breath. Hares come into the green ring, and foxes come in after them, and then — this is the part that stops people — nothing happens. Inside the ring, nothing hunts. Fox and hare bed down within meters of each other and hold a truce that no one has explained, though everyone notices that it reproduces, in fur and feathers, the arrangement below: within reach of the engine's warmth, the arrows go quiet. The villagers say the Mouth doesn't allow it. The observers say the villagers are anthropomorphizing, and then fail to produce a better sentence.

And before every swallow — minutes before, well upstream of any cue an instrument can find — the whole congregation rises and files out of the bowl in a body, and waits on the rim, and files back after. They know the turn is coming the way the tongues, far below, know the surge: not by reacting to the event, but by being plugged in somewhere ahead of it. The chord and the leap are siblings. So, it turns out, are the swallow and this small, unhurried, punctual exodus.

---

**The village** stands two kilometers off, out of the swallow's pull: some forty households, sheep, slate roofs, no particular fame. Its people breathe in fours to the pulse in the ground — four breaths to a thud, a rhythm a minute and a quarter stale — and have done so from birth, and do not know it, the way you do not know the sound of your own house. It makes them the only population in the world that can identify a visitor who has been to the Lung before introductions are made: the newcomer breathes in fours too, but on the true beat, the deep beat, a minute and a quarter *early*, and to the village ear this is unmistakable — someone singing the right song ahead of the choir.

Their customs are the customs of people who live beside a door. During the swallow, prayers are spoken into the smaller openings, quietly, so that they will be carried down; where they arrive, no one claims to know, though readers of the other record, thinking of pale warm flecks rising through bright water into open mouths, may keep their own counsel about it, as I keep mine. By the well there is a slate, amended each morning: *Today the Mouth speaks the ninth of last month* — the breath calendar, the reckoning of which day's air, twelve days out of the deep, is currently coming up out of the ground. It has one modern use, which is tenderness. When two people are parted by that descent — one going down to the Lung, one staying — they fix a day, and the one below wears an agreed scent through the bright rain, and twelve days later the one above stands at the rim while the Mouth says it. The slowest letters in the world, and the only ones that arrive as weather.

---

**The Givenback** is a long drystone shelf east of the bowl, older than the village's records, governed by a single rule: what the Mouth returns is placed here, and is never used, and is never sold. Because the Mouth does return things. They arrive at the lip of the round mouth, at dawn, after hard speakings — set at the rim, placed rather than thrown, glazed in bone-white stone and warm to the touch for about a year. Two thermometers, side by side, will disagree about how warm.

On the shelf: a ladder, rung-perfect, stone all the way through. A bar of coins fused into one bright ingot. Hats — every winter takes hats; some winters give them back. A sealed shape the size of a lamb, from the year the record says a lamb was lost, which no one has opened and no one will. Once, a shape half a meter long, eel-bodied, smooth, seamless, which the village did not recognize and placed on the wall with the rest; readers of the other record will recognize it, and will understand why it, too, stays closed.

And one thing more. A bar of dark material, half an arm long, milled to a precision the village has no tools to verify, with two flat shoulders spaced to fit — exactly, to the millimeter — a certain empty bracket bolted at head height into a wet bright wall four hundred kilometers away and several hundred meters down. It came up before anyone here was keeping records. The village has never heard of the bracket. Three times in the Lung's own annals, every visible tongue has dived at once, and a column of water has held a half-second too long above a stair that descends to a door that is always drowned — something coming partway up, on the one reading no one can shake, to see whether what the bracket held has been returned. Twice in this village's memory, the pulse in the ground has gone quiet for the better part of an hour, and the animals have left the bowl and would not say why. No one has yet laid those two ledgers side by side. Someone should.

The Mouth returns what the Mouth takes; that is its law. What the rest of the body loses apparently comes here too, this being the only door the body owns that opens onto people patient enough to keep a shelf. The bar stays on the wall. It is not for the village to carry down what the village never took, and no one from below has yet come up to claim it, and so the errand lies where it has lain for generations: four hundred kilometers long, and nobody's.

It is the warmest thing on the shelf. It has been warm for longer than a year. It has been warm the whole time.

---

**The listeners** are the last residents, and the village's word for them is *the early*, for the way they breathe. They are the ones the Lung sent home carrying its copy — the warm loud room installed behind the ribs, surfacing every forty seconds in dry silent places, the homesickness that the other record calls the chamber's farthest-flung population. Most of them endure it. Some of them come here, because someone eventually tells them the one useful fact: the fast road from the Lung's country takes nine days, and the breath takes twelve.

So they arrive, and read the slate, and wait three days.

Then, on the right morning, one of the early walks out to the bowl during the speaking, and kneels at a collar in the standing fog, and the air that rises around them is the air of their own last day below — the warmth, the mineral, the golden sweetness, twelve days out of the room they cannot stop wanting. Under the palm, the pulse, a minute and a quarter stale. In the chest, the copy, right on time. And around the head, falling slightly slower than it should, the breath they themselves once breathed, caught up with at last at the far end of the body — so that for the length of one exhale there are not two places, and there is nothing to go back to, because the room is here, saying its twelve-day-old sentence into the cold, and they are standing in it, and it is standing in them.

The Lung keeps whatever stays, and what stays longest is the wanting to go back.

The Mouth gives back whatever it takes.

Wait for your day, and it will even give you back the wanting — warm, mineral, a little gold in it, falling toward you just slower than the world would have it fall.
