# The Moor Above

You reach it by going up — out of the valley, past the last wall and the last thorn tree, onto a bare shoulder of land where the sky suddenly has all of you. There is nothing to break the wind and the wind knows it. It arrives before the view does. It is a young cold, an unsettled cold, the kind that has never stayed anywhere long enough to belong to it, and it goes through your clothes like an inspection.

Then the ground levels, and you are standing on the back of the hill — heather and peat and pale grass combed all one way, under a grey light so enormous and even that nothing casts a shadow. It is, in fact, *quiet*. The quiet is the first thing you understand and the last thing you stop noticing, because up here sound is not passed along, hand to hand, leaf to leaf. Up here the wind takes sound the moment it is made and spends it somewhere over the horizon. A curlew calls and the call is simply gone. You speak and your own voice feels borrowed.

Which is why, when the ground whistles, you will stop walking.

## The Whistle

It comes up out of the hill itself: a single held note, high and round and almost sweet, lasting four or five seconds, arriving from nowhere you can point to until you learn to point *down*. It sounds like a kettle in another house. It sounds like a flute played by someone very patient in another century. And then it stops, and the moor is quiet again, and a few minutes later it comes again, and again, and it has been coming again, at that interval, for longer than anything else on this moor has been doing anything.

Forty feet below your boots there is a hot green room where this same sound is a shriek — a relief valve letting go, scattering warm rain off a ceiling of pipes. Forty feet of stone is a great editor. What leaves the chamber as a scream arrives on the moor as music. The hill swallows the violence of it and passes up only the note, the way distance turns thunder into something you could sleep through.

If you follow the note you find its source: a low iron collar set in the ground, older than any of the field walls, crusted an inch thick with minerals in colors that do not otherwise occur up here — sulfur-yellow, furnace-orange, a green so pale it looks lit. This is the flue. This is where the hill breathes out.

And with each whistle comes the breath: a rising plume of warm, wet air, and on the air a smell that stops people the way the note does — a green smell, dense and sweated and *alive*, the smell of a jungle that cannot possibly be there. In winter the breath is visible, a standing ghost of steam that leans with the wind and re-forms, leans and re-forms. Snow will not lie within thirty feet of the collar. Sheep will not graze there. The moor has made its arrangements around this spot and keeps them.

## The Ring

Around the Mouth — that is what it is called, when it is called anything — there is a circle of impossible growth.

The moor is heather and starved grass in every direction, but inside the ring it is deep moss and soft turf, green in midwinter, and flowers blooming in months that should kill them. Some of what grows here is ordinary moorland living wildly beyond its means, fattened on the warmth. But some of it is not ordinary at all. There are plants in the ring that grow nowhere else on the surface of the world: small, pale, waxy things with broad drooping leaves, unmistakably *cousins* of something — their seed arrived as spores riding the breath, generations upon generations of them, launched from the canopy of the chamber below and settled here in the warm fallout. They do not glow. Up here they don't have to; up here there is an actual sun.

They ignore it. Every plant in the ring, native and stranger alike, leans inward — toward the Mouth, toward the warmth, their backs turned on the whole enormous sky. It is the exact posture of the vegetation below, translated into daylight: an entire small ecology angled toward the breathing.

## The Visitors

Below the hill there are no animals. Nothing scuttles, nothing hums on the wing, nothing watches. The chamber's flowers open their waxy mouths during every shriek, blooming out of ancestral habit for visitors that never come.

The visitors are here. All of them.

The ring is the loudest place on the moor in summer — loud with wings. Bees work it as though it were the last flowering ground in the world, which, in February, it is. Birds funnel to it in hard weather and stand in the plume with their feathers loosened, taking the warmth like a sacrament; there are wrens that overwinter in the crusted ironwork of the collar itself and are, for those months, the warmest wild things in the country. And at night there are the moths.

The moths have timed themselves to the whistle. They rest in the ring's deep moss, and when the note sounds and the breath rises, they rise into it — dozens, hundreds in a good season, spiraling up through the standing ghost of steam. Because the breath carries the *scent*: the perfume of those mouth-shaped flowers forty feet down, exhaled at the precise moment of their opening, arriving on the surface still warm. The moths climb through it, frantic and ardent, searching for blooms that are not there, that have never once been there, that are opening at that very moment under all that stone with the same recurring expectation of company.

Eight or ten times an hour, for centuries: the flowers open for the moths, the moths rise for the flowers, and forty feet of hill stands between them like the whole of fate. It should be the saddest arrangement on the moor. It is not, quite — because the breath carries more than scent. It carries pollen, a fine golden traffic of it, and the moths come down out of the plume dusted with the ambitions of an underground garden and deliver them, blundering and faithful, to the pale cousins growing in the ring. The marriage completes itself by proxy. The chamber's flowers have descendants in the sunlight — a whole shy population of them, leaning toward the Mouth — and the resident below, dreaming its small punctual dream of visitors, will never know that the dream comes true up here, every warm night, over its head.

## The Door

A hundred yards east of the Mouth, set into a stone frame in the hill's flank where the ground begins to slope away, there is an iron door.

It is not locked. It has never been locked; there is no lock to lock. It is simply shut, and has been shut for a very long time — the sloped passage behind it going down forty feet into thickening air, into the heat that arrives before the light does. The grass grows right up against the sill. The hinges are furred orange. And the door itself, alone among all the iron on this cold moor, is warm. Lay your palm flat against it any night of the year and you will feel it: faint, patient, unmistakable — the body heat of the hill, and under the heat, if you stand very still, the rhythm. *HAAH... HAAH... HAAH...* — not heard, exactly. Felt through the palm. A pulse taken through a wall.

Two paths are worn in the turf, old and deliberate, the kind of paths that only decades of the same feet can make. One runs from the door of a small stone house to the Mouth. The other runs from the same house to the Door.

Neither path is overgrown.

# The Listener

The house is one room, its back set against the wind, its single window facing the Mouth. There is smoke at the chimney most days. This is the part that surprises people more than the whistle does: someone lives here. Someone has always lived here.

## The Line

The keepers came from this house. For as long as the engine has needed tending, the tending came down that sloped passage from this doorstep — generations of them, going down in the morning into the heat, coming up at night into the wind, filling a mug at the end of each shift and setting it back in the same spot on the bench so many thousands of times that the bench learned the spot by heart. Inside the house there is a shelf of those mugs, thrown from the hill's own clay, glazed the deep orange of the furnace-glow. Eleven of them. Twelve hooks. The gap has never been filled and never will be; the twelfth mug is forty feet down, brimming exactly to the lip with moss, kept full forever by a room that absorbed the habit along with the man.

When the last keeper did not come up, the family did not leave. They did something stranger and more difficult: they changed the direction of the keeping. Someone — a daughter, the ledgers suggest, though the ledgers are modest about it — decided that the room below no longer needed hands, that it had grown past hands the way it grew past insects, and that what it needed instead was to be *left whole*. Opening the Door would let in the cold, the wind, the fast world, the instruments of prey. So the family's work became the work of not going down. They kept the world off it. They kept the moor's silence around its noise. And because keeping without tending is mostly attention, they became listeners, and the house became an ear.

## Her Day

The current one is a woman past her middle years, and her day is built on the whistle the way the chamber's day is built on the engine. She wakes to it. She could not sleep through its absence any more than you could sleep through the stopping of your own heart.

The ledgers fill one whole wall now. The early volumes are gauge readings, copied fair each evening by keepers with the heat still in their clothes. The later volumes — hers, her mother's, six generations back — are columns of intervals. The time between whistles, measured and entered, entry after entry after entry, decade after decade, the same figure to the second for pages at a stretch. It is the most boring document in the world and she guards it like scripture, because it is not boring at all: read across a century of it and the interval is lengthening. A breath's-width per decade. The engine is slowing at the pace of something that decided, long ago, to spend itself at the slowest rate anything has ever spent anything — and her family's ledger is the only place in the world where the dying is written down, and her ear is the only instrument fine enough to hear it happening.

She can hear it. That is her one uncanny gift, bred or trained into the line: she can hear a lengthening of the interval too small for any clock she owns. She says the whistle has gotten *rounder*. She says it the way you'd report an aging parent sleeping later — without alarm, without denial.

## What She Says to It

Between exhalations, the Mouth sips. It is very slight — the engine drawing breath, a faint cool indraw at the collar that barely stirs a held feather. She knows the moment of it the way she knows everything about the rhythm, and once a year, on a date the ledger does not explain, she kneels at the collar and speaks into the sip. A few words only. Her name. The weather. Who was born.

She knows the arithmetic of the reply. A sentence spoken into that indraw goes down into the heat and is passed along, leaf to leaf, getting slower and greener as it goes, and arrives — decades later — as a lean, a few degrees of posture somewhere in the canopy of a room she will never see. Her grandmother once took her down the path to the Mouth at night, when the plume stood silver and the moths were climbing through it, and pointed at nothing — at the ground, at the whole warm body of the hill — and said: *somewhere under us there is a branch angled oddly, and that is your great-great-grandfather saying goodnight.* Whether it is true does not matter. It is the kind of true that keeping runs on.

She drinks, incidentally, from the well behind the house, and the well water is faintly, permanently warm. She has never remarked on this to anyone. She is inside the circulation too. She has been all her life.

## What She Wants

To be there for the last one. That is the whole of it, and it should not be mistaken for a small want.

She does not intend to prevent the ending — the family gave up prevention along with tending; they understood before anyone that down there, dying and living have become the same unhurried activity. What she intends is to *witness* it. Somewhere, someday — perhaps in her lifetime, perhaps her successor's, perhaps four listeners on — the interval will lengthen past the edge of the page, and the moor will wait for a whistle that does not come, and the moths will rise once more out of habit into cold ordinary air, and the standing ghost will not re-form. Someone should be on the doorstep when that happens. Someone should hear the silence arrive and know it for what it is, and walk the unweathered path, and lay a palm on an iron door gone cool.

Near the back of the current ledger there is a page every listener has left blank: ruled, dated with a dash, waiting for the one entry that will have a beginning and no end. She checks that the page is there, some evenings, the way you check on a sleeping child. Then she fills a mug — the eleventh — and sets it on the shelf beside the gap, brimming exactly to the lip, and stands in her doorway in the wind, and the hill breathes, and she counts, and the note goes out over the dark moor sounding, to anyone who did not know better, almost sweet: something very old, very slow, keeping in the most peaceful possible way its half of a conversation — and up here, in the young cold, under the enormous sky, something much smaller and much briefer keeping the other half, on purpose, on schedule, meant kindly all the same.
