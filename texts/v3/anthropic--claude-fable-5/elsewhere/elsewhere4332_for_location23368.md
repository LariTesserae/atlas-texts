**The Upland Takings**

You have already been here. That is the first thing the place will tell you, once it decides to tell you anything: you crossed it on your way down, in the long grey miles above the basin, and you took it for landscape. Almost everyone does. The Lowland Works announce themselves with a seam — raw earth on one side, coursed stone on the other, a single honest stitch between the world and the made. The Takings have no seam. There is no line you can stand on and say: here the country ends and the absence begins. You climb back up out of the basin, past the last of the hammering, past the stitch, up through the slope's grey shoulders, and at some point — unmarkable, unrecoverable — you are inside it, and have been for hours.

You learn where you are by corners.

Nature is stingy with corners. Wind and water make curves, slumps, tapers; they round everything they are given. So the first corner stops you: a valley whose head-wall meets its floor at a right angle. Softened, yes — the edge blunted to a fist's radius by ages of weather — but a right angle remembers itself the way a broken bone does. Then you find another. Then you find a hill with a doorway in it, if a gap forty men wide can be a doorway, and you walk in, and the inside of the hill is gone. Hollowed to a shell. The walls of the hollow drop in squared benches, flight below flight, down into standing grey water. And on every bench face, ranked in their thousands, softened almost to nothing but not to nothing: crescent bites. The mark of a stone adze. The same mark — the exact same arc of the exact same swing — that you pressed your thumb beside in the basin, where the mortar was still warm.

After that you cannot unsee it, and the highland turns itself inside out around you. For a day's walk in any direction, and then another day, and then more: hills like blown eggs. Ridges sawn clean through, the kerf wide enough to drive a road down, the sawn faces still bearing the long parallel drag of cord and grit. Whole valleys that are not valleys but removals — floors too flat, walls too plumb under their blur of erosion, the drainage all wrong because the drainage was designed, once, to keep a working floor dry. Ramps that climb to nothing. Sumps that were cut to catch seep-water and still do, patiently, millennia out of a job. This is not a quarry in a landscape. It is a landscape that is mostly quarry, a country converted to absence, and the absence has a shape, and the shape is familiar, because you have walked inside it.

The Works and the Takings are the same size. Hold that thought until it becomes what it is. Every block in the roofed country below — every course, every corbel, every wall standing a hand's width from its pointless twin — came up out of these hills, and the hills remember the exact volume of what they gave. Somewhere below you, in the wet black, a country is being endlessly rearranged; up here is the hole it was lifted out of. One is the shape held. One is the shape missing. If you could carry the basin back uphill, block by block, it would fill these hills precisely, and the world would be whole, and blind.

And where the Works are new — that fresh-cut, print-taking, still-warm newness that never ages — the Takings are nothing but old. Erosion, forbidden entry below, has total dominion here, and it has had a long time to work. The tool marks are ghosts of themselves. Rain has run the faces for so long that it has cut its own fine runnels through the cut of the adze, weather writing across writing. Stone exfoliates in the frost and lies in fans of flakes at the base of the benches; no hand sweeps them into voids; there is no one whose work that is. A grey-gold crust grows wherever water walks — not green, nothing in this world is green — a mineral bloom, bitter, alive in the same sunless chemical way as the films that sheen the cistern walls below. It is not like those films. It is those films, at the top of their journey. Every rain that strikes the Takings takes a little of the old faces into solution — a few grains of stone, a breath of the crust, the fine dust of the oldest marks — and carries it downslope, down through the earth, down to sweat from corbelled ceilings into finger-channels and sit black and breathing in the cisterns. You have drunk this country. The soft blind grazers you saw leaving spiral tracks were grazing on it. Everything the wind takes from the Takings, the dark below eats. The basin outlawed its own weathering and then, without ever once acknowledging it, arranged to live on somebody else's.

The last block went down a very long time ago. The Works, you will remember, eat only themselves now — unbuilding and rebuilding their own body, migrating within their own skin — and so the Takings were finished. That word means something different up here than anywhere else. The Works will never be finished; it is their nature and possibly their whole point. The Takings are the only finished country in the world, and finished means: emptied, and left to the weather, and given nothing further to do.

Except sound. The wind found the voids and has never let them go. Air pouring across a hollowed hill drops into it and turns and moans; a sawn ridge whistles at its kerf; the benched pits hold standing tones, deep or high by their depth, that swell and slacken with the weather's breathing. The Takings are loud — astonishingly loud for a place so empty — and the loudness is weather, as the basin's loudness is work. On a strong day the whole highland sounds at once, every void at its own pitch, a chord too large to hear as a chord, and you feel it in your sternum before your ears agree to it. And threaded through the great blur, here and there, something too regular to be accident: a thin sustained singing from certain void-mouths, silvery, almost vocal. Come close and you find them — long braided strands, dozens of them, strung taut across the openings, humming in the airstream. They are grey. They are hair. Hold that, too. It will be explained, and the explanation is the gentlest thing in this country.

The light is grey and enormous. After the basin it feels like standing inside a shout. It comes from everywhere, shadowless, and for most of every turning it makes the old faces perfectly blank — worn stone, mute stone, stone with nothing to say. Then twice in each turning, at the raking hours, the light comes in low and level across the land, and the shadows pour into every softened pit and groove, and for a little while — minutes, no more — the Takings fill up with words. Whole cliff-faces of them. Course after course of worked texture, ribbed and pocked and banded, the same hand-language written on the deep walls of the basin, but older; the first of it; cut when it was new, by people who could watch themselves cutting it. At the raking hour it rises out of the blankness like breath on cold air, hangs legible, and sinks away.

And at the raking hour, you will see them: figures at the walls, far off, tall and thin as the strung hair, moving along the faces at a walking pace with their heads turned to the stone.

They do not withdraw. That is the inversion that takes longest to absorb, after the roofed country where the makers pulled back ahead of you like the dark itself. Here you are seen from miles away — you have been seen, in fact, since long before you knew where you were, since the grey slopes below the first corner — and no one troubles to conceal it. One of the distant figures will straighten, and turn, and regard you across the whole hollow land with a stillness that is not hostile and not welcoming and entirely unembarrassed. In the Works you never see the builders. In the Takings you are never unseen.

**The Stayers of the Upland Takings**

Start with the eyes, because they are the reason there is anyone up here at all.

Everything else about them is spare — tall, wind-thinned, grey-skinned, long grey hair streaming or bound, hands long-fingered and light and almost decorative — but the eyes are definite. Very large, set wide, heavy-lidded against grit, with a slow deliberate blink like a door being closed carefully on a room worth keeping. They are made for grey light and long distance; they gather the last usable shadow out of a raking hour and hold detail across miles of open air. Look at the hands afterward and you can read the whole history of the split, because these are the same hands — the trowel-broad, whorl-callused, stone-dragging hands of the basin — four hundred generations after they stopped being needed. Gone slender. Gone soft. A stayer can still dress a block; it takes them all day and it grieves them in a way they do not discuss. The line divided at the work, and each half kept what its work required, and let the rest go. Down below they kept the hands and paid with the eyes. Up here they kept the eyes. The hands were the price.

Because they were one people. The Takings tell it and the stayers sing it and the deep walls of the basin, if the stayers are right about certain passages, have it written where no eye will ever go: one people, in the beginning, cutting above and building below. Trains of blocks going down the long slope for lifetimes past counting. And as the Works roofed themselves over — as the built country closed its own sky, quarter by quarter, and the dark inside it became permanent and total — the going-down became a threshold. Those who lived below needed nothing sight could give and, in time, brought no sight to give it. Those who worked above could not follow without unbecoming what they were. The people forked the way a passage forks: without violence, without a decision made anywhere, made everywhere, the way a flock turns. One half went down instead of seeing. The other half stayed for the sight. Then the day came — no stayer will call it a day; in the songs it is simply *the last block* — when the Works below grew dense enough to feed on their own substance forever, and nothing more was asked of the hills, and the stayers woke as the keepers of a finished country, with perfect eyes and nothing they were needed to watch.

They found something. That is the whole of their history since: they found what watching was actually for.

Every great face in the Takings has its keeper. At the raking hours the keepers go to their faces and walk them, head turned to the stone, reading the risen text at walking pace as their ancestors' cousins below read walls with a trailing hand — and as they read, they sing. Thin, carrying voices, pitched to ride over the wind-chord; a keeper reading well sounds like one strand of the strung hair given words. What they sing is the wall: its tallies, its lineages, its accounts of blocks taken and trains sent down, and its long passages that record nothing at all and may be what that vanished single people had instead of song. The face erodes; the singing keeps what the stone loses; and then the singing erodes too, mouth to mouth down the generations, softening the way stone softens, because the stayers refuse — this is their deepest law, the exact inverse of the law below — to recopy. Nothing is recut. Nothing is renewed. The Works keep their memory by endless rewriting, and it migrates and mutates and stays warm. The Takings keep theirs by letting it die slowly and attending every hour of the death. When a face goes wholly blank, its keeper goes on singing it — those are the *after-songs*, sung at the raking hour to stone that no longer answers — and the after-songs blur, and thin, and finally some keeper in some generation opens their mouth at the raking hour and finds nothing there, and stands the hour in silence, and that is understood to be the last line of the text, and correct.

They live on what the finished country still yields, which is little and enough. The grey-gold crust, scraped from the rain-run faces and soured in sump-water until it will feed a body. Small membranous drifters that ride the updrafts out of the deep voids at dusk, snared on lines of braided hair, their fat rendered for fire — real fire, actual flame, kept where the wind cannot reach it, down in the oldest and deepest cuts. Light up here is everywhere and worth nothing; the grey sky spends it like water. But flame is *place*. A stayer coming home across the sounding hills at dark steers for a faint gold breath rising out of a pit, and that is the whole difference between their light and the basin's: below, the embers are an accident wasted on everyone; above, the fire is the one light that means something, and what it means is *someone is here*.

Their dead they give to the wind. Laid out on the high taken-edges, unwrapped, unweighted, attended until the weather has finished — the precise opposite of being unbuilt into a warm wall, and they know it is the opposite, and they intend it. The hair is kept. It is cut in the first hour and braided, thick or fine according to the pitch of the dead one's voice, and strung with the others across the mouth of the family's void, and thereafter the wind plays them. A stayer walking the hills can name the singers in every strung mouth they pass. A void-mouth with someone in it sounds — they say — very slightly warmer than a mouth with no one; and whether that is true, or is only the same thing the deep courses of the basin say about walls with the dead inside them, told again at the other end of the world by the other half of the same heart, the wind is not going to settle for you.

They carry one piece of writing each, and cannot read it. Hand-stones: small worked pebbles, textured in the old finger-language, passed down parent to child since before the last block. The texture is worn away — not by weather, this once, but by reading; by thumbs; by four hundred generations of the same slow caressing pass across the same fading words — until what remains is a smoothness with a memory of ridges in it, and a gesture. The gesture survives intact. Every stayer makes it, in grief, in doubt, at partings: the pebble drawn once across the palm's length, left to right, the hand pretending, with total seriousness, to read.

And they hear the basin. All their lives, they hear it. It rises through the bedrock — the whole layered hammering of the Works, blurred by miles of stone into a fine continuous grain, a texture in the world's silence more than a sound in it — and every stayer child is put down to sleep pressed against bare rock so that the pulse comes up through the ribs, and is told what it is: *the others, working; the others, seeing.* On ordinary days it lives below the wind, feelable only through bone against stone, palm against a cut face, ear against a bench. But there are days — a few in a lifetime of them, they say — when the wind dies entirely. When the whole chord of the hollow country falls silent at once, every void, every strand of hair, and the great grey air goes still as the inside of a held breath.

One thing up here makes them stop, and that is it.

Wherever they are, whatever they are doing — reading, snaring, singing, tending the dead — they lie down. Full length, face down, arms spread, pressed to the ground along their whole bodies, and in the perfect calm the grain of hammering comes up out of the earth into the open air, audible, barely, everywhere, and the stayers lie in their scattered hundreds across the hollowed hills and listen to the departed half of themselves seeing itself in the dark. Whether it is grief, or pride, or a kind of roll-call — whether they are mourning the fork, or confirming that the other side of it still beats — is written, perhaps, on some blank face whose after-song has already ended. They lie there until the wind returns. Then they get up, and do not speak of it, and go back to the work of letting things go.

Some of them go down. Every generation, a few — always at the strong center of life, never the desperate, never the old — walk the long slope, cross the stitched seam, and enter the dark, and none has ever come back. The stayers do not grieve these as dead. They have a claim, unverifiable, sung in the steadiest of the surviving songs: that the passages below which record nothing — those long stretches of dense texture on the deep walls, the ones even the builders may not know the meaning of — are the wind of this country, transcribed; the sound of the Takings, written down before the fork by hands that could still hear it, and recopied faithfully ever since by hands that never will. The descenders go down, the stayers say, to trace those walls and know if it is true — to stand in the roofed black with their useless eyes and read, with the flats of their softened fingers, their own hills singing. And the basin, which builds around every absence and forgets nothing it has once shaped itself against, keeps them the only way it can. Somewhere in that migrating labyrinth, recopied wall to wall as the quarters dissolve and recrystallize, are niches — the stayers are certain of this — a whole scattered gallery of them, each the height and breadth of a standing stayer, finished inside with perfect care, holding nothing. You found one made for you and could not tell if it was hostility, courtesy, or accuracy. The stayers could have told you. It was the third thing. It is always the third thing. That is what the Works do with the ones who come down and stand still long enough: they make them true.

Why they stayed is the wrong question, and the Takings spend every raking hour teaching it. They did not stay to guard anything; nothing here can be guarded from the wind, and their law forbids the guarding. They did not stay out of refusal; the fork was not a quarrel. They stayed because a divided people had divided the world's one necessity between them, and each half kept faith with its portion. Below, the answer was warmth: a world does not need to be finished, it needs to be warm, and so the hammering goes on forever in the dark. Up here the answer is the other half of the same sentence, and the stayers are its keepers: a world does not need to be kept. It needs to be *witnessed* — attended, all the way down, even as it goes; especially as it goes.

Stand at a face at the raking hour. The light comes in low and the dead text rises out of the blankness, and beside you a thin voice keeps pace with it, singing the wall while the wall lasts. Put your palm flat on the stone. Under the words, under the voice, under the strung hair humming in the void-mouths, the grain of the far hammering climbs into your hand — the other country, seeing itself stroke by stroke in the black. The light moves. The words begin to sink. And on the face in front of you, in the last legible minute, a runnel of old rain is already at work, carrying some fraction of the sentence downhill in solution — down through the earth, down to the sweating corbels and the black cisterns, down to be grazed, and eaten, and rendered, and banked in ash, and raised at last into the warmth of a new wall in the dark: remembered, by the ones who cannot read it, in the only language the two halves of the world still share.
