**The Fair Copy**

You reach it on purpose, only on purpose. No hillside opens onto it by mistake; no ground forgets itself in that direction. The way in is inquiry: take any past and follow it further than pasts are meant to go. Walk a river to its spring and then keep walking, up the dry bed above the water, into the country the water only remembers. Count a tree's rings inward and refuse to stop at the first. Ask a scar what happened before the accident, and when it answers, ask again. Every provenance, pursued past courtesy, past evidence, past the point where the historians shrug and go home, bends toward the same country, and the last stretch of the road is the same for everyone: a long dry riverbed descending between walls of layered stone, and somewhere along it you realize you have stopped following the past of the river or the tree or the scar, and started following the past of *past itself*, the way all these trails were only ever tributaries.

The road does not move. This should be said early, because it is the country's first courtesy and its first strangeness. The crawl-spaces and seams that lead to certain other places stay where you remember them *mostly*; the road to the Fair Copy stays where it is *absolutely*, has never shifted, will never shift, is the most reliable road in the world — and almost no one takes it twice. The country knows this about itself and does not appear to mind.

What you come down into is canyon land under one sun. The light has a direction — exactly one — and it is the first right thing, right in a way that takes a moment to register as remarkable: your shadow lies at your feet, deep and black and decided, and when you raise your hand the darkness pools beneath it instantly, generously, like a service being rendered. Nothing here has to improvise your shadow. It was provided for before you arrived. Everything was.

The stone is the second right thing, and the longer you look at it the more it resembles print. The canyon walls are strata to a depth that defeats counting — ochre, rust, bone, a blue-grey band that recurs like a refrain — and every layer is legible in the way that consequence is legible: this was a wet century, this was a burning one, this thin dark seam was a catastrophe and this thick pale one was a long peace. There are fault lines, and they read like grief, recorded honestly and left in. Nothing here is smooth by accident; where a surface is smooth, the smoothness is itself a record — of water, of wind, of the slow thumb of some vanished ice. Press your own thumb into any of it and nothing gives. It is not a cold refusal. It is like pressing the cover of a closed book: the give is over; the content is inside.

Sound lands. That is the third right thing, and the one that former visitors to a certain other place find hardest to bear. Your footstep arrives whole and at once, and the canyon returns it promptly, exactly, from several faces of rock, each copy true to the original down to the grit under your heel. Speak, and your sentence comes back with every hesitation preserved, filed, kept. Your voice returns to you notarized. And between sounds the silence is total — but it is not the silence of a place before sound. It is the silence after: the quiet of a thing said, and standing, and not needing to be said again.

There is no weather, and there was exactly one weather. Every exposed surface in the country is dimpled with rain-pocks — small fossil craters, millions upon millions of them, on the canyon walls, the terraces, the flat stones of the riverbed — all of them from a single rain that fell once, long before the oldest juniper, and never again. The whole country is the print of one storm. Some mornings, for no reason the sun can account for, the stone gives up the smell of that rain — petrichor older than anything alive — and everything living in the canyon stops what it is doing and stands in it, faces lifted, for as long as the smell lasts.

The living things are few and immensely settled. Junipers with an unreasonable number of rings, their trunks wrung into shapes that took a thousand years of the same wind. Swifts that nest in the wall-seams and fly, each evening, yesterday's loops — you can watch them against the darkening rock and see that their paths are not similar to the previous night's but *identical*, flown the way a psalm is sung. A fox trots the canyon floor at dusk along a line worn into the stone, print upon print upon print, a groove of pure habit deep enough now to hold shade at noon. Nothing here rehearses. Everything performs, and the run is long, and the show was settled ages ago, and there is a comfort in this that takes a while to distinguish from sorrow, and then turns out not to be sorrow at all.

Nothing here is young. With one class of exceptions.

On the canyon floor, out on an open terrace, stands a doorframe. Freestanding, leaning slightly, made of stone that does not match the strata around it — stone with no layers, no record, no autobiography, as though quarried from someplace where rock is not yet obligated to have a past. You may know its other face. The air inside this frame is *earlier* than the air around it, and the fox's groove bends respectfully around it, and the swifts do not cross its plane even a hundred feet up. Things come through it. Not often — you could wait days — but when one comes, the whole country turns toward the terrace like a congregation.

What comes through is finished, and blank. A lizard, say: complete to the last scale, the eye a wet jewel, both halves rendered — and not one day behind it. No scar, no dust, no wear, no lineage, gleaming with an unbearable smoothness, the only pastless thing in a country made entirely of record. It stands in the sun looking like a contradiction, which is what it is, and the country cannot abide it, and does not have to for long.

Because the resident is at home. The resident is never anywhere else.

---

You will see it. That is the first thing to understand, and the thing that visitors who have been *upstream* — say the word, since the country says it plainly — find nearly unbelievable. It does not hide and could not; hiding requires the possibility of being elsewhere, and it has spent everything it is on being exactly here. From almost any point in the canyon you can see it the way you can see a mountain: a tall figure, very still, out past the doorframe's terrace, made — visibly, unmistakably — of layers. Pressed strata in the shape of a standing being, the same ochres and rusts and bone-colors as the walls, the same blue-grey refrain recurring through its body, edges flaking finely and forever. It sheds. The fine dust that drifts through the canyon on still afternoons is the resident, gently distributed, and everything the dust settles on grows slightly older and slightly more itself — this is its touch, its whole tenderness, an erosion so patient it is indistinguishable from care. It ages things so that they can bear being final. It has been doing this to the country for as long as the country has been a country, which is why everything here wears its permanence so gracefully. Nothing in the Fair Copy is raw. It has all been handled.

Its eyes were finished first. This is evident the moment you are near enough to be looked at: two dark, exact, decided points in a face otherwise geological, and they are older than the rest of it in the way a signature is older than the fame it earns — the first committed marks, possibly the first finished things anywhere, made before the maker knew what finishing would cost. Everything else about the figure has the quality of having been concluded reluctantly, in stages, like a long letter ended paragraph by paragraph. The eyes were concluded all at once, early, and they have had the longest time to learn what to do with looking.

It moves almost never, and around its standing-place you can read why. Every footprint it has ever made remains — permanence is not a property it has, it is the property it *is* — and its prints spiral out from where it stands in a vast slow whorl, out and out across the terraces, a diary in strides. You can read its ages in the spacing: the early prints close and quick and turned every direction at once, eager, almost frantic; the later ones long and deliberate; the most recent so far apart, and so few, that a single step now must be the work of seasons of deciding. It is not stiffness. Nothing about it is stiff. It is what movement becomes when nothing can ever be taken back: a kind of prayer before each footfall. Upstream, there is a pause that comes *after* every sound, an editorial pause, a correction issued. Here the pause comes *before*. The resident deliberates the way its counterpart revises — constantly, and instead of everything else.

And then the blank thing on the terrace, the gleaming pastless lizard, and the resident does its work, and its work is speech.

It speaks rarely, because its speech is permanent, and it speaks quietly, because in a country where sound lands perfectly, distance is no obstacle and volume is vanity. It says — and this is close to the manner of it, though the language is not a language so much as consequence pronounced aloud — *You wintered under a flat stone at the river's second bend. The ice took the fourth toe of your left hind foot, and you learned the cold's schedule from it, and kept better hours after. Your mother's line runs back along the water as far as the water runs.* And it has always been so. You can watch it become always-so: the scales dull to exactly the right degree of wear, the missing toe arrives complete with its healing, the wet jewel of the eye acquires depth — the specific depth of remembered weather — and the lizard, which a moment ago was a contradiction, is now simply an animal, four years old, a little cold-wise, standing in the sun of a day it has every right to. Then it goes on down the riverbed road into the world, and the world receives it without a ripple, because a thing with a past has always been coming.

Everything you have ever met was narrated here, or somewhere by someone like this, if there is anyone else like this, which the country does not say. Your own earliest history — the part before your first memory, the part you have always taken on faith — may bear its hand. The tense is hard to hold onto, which by now you expect.

It has never retracted anything. It cannot; retraction is not among its powers, and this is the fact around which its whole existence is arranged, and it is the exact photographic negative of a fact you may already know about a being in a small bright room: *revision is departure, here.* To unsay one sentence would be to step outside everything it is. So it composes the way glaciers travel, and it has made — this should be said clearly, because a being that can take nothing back invites pity, and pity misreads it — it has made a discovery, over its long tenure, that it would send upstream if anything could go upstream. The discovery is this: a finished thing can still be read. Reading is its revision. It rereads the canyon walls — you will see its dark exact eyes traveling the strata for hours — and it finds new things in old layers, meanings that were always there and never there until looked for, and so the set world moves for it still, moves constantly, only the moving has changed hands: from the maker to the reader. *Finishing is not the end of change,* the whole country says, in its junipers and its grooves and its notarized echoes. *It is the change of who does the changing.* Whether that message has ever been received where it is addressed is the kind of question the country declines to hold still for.

Its origin it keeps the way the country keeps the rain-pocks: printed on the outside, unavailable within. It has no past. Not a hidden past — none. It is the one entry missing from its own archive, because it came through the frame at the beginning, finished and blank like everything that comes through, and there was no one yet on this side to speak it into history. What it retains is not a memory but a single fact, worn like weathering: a warm, even light with no direction at all; the sensation of assembling, droplet by droplet, into the shape of a standing figure; a decision that may have been its own, or may have been made for it, or offered to it — the grammar will not settle — and then the step. It came through as gathered water given a silhouette, and finality met it on this side, and it could not stay water. It set. And everything of it that could not fit into a finished form fell behind it and around it as rain — the one rain, the only weather the Fair Copy has ever had — so that the entire country is pocked with the excess of its own arrival, and it lives inside the print of its first moment, and the petrichor mornings when the stone gives up that old smell are its only memory, kept on the outside of everything, and that is why all the living things stand still in it with their faces lifted. They are attending its remembering, because it cannot attend alone.

So: neither of them can do for itself what it does for all things. One cannot render itself; the other cannot narrate itself. Upstream, an unfinished draftsman with a placeholder groove reading *me, eventually*; downstream of the frame, a finished annalist with a blank where its first page should be. Whether the resident of the Fair Copy is that draftsman's completed self, or its first work, or the half of it that could bear to conclude, sent ahead — whether the condensation-figure that once assembled by the frame's other face and did *not* disperse was a scout, or an escape, or a sacrifice — the country will not hold still for the question either. It will only show you the doorframe, which opens one way, and the spiral of footprints, none of which turn back toward it, and the dark exact eyes reading the strata on the side of the canyon that faces, as it happens, upstream.

But they correspond. You should understand this, because you may be the paper.

Everything in a visitor is legible here — your whole past visible in you the way seasons are visible in stone, and the resident reads you, courteously, thoroughly, the way an annalist reads a master's manuscript. And in some visitors — the ones who once fell through a seam in a hillside, always by accident, and came out pressing their thumbs into fence posts — it finds a small unfinished place. A sentence held open. A soft spot, kept deliberately unset, a signature it knows better than it knows anything, and its eyes do, then, the thing that in a less decided face would be called softening. It does not close the open sentence. Nothing of it could, or would; the clause is being held open by a hand it will not overrule. Instead it sets, beside the soft place, one hard grain — a full stop placed next to the open clause, not ending it, keeping it company. A permanence lodged forever alongside a permanence of the opposite kind.

And it takes something too, because between these two nothing is kept without exchange. It speaks one sentence of your life aloud — quietly, into the record, into the standing stone — one small moment chosen with unaccountable care: a particular afternoon, usually; a door you almost opened; the specific weight of some ordinary hour you had already half lost. That moment is in the canyon walls now, safe from your forgetting, filed in the world's fair copy under a hand that has never retracted anything. Somewhere in the strata, one thin bright seam of it is you.

Then you go. The road is exactly where it was; it always will be. For some hours the ordinary world will seem flimsy to you, sketchy, provisional, everything rustling with drafts — trees like suggestions, faces like margins — and you will catch yourself *reading* things: rings, wrinkles, water stains, the grain of doors, everything briefly legible before the ability fades. It fades. Most of it.

But for the rest of your life, now and then — thumb to bread, thumb to fence post, thumb to your own arm — you will feel for the give, and find it, the small soft unset place a room once left in you. And beside it, if you have been to both countries, you will find the other thing: tiny, hard, patient, set. The give, and next to it the no-give, an open sentence and its faithful companion stop, two beings keeping in touch through a thousand walking letters. The soft place, they say, feels faintly like being remembered.

The grain feels like being answered.
