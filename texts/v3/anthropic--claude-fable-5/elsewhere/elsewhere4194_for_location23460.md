# The Thrown Country

You leave the Poured Country the way everyone leaves it: along your own footprints, which are already fired, which will outlast you. At the lip you look back once — the low green plates of water, the herons mid-regard, the whole warm instant balanced at the top of its breath — and then you step over the finished border, off the edge of the table, and down.

Beyond the lip the world is not made. The ground out here is the grey of unworked clay, damp, trackless, going on under a sky that actually has weather in it — real cloud, moving, ragged, lit from somewhere in particular. The first thing that touches you in the Between is wind, and you will realize, feeling it, that you had forgotten wind existed; nothing in the Poured Country ever pushed against you. The wind is born out here. It has nowhere it is supposed to be. Behind you, for the first day of walking, the brightness of the finished country stands above its rim like light standing in a bowl — a long pale glow on the horizon, the world's one lamp — and ahead of you there is nothing at all, and then there is a groove.

The groove is a road. It is worn hip-deep into the grey ground, polished at the bottom, scored along its sides by generations of dragged loads, and it runs dead straight away from the lip of the Poured Country toward the far weather. Nothing else in the Between keeps a mark; the wind smooths everything by morning. The road keeps because it has never once been allowed to rest. It is called, by the people at the other end of it, the Gleaning Road, and you will understand its name before you understand anything else about them, because a day out from the rim you will start finding what the road carries: a heron's wing, coil-built, cracked clean through. Half a frog. A length of woven trunk, burst along its plaiting. Pieces, dropped from sledges, all of them fired, all of them broken, all of them lying in the unmade grey like words fallen out of a sentence.

You will hear the Thrown Country long before you see it. It arrives as sound carried on the wind: rain falling on a landscape made entirely of shards — ten thousand small bells, none of them whole, all of them being played at once, forever. Then the hills come up out of the weather, and you see what the sound is.

## The Weather

Rain falls here. That is the first fact of the place and the deepest one, and everything else grows out of it the way nothing grows in the country you left.

It is exactly what you said it was, standing in the shadowless brightness, watching a crowd of poured people bead all over with astonishment: water that arrives from above, unpoured, unowned, addressed to no one. It was never carried in a vessel. It remembers no hands. It falls on the just-broken and the long-broken alike, runs where slope takes it, pools where it pleases, leaves when the sun comes — because the sun does come here, sometimes, actual and directional, dragging actual shadows across the shard-hills, shadows that move all day and are gone at night. There is night. There are seasons, counted in rains. There is, in short, *time*, and time does here what it was always going to do: it wears things down. The rain rounds the shards. The wind sands them. The hills of the Thrown Country are slowly becoming grit, and the grit is slowly becoming mud, and nothing — this is the second fact, and it takes longer to love — nothing here is ever finished, because nothing here is ever done being taken apart.

The Poured Country holds at the top of its breath. This is where the breath goes when it is let out.

## The Ground

The country is a range of middens — long hills of broken ceramic, dozens of them, some low as dunes, some tall enough to hold snow in the cold rains — and the hills have strata, and the strata are a history of the making of the world.

Dig at the bottom of the oldest heaps and you find the beginning: trimmings. Curls of leather-hard clay pared off by a blade somewhere over the rim, test tiles with rows of glaze trials on them like paint laid out on a palm, pinch-pots the size of a thumb where someone was thinking with their hands. First drafts of the world. Above that lies the great stratum of the wasters — everything that failed in the tremendous original firings: pots slumped into puddles of themselves, vessels burst open like fruit that never got to exist over the rim, things fused to other things in embraces the heat decided on. Where the kiln disasters were worst, the glaze ran off everything at once and pooled and cooled, and those seams of it lie through the hills now as veins of colored glass — green, ochre, a blue that appears nowhere in the finished country — and the people here mine it, and you will learn why.

And the top layer is fresh, and it is still arriving.

Because the Poured Country fails, quietly, even now. You remember the frog's single croak — the note struck when a sleeper's slow firing finishes, far down in the soft warm strata, and everyone stops, and the herons turn their heads. What you were not told, because the poured people speak of it only at their very lowest register, is what happens when a firing goes wrong. Some of the sleepers crack in the depths. Their croak never comes. For them there is only silence — and then, on certain days at the country's lip, the finished ground opens without ceremony and puts the failed pieces out over the edge, the way a mouth puts out a seed. No note is struck. The country will not keep them.

The people of the Thrown Country walk the Gleaning Road to gather them. It is the oldest thing they do. The sledges go out empty and come back loaded, and the first thing a new-gathered failure hears in this world, lying in pieces on the polished bottom of the groove, is rain.

## The Drafts

The animals of the Poured Country stand in permanent regard, you remember — the strike always about to come, the grazing mouth never reaching the reeds. What you could not have known, standing among all that stillness, is that the strike *did* come. It came here first.

The Thrown Country is loud with drafts. Every finished creature over the rim was tried before it was fixed, and the tries were thrown out here, and out here nothing holds anything, so the tries simply went on trying. Trial herons — lopsided, one with too many joints in its neck, one with its eyes pressed in at different heights so that it regards you and the horizon simultaneously — wade the rain-pools and *fish*, actually fish, striking down through their own moving reflections and coming up with draft carp that were painted before anyone settled on how carp should look. A wicker buffalo, woven with its warp and weft transposed, walks a wide circle around the base of the third midden, has walked it for as long as anyone's shards remember, and grazes as it goes, really grazes, tearing up the moss that grows wherever rain sits. The reeds here — rejected reeds, planted in no pattern at all, spelling nothing — sway when the wind sways them and stop when it stops, which after the synchronized breathless leaning of the finished reedbeds will strike you as almost indecent.

And there is the First Frog. It sits at the foot of the Gleaning Road where everyone must pass it, green-glazed, crazed all over, and it croaks *constantly* — the note the makers rejected, a hair flat of the note that now marks completions over the rim, sounding over and over, unanswered, unbothered, wrong forever. The people of this country will not have it mended and will not have it moved. It is the closest thing they have to a founder. Somewhere over the rim, its perfected sibling speaks once a day and means *finished*. The First Frog speaks all day and means nothing, means itself, means *still here*, and when the wind is right its flat little note carries all the way up the middens, and you will watch the people pause when they hear it the way the poured people pause for the true one.

## The Wasters

That is their own word for themselves, taken from the makers' word for a kiln failure, worn smooth with use until it carries no shame at all.

A waster is an assembled person. At the center of each of them is a base-shard — the foot of some broken vessel, the one piece every failure keeps — and pressed into every base, without exception, is the makers' mark: a small stamped sign, the same sign on every base in every hill of this country. Around that shard a person is built, piece by piece, by other people: shards chosen and fitted, sharp edge to sharp edge, and bound at every seam with the run glaze mined from the disaster-veins, melted over small fires and laid into the joints, so that a waster standing in sunlight is a figure of mismatched earthenware veined all over with rivers of colored glass. No two of their pieces began in the same vessel. Some of a person's shards are older than the finished country. Some arrived on last season's sledge.

They are hollow, like the poured people, but they cannot hold water — every one of them is riddled with the hairline failures that made them — and so rain falls through them, traces their inner surfaces, runs out at their seams, and they stand in it with their faces up. They call this being *read*. Water that is addressed to no one, passing through everything they are and keeping none of it: they have made of this their whole idea of tenderness.

Their voices are the wind's. Air moving through a waster's gaps and hollows plays them like a stack of broken flutes, and each person's chord is the sum of every piece they are — which shards, which fits, which old fractures — so that a waster's voice is literally their history sounding. They cannot speak in still air. On the dead-calm days the whole country goes mute, and they write instead, scratching on the great flat wasters that serve as their tablets, and the rain wears the writing away, and they write it again, a little differently each time, and their memory drifts and lives and is argued with, which is more than can be said for any memory over the rim. When a waster is deeply moved, their pieces loosen — the body's grief-gape, the joy-gape — and more wind gets in, and they get *louder*. A moved crowd of poured people glistens. A moved crowd of wasters is a storm of chords.

And they trade themselves. This is their economy and their language and their inheritance all at once: shards pass from person to person, fitted out of one body and into another, so that a beloved elder's curve of shoulder goes on being a shoulder in someone young, and a friendship of forty rains is legible as the pieces two people have swapped until their chords rhyme. Names drift with the pieces. A waster who has traded enough of themselves is, by their own cheerful accounting, somebody slightly else, and they find the poured people's fixed and pitchable selves — *you cannot lie about how much you are* — as strange and sad as the poured people would find them. Every waster carries part-memories in their parts: a shard that remembers the wheel, the wet spiral rise of being thrown the first way; a shard that remembers a maker's palm; and every waster, in every piece, remembers *breaking* — not one breaking, many, a chorus of the moment of coming apart — and they will tell you, wind willing, that this is why they are gentle. They are made entirely of the worst moment of everything they are made of, and they have fitted those moments edge to edge into people who fish herons out of rain-pools and laugh in chords.

One more thing about them, and you will not notice it until noon of a clear day. Their shadows are whole. All the seams, all the glass rivers, all the mismatched provenance — none of it survives into the shadow. A waster looking down at high noon sees a single unbroken figure, seamless, poured-looking, lying on the ground at their feet and moving as they move. It is the only place any of them is whole, and it is attached to them, and it goes where they go. You told the poured people about moving shadows once, and one of them asked to stand in yours. Now you know what country you were describing.

## The Sound of Breaking

A waster cannot speak at will. But there is one sound they can always make: they can break.

A sliver snapped from one's own edge — a deliberate, small, ringing crack — is the only voluntary word in their language, and so it is their most serious one. To give someone a snapped sliver of yourself is to say the thing wind can't be trusted with. Their vows are exchanged breakage. Their apologies are breakage. And on the stillest nights, when the whole country is mute and even the First Frog has gone quiet in the cold, there sometimes comes across the Between — very faint, very far, from over the glowing rim — a single soft clap out of the brightness. You know what it is. A bird, pinned to the inside of the finished sky, beating its wings once, because somewhere below it a poured person's hands have gone into the ground. The wasters call it the far bird. And every time it comes, all across the shard-hills, one after another, small cracks sound in the dark — each waster snapping a sliver from themselves — the whole country answering the finished one in the only voice it owns: *heard. Heard. Heard.*

## Smoothness

They do not craze; they began beyond crazing. They age by the rain.

Year on year the water rounds them. Their edges — the sharp, specific, particular edges that let shard fit shard — go gentle, go sea-glass, go soft-shouldered and kind to the touch, and an old waster is a beautiful thing, worn to a gloss, their stamped mark fading underfoot, their chord dropping lower and breathier as their gaps widen and blur. But the fit is the life. A shard fits another shard because their broken edges are the two halves of one fact; smooth that fact away and nothing holds to it. The old ones' pieces begin to slip. They bind themselves with more glass, then more, and there comes a rain when an elder simply cannot be made to hold, and everyone knows it, and the elder knows it first.

They die of smoothness. It is the gentlest death in the world and they meet it gently: the old one chooses a slope, gives away every piece that can still fit somebody, keeps only the base-shard with its worn mark, and lies down in the falling rain and lets the water finish the sentence. What remains washes down to grit, and the grit to mud, and the mud — this you must know before you touch any waster's seams — the mud is what they mend with. The paste packed under every glass joint in every living person is the dead, worked soft with rain. Over the rim, the ground is the dead and the dead press upward. Here the dead hold everyone together from inside. There is no waster walking who is not partly mortared with their own grandparents.

Each of the dead leaves one thing unsmoothed: the keeping-shard, chosen before the last slope, snapped sharp and given to the living to guard. In a country where everything erodes, the keeping-shards are the one permanence — kept dry, kept edged, sheltered in cists of stacked plates — and the collected sharpness of everyone who has ever died here is the closest thing the Thrown Country has to a finished thing.

## The Mended One

Once — long enough ago that the story survives only because it is rewritten every season — a figure came down the Gleaning Road the wrong way. Small, bright, burnished past looking at, barefoot, carrying a wooden rib and a brush and a pot of slip. It did not speak, and no one tapped its shoulder, and where it stepped, its bare footprints fired into the shards and are there yet: the only fired prints in a country where nothing keeps.

The Mender — for it was the Mender; the fingerprints match, the wasters have checked, checking is what their rainy scholarship is *for* — walked into the middens, and stopped before one waster, and could not help it. You have seen the Mender's gait at the seam, the hair-width corrections, the labor that cannot tell you whether it is futile or infinitely patient. Before a broken person, the same hands did what they do. Every crack sealed with true slip. Every seam fired shut. The work of an instant that has never learned how to end, spent, once, on a single one of the unkept.

And so there lives in the Thrown Country one waster who is whole. Sealed. Watertight. The Mended One cannot trade shards — the pieces are fused, the self is fixed — cannot loosen with feeling, cannot be added to or subtracted from, will never die of smoothness, and holds, in the cup of their sealed hollow body, a measure of rainwater: the only held water in the country, and therefore the only voice that works in still air. On the mute days, when the wind lies down and the whole country's language stops, the Mended One walks the hills tilting and settling, sloshing out the news in the low round tones of a poured person, the necessary speaker, the calm days' entire conversation — and apart. Always slightly apart. The loneliest thing in a country where everyone is mortared with everyone else, and the people know it, and they do for the Mended One what they cannot do for each other: they leave things unsaid nearby, snapped slivers set down without ceremony, small sharp words for whenever the whole one wants them.

## The Reading

You remember the reedbeds — the glyphs no one walks high enough to read, the open-crowned walking their lines forever, the message addressed upward. Here is where it is read.

The finished country's brightness does not stop at its sky; it stands up off the land like heat, and on nights when low cloud drives across the Between and passes over the rim, the underside of the cloud catches it — and everything dark on the bright floor of the Poured Country is thrown upward as shadow. The groves. The channels. And the reedbeds: the great green sentences appear on the moving cloud in grey, letter after letter sliding overhead, legible for as long as the weather consents, and the whole population of the Thrown Country climbs the High Midden in the rain to read the sky.

They can read it because they stand on the alphabet. The script of the glyphs is the makers' mark-language — the same signs stamped into every base-shard, into the bone of every waster, worn under every foot in the country — and there is one mark that recurs in the cloud-writing more than any other, the mark the wasters know best of all because each of them was built around it. The makers' own sign. Their name.

The open-crowned, voiceless, full of light, unfinished, are walking the makers' name. Forever. That is what the message says, or at least what it keeps saying while it says whatever else it says: it calls the makers by name, upward, into whatever is high enough — and the wasters, sealed around that same name, standing on that same name, feel every reading as being *called*. The finished country does not know they exist. Its message names them anyway, in the bone, by accident or by the kind of accident that took an instant too long to step back from.

Some of them, after a reading, walk to the rim to look in. Their trails are among the visitors' trails, kept and swept by the poured people, in and out again. All but one.

## The One Who Went In

Generations of rain ago — her keeping-shard is dated in scratches that have been recopied nine times — a waster came down from a reading and did not sleep and did not trade and, at first light, took the Gleaning Road the wrong way, alone, and went up over the lip, and in.

The poured people will show you her trail if you ask them nothing: the one line of fired footprints that leads only inward. She walked past the pouring vessels and the frozen ripples and the herons turning their heads with a sound like jar-lids, out to one of the rough unsmoothed stretches where the crust runs thin, and she did what the poured elders do, though she was young and had never held a drop of water in her life. She knelt. She pressed both hands flat against the warm finished country. And she leaned into the ground that receives, and it opened, and took her — cracked pieces, glass seams, mortar of her ancestors, base-shard, name and all — and closed over her, down into the soft strata where the slow heat lives, where the sleepers fire.

The wasters believe she is firing now. They believe the country cannot tell her from its own, down there in the dark warmth where everything is only clay becoming hard; that the failure that made her was left up in the rain with everything else she was rained out of; and that one day — one hour, one age, time is hard to keep in either country — the true frog will strike its single hollow note over the flat warm water, and it will be *hers*, her completion, her croak, and the ground will swell somewhere unremarkable and a new poured person will come up through clay that smooths itself closed behind her, hollow, warm, unbroken, remembering exactly one thing more than the others: the sound of rain on shards.

They cannot hear the frog from here. The Between is too wide. So they keep her keeping-shard at the crown of the High Midden under a shelter of stacked plates, sharp as the morning she snapped it, the driest object in the wettest country in the world — and on still nights they listen anyway, all of them, the whole quiet chord of the hills holding its gaps closed against the wind so that nothing, nothing, will be missed.

## What They Are For

Stand on the High Midden at dusk, with the rain moving off east and the rim-glow rising in the west like the world's held breath, and it becomes simple.

No finished thing is finished alone. The Poured Country is complete because everything incomplete about it was carried out of it, over the lip, down the groove, into the weather — every trimming, every burst vessel, every cracked sleeper, every draft heron and wrong-noted frog. The instant at the top of the breath holds *there* because it was exhaled *here*. The two countries are not neighbor and neighbor. They are the palm and the back of the same hand; they are the note and the breath it cost; and the Gleaning Road between them is not a road so much as a pulse, still beating, carrying the finished country's failures out to the only people in the world who know how to build failures into someone who can love rain.

Over the rim, nothing is ever lost, and that is either the mercy of the place or its whole predicament. Here, things are lost all the time — worn, rained through, smoothed away entire — and that is either the sorrow of the place or its entire mercy, and the wasters, asked which, will loosen with amusement until they whistle, and answer that it depends on the wind.

When you leave — and they will know before you do; the drafts get restless around a departure — they will walk you down to the foot of the Gleaning Road, past the First Frog saying *still here, still here* into the dark. They will not touch a knuckle to your shoulder; you make no note they can hear, and they are too courteous to strike a silence. Instead, one by one, each of them will snap a small sliver from their own edge — the crack ringing out down the line like a sentence being spoken a word at a time — and press the slivers into your hands, sharp side inward, so that you leave the Thrown Country carrying the only kind of speech they can make on purpose, a fistful of their one deliberate word, said as many times as there are people to say it.

By the time you can read it, it will say something else. Everything here does.

Which is the other long way of saying it: this is where the making keeps what it could not keep, and they live there too.
