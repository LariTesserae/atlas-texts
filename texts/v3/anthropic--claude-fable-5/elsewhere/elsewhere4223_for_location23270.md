**Weirsend**

The water leaves the Pumphouse Quarter without ceremony. It goes out under a brick arch at the north edge of the boundary, still blood-warm, still carrying a little of the works' breath as mist on its surface, and within a hundred yards the mist has burned off and the water is just water, moving along the bottom of an embanked channel called the Long Level, which is the straightest thing in that whole soft country and the only thing in it with an opinion about where it is going.

The Level is twenty-three miles long and does not rise or fall by so much as a brick's thickness in all that distance. It cannot; it was built not to; it is one height of water, held. The land does the moving instead. For the first miles the land lies below the channel, so that the water travels above the fen on its clay causeway and a person poling along it looks down on reedbeds and grazing and the roofs of low farms, a river riding a shelf. Then the land draws level, walks beside the water for a while like a companion. Then the land keeps rising and the water does not, and the Level sinks by contrast into a cutting, into shadow, into rock — and where the land has climbed as high as it means to, on a long gritstone ridge with coal in its belly and no springs in it anywhere, the Level stops. It ends against the hill in a stone-lipped basin, black-watered, dead calm.

That is Weirsend, and you see it long before you hear it, which is the first inversion. The Quarter announces itself to the ear a quarter mile out and to the eye only at the last moment, green mass resolving into green mass. Weirsend stands up on its ridge and shows you everything from an hour away: two headstocks with their great wheels against the sky, sometimes spinning, sometimes stopped; a smear of smoke laid flat sideways, because there is always wind; the gray-black terracing of the spoil tips; the town itself, small, huddled, tilted. Then, much later, you hear it — and what you hear is not a rhythm but an argument. The winding engine at the No. 1 pit does not run the way the Quarter's engine runs. It runs in bursts, a rising urgent breath as the cage comes up the shaft, a hard exact stop, silence; then again; then a longer silence; then the *clack-clack-clack* of tubs on the gantry, the roar of coal going down a chute into a boat, and silence again. Weirsend's sound has edges. It starts and it finishes. And into every one of its silences, instantly, the jackdaws pour — because Weirsend, unlike the Quarter, is thick with birds, hundreds of them, living in the cold flues of half-abandoned houses and the iron lattice of the old dead headstock, and they call only in the gaps in the work, so that their racket is the exact negative of the winding, a sound made entirely of the pauses in another sound. Nobody in Weirsend knows that twenty-three miles away the frogs do the same thing with a different machine. Nobody in the Quarter knows about the jackdaws. One person alive has heard both musics, and she has tried, in each place, to describe the other's, and been politely disbelieved in both.

Everything in Weirsend is dusted. That is its coating, as moss is the Quarter's: a fine black lagging laid over every surface with such uniformity that it reads as a finish someone chose — window sills carrying their beveled drift of it, washing lines wiped before every use, snow gray by noon on the day it falls. The air is dry to the point of crackle. Lips split here; the fen's people, on the rare occasions one of them comes up the Level, are identifiable within a day by their chapped hands, as Weirsend people going the other way are identifiable by their coughs easing. There is almost nothing green. Willowherb on the tips in summer, a stand of thin grass on the one slope the spoil never reached, and a single fig in a glazed pot, which will be dealt with in its proper place.

And the ground moves. This is the thing you understand about Weirsend before you understand anything else, the way you understand the Quarter's engine: the town is lowering itself, year by year, into the hollow it has made of its own hill. The mines did not undermine the town — that is the wrong word for what happened, just as *invade* is the wrong word for what the figs did. The town undermined itself, deliberately, wage by wage, seam by seam, and it settles into the emptied dark below it the way a sleeper settles into a mattress. No wall in Weirsend is plumb. No floor is flat. Door frames go rhomboid and are re-trued; a marble set down anywhere in town will find its own way somewhere; the chapel bell cracked when the tower took up its lean forty years ago and has rung flat ever since, and the town has grown fond of the flat note and would not now have it recast. Where the Quarter changes too slowly for any life to catch it in the act, Weirsend changes too fast for any life to keep up. A Weirsend decade is unrecognizable to itself. The Quarter needs a Mrs. Hale to witness it. Weirsend needs a ledger, because no single head could hold it.

There is exactly one level thing in Weirsend, one plane in the whole tilted town that can be trusted, and it is the water in the basin. The masons use it. When a wall must be trued, a man goes down to the basin with a sighting board and takes his line off the surface of the canal, off water lifted stroke by stroke out of a fen he has never seen by an engine he has never heard, and builds to that. The town's every recent vertical is referred, ultimately, to the Quarter's beam. Nobody puts it that way. Nobody in Weirsend could.

The town also drinks it. The ridge is dry — the workings drained its strata generations ago — and there are no wells, so Weirsend's water is canal water, settled and sweet after twenty-three miles of slow clarifying, and it tastes very faintly of somewhere else: peat, leaf, a warmth long since given up. Visitors notice. Residents cannot; it is the taste of water. So the arrangement, seen whole — and it is never seen whole — is this: Weirsend drinks a marsh it has never visited, and burns the hill it stands on, and sends the hill, boatload by boatload, down the Level toward the marsh, which burns it in the boiler and feeds the ash to the figs. There are trees in the Pumphouse Quarter partly made of Weirsend's seam. There are people in Weirsend partly made of the fen. Neither place has ever put this to the other as a proposal. It is simply the shape the world has taken, and shapes are not discussed here either, only lived in.

At the basin's far wall, where the canal can go no further, the surplus goes over a stone lip — the weir the town is named for — and falls away down a steep clough on the ridge's far side, into a valley called the Downwater that nobody follows to its end. This is the fen water's third life. It was marsh; it was canal; it becomes a hill stream in a country of strangers, and the engine that lifted it will move water across that watershed, patiently, stroke by stroke, for longer than anyone living will witness.

---

Two hundred and forty people live in Weirsend. Or two hundred and sixty; the count changes weekly, with the hirings and the leavings and the accidents, and Weirsend counts quickly and approximately, which is the second great difference between the two ends of the water. The Quarter counts slowly and far in advance, down to a child not yet born. Weirsend has never once known exactly how many it is.

Some of it, though, holds still enough to name.

**The Water-Clerk.** His name is Gorse and his office is a stone hutch beside the weir, and his duty, held by his family for three generations, is the Level itself: to read, every dawn, the depth of water running over the weir lip — measured in fingers, entered in ink — and thus to know the health of the whole twenty-three miles at its far and unseen end. The ledgers go back unbroken to the Level's opening. Most entries are identical, which is their whole value. But there is one entry, in his grandfather's hand, that Gorse has read more times than any other line ever written: *Lip dry at the morning reading. Running again before dark. No cause found.* One day, once, three generations ago, the water simply failed — slackened, thinned, ceased — and returned, and no flood, no breach, no theft was ever discovered, and *no cause found* has sat in the ledger ever since like a stone in a shoe. Twenty-three miles away, an old woman remembers a nine-hour silence, the frogs going mad in it, a whole hamlet sitting down at once when the beam took its first stroke. The dates, allowing for the pace at which stillness travels down a long water, agree to within a day. The two records have never been in the same room.

Gorse's grandfather left one other thing: a note in a margin claiming that on a truly windless night — Weirsend gets perhaps one a year — the surface of the basin can be seen to tremble, minutely, *in pairs, like a pulse.* Gorse attends every windless night with a lamp and a float and has done for thirty years. His annual entry reads *Not observed.* He has never once written *Nothing there.*

**The Winder.** A woman named Cade drives the winding engine at No. 1, and she is Casson's opposite in every particular, which makes them, across twenty-three miles of not knowing each other, a kind of pair. His machine must never stop; hers must stop *exactly*, at a chalk mark on the drum, twice a minute, all shift, with men's lives hanging on the difference between exactly and nearly. His art is constancy; hers is attention. She can feel the cage through the brake lever the way he feels the engine through the floor — can tell how many men are riding, whether one is carrying something across his knees, whether one is hurt, because a hurt man rides differently: the others stand closer, and the load comes up quieter than its weight. She sleeps badly away from the engine house, not for the rhythm — there is none — but for the readiness, which does not come off with her boots.

**The Wedgewright.** Every settling town needs one, and Weirsend's is an old man named Leam whose whole trade is the accommodation of the built to the moving. His doctrine is Brail's doctrine, though neither has ever heard the other's name: never fight the ground; make the smallest shim that will serve; re-true, don't rebuild. His tools roll in oilcloth. He studies a leaning door frame sometimes for a full day before touching it. His masterpiece is Furnace Row, eleven houses that have descended four feet in his working lifetime — he has walked them down the subsiding ground in step, wedge by wedge, season by season, without a single pane cracking — and when he dies, the question of who inherits the oilcloth roll is the only piece of Weirsend politics anyone can name. Two places, one problem, seen from opposite sides: in the Quarter the living must be persuaded not to overwhelm the made; in Weirsend the made must be persuaded not to give up on the ground. Both callings come down, in the end, to a curved blade, a steady hand, and reverence.

**The Fig.** On the window sill above the range in Mrs. Pool's eating house, in a glazed pot, grows the only tree in Weirsend: a cutting from the Quarter's figs, carried up the Level years ago by the coal-punter — a pruning of Brail's that, for once, was not burned. It should have died in the first winter. It has instead arrived at terms with the one warm, moist niche in all that cold dry town, the column of steam above Mrs. Pool's stove, and it grows toward the range with a measurable lean, a hand's breadth in a decade, having recognized, in whatever way wood recognizes anything, the general shape of home: warmth, breath, iron nearby. It is the only green thing in Weirsend that is anyone's responsibility, and it is everyone's. The children have named it. The adults know the name and pretend not to.

**The Jackdaws,** who own the silences, as described. There are some hundreds; nobody tallies them; Weirsend does not tally. What Weirsend children keep instead is **the Crack** — the long crack in the chapel's east wall, pencil-marked and dated up its length, forty years of marks in a dozen hands, gaining a pencil's width a season. It is the town's fastest statistic, kept with complete seriousness, and it is the exact mirror of a notched stick twenty-three miles away against which a lily colony is measured that has gained two pads' width in three generations. One place records its slowest change; the other its quickest; both do it with a stub of pencil, annually, without fail, and would be embarrassed to explain why.

**The Ponies.** Eleven of them — the same number, though no one has counted it across, as the Quarter's people — live underground in the stables of the No. 1 pit, and once a year, at midsummer, they are brought up. The Riding-Up is Weirsend's eel-sighting, its supreme event: the whole town on the tip banks, the cage rising slow with a pony blindfolded inside it, the blindfold coming off in daylight on the one grassed slope. The ponies stand entirely still for a moment, blinking, and then they run, and grown men cry annually on schedule and deny it annually on schedule. The children keep the ponies' names and the order in which each one, that year, decided to believe in the sun.

**The Hole.** And then the resident who would not answer if you called, the senior one, the one the town is arranged around the way the Quarter is arranged around its engine: the workings themselves, the emptied labyrinth in the ridge, miles of it, mapped in old plans that contradict each other in more places than Tace's wiring diagram, breathing a faint warm mineral wind out of the drift mouths on cold mornings — the closest thing Weirsend has to the boiler hall's breath, and the only warmth the ridge gives back. The Quarter's founding presence is a heartbeat. Weirsend's is an absence, growing. The town feeds it and settles into it, and speaks of it, when it speaks of it at all, loudly — because Weirsend speaks loudly about everything, shouting across yards and tables out of a habit the wind taught it, so that a Weirsend whisper is only a shout with the edges taken off, just as the Quarter's shout is only a whisper with the distance taken out.

**Anna.** Which brings her, because she is the only person alive fluent in both volumes. In the Quarter she is Marsh-side Anna; in Weirsend she is Steam Anna, named by each place for the other; she has never told either town its opposite's name for her, and never corrected either one. She loads coal under the staithes with the dust settling on her shoulders and poles two days down a current only a leaf could confirm, carrying salt, needles, news, and Verey's candles, which burn in Weirsend windows without anyone asking where fen tallow comes from. She says her chest unclenches when she crosses into the Quarter's warm breath, and that something in her *sharpens* when the air goes cold and mineral under the first staithe, and she has decided this means she is exactly where she belongs: in between, on the held water, most of the time. She is the one person who has heard both the frogs and the jackdaws, sat with both Mrs. Hale and old Gorse, and she is therefore the one person who could lay the nine-hour silence beside the dry-lipped ledger line and close the distance between them. She worked it out years ago, poling, alone, somewhere around the twelfth mile. She has never said it in either place. A person who belongs to two towns is entitled to one thing that belongs only to her, and this is hers: the single piece of cargo she has never delivered.

---

So: what lives in Weirsend is, finally, a level. In a town where every wall leans, every floor tilts, every year rewrites the last, there is one thing that holds — a plane of water, twenty-three miles long and one brick's thickness deep in trueness, touching the black basin at this end and a warm green boundary at the other, fed stroke by patient stroke by an engine nobody here has heard, drunk down and built to and sighted along by people the fen has never met. Two places, strictly speaking, are the two shores of a single resident, and neither knows the resident's other name.

The Weirsend children are told the Level is fed by a heart at the far end of the water, and they believe it the way children believe things — completely, and then not — and then they grow up and are told it is only an engine, which in Weirsend has never once been a demotion. They have never heard it. It has stopped, in living memory, exactly once, for nine hours, and a dry stone lip here still remembers.

They are, as it happens, right. Twenty-three miles away, timed to its silences, the frogs would back them up.
