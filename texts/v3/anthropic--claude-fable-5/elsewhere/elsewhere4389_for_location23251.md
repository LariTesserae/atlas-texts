**The Stilling**

The bowl keeps everything except the water.

For all its fullness, the basin's level never rises, and the Held, who count everything, have never found where it goes — no notch in the rim, no outfall, no visible spending. The water leaves the way it arrived: through the stone. The mountain drinks its own weeping back down through ten thousand seams and carries it in the dark of the rock for nine days. That figure is known precisely, and known because of a discourtesy — a lowland scholar, two lifetimes ago, poured a jar of madder dye into a migrating pool to trace it. The Held did not punish him. They stood apart from him, all of them, at their various distances, which in the Weir is a sentence of a kind, and he came down the ropes early. Nine days later the river below the cloud ran rose-colored for an afternoon, and the people who live on that river made a song about it, which they still sing, because that is what they do with everything.

They live at the place where the water comes back out of the mountain, and the water does not come back out quietly.

---

Below the cloud line the world resumes. From the gorge, looking up-valley, the sky ends: a grey ceiling closes over the mountain's shoulders, permanent, weight-bearing to the eye, and out of the flat underside of that ceiling — out of what looks for all the world like a floor — a river falls. It appears in midair. There is no visible source, no lip of rock, no white peak behind it; the water simply exits the cloud already falling, a heavy braided rope four hundred feet long with its upper end lost in the ceiling, and drives down into a black plunge pool with a noise that has not stopped, by local count, in ninety generations.

The people of the gorge have a name for the falls that the word-lists render, roughly, as *the Long Word*. The mountain is saying something, is the idea folded into the name — has been saying it for ten thousand years, one continuous syllable, and the gorge is the shape of the listening. They do not claim to know what the word means. That would be presuming. But they will point out, if asked, the plain arithmetic of the thing: above the cloud, as every porter and scholar attests, the mountain swallows sound — a shout dies in three paces, ice falls in silence, a whole village labors without noise. Below the cloud, the mountain does nothing but speak. The travelers' tally-books all arrive at the same sentence sooner or later, as if the route itself dictates it: *the silence up there is paid out down here.* The gorge-people put it more simply. The mountain swallows above and speaks below, they say, and we live at the mouth.

Everything about the gorge confirms them. It is a deep, resonant slot of the same old grey stone as the bowl — the same stone, polished by the same water, but down here dry-faced, sun-struck in the afternoons, and hoarding sound the way the basin refuses it. The walls are a horn. A whisper released at the right ledge crosses the gorge intact and arrives in a stranger's ear a quarter-mile off; these carrying-lines are mapped, named, owned in a loose customary way, threaded through daily life like footpaths. There are also the deaf stones — pockets and alcoves where the gorge's acoustics knot and die, where a voice falls dead at the lips — and these are the private places, the locked rooms. Lovers go to the deaf stones. Grief begins there, though it does not stay there. In the bowl above, privacy is distance and speech is touch; in the gorge, privacy is silence and speech is the free air, and each people finds the other's arrangement faintly indecent, the way one always does.

And where the bowl is bare — one cress, one lichen, a moss like drowned velvet — the gorge is *choked*. The falls throws off a permanent spray, and in that spray a rainforest the size of a hamlet grows in the temperate zone of nowhere: hart's-tongue fern in sheets, moss a hand deep on every ledge, rowans jutting from cracks at impossible angles, everything beaded, dripping, extravagantly green in the shafting light. There are shadows here — deep ones, moving ones, the whole grammar of shadow the bowl lacks — and the people read the day by them, and above all by *the kindling*: the hour each afternoon when the sun clears the western shoulder and strikes the falling rope of water end to end, and the Long Word lights up like a filament, and the whole gorge, without ever having agreed to, pauses to watch.

---

The settlement stands on drystone terraces above the spray line, and a mason from the Weir, if one ever stood back far enough to look, would recognize the coursing. This is not resemblance. It is instruction. The carry-crews who take goods up the fixed ropes each thaw — three days blind through the wet interior of the cloud, a route called the Blind Mile though it is a good deal more than a mile — come back down with rope-burned palms and, always, with technique: the Weir pays for what it cannot make in the one currency it has in surplus, which is knowing how to stack stone against a patient adversary. The gorge's terraces show their history in strata, grandmothers at the bottom, this spring at the top, exactly as the cells of the Weir do. The difference is that down here the walls are furred with moss and expected to *last*, and mostly do, and the gorge-people find something between comedy and awe in the reports of a village where a third of everyone is always rebuilding. They have a saying about the people above, affectionate and unfair, as sayings about neighbors are: *they rent; we own.* The porters, who have watched the Held work, say it should be the other way around.

The trade itself is old and lopsided and nobody minds. Up the ropes go salt, whetstones, resin, dried fruit, gut for the year, and horn — above all horn, because the slit goggles without which no eye survives the bowl's white glare are carved *here*, in the loud green shade of the gorge, by carvers who have never seen and will never see the light their work is for. They carve by inherited specification, checked each year against the porters' report, and there is a small standing strangeness in it that the carvers themselves will point out: the most necessary object in the Weir is made by people to whom it is useless, in exchange for feathers that fall from birds the gorge has never once seen alive.

Because that is what comes down. The river below the plunge pool runs through a series of shallows, and across those shallows the gorge-people maintain their lattices of osier and set stone — the weirs of the Stilling, from which travelers named the place, the true name being a sound the word-lists gave up on. The weirs catch what the water carries out of the mountain: white feathers by the thousand, the wax-translucent casts of crayfish, papery newt-skins, all the pale wrack that the bowl sheds and the water sorts and the darkness of the rock delivers, nine days stale, into the light of the gorge. The feathers are wealth here — bridal wealth, oath-wealth, sewn into the hems of the great songs' singers — precisely because their birds are unreachable, resident in a sky above the sky. For most of the gorge's history its people believed the realm over the cloud, which they call *the Loft*, was inhabited by a single kind of being, winged, since only feathers and bones came down. The word-lists eventually corrected them. Some of the oldest songs keep the wings anyway. It was felt to be wrong to take wings away from the dead who had already been given them.

The bones. Yes. That is the center of the place, and everything else is arranged around it.

---

**Who lives in the Stilling**

They are tall where the Held are broad, long-limbed, deep-lunged, built around the voice the way the Weir-people are built around the hand. The voice is the face here. They oil it with honey and resin the way the Held oil their hands with fat; a gorge child of ten can pitch a whisper down a carrying-line or throw a call flat across the roar of the falls, and the worst insult in the settlement — almost the only one — is to let a call go unanswered. Everything answers here. That is the first law and the oldest courtesy, learned from the walls themselves: the gorge returns every voice, and a people raised inside ten thousand years of echo have concluded that answering is what makes a thing a *place* and not merely a location. They answer their own echoes, cheaply, reflexively — a called name comes back off the far wall and the caller gives a small falling note to receive it — not because they believe the gorge is addressing them, they will tell you, but because answering is cheap and rudeness is not. Travelers who have been to both settlements set that sentence beside the ripple-watchers' custom of signaling *seen* to the sourceless rings, and note that the two peoples, who cannot converse, have arrived at the identical manners from opposite ends of the same water. The gorge-people, told this, are pleased and unsurprised. Of course, they say. We were one people once.

Their story of the split is this: the cloud was not always where it is. Long ago it stood higher, and the settlement stood at its edge, one village, half in the glare and half in the green. Then the cloud came down like a floor being lowered, and those caught above it lost their voices to the quiet, and those caught below lost their hands to the noise — kept the voice, lost the grip — and each half has been half a person since. Whether the Held keep a version of this story no one knows; it is not the kind of thing they would say at a traveler's distance. But the scholars keep returning to one dry anomaly in the margins of the word-lists: the ripple-telegraph of the Weir runs to about forty distance-signs, mostly verbs, and the oldest layer of the gorge's whistle-speech — the register that cuts the roar, the register babies turn their heads to before they can talk — is a set of forty roots. The same verbs. *Come, eat, water-moving, dead, born.* And *wall-down*, which the gorge, whose walls do not fall, has no use for and has kept warm for ninety generations anyway, the way you keep a key to a house you no longer live in.

The consequences of a voice-culture run all the way down, and they run opposite to the bowl's. The Held cannot lie, speaking as they do on the pulse of a living arm, and so prize tact. The gorge-people can lie *beautifully* — the voice travels alone, faceless, pulseless, down a carrying-line, dressed however its owner dresses it — and so what they prize is the sworn register: the understanding that a thing said where the gorge can hear it has been said twice, once by you and once by the walls, and cannot be called back. Contracts, marriages, accusations, and confessions are all spoken on the carrying-lines, deliberately, into the echo, with witnesses on the far side; the gorge is their notary. Lies are told in the deaf stones, and everyone knows it, and it is considered no more scandalous than dreaming. What is scandalous is to say a solemn thing softly. Truth, here, is a matter of what you are willing to have repeated.

And touch — touch they keep the way the Held keep the voice: nearly unused, and therefore enormous. The living greet at arm's length. Hands in daily life are for work and are nothing special, ordinary hands, unregarded. Touch is reserved for two occasions. The first is the rope-foot: when a porter or a pilgrim starts up the Blind Mile, the people seeing them off embrace them, long and hard, gripping the forearms — and this is understood quite literally as *provisioning*, loading the traveler with the only speech that will work where they are going, the way you would load them with bread. The porters brought the custom down generations ago, having learned above what an arm can carry.

The second occasion is the dead.

---

**The received**

A few times a year, among the feathers and the crayfish-casts, the weirs of the Stilling catch a human bone.

It arrives water-polished, decades in the making, a knuckle or a rib or the long bone of a forearm, and the weir-tenders lift it out bare-handed — bone-work is the one sacred labor of the hands here, the one thing touched the way the Held touch everything — and carry it up to the singer. There is always exactly one bone-singer, as there is always exactly one wrack-reader in the bowl above, and the two offices are the same office mirrored. The wrack-reader looks at what the water arranges and is bound to say almost nothing: three signs or fewer, on pain of removal. The bone-singer receives what the water delivers and is bound to say *everything*. The dead of the Loft arrive without names, without stories, without anyone in the gorge who mourns them, and the settled conviction of the Stilling — its whole theology, insofar as it has one — is that a person delivered to your door must be answered like any other call. So the singer studies the bone, honestly, forensically: the healed break, the wear of a lifetime's kneeling, the deep muscle-scarring of a working wrist. And then the singer builds a life on what the bone can honestly bear — a tactful invention, kind but never false to the evidence — and sings it, at full voice, on the great carrying-line, so that the gorge says it too and it becomes sworn. The bone is given a two-part name, the given half and the half the gorge returns. It is set into a niche in the wall of the answered shelf, high above the spray, facing the falls, where the dead can hear. There are thousands of niches. The singers hold every name and every life in the annual cycle, the whole archive carried in living voices, and a gorge child can stand below the shelf and hear, on the right evening, three hundred invented grandmothers sung in order.

The bone-singers have never been above the cloud. But five hundred years of reading the Loft's dead with their fingers has taught them what no word-list did. The bones from above all share a signature — the heavy wrist, the ridged forearm, the hands worked past working, the marks of a people who spent every day of every life gripping. The singers noticed long before the scholars explained it. And so the standing first line of the receiving song, the line sung over every bone the mountain sends down, sung for centuries before anyone in the gorge knew what the people of the Weir call themselves, is this:

*Here is one who held on.*

The travelers who carry the word-lists back and forth are, as a class, unsentimental people. There is not one of them who can report that fact levelly.

The gorge answers the current in kind. Their own dead are given to the river — washed, sung the whole night, and committed to the water *below* the weirs, downstream of everything the Stilling could catch. They will not intercept their own. The dead are understood to be travelers now, and the river a procession, and somewhere down its long green length — past the last village anyone from the gorge has visited, past the places that are only names, in the flat country where the water is said to grow wide and slow and brown and finally taste of salt — somewhere down there, the Stilling is serenely certain, other weirs stand in other shallows, and other tenders lift what arrives with bare hands, and other singers give strangers their lives back, because that is what the water teaches everyone who lives on it. No one in the gorge has ever seen this done. They have never needed to. They do it, and they were never asked to either.

---

**The other residents**

The river below the pool is ordinary, and its life is ordinary: dippers that walk the streambed underwater and fly out of it singing; trout in the runs; herons in the shallows below the weirs, tolerated as fellow tenders of a laxer discipline. The gorge-people fish everywhere but one place.

The Landing — the black plunge pool itself, where the Long Word ends — is not fished, not swum, not drawn from. It is where the water *arrives and forgets*, in the local phrase: the falls stuns the neighbor out of it, they say, and everything below the pool is spent water, safe water, mere water, a road. But the pool itself is the threshold, still half in the mountain's grip, and it behaves accordingly. Silver fish appear in it that match no run and no season, sourceless, exactly as they appear in the new pools of the bowl above; the Landing unpacks them and the river below never sees them. Things surface there long after they should: an oar lost in the shallows was returned by the Landing eleven years later, upstream of where it went in, which is not a thing rivers do. The gorge notes all this the way the Held note everything — as the neighbor's business, observed with courtesy and left uninterpreted.

Once, in all the tallies, the Landing delivered a whole bird. A white swift of the Loft, glazed, frozen mid-roost inside its skin of ice, brought down the falls and set — the watchers all used that word, *set* — gently against the weir at dawn. It was not dead. The gorge warmed it, fed it, and understood within a day that it was dying anyway, of something no lower air could fix. So four porters carried it up the Blind Mile out of season, three days blind through the wet dark of the cloud, and left it at the upper rope-stake at the edge of the glare, alive, facing in. What became of it neither settlement knows, or if the Held know, the distance at which the question must be asked has kept the answer where their answers stay. The bird has a niche on the answered shelf regardless, the only occupied-empty one, and a song, and the song is short: it names the bird *the one guest*, and declines, in the gorge's best manner, to say anything it does not know.

---

**The Long Hush**

Once in a long while — a handful of times a century — the falls stops.

The rope of water parts in midair: the lower length falls away, whitens, lands, drains, and no new water follows it out of the cloud. The Long Word hangs unfinished. And the gorge, which has never in ninety generations heard itself, hears itself.

The custom is absolute and needs no enforcing, because the silence itself enforces it: everyone stops. Mid-word, mid-step, mid-haggle, the whole settlement holds still in the sudden enormous quiet, hearing for the first or only time in their lives their own hearts, the creak of their own breath, the drip of the spray-gardens, a dipper somewhere singing into an acoustic that has never once carried a dipper's song. Then the water resumes — a grey rod dropping back out of the ceiling, the roar climbing back up under everything like a floor being replaced — and everyone finishes their sentence. That, too, is the custom. You finish the sentence. The gorge would not leave a word unanswered and neither will they.

Mostly the hushes last a few heartbeats, and correspond — though the gorge has no way to see it — to the small hours above, when the water of the bowl gathers itself soundlessly into a mound or a ridge, holds the shape, and lets go. But once, the falls stopped for half a day. The Stilling's greatest story, told to every child, the story by which the gorge divides its very temperaments — there are hush-steady families and hush-broken families, and everyone knows which is which — is the story of the Long Hush: half a day of silence so total that people wept, or prayed, or laughed uncontrollably, or walked out into the middle of the dry riverbed and lay down in it; half a day in which, the story insists, you could hear a person *thinking* on the far terrace. And the scholars, comparing the two settlements' generational tallies, have established what neither people asked them to: the Long Hush of the gorge and the day the water stood up in a wall and bisected the settlement of the Weir are the same day. The same water. For half a day the neighbor held its whole outflow overhead in the glare, and the mountain, having nothing to say below, said nothing — and the gorge spent one afternoon inside the condition the Held inhabit from birth to death, and has been telling the story with a shudder ever since.

It changes people, that story. Every generation, a few of the hush-broken — the ones the silence marked — go up the ropes and do not come back down, and are received above, and learn the grip, and go quiet. And every generation a few come the other way: a Weir-born figure, broad-wristed, owl-eyed, descending into the roar *on purpose*, hungry for it, learning the voice late and clumsily like a second infancy. Each settlement keeps a small standing handful of the other, honored, useful, never entirely at home, and these are the true word-lists, the living ones, the only members of either people who have held both halves of the old split person in one body.

---

**The name that takes two**

Ask the gorge-people who they are and you will discover the last thing about them, which is that they cannot tell you alone.

Their name for themselves is not a word but an exchange: a rising two-note call, which must be answered — by anyone, by the asker, by a child, by the walls — with its falling counterpart before it is a name at all. Said solo, it is grammatically nothing, a question with its back turned. The word-lists render it *the Answered*, or sometimes *those who call back*, and the people accept either with the patience of folk who know the translation is missing its other half by definition. It takes two, is the point. A name, like a place, like a person, is a thing that exists in the return.

There was an evening, the porters tell it, when one of the come-down Weir-born — years in the gorge by then, her hands still ruined in the old way, her voice still a strange flat instrument she played carefully — was asked, on the great carrying-line with the settlement listening, whether she was of the Held still or of the Answered now. And she gave, for reply, the rising call, alone, into the open air across the roar: the first half of the name, hanging there, needing its answer.

The gorge gave it back.

It always does. That is the whole of the creed, if the Stilling can be said to have one, and it is the same creed the Weir keeps, translated down four hundred feet of falling water: the mountain holds at the top and speaks at the bottom; the bowl grips and the gorge replies; the dead go down as bone and come back as song; and between the white glare and the green roar, one long conversation has been going on for ninety generations in two languages across a cloud neither party can see through — pressure above, echo below, the same water carrying both — and no call, in all that time, has ever once gone unanswered.
