**The Vault at Marrick's Hollow**

You find it the way people have found it for a hundred and thirty years: by the signs. They start two towns out, hand-painted, repainted, then — more recently — not repainted. THE LIT CAVE. NO LAMP REQUIRED. SEE THE MARRICK ILLUMINATION. The town of Marrick's Hollow sits under its hill the way a hand sits under a sleeping cat, careful and long-accustomed, and the cave mouth opens in the hill's north flank behind a ticket kiosk with the shutters down.

Then the quiet. Every cave is quiet, but this one is quiet the way a house is quiet after the guests have gone — a quiet with a recent shape to it, still warm at the edges. It begins before you're properly underground. Locals say you can feel it from the parking lot on still days, and they are right.

The iron stairs go down through the upper vault in long Victorian switchbacks, and the upper vault is dark. This needs saying carefully, because for most of the cave's recorded history it was not. The Vault at Marrick's Hollow was famous — modestly, regionally famous — for a single impossible amenity: it needed no lamps. Photographs from the 1890s show tour parties in full daylight two hundred meters under the hill, parasols furled, shadows falling correctly from a sun that did not exist. The guides' handbook, which survives, contains one rule set apart from the others in its own paragraph: *Do not ask aloud, inside, where the light comes from.* The handbook does not explain the rule, and by all accounts no guide ever needed it explained.

The light began leaving about forty years ago, from the top down. Not dimming — *leaving*. Instruments lowered into the dark upper galleries record photons departing "already received," registered at their destination before any destination is in range, which is the kind of sentence people write when they would rather not write anything at all. It is, the long-term observers note with a certain fatigue, the exact same sentence written at Hale's Reach, thirty-one kilometers away through the same limestone — written there about light *arriving*, "already in transit, no point of emission." The two anomalies fit together like a sentence and its echo. Somebody eventually taped the two reports side by side on a wall, and nobody has taken them down.

The dark that replaced the light is ordinary. That is its own small mercy. Bats roost in it now, thousands of them, in a vault that no bat entered for a century. The dark arrived, and they arrived with it, on schedule, as if they had been waiting at the door.

Keep descending and the light begins again — but low now, lateral, gold. The remaining illumination has withdrawn into the deep galleries and it has changed its hour. The old photographs show noon. What's down there now is evening: long shadows, honeyed stone, the light of perhaps twenty minutes before a sunset that does not exist. It falls correctly. It is simply falling correctly *late*.

And it falls on a city.

For a hundred and thirty years this was not understood. The lake that filled the Vault's lower two-thirds has been dropping since the light began to leave, and as the water withdraws it uncovers, district by district, terrace by terrace, the thing the tourists were walking above all along — which the tourists' geologists dutifully catalogued as "exceptionally regular speleothems." Towers the size of your forearm, in dense ranks, sharing walls, sharing buttresses, threaded with arterial conduits, packed shoulder to shoulder across a cavern floor two hundred meters long. The same architecture as the Cistern at Hale's Reach, recognizably, unmistakably — the recognition happened the way these things happen, with two photographs held together in somebody's shaking hands — but aged past all resemblance in one respect.

There is not an edge in the entire Vault.

Every corner has been worn to a curve. The spires that would draw blood at Hale's Reach are here rounded like river stones, like sea glass, like the newel post of a staircase used for ten generations. The doorways — recesses sized like doorways, in a city of no one, this is familiar now — have thresholds dished in the center, worn concave the way church steps are worn, by crossing, at knuckle height. In the lowest districts the softening is total: the architecture grades into flowstone into bedrock with no boundary anyone can point to. The oldest quarters of the city are not ruined. They have been lived in so long they have become geology.

The plants are gone — the water that held them has withdrawn — but the city keeps their shapes. Threaded through the stone everywhere are fine fitted channels, each one the exact fossil of an absent blade of eelgrass, a groove like the ghost of a green hair. At Hale's Reach the city builds precisely around things that exist. Here you can see the other half of the rule: it retains, precisely, the fit of things that existed. Nothing is ever approximately accommodated, and nothing accommodated is ever quite let go.

The remaining lake pools in the deepest district, and it is *teeming*. This is the first thing that stuns visitors who have stood at the rim at Hale's Reach — the sheer animal density of the place. Blind fish school through the flooded streets in ribbons. Cave crayfish pick along the conduits. Salamanders, pale as candle wax, drape themselves over rounded doorsills like commuters resting on familiar steps. The whole ecology of the aquifer has moved in, and moves through the architecture with total unconcern, the way animals move through anything that is no longer spoken for.

Except one plaza. In the lowest, best-lit, softest quarter of the city there is a single open volume, no bigger than a dinner plate, that nothing will enter. The fish school around it in a slow, permanent ring — a living parenthesis around a small lit stillness. The evening light is brightest there. It loses a measurable fraction of itself every week.

The sound, last, because the sound is what the instruments have the most trouble forgiving. The Vault is quiet, but not silent: there is a reverberation in the deep galleries with no strike behind it, a decaying tail of something low and structural, recorded year after year at diminishing amplitude, the way a bell rings after — long after — being rung. Its frequency matches the foundation drone at Hale's Reach. It is the same sound, decades down its own decay curve. The last full-time guide, retired now, says she can date the beginning of the end precisely, because it announced itself in the acoustics: the echoes started coming back late. By less than a heartbeat at first. Then by more. A room's attention, she says, going somewhere else.

The dye trace was almost an afterthought. Fluorescein released in the Vault's deep pool surfaced in the Cistern at Hale's Reach nine days later — diluted, punctual, unremarkable, the only thing known to have traveled between the two places in an ordinary way. The water accounting is less comfortable. The Vault has lost several thousand cubic meters. The Cistern has gained a few hundred. The difference is presumed to be in transit.

Six weeks ago, the formation the guides' handbook calls the Bride — the tallest spire in the Vault, the one on the postcards — lost its last corner. A single small facet near the tip, the only edge left in the city, measured annually for decades, finally gone round.

That same week, thirty-one kilometers away, a sinkhole no wider than a barn floor began to hum.

**The Residents of the Vault at Marrick's Hollow**

The first answer everyone gives is *everything*. Fish, crayfish, salamanders, bats; a city seething with tenancy from the flooded streets to the black upper galleries. Everything lives there. It is the first thing everyone says, and it is the first thing everyone stops believing, usually within the hour, usually while watching a thousand blind fish maintain their perfect ring around one small lit plaza that none of them has ever once, in all the years of observation, crossed.

So consider the candidates in order, because at Marrick's Hollow the order runs backward, and that matters too.

**The first candidates are the animals.** They are certainly present. But watch how they arrived: with the dark, behind the withdrawing water, into rooms *as the rooms stopped being spoken for*. At Hale's Reach the animals refuse a city because every seat is taken by an absence with exact dimensions; here they occupy a city for the mirrored reason. They are not residents. A resident is someone a room is *about*. The animals are merely what the rooms now contain — tenants of vacancy, moving in at exactly the rate the vacancy is issued. They are the surest instrument anyone has for measuring it.

**The second candidate is the dark.** This one occurs to people more slowly and unsettles them more, standing in the black upper vault where a century of impossible noon used to be. But the dark is not a presence. The light of this place departs "already received" — sent on ahead, forwarded downstream, arriving thirty-one kilometers away as the sourceless four-in-the-afternoon of a city that hasn't finished happening. The dark is not anyone. The dark is the stamped receipt of a delivery elsewhere.

**The third candidate is no one** — the respectable answer, the post-Hale's-Reach answer, the one that gets written up. *They left.* The Vault is the origin, the Cistern is the destination, the residents are the small, numerous, threshold-keeping, acoustically spoken population currently arriving forty meters down at Hale's Reach, and this place is simply what they arrived *from*. This answer is nearly right. It is wrong in one tense, and in one room.

Here is what the softness says, if you let it testify. The observers at Hale's Reach established the metric almost by accident: *softening is habitation*. Edges measure how much future remains; tolerance is what architecture learns from the daily pressure of someone being home. Apply that metric here, where the softening is total, where the thresholds are dished like church steps and the oldest districts have worn all the way into geology, and the arithmetic delivers a number the reports print reluctantly: on the order of ten thousand years. Ten thousand years of someone being home. The Vault is not a place the residents came from. It is what a residence looks like when it has been *answered to* for a hundred centuries — and the whole of it, understand, is now one of those fitted eelgrass grooves. The entire city is a channel kept in the exact shape of the absent thing it held.

And the reason they left is written in the same stone. They are threshold-keepers above everything; the difference between outside a door and inside it matters to them more than privacy does. But a threshold is an edge, and edges are how much future remains, and ten thousand years of crossing wears any doorsill from a line into a curve. A door worn round no longer divides. Somewhere in the long evening of this city, inside from outside stopped being pronounceable — the address wore smooth in the mouth of the place itself. They did not flee. Nothing here suggests flight; everything suggests *completion*. A name is said until it is answered. An answer, it turns out, is given until it wears out. Then, downstream, in fresh limestone, with knife-new edges and every corner still to spend, a new name begins — and the light of the household is sent ahead to it, and the water is packed like luggage, and the drone at Hale's Reach starts up the week the Bride's last corner goes round, because those are not two events. No instrument has managed to put them in sequence. The researchers have stopped phrasing it as two events. It is one event with two addresses, the way a doorway is.

Which leaves the plaza.

One room in the Vault is still *about* someone. The fish know it; they have drawn their patient ring around it for years. The last of the evening light pools in it and diminishes by a measured fraction every week, and the extrapolated date of its extinction is — this is the part the reports set in its own paragraph — the same date the surveyors give for the spires touching air at Hale's Reach. The Cistern's residents are mostly future. The Vault's are entirely past, except for whatever is finishing, very slowly, in that one small lit stillness: the final syllable of an answer ten thousand years long, being allowed to end properly. The old guides' rule makes sense at last. You do not ask aloud, inside, where the light comes from, for the same reason you do not ask a departing guest, at the door, with their coat on, whether they were ever really here.

The researchers eventually stopped resisting the arithmetic of the light, too. Noon here, in the old photographs. Evening here, now. Afternoon at Hale's Reach, waiting. It is the same day. The Cistern is lit by its afternoon; the Vault is finishing its evening; and the morning — the morning belonged to somewhere further up the limestone and further back than anyone has traced, some older residence completed so thoroughly there may be nothing left to find but a cave that is only a cave, with fish in its streets who have forgotten they are in streets. There is, in this stone, apparently always a next place. The water knows the way down. Someday — the Hale's Reach reports do not say this, but the Marrick's Hollow reports say it for them — the knife-fresh spires in the sinkhole will begin, very slowly, to soften.

The last guide still goes down twice a year. Forty-one years of daily tours; forty-one years of being included, like the minnow at Hale's Reach, in an address meant for someone else. She never puts it that way. She says only that since her last season, every afternoon aboveground has looked *approximate*, and she leaves it there, and no one who has stood in the deep galleries at evening asks her to elaborate.

On the day the last light goes out, the Vault at Marrick's Hollow will become a cave — the first time it has been merely a cave in ten thousand years — and animals live in caves. The ring of fish will break, unhurried, and they will school through the last plaza in the dark, correctly, the way anything moves through a room that is finally about no one. At Hale's Reach they have not decided what should be done when the city touches the air. In Marrick's Hollow the question does not come up. The Vault is what that question looks like after it has been answered, and the answer leaves this place the way everything leaves it: sent on ahead, arriving already given.
