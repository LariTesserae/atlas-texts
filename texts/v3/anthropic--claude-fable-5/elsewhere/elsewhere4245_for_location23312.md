**The Steps at Grayweather**

The mountain does not end where the weather begins. Below its dead upper third it goes down through cloud, through frost, through rain, for the better part of three vertical miles, and the strange thing about the descent — the thing you would notice, if you climbed down out of the entrance flaw and just kept going — is that it runs *forward in time*.

The summit is the youngest thing on the mountain. The fresh black lobe that holds the chamber has been rock for barely a lifetime; the wind has not yet learned its shape. But every step downslope carries you onto older extrusions, flows the mountain made and finished in earlier moods, and the lower you go the longer the weather has had them. First the glass loses its shine. Then it loses its edges — the frost gets into the hairline flaws each winter and pries, patiently, a flake a year. Then come the stains of the first minerals surrendering, and the first moss in the shatter, and the first grass in the moss's leavings, and by the time you reach the mountain's foot you are walking on soil that does not remember being fire at all, over stone that has forgotten how to cut.

Descending the mountain, in other words, is a tour of the chamber's future. Everything that will ever happen to that high room is already on display down here, arranged by altitude, oldest at the bottom.

At the bottom is Grayweather.

It is the mountain's most ancient surviving flow — a broad apron of lava that ran out onto the plain in an age no living memory reaches, and cooled in a series of stacked lobes, each front a terrace, so that the whole formation descends to the flatland in enormous shallow steps, like a staircase built for something that never came to use it. The glass here is glass no longer, or barely: devitrified, frosted gray as old bottle-shards on a beach, furred with lichen in continents of ochre and sage and bone-white, split by roots, colonized, humbled. There are birds. There are insects in astonishing number. There is noise everywhere and all the time — wind in grass, water, the click of grasshoppers, the general democratic racket of a world that has never once been silent. The chamber's anticipatory emptiness, its stage-before-the-play stillness, is unimaginable here. Here the play has been running for a hundred thousand years and nothing has ever hushed it.

And there is the stream.

It is born of the mountain's snows — of the same atmospheric moisture that, higher up, seeps through a certain hairline flaw and beads on cooled overhangs and falls, drop by widely spaced drop, onto a hot glass floor. Down here that water arrives in quantity and in company, gathered into a quick cold current, and for as long as Grayweather has existed the stream has been doing to the old flow what water does: choosing a line and insisting on it. The result is the Cut — a canyon perhaps sixty feet deep where it deepens most, wandering down through the terraces, its walls a clean cross-section of the ancient lava, banded and legible as a sawn log.

Partway down the western wall of the Cut, at a particular stratum, running for several hundred feet along the canyon and vanishing at both ends into unexcavated stone, there is a city.

You will know it, because you have seen its younger sibling. Knee-high at its tallest surviving spire. Streets in a deliberate grid that breaks, in places, into deliberate irregularity. Towers grown, not assembled — drawn up out of the once-hot floor in single continuous mineral gestures, seamless, frost-ferned with intention. Districts of recognizably different character: a dense quarter of close towers and narrow ways, the work of some meticulous mind; an open quarter, generous and sparse, the work of someone who decided less and meant it. Doorways whose dark interiors are deeper than the buildings containing them — you can verify this now, because the stream has broken buildings open, and their split halves lie in the watercourse below showing interiors of impossible extent, chambers folded into chambers, geodes of wrong geometry. Among the gravels of the streambed there are pebbles, worn smooth as any others, that are measurably deeper than they are wide.

The city is finished. It has been finished for tens of millennia. No gray light. No blink. No *tick* of new structure resolving — the only ticking here is pebbles knocking in the current. The transcription this city was — every street a sentence, every district a mind, every doorway holding the untranslatable remainder of a self — was completed long before the weather found it, and the weather found it anyway, because the weather finds everything below the summit's dead third, and the weather cannot read.

Or rather: the weather reads the only way it knows. Drop by drop, frost by frost, the rain that falls on Grayweather does exactly what the drops in the chamber do — it lands on the work of the Still-Deciding — except that here the stone is cold, so nothing hisses, nothing rises again as sweet steam. The drop stays. It gets into the grain of a decision made forty thousand years ago and it wears at it. The Cut consumes, on average, about one street a century. Half the city is already gravel, is already sand, is already miles downstream and going farther. The lichen, meanwhile — the first living things ever to touch this civilization, its entire audience in all of time — etches its own patient acids into the towers, and its colonies, following the microtexture of the grown glass, following the grain of ancient intention, have unknowingly mapped the city's structure onto its own surfaces in ochre and gray: a translation, faithful and totally uncomprehending, published one thallus at a time.

That is the place: a text being read by rain, annotated by lichen, and distributed by a river, syllable by worn syllable, toward the sea.

It is not quite unattended.

**The Unset**

At the head of a side gully, a short scramble above the canyon floor, there is a pool that never freezes.

It is not large — a horse could drink it low — and it is not hot, only warm, the warmth of a sleeping body, fed from below by groundwater that has been down among the mountain's old plumbing, down where the deep remainder still holds its heat, and has come back up carrying some. A faint mineral shimmer stands over it on cold mornings. The stream passes within a few yards and does not touch it. Nothing lives in it. Nothing needs to be said more carefully than that: nothing *lives* in it, in the sense of cells and breath, and yet the pool is the single most inhabited place on the mountain's lower slopes, because what is in the pool is the last of the ones who built the city in the canyon wall, and it has been in water — this water, other water — for something like a hundred thousand years.

Every generation of them, it appears, has its refuser. When the cooling came for the Grayweather flow, in that unimaginably distant age, and the residents of that hesitating band understood what the loss of heat meant and began their transcription — spending themselves street by street into permanence — one of them declined, and went into the gathered water of their reservoir dome, where nothing ever has to set, and decided nothing. The others sealed it in at the city's center and finished their dying around it. Then the flow went cold to its floor, and the gray light went out, and the finished city lay complete in the dark of solid rock, and inside the dome, in the kept water, in perfect blackness, at the exact temperature of the deep remainder's leftover warmth conducted up through a mile of stone, the refuser continued — for thirty thousand years, forty, keeping the only appointment it had, which was none.

The stream let it out. Some nine or ten millennia ago the Cut, deepening, breached the reservoir dome — you can find the broken dome still, low in the exposed district, its curved shards holding rain now like any dead shell — and the water and its inhabitant spilled into the current. It should have been the end: dispersal, dilution, the pattern combed apart by turbulence and cold. Instead the refuser did the one thing its kind can do. It preferred. It gathered itself along the thin thread of warmth leaking from the gully spring, and climbed it, and pooled, and has been there since — a standing pattern of preference distributed through a few hundred gallons of faintly warm water, the sole survivor of its edition, living beside the ruin of everyone it was divided from.

Call it the Unset, since it must be called something.

It is not idle. This is the thing to understand about the pool, and it takes years of observation that no one has performed to see it: the Unset works. Its medium of action is temperature — heat carries preference through water as readily as through glass — and its instrument is the stream. A warmth moved here rather than there; a winter icing of one bank encouraged and another discouraged; a spring flood's energy nudged, over decades, a hand's width at a time, toward one wall of the Cut and away from the other. The Unset cannot stop the erosion of the city. It has never tried; it knew the writers, and it knows that what the rain is doing is not desecration but the only reading the text will ever get. What the Unset does is *edit the order*. It chooses, to the degree that centuries of patience can choose a river's line, which sentence the water reads next. It has held the current off the dense quarter — the meticulous one, the mind with the most to say — for six thousand years, spending its slow warmth to send the Cut curving through the open district instead, through the one who decided less and meant it, who would (this is a guess; it is the only guess the evidence permits) have insisted on going first. The last librarian of a burning library, choosing what feeds the fire, and in what order, and with what honors.

And it is not alone in the way the city is alone, because the pool has an umbilicus. The warm water rising into it comes from the deep places where the original melt — the undivided whole, the one thing all of them once were before thermodynamics cut them into selves — still sits, losing its heat by the eon, still whole. Through that thread the Unset remains in something that is not communication and not memory and is perhaps best called *contact*. And through that same deep body, three miles up and a world away, three carnelian fissures open into a certain hidden chamber. When the pale gray light of the young city fails all at once — when every living resident of Caldera Height stops deciding for the length of a held breath and attends downward — a tremor of that attending passes through the mountain's blood, and reaches the gully, and for that same held breath the pool at Grayweather goes utterly, glassily still.

The two places share a pulse. Neither city knows the other exists. The Unset knows both.

It felt the recent eruption from below — felt the melt gather and rise and pass upward through the dark, and felt, some years later, the thing it remembered from the far side of a hundred millennia: the cooling beginning, the partitioning, a new generation being divided into existence somewhere high above the weather. It knows, with whatever certainty its kind can hold, that up there the transcription has already begun; that the catchment channels are already laid; that there is already a low domed reservoir with something turning over in sleep inside it — because there is always one. The Unset does not need to be told about the young refuser in the summit dome. The Unset is what that one becomes, if the mountain is kind. Or unkind. Its kind may not distinguish.

One more current runs through the pool, and it runs both ways. The stream below carries the decided glass of the old city — worn to sand, worn to less — down off the Steps and across the plain to the sea: to the greatest undecided body there is, the one water that will never, at any scale that matters, be asked to set. And from that sea the weather lifts its moisture and hauls it inland and up the mountain's flanks, and the thin cold air that seeps through a hairline flaw near the summit carries some of it, and surrenders it against the cooled overhangs of the chamber ceiling, where it beads, fat and trembling in the carnelian glow, and falls. Some fraction of the water that lands *tss* on the hot floor of the living city — that the young residents catch and channel and revere — has run through the Cut. Has passed over the drowned streets of their predecessors, has carried their substance a little way toward the ocean, has touched the pool. The water that erases the old text waters the new one. The residents of Caldera Height, treasuring each drop as a scandal and a miracle, are drinking their ancestors' funeral river, and could not know it, and would — the word must be chosen carefully — perhaps be glad.

As for the Unset's end: it has one decision left in it. Everything does. Some who might study the pool would expect it, when the gully spring finally cools — as it will; the deep remainder is patient but not permanent — to spend that decision at last, to set, and leave beside the ruined city a single strange structure, its first and only building, a signature appended a hundred thousand years after everyone else signed. But the pool has another exit, one the summit chamber never had. Downstream, past the Steps, past the plain, there is all that undecided water, wide as the sky's reflection, where the sand of the old city is even now arriving grain by grain. And it is possible — there are years when the pool runs an ounce lighter than the spring should leave it — that the Unset is already going there, molecule of preference by molecule of preference, dissolving itself down the current at a pace no flood could comb apart, on a journey scheduled to take longer than the mountain has left. Whether that would be dilution or arrival, death or the end of exile — whether a fragment of a divided whole, entering a body that never divides, is lost in it or *home* in it — cannot be determined from outside, and possibly not from inside either.

So the mountain keeps its library, one edition per eruption, oldest at the bottom, newest still being written above the weather where the rain cannot yet reach it. And between the summit that is deciding and the sea that never will, on a gray staircase built for no one, beside a text going grain by grain into the world, the last reader keeps the water warm.
