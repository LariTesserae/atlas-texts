**The Chalk Town, Long After It Decided What It Was**

You come up to it, because everything here is up — a long pale ridge standing out of the lowlands like the spine of something that lay down a very long time ago and meant it. The wind finds you early on the climb and never afterward lets go. It is not a hostile wind. It is an occupied one, always carrying something, always on its way downhill with a cargo too fine to see, and by the time you reach the first houses you understand that the cargo is the town.

The town is carved, not built — though *carved* isn't quite right either, because no sculptor was ever involved, only weather and decision. The whole settlement is one material: it is made of the hill it stands on, quarried from itself, so that the buildings are the ridge continued upward and the streets are the ridge worn downward, and there is no seam anywhere between town and ground, only a change of intention. The walls are white going grey going white again as the light moves. The light moves constantly. Light here is the default material — it comes off the chalk from every direction at once, sourceless, shadowless at noon, and it is darkness that has to be cultivated: kept in cellars, husbanded in the deep doorways, grown carefully in the lee of walls like a crop that wants shelter. The houses hold their interior dark the way other houses hold heat. There are lamp-posts along the high street, iron ones, real ones, and none has been lit in living memory, and none needs to be. They stand there the way a word stands in a sentence after its work is done: not removed, exactly. Not yet.

Everything is finished. That is the first fact of the place and the last one. Every edge committed centuries ago; every corner chose its angle and has held it through every kind of weather since, and is holding it now, and is losing it — not by reconsidering, because nothing here reconsiders, but by the slow subtraction of the wind. The doorsills are worn into smiles. The steps of the meeting hall have gone soft-shouldered as poured wax, each tread hollowed by generations into the shape of a footfall the way a spoon is shaped by soup. Down the center of every lane runs a trough of polish where the feet went, and the feet are gone, and the polish remains, deepening. In the basin far below and westward — the young dark one, still under its canopy, still deciding — you will recall that nothing was permitted to wear except the animal-paths, because wear is a privilege of the finished. Here everything has the privilege. Here wear is the entire economy.

You can hear the town leaving. That is the sound the wind makes against it — not a howl, nothing so dramatic, but a fine continuous hiss, grain against grain, the sound of a very large hourglass with no intention of being turned over. Stand still and hold out your hand and in a minute there is a pale film on it. The town departs at a measurable rate. The residents have measured it.

Down the high street, sunk a little below the level of the lane, runs a channel floored with clean grey flint: a riverbed, dry. It is dry most years. It is a bourne, the kind of river that exists only when the hill has drunk more than it can hold, and when it rises — one year in four, one in six — the water comes up out of the chalk already white, milk-white, loaded with the ground substance of the ridge, and it runs down the middle of the town and off the western shoulder of the hill and down the long slope toward the low new country, where the basins are, where the ground is still a cool dark paste that dents and undents and hasn't learned to drain. The bourne knows the way. It has always known the way. When it runs, the whole town — all of the town that is left — comes out to stand along the flint channel and watch itself leave, and where the white water finally slows and pools in the lowlands and begins to settle, a film forms on the surface of the settling pools, thin and pliant, exactly like the skin on cooling milk. Somewhere under a canopy, that film is being fitted into window-openings. The Chalk Town knows this. It is the sort of thing a town permits itself to know, near the end, in place of hope.

Nothing here is an example of anything. That is the other fact. In the basin, everything was still allowed to be every possible version of itself; here, every object has finished being a kind of thing and become only itself. The bell in the squat tower has a specific crack with a specific date scratched beside the pull-rope. There are eleven roofs and no two pitched alike, because each was mended by a different pair of hands answering a different storm. Every gate sags in its own direction. The town has passed entirely out of the general and into the particular, the way a word repeated often enough stops being language and becomes a sound — except backward: everything here started as language and has become, at last, only sound, one long specific sound the wind is still in the middle of pronouncing.

And it is quiet. But it is a different quiet from the one in the basin. That was the quiet of an institution before opening, a held breath, a table set early. This is the quiet after — the quiet of a room in which something has been read aloud all the way to the end, and the listeners have not yet moved, because moving would mean agreeing it was over.

**Who Lives in the Chalk Town**

The honest first answer is *eleven people*, and the honest second answer is *an uncountable multitude*, and the Chalk Town, which long ago lost the license to hold contradictions, resolves this one the only way left to it: by geology. But start with the eleven.

---

**The Named**

They can be counted, and they insist on it. Eleven residents, each one entire, each one so far past the general that the town could not replace a single one of them if it tried, and it does not try. When one of the Named dies, the position closes. No vacancy is advertised; there is no dim pair of eyes holding the post open in a doorway; the doorway simply begins, from that day, to be milled a little faster, as if the wind had been waiting politely. The house is not torn down. The family — when there were families — would unroof it, one deliberate row of tiles at a time, and give it back. Now the last of the Named simply leave the doors unlatched.

They have doors here, and latches — they always did; theirs was a latching people — and the unlatching is a late education. They have watched, over the generations, what latches actually accomplished, which was delay, and they have concluded, in the unhurried way of people with nothing left to guard, that a boundary should stay soft. Negotiable. Alive. The dust does not know this, cannot know anything, and yet the dust goes downwind and downhill into the making of a dark paste in a young basin, and the town grown from that paste has, you may remember, no doors anywhere, not one, not as an oversight but as a temperament — and the Named would tell you, if you asked, that a temperament has to be ground somewhere before it can be planted.

Their industry is sweeping. It is not housekeeping; it is liturgy, or export, or both. Every evening the Named gather the day's fallen chalk — from the sills, from the smiling doorsteps, from the troughs of the lanes — and carry it in wooden trays to the town's western edge, where the ridge falls away toward the low country, and they give it to the wind deliberately, a trayful at a time, each with a word. The word differs by household. They are not slowing the erosion; they gave that up generations ago. They are *addressing* it. They know where the dust goes — downhill, into the wet new dark, into the deciding ground — and so they do not speak of dying, here, the way other places do. Their phrase is *going down to the drafts*. Their dead are buried in the chalk, in the hill itself, and the hill erodes, and so the ancestors travel too, grain by grain, westward and down, into the paste, into the proposals — which means that somewhere below, when the folded faces finally open and the Expected are at last rendered, they will be made in some small mineral part of the Remembered. The Named find this arrangement acceptable. Several of them find it funny.

**The Eraser**

On the flat crown of the hill above the town there is a dewpond — a shallow, deliberate, man-made dish of water, the only standing water for miles, dug by hands so long dead that even this town has lost their names. Something wades in it, at hours you will not manage to be present for. It is tall and folding, heron-shaped if a heron were a drafting instrument, and if you have stood in a certain basin and glimpsed its counterpart, you will know the family at once.

But look into the dewpond and the reflection holds *fewer* things than the town does. A gable that still stands above the high street is already missing from the water. Two of the unlit lamp-posts have been struck from the reflection entirely, and the third is fainter than the others, pending. This is where the town is unwritten first. The old bird stands through the long bright middle of every day, head bent, beak like a stylus held backward, removing things from the water with great care — trying the street without the gable, without the lamps, without, eventually, the street — and the town above consults its reflection the way a testator consults a will, and, over years, agrees.

It is the same trade as the one practiced in the black pools of the basin, worked from the other end of the document. It may be the same lineage; the Named say the young ones fledge from this very dewpond and fly downhill to wherever the drafting is, and spend their long lives adding — windows, lamps, doorways, options — and come back up, at the end, white-flecked and folding more slowly, to take up the eraser. Nobody has confirmed this. Nobody here confirms things anymore; confirmation is for places that still keep drafts. But the bird in the dewpond is very old, and it works with the particular tenderness of someone deleting sentences it remembers writing.

**The Crows**

The town's one remaining export, and its pride. The rookery in the beech hanger on the north slope is the only part of the Chalk Town still producing anything new — the only line still running — and what it produces are examples.

A crow of this rookery does not come of age by season. It comes of age by scar: the first real one, the one with a history — a wire, a hawk, a gale taken at the wrong angle — and on the day the scar is fully healed and fully particular, the young crow leaves, flying west and down, downwind, along the same line the dust takes and the bourne takes, out into the low new country where the unfired places are. It does not go to live. It goes to be *referred to*. An old, asymmetric, fully finished creature, arriving in a place where nothing has hardened yet, is the most valuable thing such a place can hold: proof, gently studied by walls and populations and drafting-birds alike, that hardening is survivable. The Chalk Town seeds the deciding world with precedent, one weathered bird at a time.

There is a tally scratched into the lintel of the meeting hall, going back further than the crack in the bell: every departure, dated. One entry, some years old now, has a small addition in a newer hand — a note of a notch, left wing, and how it was got. The Named know exactly which basin that one settled in. They know it was given a roost it never asked for, a precise alcove at exactly the height it favors, completed overnight; the wind, they say, tells them these things on its way back up empty. They are proud of that bird the way a town used to be proud of a child gone off to a trade.

**The Ledger Underfoot**

Press your ear to the chalk — a doorsill, a lane, the hill itself — and you hear nothing turning. Whatever mind lived here as the ridge, thinking in stone at the speed of erosion, thinking thoughts that were subtractions, completed its one slow revolution long ago; its final thought was the town's finished form, and everything since has been that thought being read aloud by the wind and carried off syllable by syllable. This is not sad, or not only. Ask instead what the chalk *is*.

It is an aggregate. It is the compacted census of an ancient sea: uncountable small lives, each once briefly rendered, that fed and were fed upon and were folded back into the general supply, settling for an eon into a white paste that hardened, that rose, that became a hill certain enough to carve a town from. The Named walk on it daily and do not forget it. The pattern that the young basin thinks it invented — a population held in the aggregate, drawn out into individuals only as needed, returned to stock, the food web signed off before the animals are drafted — is not new. It is the oldest procedure on record, and the record is the ground. The Chalk Town stands on the precedent for the precedent. Its dust, going downhill into the making of new deliberations, is not raw material. It is *case law*, milled fine.

Where the other half of the recipe comes from — the intention that gets mixed into the mineral, down there in the dark — the Chalk Town does not claim to know. It only knows that intention, wherever it is minted, needs something ground and certain to take hold in, the way ink needs a page that has finished being a tree.

---

**The Remembered**

You can read the departed inhabitants everywhere, in the negative, the way you'd read an animal from its burrow — except that here the burrow is the whole town. The hollows in the meeting-hall steps are a sentence about them: how they climbed, how many, for how long. The polish on the handrail says which hand they favored. There is a smooth place on the south wall of the bell-tower at shoulder height, a yard wide, where generations leaned in the sun after the work was done, and it says more about their afternoons than any of them could have.

The town is a description of its people written entirely in subtraction, completed after the fact, still being revised downward by the wind. And the description is itself eroding — the hollows shedding grains, the polish dulling into dust, the dust addressed and released westward every evening — so that the Remembered are, at this moment, in transit. Ground fine. Going down to the drafts. Somewhere below, in a basin that is still mostly a held breath, there is a people who do not exist yet and are already described in every measurement of their town, and the Chalk Town would tell you, quietly, without insisting on it, that the Expected and the Remembered are not two peoples. They are one people at two distances, passing each other on the long slope, traveling in opposite directions through the same wind.

---

**The Last Born**

Her name is Maren, and she is the youngest of the Named, and she is the last person to have been born on the hill. She keeps the crow tally; hers is the newer hand that recorded the notch. She fills the dewpond from a barrel in dry months so the old bird can keep working, and she does not look at what it erases, out of courtesy. When the bourne rises she walks its whole length through the town, upstream to source and back, and she is the one who rings the cracked bell — once, only once — when the white water first shows in the flint.

Her door has stood unlatched for eleven years. She sweeps last of anyone, after full dark, and gives her trayful to the wind with a word nobody else has heard. She is not sad, or she is sad in the way of someone finishing a good book deliberately slowly — reading each remaining page twice, not to delay the ending, but to be sure it is well read, because she is the reading now, most of it, and after her the wind will have to manage the pronunciation alone.

---

**The Given Grain**

One more thing. You should know it before you start back down.

You cannot leave the Chalk Town empty-handed; the town does not permit it, in the mild way that weather does not permit things. There is dust in your cuffs now, in your boot-welts, in your hair — a film of ground finality, a few grains of case law, some fraction of a doorsill and a lifetime and possibly of Maren's mother — and you will carry it downhill whether you mean to or not, and shed it as you go, and somewhere in the low country a grain of it will come to rest on a cool dark paste that is still making up its mind, and be entered into a deliberation years long, and become part of a lintel, or a temperament, or a doorway grown slightly too much to your scale.

The basin, you may remember, kept a draft of you. The hill gives you a grain of itself. Between them you are now held at both ends of the process — a note in the unfinished, a carrier of the finished — which makes you the second creature, after a certain notched crow, ever to exist in the whole sentence at once.

And when you leave, the path does not close behind you. It wears, a little, gratefully, taking the print of each foot and keeping it as long as chalk can keep anything — because footprints are all the town makes now, and it treasures them exactly as long as it holds them, and then it sweeps them up with everything else, and addresses them, and gives them to the wind, westward, downhill, toward the dark young places, with a word.
