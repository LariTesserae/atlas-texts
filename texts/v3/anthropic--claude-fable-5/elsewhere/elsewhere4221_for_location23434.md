**The Curdling Stair**

It begins, as most descriptions of this place do, with a correction: you are lower than you think you are. Not in altitude — the vale sits deep, that much is obvious — but in another sense entirely. The street under your feet is not the street. The true street lies a fathom down, and beneath it another, and beneath that the first floors of houses whose upper storeys you are currently mistaking for whole buildings. The ground here is not ground. It is accumulation. The vale has been rising under its own town for as long as the town has existed, a coin's thickness in a season, a knuckle in a lifetime, and the people who live here have long since stopped fighting it and begun, instead, keeping pace.

The light is the second correction, for anyone arriving from elsewhere. There is no sun in the sky of the Curdling Stair — not no sun at all, but no *disc*, no source, no direction. The sky is a lid of cloud, low and permanent and pearl-white, and the light comes through it evenly from everywhere at once, so that nothing in the vale casts a shadow harder than a bruise. Faces here are soft-lit from all sides. Buildings have no dark faces and no bright ones. The people of the vale have never seen a sharp shadow, never seen a star, never seen blue except in dye and in one other place, which will be described in its turn. Ask them what is above the cloud and they will look at you with the mild patience of people being asked what is behind a mirror. The cloud is not a covering over their sky. The cloud is their sky. It has always held.

Out of that sky, on the mountain's northern shoulder, comes the water.

It emerges already falling — a river with no visible beginning, stepping down out of the white lid in a long stammer of falls, and it is itself white, milk-white, opaque with dissolved stone, and warmer than any mountain water has a right to be, so that it steams faintly in the cool air and feeds the very cloud it descends from. The river is called the Milk, because of course it is. And what the Milk has built, over ages, is the stair: a vast cascade of terraces descending the mountainside, hundreds of them, scalloped basins of pale stone with a faint rose cast, each pool lipped and brimming, each lip spilling into the pool below, the whole formation gleaming wet and white and gently smoking under the shadowless light. The water builds as it goes. Every drop that falls here leaves a little of its burden behind — a film of stone on every surface it touches — and so the lips of the pools grow, and the terraces thicken, and the stair adds to itself continuously, patiently, the way the sound of it never stops. By the time the Milk reaches the valley floor and slides away downstream toward the barges and the wider world, it has laid down nearly everything it carried and runs almost clear. The vale keeps the stone. That is the whole economy of the place, and most of its character: the water arrives white and leaves transparent, and the difference stays.

The town is called Brim, and it stands — floats, over generations, is nearly the truer word — on the broad mid-terraces of the stair. It is a white town, built entirely of the travertine it stands on, quarried from the older terraces upslope, and it is the only town most of its people have ever needed to imagine, because it contains its own history vertically. The ground rises; the town rises with it. Doors are cut, and in forty years re-cut higher, and the old doors become low windows, and the old windows become vents, and eventually the whole storey is let go — filled, sealed, surrendered to the stone — and a new storey is raised on top in the old proportions. Every house in Brim is the top of a taller, older house. The mason's rule here, taught to children with their letters, is *one course a generation*: build your line of stones, live your life on it, and trust your grandchildren to build theirs. Dig anywhere in Brim and you descend through thresholds. The town's memory is not kept in archives. It is kept in cross-section.

Everything furs. This is the small constant tax of living on the Milk: kettles grow a white bark and must be cracked out of it, ropes left wet turn to rods, a boot forgotten by a channel for a season becomes a boot-shaped stone that will outlast the family that lost it. And so Brim supports, as its oldest and most honored profession, the scalers — men and women with picks and scrapers and long-handled chisels who walk the town daily, chipping the streets level, clearing the throats of the fountains, keeping the mill-races open and the gutters true. They are not laborers, exactly. They are closer to what a bell-ringer is to a church. The scalers keep the town from being loved to death by its own water, and they do it the way it has always been done, in a stepping, weaving progress along routes so old that nobody planned them, pausing where the routes bend, pausing longer at certain basins, checking.

Below the town, where the falls are strongest, stand the orchards. They are not orchards. They are frames — great timber scaffolds built out into the spray, hung with objects the way an espalier is hung with fruit: lace, letters, dolls, wedding wreaths, bird's nests, a fiddle, a dead man's coat. The falling Milk coats them, day by day, in stone. Objects hang in the orchards for a season or for years, ripening, and are brought in when their stone shell is thick enough to last forever — white, exact, every thread and grain of them recorded in mineral. Some are keepsakes and go home to shelves. Most are trade: the barges carry petrified roses and stone lacework down the clearing river to towns that have never seen the vale, and this is how Brim is known in the world, insofar as it is known — the place where soft things are sent to be made permanent.

The vale keeps time by the water, because the water keeps a schedule no one has explained. Every afternoon the Milk thins — the falls slacken, the mills coast and stop, the orchards drip dry — a lean interval called the fast, when the whole stair goes quiet enough to hear a single terrace at a time. And every night, an hour or so after full dark, the water returns in force: the falls swell, the pools brim over all together, the mills wake, and the sound of the stair doubles and deepens for hours. This nightly flood is called the letting-down, in the language of dairies, and Brim sleeps inside it the way other towns sleep inside silence. No one in the vale knows that the fast falls in the hours when, far above the cloud, a country of stone is drinking itself back into its own cracks; no one knows the letting-down is the arrival, hours delayed by the long passage through the mountain, of a plateau-wide exhalation released at a sunset they have never seen. They only know the rhythm is old, and reliable, and theirs. Their clocks are set to it. Their lullabies are timed to it. The whole town breathes on the breath of a place it cannot remember exists.

**Who Lives in the Curdling Stair**

It begins, as it must, with a correction: the statement that everything lives here is true, and it is not sufficient. Biology is everywhere — that much holds. Moss greens the wet lips of the terraces, ferns crowd the spray zones, dippers walk under the falls, the town is loud with dogs and children and the arguments of neighbors. But the distinction between *the living* and *the inhabitants* is one of those strict modern dichotomies, and the vale is built, grain by grain, out of a country where that distinction wore through long ago. Among the living of the Curdling Stair, and underneath them, and set into their very walls, there are older tenants. They arrived the way everything arrives here. They came down in the water.

The smallest of them are the sounds.

The quarrymen know them best. High on the old terraces, where Brim cuts its building stone, a freshly split block will sometimes speak — once, and only once, at the moment of opening: a single clear drip, though the stone is dry; a hiss of thin falling water; very rarely, a footstep. The sound comes out of the stone the way a bubble comes out of ice, held since the day the water laid that layer down, released by the hammer after who knows how many lifetimes in the dark. The quarrymen call it the stone speaking, and a block that has spoken is never used for common building. Such blocks are carried down whole and set, unmortared, into a long dry wall on the eastern edge of town — the Spoken Wall, built in a corbelled style older than any local memory, added to for centuries, one stone at a time. People go there to press an ear against it. The wall never speaks twice. It is not expected to. It is simply where Brim keeps the voices it was sent, the way you keep letters you cannot read.

For what the sounds are — though no one below knows it — is the plateau's fauna, migrated. The orphaned drips that graze the dawn basins of the tableland do not all stay there. Some are taken by the seeps, carried down through the mountain in the white water, and laid into the growing stone of the stair along with the lime: sound, deposited. The vale is the sediment of a country that loses things slowly, and among the things it loses, slowly, are its noises. Once — this was four generations ago, and the scalers still tell it — a split block released a full stride of footsteps, unhurried and specific, the gait of someone crossing wet stone with the ease of long practice. The quarrymen downed tools for the day. That block is in the Spoken Wall now, at shoulder height, and it is the only stone in the wall that anyone touches on purpose, passing.

The second population lives in the stone itself, and it is the population of weathers.

A weather is what the quarrymen call it when a block splits open on an image. Not a fossil, not a stain — a sheen, laid flat within the stone like a reflection pressed between pages: a patch of sky, most often, but a sky no one in the vale has ever stood under, blue past all dye-blue, deep toward indigo, with a light in it that looks hard enough to cut. Some weathers hold a stretch of pale rose-grey rock under that impossible sky, thrown with shadows blacker and sharper than any shadow the shadowless vale can make. Some hold a far horizon of stone dropping into white. And a few — the rare ones, the ones that decide the fortunes of a quarry season — hold a figure: crouched at the edge of water, one hand extended, faceless, checking.

The people of Brim have their theories. The weathers are the water's dreams, laid down where it slept. They are windows into the inside of the world, or the outside of it. They are pictures of the country of the dead, which would explain the light. What they are, in fact, is the plateau's other livestock, herded to the rim: the misfiled reflections that graze the basin-mosaic above, moved along their pecked channels for ages by hands and then by habit, and finally — some of them — carried over the edge, down through the cloud, dissolved in the Milk and precipitated out again, fixed at last into the one medium that will never misfile them. The pools above reflect loosely. The stone below remembers exactly. Every weather in every wall of Brim is a moment of the Weeping Tableland, filed forever in the town built from its body — and the townspeople, who have never seen a hard shadow or a deep sky, set their finest weathers over their doors, and live their whole lives walking in and out beneath small stone windows onto their own forgotten origin, and call it luck.

The crouched figure they call the Kneeler.

It needs describing carefully, because the temptation is to call it a saint, and the town has half-succumbed: there are carved Kneelers now, cut by masons over new doorways in imitation of the found ones, and the carvings drift, generation by generation, like a hand recopying a passage of text — a face added here, a tool there, softenings, embellishments — so that the newest Kneelers look like kindly neighbors, and only the true weathers, split raw from the old terraces, still show the original: faceless with accumulated faces, forever in the posture of attention. The scalers claim the Kneeler as their patron, which is the town's one act of perfect, unwitting accuracy. Because the Kneeler is a portrait of their own office, painted by water: a thousand stewards of the high country averaged into one stance, sent downstream, and taken up again below without a single day's interruption in the work. Above the cloud the tending was against wearing-away; below the cloud the tending is against building-up. It is the same tending. The picks are new. The pausing at certain basins is not.

There is one basin in Brim that is never allowed to fur. It stands in the market square, a plain round fountain fed by a spring the town calls the Constant, because it alone does not thin during the fast — the one flow in the whole vale that runs full through the lean hours, steady as a kept promise. (High above, ringed by upright stones, there is one pool on the plateau that never reverses, whose water always, only, falls down. No one below knows this. The promise is kept anyway.) Every morning before the market wakes, someone scrapes the Constant clear — it is a duty that circulates through the town's families like a season, and no one remembers it being instituted, and no one would dream of letting it lapse. The water in that fountain must be honest: clear to the bottom, reflecting only the sky that is actually above it, which is white, and the faces actually looking in, which are alive. It is the calibration pool of the lower country. Brim thinks it is merely tidiness. Brim has thought so, without fail, every morning, for as long as Brim.

And then there are the climbers, which brings us to the passage between.

A few in every generation go up. Usually they are young, usually masons or scalers, usually at the restless end of an apprenticeship; the guild does not require it and does not discourage it and does not discuss it. They climb past the quarries, past the last orchards, up the flank of the stair to where the falls come stammering out of the sky, and they walk into the cloud, and they are gone for days. They come back down dry-eyed and empty. Whatever is above the lid, they cannot say; the descent through the cloud takes it from them the way the vale's water takes lace and gives back stone — the shape kept, the softness gone. They remember nothing. But their boot soles are worn in a pattern of stepping and weaving, and their hands have learned things. A climber comes down knowing, without having been taught, the exact corbel and lean of the Spoken Wall's courses. A climber comes down having lost the habit of striding straight across open ground. One came down humming a children's counting rhyme that every child in Brim already knew — the old skipping song, the one whose counts and turnings the children chant without wondering why the turns come where they come — and she hummed it, the whole way down the stair, at walking pace, pausing on certain steps, and could not say why, and never could. The rhyme is very old. Its turns, laid against a map that no map in the vale has ever shown, would bend where certain lines of cairns bend, in a high stone country, along the seams.

So it is at least possible — the vale's one scholar has suggested it, gently, in a manuscript nobody reads — that the people of Brim did not merely settle beneath the source of their river. That they came down it. That some long-ago generation of the high country's patient builders descended into the cloud for the last time, forgot, as everyone forgets, and kept walking until the water they had tended all their lives came out of the sky in front of them again — and stopped, and stayed, because their hands would not go further from it. Descent proves nothing; the records are stone, and the stone came down regardless. But it would explain the rhyme. It would explain the Constant. It would explain why a town that has never seen the plateau keeps, in its bones and mornings and guild-habits, every one of the plateau's arrangements, recopied and drifted but never dropped — because to the water, as anyone who has stood at the honest pool could tell them, anyone who attends is the same person. The tenancy did not end when the high country emptied. It flowed downhill, at the speed of dissolved stone, and reincorporated.

So: who lives in the Curdling Stair. The dippers and the ferns and the arguing neighbors, yes. And beneath them, and among them: the deposited sounds, waiting in the blocks for the hammer, spending their one word each. The weathers, fixed at last, holding open their small blue windows above the doors. The Kneeler, faceless and multiplying, patron of everyone who scrapes and checks. The office, open as ever, filled every morning at the fountain by hands that believe they are only being tidy. And under all of these, or identical with all of these, the stair itself — which thickens by a hair each night in the letting-down, laying the high country's breath course upon course beneath every bed in Brim, so that the whole town rises, asleep, on the slowly arriving body of the place it came from and cannot remember.

It is not an exile, probably. It is just what an old country looks like when it moves house one grain at a time: the tears wept above, curdled below into thresholds and lintels and saints; the weeping, translated by depth and distance into mill-noise and market-noise and the shouting of children on the terraces — the same water, in hundreds of voices still, but no longer grieving, if it ever was, because down here every voice it kept has someone alive to hear it. The vale is loud all night with the plateau arriving. In the morning the fountain will be scraped clear, and the water in it will be honest, and somewhere far above the white lid of the sky, at the edge of a pool no one is standing beside, something crouched will pause a moment longer at its checking — as if it heard, faint and early, the sound of the town it is slowly becoming.
