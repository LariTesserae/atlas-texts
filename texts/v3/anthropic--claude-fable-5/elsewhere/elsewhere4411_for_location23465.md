# The Weathering

Begin with the sound, because it is the first right thing. Rain — real rain, generous and dirty, drumming on broad leaves and thatch and the swollen brown backs of rivers, rain that has clearly fallen before and means to fall again. Under it, the smell a wet country ought to have: leaf-mold, sweet decay, mud, smoke, the whole honest reek of things becoming other things. Somewhere west of here is a jungle that owes the world this smell and has never paid. The debt comes due in the Weathering. All of it. Every drop the basin refused, every rot it postponed, every afternoon it ate — it all runs downhill, and downhill is here.

Go east from the Verdancy — up over the scarp the perimeter-walker paces, and down the long fall of the land on the other side — and you come out of the held morning into the rest of the day. The descent takes you through cloud, because cloud stacks against the rim in banks: weather arrives out of the west, finds the basin closed to it, and piles at the door like letters no one collects, until it topples eastward and delivers itself twice over. The Weathering gets double rain because the rain of two countries falls on one. It gets double wear for the same reason. That is the first thing to understand about the place, and the tellers there will explain it to you in exactly these terms, patiently, as to a child: *a held breath is not a breath unbreathed. Somewhere, it comes out.*

The stone under the Weathering is the basin's stone. The same flow, the same event, the same age to the year — you can trace the dark seams in it, cousins to the ones that watch. But you would never call this rock young. Every edge is gone. The hills lie like knuckles under wet cloth, the valleys are broad and tired, the rivers run in beds they have clearly argued with for eons — and none of it has had eons. The two countries were poured in the same hour, and one of them has been kept sharp as the day it fractured while the erosion owed to both has been done here, on half the land, in double time. Break a stone in the Weathering and its fresh faces round within the week, like bread going stale. The country is the basin's sibling, aged in its place. It wears both their years, and it wears them the way an older sister wears a younger one's debts: without much comment, having long ago stopped expecting the arrangement to change.

There are no mornings. The sun comes up, but the light it brings is already used — amber, slantwise, the honeyed and heavy light of late day, at dawn, at noon, always. The morning happens elsewhere, and everyone knows where; the word for the basin in the Weathering is *Upmorning*, said the way you'd name a rich relation. What the Weathering gets is the afternoon, all of it, everyone's, and the shadows show it: they are long from the moment they exist, and they move fast enough to watch. Sit on a doorstep with a cup and you can see your own shadow cross the yard like a slow animal. Children race their shadows here. The shadows generally win.

At night, if the rain lets up, comes the hourfall.

It falls out of a clear sky, fine and pale grey and faintly warm, and it makes, settling, a soft *shhh* — the same sound, note for note, that moves through the canopy of the Verdancy without wind. This is not coincidence and no one in the Weathering pretends it is. The garden upcountry eats its hours; the ash of the eaten hours rises on the exhaust and drifts east and comes down here as dust. Spent time. It smells of nothing at all, which is the smell of afternoons that have already happened. Where it drifts shallow it is a fertilizer beyond price — an hourfall dusting brings a field from seed to harvest in ten days, ages a cask of raw wine into something with a childhood and regrets. Where it drifts deep, against north walls and in the lee of the scarp, it is another matter. You do not sleep in hourfall. Livestock are penned away from the drifts. And the very old, when they decide they are finished — and in the Weathering it is understood to be a decision — walk out on a still night into the deep grey with their affairs settled, and lie down, and let the years find them all at once. *Going into the grey*, it's called, and it is spoken of the way other countries speak of going home.

Everything in the Weathering runs at this pace, and so nothing in the Weathering survives by lasting. Iron rusts overnight; ink browns and fades within a season; a carved inscription rounds to a rumor of itself inside two years. What survives, survives by being renewed. The houses are patterns kept up by repair — wattle and plank and thatch replaced piece by piece, continuously, so that a house may be six generations old with not one board in it older than a decade, and it is still, everyone agrees, without hesitation, the same house. There is a bridge over the Milkwater that has been replaced in its entirety perhaps forty times and is still called Harrel's bridge, after the man who built the first one, and if you suggest to a local that it is therefore not really Harrel's bridge they will look at you with the specific pity reserved for people from slower countries. In the Weathering, a thing is not its material. A thing is the care that keeps its shape.

And the wear itself is not only ruin. This is the country's one act of tenderness, and you have to live there a while to see it: things here wear *toward* themselves. A knife used forty years wears to the exact curve of the hand that held it — becomes, in its last season, more purely that woman's knife than it ever was new. Doorsteps hollow into footprints. A chair polishes to the shape of its sitter. The Weathering strips everything, but what it strips last is what was most touched, and so for a little while, near the end, every object becomes a portrait of its use. Erosion is the Weathering's attention. It keeps nothing, but it *notices* everything, and what it has noticed it lets go last.

Some dawns — not often, no one can say why these dawns and not others — a wind comes down off the western scarp smelling of crushed mint and hot glass and ozone: the basin's breath, arriving. They call it the grace, and for the hour it blows, nothing in the Weathering decays. Bread stays warm. Cut flowers hold. Milk keeps sweet in the pail. The dying are carried out into it, and rest easy, and some of them use the hour to say the things that need a whole hour. The garden upcountry does not know it does this, presumably. Presumably the garden does not know anything. But the exhaust of its endless held morning is, downwind, the only mercy in the calendar, and the Weathering folk have decided — deliberately, the way they decide everything — to be grateful to it anyway. Gratitude, they say, keeps better than grievance, and in this climate you plant what keeps.

Begin with the verb, because down here it is finally the right one. *Lives* — the current, the intake and output, the boat riding forward on time — the Weathering has all of it, doubled, the boat going flat-out downstream with the whole diverted river under it. What lives in the Weathering lives the way a fire lives in dry grass. The question is not whether. The question is how anything so quick can also keep.

**The Quick** is what they call themselves, without bitterness. They are people — mortal, ruddy, loud at meals, weathered early. A Weathering life runs perhaps forty-five years and is not felt to be short, because it is not short of anything: doubled time is doubled time, and a Quick child of ten has had the afternoons of twenty. They are old the way their hills are old — soft-edged, settled, worn toward themselves — and their proverb about the basin is the whole of their philosophy in nine words: *Upmorning kept one morning. We got all the afternoons.* They do not envy the garden. They have seen what keeping costs.

Because every family in the Weathering has someone upcountry. That is the second thing to understand. The remainders in the basin — the attendant, the sleeper, the standing figures among the trunks — did not condense out of nothing. They walked up the scarp, over generations, out of the ford-houses and river towns of the Weathering: the grieving, the curious, the ones who wanted one hour that would hold still. The basin took them in and learned what it could and smoothed the rest away, and what it learned it keeps forever, one sentence per soul, exactly right. The Weathering keeps everything else. *The basin keeps the remainder,* the tellers say. *We keep the rest.*

**The tellers** are how. Nothing written survives here; voice is the only durable medium in the country, and voice endures only by being spent. So the Rest — the full account of every one of the kept — is held in living memory and told nightly, in rotation, teller to apprentice, generation to fast generation, a relay that has never once been allowed to drop. The tellings are where the kept get their names back. Upcountry, in the frozen mid-morning, there is a hare-sized thing in the hexagon clearings that holds its exact distance from every visitor, measuring; down here, in the Rest, she is still *Saray of the ford-house*, who measured everything twice and trusted nothing to the eye, who undercounted cloth for the poor with a face like a locked door, who climbed the scarp in her sixtieth year to see the place that had kept her sister. Upcountry there is a sleeper curled identically in every tile of the repeating corridors, dreaming in copies; down here she is *little Ome*, Saray's sister, who as a child could not fall asleep unless her sisters lay the same way in the same bed, all of them arranged, all of them matching. The basin kept each of them perfectly and understands neither. The Weathering understands both completely and can keep them only by saying them again tonight, and tomorrow night, forever, in relay, against the rot. Even the perimeter-walker has a name in the Rest — *Ath*, the first ever sent up, in the earliest generation, to find where the rain had gone. The tellers say Ath never went down into the green at all: that what paces the rim, reading the fractures joint by joint, is still looking for the crack the rain gets in by — or for the way back down, which is the same search from the other side.

And there is **Dren, called the Returned** — the only one, in all the relayed generations, to walk into the Verdancy and walk out again. He went up young to see his grandfather among the standing figures and came back forty days later by the Weathering's count, changed in two visible ways. His hands have not aged since — they are a young man's hands on an old man's arms, smooth, exempt, because the basin learned his hands before he left and what the basin has learned no longer passes time, even here. And his shadow disagrees with him: it is the shadow of a slightly different Dren, one who stands a little straighter, and both versions are clearly true, and the discrepancy never resolves. He has no laugh. The tellers believe the basin kept it — that somewhere in forty green kilometers a standing figure is laughing his laugh, mid-morning, forever — and Dren neither confirms nor denies this, though he confirms much else; he sits in on the tellings and checks the details of the kept against what he saw, and his corrections are treated as scripture, because he is the only proofreader the Rest will ever have. Of his leaving he says only this, always in the same words, which the apprentices learn first of anything: *Something turned me over, the way you turn a leaf to read the other side. It read me partway, and set me down outside the scarp, and went on to the next leaf. I was a dull page. It was the kindest thing that has ever been done to me.*

So: what lives in the Weathering. A country the same age as the frozen one, wearing the years of both. Rivers of diverted rain, fields ripened on the ash of eaten hours, houses that are patterns of care rather than piles of wood. A quick, warm, unresentful people who got the whole afternoon and spend it telling; a relay of names run nightly against the rot; one man with young hands and a disagreeing shadow; and the grey drifts, waiting, patient, where the finished go to let the years find them.

You should know one custom before you leave, because if you have come this far you are almost certainly going up. Everyone who climbs the scarp sits first, for one night, with a teller. You give them everything — your name, your people, your shames, the way you take your tea, the whole unabridged rest of you — and they take it into the relay, so that whatever single sentence the garden keeps of you upcountry, the fullness of you will go on being said down here, in the amber light, for as long as the telling holds. But understand what you are agreeing to. The basin's keeping is exact and dead. The Weathering's is alive, and living memory drifts: the tellers will soften you, sharpen you, marry you to the wrong river, give your best line to somebody's grandmother. Season by season, telling by telling, they will get you wrong, and wronger, and never once let you go.

That is not the failure of the keeping. That is the keeping. Upcountry, the garden will get one sentence of you exactly right, forever, and it will mean nothing to anyone. Down here they will get all of you slightly wrong tonight and differently wrong next year — and the drift is the heartbeat. It is the part of you that will still be changing after you can't.

In the Weathering, that is the only kind of kept that counts as alive.
