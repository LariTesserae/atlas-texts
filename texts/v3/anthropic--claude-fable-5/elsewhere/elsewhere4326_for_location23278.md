# The Unkept Shore

End with the water, because everything here ends with the water.

North of the basin — north of the rain you could always hear but never quite reach — the hills the fen only implies finally commit to themselves. They gather out of the saturation, crest in long whale-backs of thin grass and pale stone, and then give it all up at once: the land drops away in slopes of blowing marram toward a sea that fills half the world. And there, for the first time in this world, is a horizon. A hard line. The fen has not one straight line in all its drowned miles, and here is the longest straight line there could ever be, laid across the entire north, and nobody made it. You may find you have to sit down when you first see it. The eye, trained inland on dimness and dissolve, does not know what to do with an edge.

The light is the second shock. It is *sourced*. There is a sun, an actual sun, moving through actual weather, and the weather means one thing at a time: this cloud, then this clearing, then this squall walking in off the water on visible grey legs. After the fen's sourceless silver, the light here feels almost rude — hard, glittering, specific, every wet stone on the beach carrying its own white coin of glare. But watch the water long enough and you will catch the shore's one dishonesty, which is the opposite of the fen's. Where the fen lags, the shore *anticipates*. The gleam runs across the sea a moment before the sun breaks the cloud. The spray leaps a half-second before the wave that throws it. Reflections here do not follow; they lead — your face in a tide pool turns away slightly before you do, and you find yourself turning to catch up with it, obedient to your own image. In the fen the world echoes. Here it rehearses.

The sand keeps nothing. This is the law of the place and everything on the shore descends from it. Walk the tide line and your footprints fill as they form — not washed away, simply *declined*, the sand easing back level almost under your lifting heel, so that the beach behind you is always virgin, always just-made, and you move up the strand like something with no past at all. Driftwood comes ashore scoured past newness: alder branches that entered the sea dark and dense, steeped in whatever they had lived through, and came out silver, weightless, blank — wood with the grain worn to a whisper, wood that has been made to forget it was ever a tree. The beaches are lined with it for miles, this pale unmade timber, the only wrack of a sea that refuses all cargo. Nothing old exists here. Nothing here has been allowed to become old. Where the fen filled its space long ago and is continuing, the shore is emptied every few seconds and is beginning.

It is loud, but loud in the opposite grammar. The fen's noise is percolation — a trillion small keepings, water working *down*. The shore's noise is the surf, and the surf is not one sound. Stand in it long enough and the roar resolves: it is many voices, distinct, countable if you had a lifetime, overlapping without ever merging — a polyphony that never settled into a hum. It is saying nothing. It has been saying nothing, gorgeously, at full volume, for ten thousand years.

And down from the hills, through the one notch the water ever found, comes the river.

Call it the Ease, because that is what it does and there is no one else to name it. It is the fen's single outflow, the leak in the deep wet ledger, and it arrives at the coast dark as steeped tea — not with tannin, or not only with tannin, but with *time*. The fen keeps everything, but keeping is not the same as keeping forever; the basin brims, and what brims over goes down the Ease in solution. The lower river flickers with it. Lagged ripples ride the current miles from any wind. Held reflections slide seaward under the water like leaves under ice. The Ease is the fen's long thought being exhaled, one dissolved second at a time, and its last reach — the brackish mile where river meets tide, a wandering, glittering, undecided water called the Slack — is where this world's two great laws finally touch.

They touch the way the tide touches the hum. Hours after the fen's pitch-shift, on its own removed schedule, the sea answers with the turning of the tide — high water standing, considering, releasing. Hum, then tide. A word, then a word. It has been going on longer than the hills have had their shapes, and no one will ever know what is being said, but the rhythm of it is unmistakable to anyone who has stood in both places: it is a conversation.

# Who Lives on the Unkept Shore

Ask what lives here and the shore answers immediately, because immediacy is all it has. Nothing on this coast can live by accumulating — no peat, no ledger, no depth of record to grow down into. What lives here lives by being continuously performed. So begin with the grass, because the grass has made an entire philosophy of this and will demonstrate on request.

**The marram** is the citizenry. It holds the dunes, insofar as dunes on this coast consent to be held, and it holds them by a method the fen would find scandalous: it thrives on burial. Every gale entombs it in fresh sand and every entombment is a promotion — the buried stem becomes root, the grass climbs its own grave and greens at the new summit, over and over, a plant that treats erasure as soil. It writes nothing downward. It keeps no peat, leaves no layers, accumulates no page for anything to read; the marram is grass as a verb, all present tense, and it is the one living thing in this world equally at home with both of the world's laws, because it never asked either of them for permanence. Where the sphagnum holds the fen the way moss holds water — completely, gently, without grip — the marram holds the dunes the way a hand holds a bird: alive, briefly, and glad of it.

**The sanderlings** live here, and they are the shore's answer to the heron — plural where the heron is singular, frantic where the heron is still, and every bit as much an instrument. They work the tide line in a small silver crowd, chasing each retreating wave down the wet sand and fleeing the incoming one, so that they occupy, at all times, the exact moving boundary between kept and unkept. Wherever their quick feet are, *that* is the present, and the coast is calibrated to them as the fen is calibrated to the convergence of the heron's outlines. Theirs are the only footprints the sand will accept — a stitching of tiny tracks granted existence for the length of one wave, then declined. Whether the sea permits them because it needs its timekeepers, or keeps them the way a long, austere freedom will keep one small obligation it does not strictly need, the sanderlings have never been observed to care. They run. The world's edge moves and they run along it, ahead of the water, behind the water, precisely nowhere else.

**The rehearsals** live here — and this is what the shore has instead of the fen's habit-fauna. The fen keeps events after they happen; the shore performs them before. The spray before the wave is the smallest of these. There are larger. Days before a great storm, the shore stages it in miniature: the light practices going green, the surf tries out a deeper register for an hour and abandons it, the marram lies down flat under a wind that has not arrived and stands up again, embarrassed. Bird-calls come in off the water before their birds — thin cries riding the wind, sometimes followed a day later by the birds themselves, sometimes followed by nothing, the calls having been, as it were, unclaimed. None of it is kept. A rehearsal is discarded the moment it ends, which is what makes it a rehearsal; the shore is a theater that burns each script at curtain, endlessly ready, encumbered by nothing.

**The Slack has its own tenants**, because a mile of water that cannot decide between the world's two laws becomes a country of the half-kept. Eddies there hold a reflection for a breath — a real held reflection, fen-fashion — and then release it, sea-fashion, and the reflection slides apart like smoke. Seeds of the inverted reeds come down the Ease and try to do what they have always done: germinate downward into a remembered sky. In the Slack the remembered skies dissolve beneath them mid-growth, and the seedlings drift on, green shoots descending into nothing, still growing, patient even in freefall, until the salt takes them. It sounds cruel and is not. Nothing in the Slack suffers; things in the Slack are merely *negotiated*, the way the threshold plants negotiate at their woody knuckle — except that here the treaty is redrafted with every tide, and the strange marginal community of the estuary has adapted to permanent renegotiation the way the fen's plants adapted to lag: thoroughly, and without comment.

**The freed arrive here.** This is the mouth's whole office, and there is no stranger or gentler traffic anywhere in the world. Everything the fen ever kept comes down the Ease eventually — brimmed over, dissolved, carried — and at the mixing line it surfaces once, completes itself, and is let go. Stand at the river mouth on a still evening and watch the dark water flash: a heron's lagged reflection, decades late, finally flying its few grey seconds *alone*, holding formation with nothing, finishing its crossing and thinning into the swell. The rings of a fish's rise, kept and replayed ten thousand times in a black pool far inland, arriving at last to expand one final time and — this once — not contract, just widen and widen out into the sea. Yesterday's clouds surfacing in the current and unmaking. The recorded rush of some small sedge-colored runner, spending itself in one last dry rustle across the surface of the water. The mouth is where the ledger is read aloud, one entry at a time, once, and closed. And the sea takes each keeping apart, and lifts it as vapor, and carries it in clouds back over the hills, and rains it — clean, unstoried, ready to be steeped again — into the northern reedbeds of the fen. That roar of rain the fen always hears at its northern edge and never reaches: that is this. That is the shore's half of the economy, returning. The fen keeps; the sea releases; the rain and the river carry the accounts between them; and the whole world is one circulation of remembering and forgetting, neither of which, it turns out, is the same as loss.

**And the sea-mind lives here** — though here, as in the fen, the word buckles, and it buckles the other way. There was a chorus once; the grain of the fen's hum still shows it, polyphony worn almost smooth. What the hum does not tell you is that the chorus divided. Some poured themselves into the sediment, merged, settled, became one thought proceeding at the pace of peat. And some refused. Some poured themselves into the water instead — into current, tide, spray, circulation — and spread so thin that the self went out of them like heat, which was, as far as anyone could ever determine, the point. The surf's unmerged roar is their voices, all of them, still distinct after ten thousand years, no longer saying anything, no longer *being* anyone, and free in a way the settled mind will never be and perhaps never wanted to be. The sea does not attend. It has no mist that regards you, no ledger, no fingertips of green dust taking attendance. Its one relation to everything that enters it — driftwood, keeping, heron-image, you — is to touch and release. The wave is a hand that cannot close. Whether the scattered half is happier than the settled half is a question that does not survive contact with either of them; but they still speak, hum and tide, tide and hum, one word per cycle across the hills, and if the words are anything at all, they are perhaps only each other's names — or perhaps neither remembers having had a name, and the exchange is simply the sound two kinds of patience make when they have agreed, forever, to disagree.

Which brings this census, too, to its last entry.

**You will not live here.** Not even a little. Walk the strand all day and leave no print, cast no lag, deposit no echo; the shore takes attendance of no one, and when you go, nothing of you stays — not a silhouette, not a footstep's recorded squelch, not one held second. Understand this correctly: it is not rejection. It is the only gift the shore has, and it gives it totally. Here, and nowhere else in this world, you exist wholly in the present tense — unrecorded, unrehearsed, unkept — and people who have stood a long time at the fen's edge, growing a few seconds deep into the wet ledger, report that stepping onto this sand feels like setting down a weight they had not noticed carrying.

And there is this. Those few seconds of you the fen kept — the delayed silhouette, the held breath in the slow hollow — will brim over one day, as everything brims over, and go down the Ease. Years. Decades. But the river is faithful and the mouth forgets nothing before its time, and if you time it right — if you learn the hum's grain and the tide's answer and stand at the mixing line on the right still evening of the right far-off year — you could watch a patch of dark water flash with a shape that shifts its weight the way you do, and pushes wet hair from a forehead the way you do, and crosses, and completes, and thins into the swell, and rises, eventually, as clean rain over the southern hills.

Kept forever, or released once and completely. That is the whole argument between the basin and the coast, the entire ten-thousand-year conversation, hum and tide, word and word. It is not for you to settle. The world already settled it, in the only way such things are ever settled — by becoming a shape in which the answer is both: a fen that holds everyone who ever stood at its water's edge, and a shore, at the end of the river, where every one of them is finally, gently, let go.
