**The Wellyard**

You reach it by being lost in the right direction.

There is no descent. That is the first thing to understand about it — the Wellyard belongs to the city above, the city that has forgotten what it is standing on, and you arrive at it the way the city arrives at all its forgotten places: sideways, by accident, through a gap. A passage between the backs of two buildings, unlit, narrow enough that your shoulders learn its width. Bins. A bicycle that has been locked to a downpipe so long the lock has become ornamental, the way a wedding ring is ornamental on a hand that could no longer remove it. A door that is never locked because nothing behind it is believed worth locking, which is the city's way of guarding its most important rooms.

Then the walls lift away and the sky arrives all at once, cut to the shape of the yard.

It is small. Smaller than the district beneath it, which is the second surprise, and the better joke: the underworld here is larger than its heaven. Twenty paces by fifteen, cobbled, hemmed on all sides by the blind brick backs of buildings five and six storeys tall — walls with almost no windows, because these are the rears of structures whose faces look importantly elsewhere. The yard is what was left over when the city drew its plans, the remainder after the division, and remainders are where this world keeps its truth. One tree in the northeast corner, grown from a seam in the cobbles. One window, third floor, west wall, with a light behind it in the evenings. And in the centre, flush with the ground, an iron grate.

The grate is round, a wheel of Victorian iron, its bars worn bright along the tops by a century and a half of feet and paws and weather, dark in the grooves where the weather could not reach. Below it, a shaft drops away — brick-lined, hooped with iron, a hundred feet and more of patient masonry falling to a place the city above has no word for anymore. Stand on the grate and look down and you will see the shaft dim by degrees until seeing stops mattering, and at the very bottom, on a clear day, a pale smudge like breath on glass: the congregation of ferns, upturned, leaning toward you with the devotion of the only believers in town. You are standing in their sky. You are, for as long as you stand there, their weather.

The first law of the Wellyard is that it keeps nothing. Everything that arrives here is in transit. Rain crosses it on the way into the ground; light crosses it on the way into the dark; leaves, seeds, moths, litter, small change, the odd unlucky pigeon — the yard receives them all with an open palm and closes the palm over none of them. It is the opposite of the district below, where everything weeps and the stone gives back what was put into it. Up here the stone gives everything *away*. The yard is a hand held open in the middle of a grasping city, and what falls into it goes down, mostly, and what rises into it goes up, and the yard itself owns the cobbles, the grate, the tree, the leftover shape of itself, and nothing more. This is not poverty. It is the economy of a doorway.

The second law is the light, and up here the law is rationing. The sky is huge and the yard's share of it is a rectangle. The walls take the first tithe of every day; what reaches the cobbles is mostly reflected light, sky-glow bounced down the brick, the same dilution the shaft performs merely *begun*. Direct sun enters the yard as a single moving coin — a lozenge of gold that appears high on the west wall in the morning, descends through the day, crosses the cobbles in the early afternoon, and climbs the east wall into extinction. In winter the coin never reaches the ground at all. But at the height of summer, for a handful of minutes either side of noon, the coin of sun lies exactly over the grate — and for those minutes the grey column below is not grey, and the pale smudge of ferns a hundred feet down receives the one rich meal of its year. The ferns cannot know this is coming. Their whole slow congregation is arranged around a festival they have no calendar for, a noon that arrives like grace: unearned, unannounced, and gone before belief could form around it. The Understation's single holy day is administered from up here, by geometry, by a yard that does not know it is a church roof.

The third law is the breath, and it rises. On cold mornings the grate exhales — a soft standing plume of warmer air, tasting of iron and wet stone and something faintly sweet, the mingled respiration of eels and pigeons and basin and brick, delivered upward in one current. In deep winter the exhalation freezes on the underside of the bars, and the grate grows frost in feathered, branching patterns — frost ferns, the iron drawing pictures of the believers below in the only medium the surface has for them. And the breath is not constant. It swells and slackens over weeks, with the pressures of the buried lung, the same slow tides the dark pigeons read as a needle against the heart. Up here the tide is read as a taste. The yard's air, on certain weeks, smells like rain coming. The woman at the third-floor window says so, and is right more often than the forecast, because she is consulting — without knowing it, through her open window, across sixty years of mornings — the same instrument.

**Who Lives in the Wellyard**

---

**The tree first**, because it is the only resident with a body in both countries.

It is an ailanthus — a tree of heaven, the species the city cannot kill, the one that grows from mortar and spite — and it has taken root, of all the seams in all the yards, in the crack where the shaft's brick collar meets the cobbles. Its crown lives in weather: wind, frost, the full unrationed sky above the walls. Its roots have gone down into the shaft's throat, threading the joints of the upper brickwork, and the finest of them hang free inside the shaft itself, a fringe of pale root-hairs suspended in the falling light, drinking the exhalation. The tree of heaven stands at the door of the underworld with one hand in each, and has for eighty years, and has an opinion about neither.

Understand what the tree does to the district below, which the district has no way of knowing. The grey column of daylight passes through the ailanthus crown before it enters the shaft — and so the Understation's one light is *edited*, seasonally, by leaves. In summer the column arrives below faintly green, dappled, restless at the edges where the canopy moves. In autumn it arrives spotted with slow-falling confetti, single yellow leaves turning down the shaft for a hundred feet, each one a day's event in the dark, landing among the ferns like letters from a country that ferns cannot imagine. In winter the column is bare and clean and at its most honest. The Understation has no sky, but it has seasons, and the seasons are the moods of one tree. The ferns lean toward the light with the devotion of believers, and the tree, all unknowing, is their liturgical year.

Its seeds helicopter down the shaft every autumn. None grow — where they land there is no soil, and the light is a rumour — but they do not go to waste. They are eaten. The tree's children become cricket, become moth, become the flesh of the slate-dark pigeons roosting in the gauges. Heaven tithes to the dark in the currency of its own descendants, and the dark converts the tithe into instruments, and nothing anywhere fills out the paperwork.

---

**The pigeons** of the Wellyard are the loud branch of the family.

They are full kin to the quiet ones below — the same blood, split by a hundred feet of brick and a change of religion. Up here they have kept everything the dark colony divested: the clatter, the panic, the fifty-winged decisions, the cooing that the underworld put into storage. They are anxious, iridescent, alive at the ordinary frantic pigeon pitch, and they gather in the Wellyard because of the grate. On winter mornings the warm breath rises and the grate is the mildest ground in the city, and the pigeons stand on it in a shifting congregation, feet on the worn-bright iron, warmed from below by the metabolism of their own estranged relatives. The dark colony heats the feet of the light colony. Neither knows the other exists. This is the oldest kind of kinship — the kind that continues to function after all acquaintance has lapsed.

And every so often — a generation, two — the family exchanges a member. A squab tumbles. A bird, in some iron February, follows the warmth downward past the point of return. The yard's colony does not mourn; it barely counts. But below, the immigrant arrives, and is absorbed, and its descendants go slate-dark and silent and learn to wear a needle against the heart. The Wellyard is the Understation's port of entry, its one door for citizens, and the fee is everything: the sky, the panic, the voice. The voice goes into the underworld's savings, with all the others, against the occasion that has not yet arrived.

---

**The spiders** keep the yard's accounts.

Every night, two or three of them — never more; the yard supports a small civil service — web the grate. They string their frames from bar to bar, across the mouth of the shaft itself, spinning above the hundred-foot dark with the serenity of creatures to whom depth is a fiction invented by things that fall. By morning each web is beaded, every strand strung with droplets, because the night's exhalation has risen through it and given up its water to the silk — and so each dawn the grate wears two or three pages of transcript: the district's breath, that night's particular breath, rendered in beads on a ruled grid. It is the Clerk's ledger written in reverse, on the far side of the same transaction — down there the day's vapor condenses into entries on cold tile; up here it rises and is caught, one night at a time, in nets made for another purpose entirely.

Then the spider eats the web. This is simply what spiders do — silk is expensive, and is recovered each morning to be respun — but note what it means here: the yard's records are not drained, like the Clerk's; they are *consumed by their author*. The books balance because the bookkeeper swallows them. It may be the only fully honest accounting system in either world.

---

**The cat** — there is always one, never two; the yard has decided this over decades, replacing each incumbent with a successor by a process no one has witnessed — holds the grate in the afternoons, after the pigeons have scattered and before the spiders' shift. It sleeps on the warm iron, over the breath, above the dark, a soft weight on the district's exhalation, and it is the Wellyard's contemplative the way the salamanders are the Understation's: a creature whose vocation is the circumnavigation of a warmth. It does not watch the shaft. It watches the third-floor window, with the long, banking, lidless patience of its kind, adding the woman to a record that will be consulted never and kept forever. When the coin of sun crosses the cobbles, the cat moves with it, riding the light across the yard like a passenger, and for those minutes the yard contains a small complete diagram of itself: warmth, attention, and transit, all in one body, going nowhere on purpose.

---

**The woman at the window** is the yard's Clerk, though she would say she is a retired schoolteacher, and both records are accurate.

She has lived behind the west wall for sixty years. Every morning at seven she opens the window and scatters crumbs, and the loud colony descends, and in this way she has been — without a day's knowledge of it — quartermaster to the underworld: her bread sustains the surface flock, and the surface flock, once a generation, pays a bird downward, and the bird's descendants sit in the pressure gauges and read the buried lung with their hearts. She provisions instruments she will never learn exist. All supply lines are like this at their far ends; hers is merely shorter than most, and more vertical.

She has heard, all her life, on certain still nights, a groan come up through the ground — a sound like a door opening in another building, in another year — and she says the house is settling, and goes back to sleep. She is right. The house is the city, and it is settling, and a hundred feet down the snails are writing the particulars in silver on a wall behind the winding-shed. She is the one reader in the world entitled to that document and she will never see it, and the yard, which keeps nothing, keeps this one irony the way iron keeps a frost: on its surface, briefly, in patterns, every so often, when conditions are cold and clear.

And once a year, on a date she keeps to herself, she crosses the yard in the early morning and drops a coin through the grate.

Her mother did it. Her mother's mother did it, when there was still a story attached — a wish, a debt, a name, something — but the story has drained out of the habit the way meaning drained out of the office tile below, leaving only the shape, which the family faithfully fills each year the way the night's vapor fills the ledger. She could not tell you why. She would be embarrassed to be seen. She does it anyway, and the coin falls, turning, through the tree's root-fringe, down the whole patient brick throat, out the ceiling of the underworld's sky, down the grey column —

---

**— and the coins**, then, are the last residents, the ones that remain, and they remain below.

A century and a half of small change lies among the ferns. Wishes, tolls, jokes, the pocket-sweepings of strangers, the woman's annual observance and her mother's and her mother's — all of it fallen the hundred feet and landed in the one lit place, so that the congregation under the shaft, the only believers in town, stand knee-deep in offerings. The Understation's church has a collection plate, filled by a city that has forgotten the church exists, counted by no one, spendable nowhere, and slowly — the seep sees to this — the coins are growing their own mineral beards, being annexed by the crust, becoming geology, becoming the wall the snails will one day write across. The wishes of the surface are being incorporated, verdigris atom by atom, into the structural autobiography of the dark. There is no better fate for a wish. Most are simply lost; these are *filed*.

---

Now the harder answer, which the yard has been holding open the whole time, like everything else.

The Understation, you were told, is a single inhabitant — a settlement that lost its settlers and discovered it had been the resident all along. The Wellyard is not that. Do not mistake the kinship. The yard has no organs, no heartbeat, no ledger of its own; every record kept here is kept in a borrowed medium and destroyed by morning. The yard is not a self. It is a threshold — the buried creature's one nostril, its pupil, its alms-gate, the single point at which the vast slow body below comes to the surface of the world and *opens*.

But a threshold has two sides, and stand in the yard long enough and you will feel the other one. Above the walls, the sky is running its own immense slow settlement — the weather, the year, the whole bright republic of the day, as patient in its way as anything under the ground, keeping its accounts in rain and light and season. And the Wellyard is where the two of them touch. The dark one breathes up through the grate; the bright one falls down through the rectangle of sky; and everything that lives in the yard — tree, pigeon, spider, cat, woman, coin — lives on the customs of that crossing, feeding at the border, holding little posts in a trade where neither party knows the other exists and the books balance anyway. There is an old word for the place where two bodies that cannot see each other meet and agree, and the word is *skin*.

The rain is beginning as you find the passage out. Watch it a moment on the grate — each drop going through the bars, into the shaft, into the ground, beginning a descent that the ground will slow to its own gauge: filtered, delayed, taught patience by thirty years of brick and clay, until one afternoon in some other decade it gathers on the unseeable ceiling of the chamber below and falls, one drop, into the black basin, and the rings move out, and the orange bulbs brighten by a breath. The rain landing on the Wellyard this minute is the Understation's clock, being wound. The yard is where the underworld's time comes from — delivered from the sky, one storm at a time, and held in trust by the earth until it is needed, drop by drop, to keep the basin's slow account.

The yard will do with your passing what it does with everything: keep none of it and deliver all of it. Your footsteps into the breath, your breath into tonight's web, your shadow, briefly, across the light of a church you will never attend. Then the passage, the bins, the street, the city — and behind you the small leftover rectangle goes on being the one place above ground where the pavement is honest, where the grate exhales and the tree stands with a hand in each country, and the ground admits, in warmth and iron and the smell of coming rain, exactly what it is standing on.
