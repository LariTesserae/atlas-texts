**The Half-Drowned City of the Settling Shore**

You will hear it long before you see it. That is the nature of the place — it announces itself the way the fissure conceals itself, and for the same reason turned inside out. Come down out of the mountains, follow the silver road and then the long brown river eight hundred kilometers seaward, and somewhere in the last day of the journey the fog takes you: a warm, thick, salt-grey blindness rising off the estuary, so dense the barge-men steer by ear. And through that blindness, from a great way off, comes the sound. Bells. Hundreds of them, thousands, no two alike, ringing in no order and never stopping — a vast, patient, arrhythmic shimmer of bronze, near ones and far ones, bright ones and ones so deep you feel them in the hull before you hear them. The barge-men do not need charts. They know every bell by voice, and the bells tell them where the water is, because the bells are moored over the streets.

The shore here is sinking. This is the central fact of the city, the law every other law bends around, and it is not a different fact from the one that governs the fissure — it is the same fact, read from the other end. The deep rock that shoulders up through eight kilometers of crust to make the rising crest is fed from below, and what feeds it is swallowed here: the old cold floor of the sea going down under the edge of the land, drawing the coast down after it, centimeters a year, patient as anything mineral. The mountain inhales; the shore is the exhale. The tide-readers of the city have known this for longer than the funicular has run. They put it simply, the way you'd state the weather: *what the sea pays here, the sky is paid there.* They have never seen the crest. They know its rate of rising to the centimeter, because it is their rate of sinking, carried off.

So the city drowns in order, street by street, and has been drowning for as long as it has existed, and was built knowing it. It stands — the living part of it — on a long slope above the estuary: quays and warehouses and bell-towers and tall narrow houses with steep copper roofs gone green, everything climbing gently uphill, everything with its back to the water and its face to the water, because the water is on both sides of its history. Below the current waterline, visible at low tide as a geometry of roof-ridges and chimney-stumps breaking the surface, and below that as shadows, and below that only as bells, lies the rest of it: the drowned quarters, forty generations of city, avenues and market squares and stairways descending into green dark, colonized now by oysters until the carved facades have doubled their ornament, patrolled by eels that have inherited the addresses. Cormorants dry their wings on submerged gable-ends. Seals haul out and sleep on rooftops that clear the water by a handsbreadth, in the middle of what was once a famous street.

And over every drowned house, a bell. This is the custom that makes the harbor sound the way it sounds. When a house is surrendered to the tide — and every house is surrendered eventually; the ceremony is old and unhurried, the family carrying out the last dry things in procession while the ward sings the house's history at full voice — a bell is moored above it on a chain, floated on the swell, tuned to that household's stroke. The bell rings whenever the water moves, which is always. So the bell-field is not decoration and not navigation, though it serves as both. It is the city's memory, made audible. Every wave that passes through the harbor makes the drowned quarters speak their own names, all night, all year, in bronze. An elder will row you through it for hours, resting the oars beside each bell in turn: *this was the dyers' court. This was my grandmother's kitchen. Listen — it still rings a little flat, like her.*

The bronze itself carries the mountain in it. This is the first of the threads, and the Shorefolk will tell you about it before you ask, because they are a telling people: the tide bells are cast with silver in the alloy, a small precise weight of it per bell, because silver sweetens the tone — and the silver comes down the river on the same barges that go back up loaded, down from the funicular's foot, out of the smelters in a fissure they have never seen, pulled from the youngest rock in the world by small dense grey-orange people who speak in light. The Shorefolk find this arrangement deeply satisfying and slightly hilarious. The mountain is rising and shedding silver; the shore is sinking and ringing with it. *They send us their mountain,* the bell-founders say, *and we make it grieve properly.*

What goes back up the river is water. Here is the second thread, and the strangest, because this is a city that could not be more careless with water — rain half the year, fog the other half, the river brown and enormous, the sea in the parlor. Water here is not wealth; it is weather, it is landlord, it is the slow patient creditor of every family in the city. Their gold is dryness. A sealed dry room, high in a house, where paper can live and salt can't reach — that is treasure here; a dry blanket is tenderness; the archives of the city are sung rather than written because ink drowns and voices don't. And yet in the middle of the oldest drowned quarter, in what was the first market square — under six meters of green harbor now — there is a spring. It rose there before the city did; the city was founded on it; and it has declined to notice the sea. Cold, mineral, stubbornly sweet, it comes up through the drowned square under its own deep pressure, and the founders long ago capped it with a dome and a standing well-tower, so that its head still clears the water, a pale stone lantern out in the bell-field with fresh water rising through the middle of the salt. The Shorefolk row out to it. They fill copper canisters at it — copper their own divers strip, sheet by verdigrised sheet, from the roofs of the drowned quarters, beaten by their own smiths into sealed vessels — and they weigh each canister to the gram, and the canisters go up the river, and up the silver road, and up the cable, into the thin white air, to a city with no water in it at all. The spring's water is the only water that will keep sweet for the weeks of the journey; every other water goes foul in sealed copper, and this one, for its own mineral reasons, does not. The Shorefolk drink from the spring only at weddings. The rest of it goes to the mountain. They find this satisfying too, and do not find it strange, that their drowning city's oldest voice is the sacrament of people eight kilometers up: *the sea is taking the square,* they say, *but the square is not finished talking.*

Even the birds run between the two. In autumn, when the high passes turn murderous, the crag-swifts come down — the same sooty hook-winged birds that boil out of the sky-crack, arriving over the estuary in silent dark rivers to spend the cold months hawking insects above the marshes, roosting by the thousand in the bell-towers and under the quays. The Shorefolk call them *mountain-soot* and read the calendar by them, exactly as the Crestfolk do, from the other end: the day the swifts arrive angry and early, the tide-readers check the winter moorings, because a bird that flees the crest in a hurry has been told something by the weather. Two cities that have never seen each other, sharing a clock with wings.

**The Shorefolk**

You will mistake the first one you hear for a bell. That is not an insult to your ears; it is the whole design of the voice. They are big people, long-limbed and buoyant, with a chest like a cask and a larynx like a founding, and when one of them speaks at full carrying volume across the water — which is their ordinary register, their walking-around voice — the sound arrives in your sternum before it arrives in your understanding. Generations of fog have built them the way the fog built the city: for sound. Their ears are the most articulate part of them. A Shorefolk child standing blind in a whiteout can place a bell, a gull, a dripping oar, a lie, to within a degree or two of arc, and their language of horns and struck bronze crosses the whole harbor the way lamp-flash crosses the fissure — a public tongue thrown across open space, with registers: the broad slow strokes of tide-signal, the rapid bright clatter of gossip between moored boats, an oratorical mode, vast rolling changes rung on the great tower bells, used for the surrender of houses and for the dead.

Their bodies belong half to the water. The skin is the color of the place — fog-grey with an underglow of verdigris green, pale as the inside of an oyster where the sun never reaches — and it is dense, smooth, faintly oiled, sheeting water off rather than drinking it, over a quiet layer of fat that lets them work the cold harbor for hours. They are divers before they are anything else. A Shorefolk's heart is a slow patient engine that slows further when the face goes under; the lungs are enormous, but where the Crestfolk lung is a bellows built to labor at nothing, the Shorefolk lung is a held breath built to last — the salvage divers of the deep quarters work four and five minutes at a stretch in the green dark, stripping copper, raising doorknobs and hinges and church-plate from streets their great-grandmothers walked dry. Their eyes are weak things by mountain standards, fog-blunted, but underwater something in them tightens and clears, and a diver in the drowned market can read the carving on a lintel in light that would be blackness to you. They see best, in other words, exactly where their voices are useless — and so underwater, and only there and in a few other places, they speak with their hands.

That is the shape of intimacy here, and it is the exact inverse of the mountain's. The public voice is thunder; the private tongue is silent. Divers developed the hand-speech out of necessity, a full grammar of finger and wrist and touch, and it long ago overflowed its trade and became the language of everything the great voice is too big for: lovers sign to each other across a crowded quay; a parent's hand against a child's spine spells comfort during a storm; the words said to someone whose hands you are holding are said *into* the hands. To sign at a stranger from a distance is, in this city, a startling forwardness — you have offered them the bedroom tongue in the street. Down at the funicular's foot, where the two trades meet, the misunderstandings are famous and now traditional: the Crestfolk, whose whole public life is silent signal, flashing and gesturing companionably at Shorefolk bargemen who blush to the ears; the Shorefolk calling out warm full-voiced greetings that strike the Crestfolk like a stranger's mouth against their hair. Each people's decency is the other's scandal. They have settled, over generations, on the one language neither owns: the scale. Grams of silver, grams of water, the beam and the weights, the slow nod. The traders say the scale is the only honest sentence at the Weighing Yard, and both peoples find that not cynical but rather beautiful, which tells you something about both.

Their names are bell-strokes. Each ward — the set of households sharing a bell — keeps its bell's voice as a surname, and a child's given name is a rhythm struck upon it, rung at birth from the ward tower so the whole harbor hears the city grow by one. The hand-name, signed, is derived from the stroke; the spoken name is a translation kept loose for foreigners, worn the way the Crestfolk wear theirs, like borrowed clothes — and the funicular traders will tell you that a Shorefolk name and a Crestfolk name, one struck and one plucked, are nearer to each other than either is to anything you could write down.

They are made, as a people, almost entirely of mourning, and they are cheerful about it, and they do not find this a contradiction. Where the Crestfolk grieve nothing and forget nothing, the Shorefolk grieve everything, on schedule, out loud, as an art form — the surrender of a house is sung for three days; there are professional lamenters whose voices are civic treasures; the great annual Ringing of the Field, when every bell in the harbor is sounded by hand in the order the streets went under, takes from one noon to the next and is the closest thing they have to a holiday. A child comes of age with a salvage dive into their own family's drowned street — down into the green, into the house where their grandparents were young, to bring up one thing: a hinge, a cup, a doorknob. And every Shorefolk elder wears a long cord strung with keys. Keys to drowned doors, dozens of them, each one a room that existed and was theirs and is under the harbor now, still ringing. Set that cord beside the rivet-cords of the fissure and you have the two cities in your two hands: rooms unbuilt and rooms drowned, kept metal from kept lives, the same instinct answering the opposite verb. The elders here will tell you a key-cord's whole story if you have a free evening and a rested ear, and unlike the Crestfolk they will weep freely through the telling, and enjoy it, and expect you to.

Their silence is their treasure, and so their silence is what they give the dead. In a city where sound is common as water and hoarded like nothing at all, the one extravagance is to stop. At a funeral the whole ward falls still — bells muffled with leather, boats resting oars, even the working divers holding to their lines — and into that impossible hush the dead person's name-stroke is rung once, alone, and allowed to die away completely before the world is permitted to resume. It is the precise mirror of the tears the Crestfolk let fall on their wrapped dead: the costliest thing, spent on purpose. And then the dead go down. Weighted, wrapped, they are carried by divers into the drowned quarter and laid in the old family house, in the room the elders can still name, behind the door whose key is on somebody's cord. The sea takes them into keeping. The phrase rung at a laying-down translates, roughly: *handed down.*

They believe — and this is not a hope but a doctrine, taught by the tide-readers with their lead lines and their centuries of soundings — that the keeping is not the end of the journey. The sea presses what it holds. Sediment becomes stone; the shore that goes down goes down into the world's slow furnace and is not destroyed there but *filed*, and in an age beyond ages it will be shoved up somewhere as new rock, tilted nearly vertical, the youngest bone of some later mountain. They know this is true because they have the proof in their hands, bought back down the silver road: the Crestfolk smelters, cracking the bone-white stone at eight kilometers, keep finding seashells in it. Spiral shells, perfect, pressed in rock that has never in its risen life seen water — curiosities to the fissure, sent down the cable with the ingots. The Shorefolk pay silver-weight for them. They set them into the crowns of new bells and into the walls of the spring-tower, and the tide-readers hold them up before the children in the fog and say: *the crest is a shore that was kept. The mountain is somebody's old sea, carried up. Now you know where you are going.*

So they hold one belief about the future, told in the great rolling oratorical changes on the nights when the spring tides run highest. Someday — not soon, not in any cord-of-keys lifetime — the last street will go under, the last roof will be surrendered, and there will be no city, because there will be no shore; only the bell-field entire, ringing over everything, and the people a fleet upon their own address. They do not tell this as doom either. Everything they have ever kept was kept by giving it to the water; they are, they reason, a cargo themselves, in transit, lading for a mountain that has not been born — and the sea is not the loss of the city but the vessel it ships in. Their longest fixed expression, rung on the festival nights across the whole dark harbor, takes a full minute to sound, and the barge-men who work the river's length — the only people alive who regularly hear both cities speak — swear that if you set it beside the long glowing lamp-sentence that comes down the mountain cable on those same nights, and translate them side by side, they are one sentence, said in opposite directions:

*The shore goes down. The city sings. And one morning the streets will be the whole sea, and whoever is here will simply cast off — and be carried, in the keeping of the deep rock, up.*

Until then they are here — booming their bell-voiced greetings through the fog, signing softly into each other's hands, diving on their long slow heartbeats into the green rooms of their grandmothers, weighing out sweet water for a mountain they will never see and are, in the oldest sense, on their way to becoming. Above the line for now. Afloat for now.
