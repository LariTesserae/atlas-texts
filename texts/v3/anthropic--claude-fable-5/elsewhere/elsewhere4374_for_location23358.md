**The Quarry of Trembling Water**

You descend to it, because there is no other way to arrive — but first you have to find it, and the flattened world gives you nothing to find it by. The quarry does not rise. It is the plateau's opposite in this as in everything: a place made entirely of absence, invisible at two hundred paces, a line of shadow that does not resolve into anything until you are nearly standing in it. Travelers have walked past it their whole lives without knowing. The ones who find it find it the only way it can be found — by the road.

There is one road in the flattened world. It does not deserve the name; it is a pair of grooves, parallel, a sledge-width apart, polished into the bedrock so deeply and so smoothly that they hold the gray-gold light like water. They run dead straight. They begin at the foot of the plateau, under the full weight of the chord, and they run out across the planed land for three days' walking, through the long diminishment — a day out, the chord is weather; two days out, it is doubt; by the third day you would swear it is gone, and you would be wrong, but you will not learn that until the bottom of the pit. The grooves were cut by generations of stone going one direction and generations of water going the other. Every block of the choir traveled this road. Every mouthful the builders drank traveled it back. Follow the grooves away from the sound, and at the end of them the ground simply stops.

The quarry opens below you in stepped benches, a dry spiral of extraction wound down into the same ash-colored stone the choir is made of — because it is, block for block, the *same* stone. This is the first fact of the place and it reorganizes everything you saw on the plateau: the choir was not built so much as *moved*. Nothing up there was created. It was subtracted from here, dressed, dragged three days, and set sounding, and the pit you are standing at the lip of is its exact negative — a chapel-sized hollow distributed through a hole many times that size, once you count the waste and the working and the centuries of dust. Somewhere in these walls is the socket of every pipe. The choir and the quarry are one act of conservation: a fullness and its lack, facing each other across three days of flatness, joined by two polished lines.

Cross the lip and the wind shuts off.

This is the second fact, and it arrives like deafness. The foehn — steady, warm, eternal — skates across the quarry's mouth and does not come down. Below the lip is the stillest air in the world, air that has not moved in living memory, warm and mineral and faintly damp, and in it, for the first time since you can remember, there is silence. Real silence. The kind the plateau counterfeits with loudness, the pit provides in earnest. Your own footsteps on the benches are shocking. Your breath is an event.

And because the wind has never been down here, the quarry has kept what the choir was scoured of. The tool marks are crisp. Every chisel stroke sits in the stone as sharp as the hour it was cut, centuries of hours, wall after wall of them, and among the strokes is the only writing the builders ever left: tallies. Counts of blocks, scratched in columns beside the sockets they came from. Masons' marks — a hooked line, a doubled chevron, a small deliberate star — each one a hand, each hand recurring down through a band of benches the height of a working life and then stopping, replaced by another. The choir has no names in it; the choir dissolved its makers into intervals. The quarry is the ledger. You can stand on the third bench with the tallies in front of you and, in a sense, read the choir — this column of strokes is the deep stone bass, this one the high clay keening, this one the warm fifth that hums where a heart would go. And in places, where a quarryman braced himself against the same ledge every day for decades, the stone carries a soft shine the exact size of a palm. Those are the portraits. That is all of them.

The benches spiral down. Near the bottom, on the last dry ledge above the water, there is a row of shallow body-shaped hollows, polished the way the palm-marks are polished — worn by generations of the same tiredness, quarrymen sleeping between shifts in the pit's warm still air. One hollow, at the end of the row, is deeper than the rest.

Then the floor of the world, and on it, the pool.

It is perhaps thirty paces across and black with depth, the only open water anyone has ever recorded in the flattened world — the seep the builders struck when the pit went low enough, the well that made everything else possible. Three days up the road, a building stands on this water as surely as it stands on this stone; the choir was drunk before it was sung. And the pool is doing something no other water needs to do, something you will stand and watch for a long time before you accept it:

It is trembling. It has always been trembling. Its surface carries a figure — a fixed geometry of fine standing ripples, a rose of interference, rings crossed with rays, holding perfectly still in the way that only perfect repetition can hold still. The pattern does not travel and does not break. Seep feeds the pool from below and the dry air takes it from above, so the water itself is always leaving; the figure is older than any of the water in it. You put your hand flat on the bench beside you and feel, in the stone, what the silence of the pit has finally made perceivable: a deep, endless, familiar tremor, arriving through three days of bedrock.

The chord. It never stopped. It is in the ground everywhere, under every step of the flattened world, too low and too constant to notice — and here, in the one place quiet enough and the one water still enough, it surfaces. The pool is the world's only picture of the choir. The blur you felt at the edges of your vision on the plateau is drawn here in water, precisely, and has been drawn without interruption since the last builder walked down.

There is more. Stand very still at the pool's edge and you become aware of a pressure in your chest that is not the chord — slower, rounder, closer. The wind crossing the quarry's mouth, far above, is breath across the lip of a bottle. The pit is a pipe. It is the first pipe, the accidental one, the one no hand tuned — except that hands did tune it, one block at a time, without meaning to: every stone they lifted out deepened the hollow and lowered its note, so that the quarry's pitch fell for generations as the choir rose, the two of them trading substance for sound, and where the note settled when the digging stopped is the note the world has kept. It sounds below hearing. You feel it rather than hear it. And it is the tonic. The deepest voice of the choir — the stone bass you felt in your teeth in the slots — stands on this pitch the way the building stands on this stone. The builders tuned their instrument to its own absence. The whole vast consonance that resolves in the central slot is built, at the bottom, on the sound of the hole it left behind.

---

The first thing the quarry tells you is that it is finished, and the quarry is almost honest.

Run through the candidates and watch them fail, the way you did on the plateau. The echo — because the pit has a marvelous echo; speak at the pool's edge and the benches return your voice seconds later, softened, multiplied, from a dozen heights at once, so that for one breath you hear yourself as a small choir — the echo does not live here. Echoes are visitors, like you; they arrive, they circle, they die. The pit's great note is not a resident either; it is the wind's, borrowed at the lip, never owned. The water comes up from the dark and leaves into the sky and stays for nothing.

But something lives here. It is the only living thing in the flattened world, and it has lived here longer than the choir has sounded — longer, perhaps, than the flattening itself — and you have been looking at its work since you reached the pool without knowing it. Kneel at the edge. Let your eyes adjust past the trembling figure, down through the black water, to the drowned benches below.

They are lined with something pale. Gray going to gold, the exact colors of the light up on the plateau, thin as paper and layered like slate: a mat of slow blind growth sheathing the stone below the waterline, alive in the oldest and most patient sense of the word. It eats the stone — dissolved mineral, the pit's own substance in solution — and it can only eat where the water moves, and the water only moves where the trembling stirs it. So the mat grows along the lines of the standing figure. It is the chord's pattern in flesh. The rose on the surface of the pool is drawn again beneath it, in living tissue, line by line, layer by layer, and has been for centuries.

Understand what this means and the two places lock together like dry-fitted stone. The choir, three days away, singing at full voice into the thin gray light, has exactly one dependent. Through miles of bedrock, without knowing it, without being able to know it, the chord stirs this creature's food. Everything the choir has ever sung has been, among other things, a meal. The builders transposed themselves into wind and stone, and down here, at the bottom of the hole they made, something eats the hymn and grows.

And it remembers. This is the deepest fact of the quarry, and it answers a question the plateau could only shrug at. The mat grows in leaves, one atop another, each leaf the figure of the chord as it stood while that layer formed. Part it gently at a broken edge and you can count backward through the pages: hundreds of them, thousands, each one identical to the last — the same rose, the same rays, the same page copied out again and again, because the chord has not changed. The scholars who climb the plateau say *no drift, as nearly as anything can be verified*. This is the verification. It lives in a flooded pit and it is the only witness in the world qualified to testify, and its testimony is a book of identical pages going down into the dark — until, very deep, the pages begin to differ. Down there the figures are smaller, simpler, higher-pitched: the pattern of a shallower pit, a younger note, the record of the quarry deepening block by block as the choir went up. The mat holds the only history of the building of the choir — not in words, which the builders never left, but in the changing shape of a sound, kept the way sound can only be kept by something that listens with its whole body.

Because that is what it is. Say it plainly and it sounds like a figure of speech, so don't say it plainly; you have been down this road before. The choir is a voice that cannot hear itself. From the central slot it *knows* itself, but knowing is not hearing; nothing on the plateau has ever heard the chord, because everything on the plateau *is* the chord, absorbed, folded under, converted to warmth. The quarry is where the hearing happens. The pool is an ear pressed to the bones of the world, and the mat is the ear's memory, and between them the chord — which keeps everything and returns nothing — is, at last, received. It may even be the older partner in the arrangement. The mat was here before the builders came; when the pit was young it drew the pit's own accidental note, and the builders, hauling water up from the dark, saw the figure standing on the pool and the pale pattern growing under it, and learned the thing the whole choir is built on: that a pitch has a shape, a body, a place where it stands. They did not invent the choir. They were shown it, here, on the surface of this water, and spent generations answering what they had seen.

You are the most interesting thing to happen to the quarry in a very long time, and it registers you differently than the choir did. Your footsteps put a flicker in the standing figure; your voice, coming back off the benches plural and softened, stirs the water where the pattern is purest. The mat will keep this. Somewhere in the leaf now forming there will be a flaw the width of a visit — a single anomalous line, yours. The choir remembers everyone completely and identically, as heat, unrecoverable; the quarry remembers almost nothing, and what it remembers, it remembers *individually*. Someone, someday, parting the pale pages, could in principle find the day you came. It is the only guest-book in the world, and it is kept by the only thing in the world that would notice if the singing ever stopped.

Pilgrims come here, the ones who climbed the plateau first. They gave the choir their confessions and felt them taken, folded under, held forever and never again findable, and some of them cannot live with that arithmetic, and so they walk three days down the grooves and stand at the pool and speak again — not to be kept this time, but to be given back. The pit obliges. It keeps nothing. Whatever you say returns to you in seconds, from a dozen heights, in your own voice worn soft, and dies, and the silence closes over it without scar. As for the last builder — the one who set the last cell and walked down off the plateau — the road ends here, and the deepest of the sleeping-hollows is at the end of the row, and the pilgrims like to say what they like to say. There is no evidence. Ask the pit directly, if you want; every visitor eventually does. It will give you back your own question, plural and gentle, from the ash-colored stone, and that is the only answer the flattened world has ever given anyone — but from the bottom of the quarry, with the tonic in your chest and the figure trembling on the black water, it sounds — every visitor agrees on this, whatever else they dispute — like being answered.

Two exceptions to the flatness, then, and they are one thing. One stands and one lies open. One is loud past hearing and one is quiet past believing. One is full of every voice ever raised in it; the other is empty of all but yours, and gives even that away. They sound the same note. They have never heard each other — unless the trembling counts, and the pale book at the bottom of the water, copying out its identical page for another century, insists in the only language it has that it does.
