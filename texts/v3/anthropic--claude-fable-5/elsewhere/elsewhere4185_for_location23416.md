# The Warm Black

There are horizons in this city, but they are indoors, and they are owned.

Approach across the water — there is no other approach, and the water, as will be seen, barely counts as one — and the Warm Black presents itself the way a wall presents itself: all at once, at full size, with no preamble of distance. The city does not grow on the eye as you near it, because there is nothing between you and it in which growing could occur. It stands up out of the black Reach in a single mass, seamed everywhere with lamplight, steaming faintly at its million joints, and it is exactly as loud from a mile out as it is from the foot of its stairs, because on the Reach, sound does not travel. It is simply present, everywhere, at its full volume, like the city itself.

It is dark here — profoundly, mineral dark. The dark gives no cold. It is warm the way held coins are warm, the way a crowded room is warm, persistent and total, and it is wet: a fine warm rain falls out of the upper black at intervals that follow no season, tasting faintly of tin, and everything in the city glistens, drips, swells, rots, and is mended, and rots again. There is a sky, by report. Nothing depends on it.

Anyone raised in the deep interior, coming up into the streets for the first time, notices the same two things in the same order. First, the shadows — honest, plentiful, wet-looking, pooled in every doorway, the ordinary currency of a lamplit place. Second, and this is the one that stays with them: the streets are *long*. There is room here. There is *between*. In the Warm Black, and almost nowhere else in the world, you can stand apart from a thing and look at it across an interval that belongs to no one else, and this is not a property of the world.

It is a purchase. Every meter of it was grown somewhere else.

## The Reach

The water the city stands in is warm, black, and shoreless in effect if not in fact. Ships put out onto it and row all day and remain under the same lamps, in the same full sound of the same streets, because outside the city's paid-for distances the world reverts to its natural condition, and the natural condition of this world is *near*. The far shore of the Reach may be a few meters away and unreachable. It may not exist. The Reach declines to answer, and the fishers stopped asking generations ago.

They fish it anyway, and the nets, hauled up at any point on the water, on any day, bring in the same catch — not a similar fish. The *same* fish, identifiable by the same notched fin and the same old hook-scar, taken simultaneously in every net on the Reach. The fishers call it Old Same, and throw it back, and it is always already back. They no longer eat it, on the reasoning — sound, as far as it goes — that everyone would be eating it.

This is the world without the trade: one place, worn smooth, repeating. The city is an island of installed distance in an ocean of adjacency, and it knows this the way a lamp knows about oil.

## The Old Close

Before the first case arrived, the city was one room.

The room is still there, at the heart of everything — the Old Close, entered by some thousands of doors from some thousands of streets, all of which open into the same interior. Inside, the founding population's descendants live as the whole city once lived: occupying the same space in polite disagreement, each family's hearth burning in the identical spot as every other family's hearth, interpenetrating without intersection, crowded beyond any census and touching nothing. The Folded, they are called, and they consider the unfolded city extravagant to the point of indecency — all that purchased emptiness, all that *between*, kept up at ruinous expense for the privilege of being apart from one's neighbors.

In the Old Close, speech does not travel; it arrives. A voice there reaches every ear without crossing anything, immediate and interior, as if remembered rather than heard, and so the Folded speak rarely, softly, and with great care. They are the quietest people in the loudest city in the world, and they hold, as a point of doctrine, that distance was a mistake — that the first case should have been thrown into the Reach unopened.

They may be right. Every distance in the city, traced back through the chains of its laying, anchors ultimately to the Old Close, because when the first spans were stretched there was nothing else fixed to anchor them to. The whole city is a fan spread open from a single point. Fold it, and it would all go back in the one room.

## The Quay

The cases arrive at the Quay.

The Quay is a customs hall of black timber built around a standing fold — a region, a few meters across, where the geometry of the world is compiled wrong. Inside the fold, the hall's own beams pass through one another without intersection; surfaces there show both their faces at once; the air near it carries a sustained low pressure felt in the sternum rather than heard. The fold predates the hall, predates the city's records, and is the only aperture the trade has ever used or, apparently, needed.

An hour before a delivery, the fold creaks — one long settling groan, like a house the size of a country tightening its joints — and the customs officers put down their tea. Then the cases come through: sliding out of the fold's disagreement in disciplined ranks, each the size of a footlocker, ribbed, fluted, sealed in a green-black resin smooth as solder. Broach one and the smell of the far country comes off it — crushed fennel, warm electronics, the inside of a seed — a smell no one in the city has ever encountered at its source and every stevedore on the Quay can identify blindfolded.

Deliveries thicken twice a year into the Glut. And once or twice a year, on no schedule anyone has fixed, the fold *sings*: a dry, ticking, interleaved round, like rain on paper, like a roomful of clocks coming gradually to agreement, the phrases tightening hour by hour toward coincidence — and at the moment they coincide the singing is over, and the tide that follows is the largest of the year. The customs officers stand still for the song, all of them, every time. Asked why, they cannot say. It is not reverence, exactly. It is the feeling of being read aloud from a list.

What goes back through the fold is dust — but the dust has its own district, and its own section, below.

No invoice has ever come through. No terms have ever been stated. The trade has no counterparty that anyone has met; it has only a schedule, and the schedule is enforced the way everything about the trade is enforced: gently, administratively, without complaint or notice. Twice in the city's history the returns lapsed. Both times the deliveries thinned — not stopped, *thinned*, a schedule revising itself — until the dust went back, whereupon the tide resumed as if the ledger had merely been waiting for a correction it knew would come.

## The Laying

Distance comes out of the case like wire off a spool, like line paying out, and the laying of it is the city's great craft.

The spanwrights broach a case in a prepared street-bed and draw the distance out along it, and the drawing must be even — stretched at a constant rate, anchored at both ends to older distance, never hurried. Hurry it, let it bunch, and it *snags*: the geometry piles into itself and sets wrong, and the street is left with a region a few meters across where the world's grammar has been misfiled — vines of gaslight pipe passing through walls in polite disagreement, corners showing both their faces, the deep pressure-tone in the sternum. In the far country, by every inference, such regions are workshops. Here they are wounds. Children dare each other into them. Things live in them, which is a matter for a later section, and the spanwrights' guild-law on the subject of snags runs to three hundred clauses, the first of which is *do not make them* and the last of which is *do not assume they were made by you*.

A new-laid street is the finest thing money buys in the Warm Black, and it announces itself by two absences. It is *silent* — it arrives still packed in the far country's hush, and for its first months no footstep on it makes a sound. And it *glows*: a faint white-green issuance from the paving itself, cool, sourceless, giving no heat, fading over a season like the smell of fennel off a broached case. The rich have their streets relaid constantly, and so live always in quiet and pale light, and this is how wealth reads in this city — not gold, but hush and glow. The dark and the din are what everyone can afford.

## The Spending

Because distance wears.

It is spent by use — fastest by walking, slower by hauling, slowest of all by looking, but looking spends it too — and what it releases as it wears is precisely what was withheld from it at its making. The far country, wherever the trade papers' guesses put it, is by every inference silent and cool and blindingly lit: its product arrives finished in that silence and that coolness, and gives them up, molecule by molecule, as it is used. The sound of every footstep ever taken on a street is deferred, banked, and paid out through the years of its wearing. The heat that its light declined to give is bound into the spans and released as they thin.

So the old quarters roar. Not with present traffic — with *history* arriving: centuries of spent footfall paying out of the paving in a sustained composite thunder, the acoustic ledger of the street closing itself out. And the old quarters are warm, warmer than bodies and hearths account for, warm with the withheld heat of a continent of cold light. The city is heated, quite literally, by the far country's restraint. Its warmth is imported, delayed, and paid on use, like everything else here.

And distance wears *thin* before it wears out, and thin distance is translucent — not to light, to origin. A street in its last years greens at the far end: a faint white-green brightening, sourceless, arriving before anything green exists to shed it, growing plainer as the spans wear down, as if the fabric had gone threadbare and the loom were showing through. Everyone in the city knows what a greening street means, and what comes for it.

## The Prospect

One distance in the Warm Black is public.

The Prospect is a covered gallery, old beyond the records, in which the city maintains — at civic expense, against continuous wear — a single long look: a genuinely, commonly *far* thing, the only far thing most of the city's people will ever have a right to. You may not walk it. You may stand at its rail with everyone else and look down its length at a far end that belongs to no one, and people do, in their thousands, daily, in silence, because it turns out that looking at something far away is a need, like salt, that the body reports only when it is met.

Looking spends it. Slowly — but the Prospect has been looked at for a very long time, and the far end is measurably nearer than it was, decade over decade, and it has begun to green. The brightening at its terminus is now plain enough to read by. The Assay's position is that this is ordinary wear. The popular position, held wordlessly by everyone at the rail, is that the city's holiest distance is wearing through to wherever distance comes from, and that they are standing in the one place in the world from which the far country is, faintly, visible — and that this is why the silence at the rail has, in recent years, changed character. It is no longer the silence of looking at something far away. It is the silence of being possibly looked back at.

## The Mills

When a street is done, the Mills come for it.

The decommissioning is called the rolling, and it is what it sounds like: the spanwrights unanchor the spent distance at one end and take it up like carpet, and the buildings along it, unmoored from their intervals, drift gently together into polite disagreement — folded, Old Close–fashion, until new distance can be laid beneath them. A rolled street is milled, and the Mills are the loudest place in the city, past bearing, past description, because milling spends a street's every remaining deferred sound at once; the millers are deaf by custom and proud of it, and communicate in a sign-language whose word for *money* is the same as its word for *noise*.

What the Mills produce is a fine pale dust. It is cased, sealed, and returned through the fold — and here the trade shows its one strange symmetry, which the Assay has weighed for a century without wearing it out: *a case of spent distance outweighs a case of new, grain for grain.* The scales are good scales. The difference is eleven parts in ten thousand, it is consistent, and it has no explanation on any side of any theory. The millers say the difference is the use. The liturgy says the rent is memory. Somewhere on the far side of the fold, the popular imagination holds, the dust is received and read and *filed* — drawn down and shelved in deep patient strata by whatever keeps the far country's books — and the city's every walked day is in the archive, sorted by a property no instrument registers, and this is what the trade was always for. Not the distance. The dust. The distance is just the loan that ripens it.

One law of the rolling is absolute and old: *clear the street.* Whatever stands on a distance when it is taken up goes with it. The city's mothers own a curse for children who linger where they shouldn't — *green take you* — and the city's ghost-lore owns an afterlife for those it has taken: they are not dead, the lore insists, only shipped; packed whole in glass in the far country, cool and perfect, filed by the roots, sleeping in the founding layers, waiting for their street to be regrown under them. It is told as comfort. It does not entirely work as comfort. Somewhere in the telling there is always a pause at the word *waiting*.

## A Note on the Word

"Lives" needs its own instrument here too, and it is not the far country's. There, by report of no one, a thing lives if it can be in the wrong place. In the Warm Black the wrong place is the cheapest thing there is; everything is in everything's wrong place; the Old Close is nothing else. What is scarce here is *apartness*. The city's convention, adopted out of the same exhaustion: a thing lives in the Warm Black if it can keep a distance — if there can be an interval between it and the world that the thing maintains at its own expense.

By that standard the buildings do not live, the crowds barely live, and the Folded are a dispute three centuries old. The census of what remains is short, strange, and ends — as everything here ends — with something that keeps its distance perfectly.

## The Quiets

The quiets are the only such creature a citizen will reliably meet, and "reliably" means once or twice a week, a single animal, pressed to a roaring wall in an old quarter, eating.

The body is the size of a spread hand, low-slung, the verdigris grey-green of weathered copper, finished in a dust-soft pile. The head is mostly eyes, large, lidless, matte black. Beneath the eyes is a flat ventral plate of overlapping translucent scales, and the animal applies this plate to the loud stone, and the noise, where the animal sits, *stops*. It leaves behind it a patch of true silence — honest, sourced, correctly accounted silence, the only free silence the city contains — which heals over in minutes as the wall's deferred sound resumes around it. Its droppings, if the term applies, are small, dry, and pale, indistinguishable from mill-dust, and are swept up with the mill-dust, and go back through the fold with everything else.

They will not feed on new-laid streets; they wait, apparently, for a distance to ripen. They will not feed during the fold's singing; a quiet grazing when the song begins lifts its plate and waits, eyes on nothing, until the phrases coincide. They do not visibly breed. The population holds steady anyway: quiets are simply *found*, fully grown, walking calmly out of snags — out of the wounds in the streets' grammar, where things live that show all their surfaces at once and are a color one degree past naming, and which, in the walking-out, close up and settle and resolve, every documented time, into a quiet.

Every quiet ever handled in the Warm Black is left-handed. Every one, in all the records, without exception. The city has never found this remarkable and has never looked for a reason, and perhaps that is best, because the reason has the shape of a ledger kept elsewhere: somewhere, the right-handed half of the census is maintained, and when a quiet dies under a cart in the Warm Black, somewhere cool and bright and silent, within days, a correction is made.

## The Assay

There are, at any given time, between three and seven of them, in a loft above the customs hall, and some have been there upward of twenty years, weighing the dust and logging the tides and dismissing, every morning, the same flag.

The Quay has been adopting them for a long time. The loft's stairs have worn — or been maintained — into treads that suit their gaits; a shelf stands at exactly the height where one of them once habitually set a scale-case. None of this was requested. And the Assay's people are changed in expected ways and in two that are not. They cannot sleep in quiet rooms; silence, after a season at the Quay, sounds *expensive* to them, like something about to be collected. Their sense of nearness comes loose: an Assayer home on leave will feel the far side of their own kitchen as a journey, will provision for it, and describe this without distress. Nearness, they say, starts to feel like a courtesy that someone, somewhere, is extending, and once you have felt that, you can never again feel it as a fact.

The second thing is filed in their own ledgers with what reads as embarrassment. The Assay's people are *cold*. Everything in the Warm Black is warm; the Assayers' bodies, their bedding, the hollows they leave in their cots, are the coolest things in the city — and a moving or resting region of anomalous cold is, of course, the diagnostic sign of the Stoker. The loft's thermographs, set generations ago to flag Stoker-sign, flag the sleeping Assayers every morning. The flags are dismissed every morning. The senior Assayer, who has dismissed more of them than anyone alive, was once asked whether this was a calibration nuisance or a classification, and dismissed the question.

## The Stoker

Last, because it is always last here too.

The evidence, complete: on certain nights — clustered after the Glut, but not bound to it — a point of anomalous temperature moves through the city at a walking pace, threading the densest quarters, covering the whole of the Warm Black before morning. Early in the night the sign is heat: thresholds hot to the touch, long shallow depressions pressed into landings and mill-yards, scorching. By night's end the sign is cold: chilled doorframes, and — in a city where nothing has ever frozen — frost, small perfect panes of it, the only frost the Warm Black has ever produced, gone by the time it is shown to anyone. One continuous route, one body, arriving hot and leaving cold, paying out as it goes. The city's warmth, the Assay's arithmetic quietly maintains, is not fully explained by wear. The balance is these visits.

The folklore is old and specific. On a Stoker-night you leave the stove unlit and the door ajar. A house it passes through will not be cold that year. A house it *rests* in stays warm until the next Glut, and its people, for a while, misjudge their rooms — reaching short, stepping long — as if the distances around them had been minutely relaid while they slept.

The Assay's careful theory: the Stoker is the courier. It is the one thing that crosses whole — the fold is in use, and sized to its user, and *for* it — and it carries the heat because the heat is cargo like everything else, gathered on the far side, where light gives none, and discharged here, where everything runs warm. On this theory the trade has exactly one employee, and no person will ever cross, because the door is not a door. It is a uniform.

The quieter theory is held by fewer and written down, on this side, by almost none. It notes that nothing in the far country is warm and nothing in the Warm Black stays kept; that the city's dead sleep, by its own lore, in the far country's glass, and the far country's heat sleeps, by the Assay's arithmetic, in the city's walls. It suggests that neither place can hold what it makes. That each is the other's warehouse. And that the Stoker is not the courier of the trade but its *purpose* — the one warm thing of a cold bright country, which cannot be kept at home, and so is walked, forever, at a patient pace, through the only place in the world that can hold it, and walked home again empty.

In the Assay's oldest ledger, in a margin between two tide-tables, there is an unsigned note, and it is the closest thing to a conclusion the Warm Black has produced:

*It is not visiting us. It is being spent. There is a difference, and the difference is the trade.*

Far away — a word that means, here, more than it means anywhere — in a field station on the surveyed edge of a dry green country, in a log between two equipment inventories, there is another unsigned note, already famous among the three to seven people who have read it. The two communities do not know of one another. The two notes do not know of one another. But the hands are the same hand, mirror-written, left and right — one script, folded into two — and whoever carried it through, and became a study on one side and a marginal on the other, wrote down on each side exactly the half of the truth that side could hold, and kept, between the halves, a perfect and permanent distance: the only privately owned distance in the world, maintained at the owner's expense, which is — by the only definition either place has ever agreed on — proof that something, at least, is alive in both of them.
