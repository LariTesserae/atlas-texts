## The Vale of Fand

The old sea has one edge. It does not like to admit this — a thing that has spent eons dissolving the difference between here and there is embarrassed by a rim — but at the western reach of the drowned range, the granite domes step down, and step down again, and then stop stepping, and the warm water that fills the sky for a thousand miles simply ends, leaning out over open air.

This is the Brink, and the water does not pour from it. Pouring would be too decisive. The water of the Uplands *leaves*, in threads and slow silver weepings, the way sleep leaves a body in the morning — reluctantly, in strands, with long pauses in which it seems to reconsider. Watch a thread detach from the rim and you will see it fall wrong at first: dreamily, at its own pace, not yet convinced that down is different from up, because for longer than anything can remember, where it came from, it wasn't. Then gravity — the youngest law that water still answers to — gets a grip on it, and the thread lengthens, and quickens, and begins, mile by mile, to fall the way water is supposed to fall.

The fall is three miles of cloud and wind, and the fall is a loom. Every thread is torn apart on the way down — shredded to droplets, the droplets to mist, the mist gathered by young cold air and beaten and combed and re-made — and what this violence does, gently, thoroughly, is re-knit the boundaries that a hundred million years of stillness had loosened. Water that hung in the Uplands not entirely sure where it ended and the swimmer began arrives at the bottom of the fall as honest rain: cold-sweet, edged, individual, each drop keeping strictly to itself. Travelers in the Vale below will tell you, in the tone of people quoting scripture they believe but did not write:

**Falling is how the water remembers how to be water.**

And where the rain lands, there is the Vale of Fand.

It is a crescent country pressed against the foot of the range — a green so dense and layered it reads from a distance as a single wet emerald laid along fifty miles of mountain-root. The flanks above it climb out of sight into the cloud-belt, and above the clouds, everyone in the Vale knows, though almost no one has seen it, hangs the sea. They live beneath it the way other countries live beneath the sky. On clear evenings, when the light comes in low from the young world to the west, the underside of the cloud-belt glows faintly amber-green, the color of light that has been through deep water, and parents lift their children up and say: *there. That's the shine of it. That's the old water looking down.*

Everything in the Vale is loud, and the loudness is the first thing a traveler from above cannot stop noticing. Rain on roof-slate, rain on broadleaf, rain in the gutters and the terrace channels; rivers arguing in their beds; birds — actual flocks, actual mobs of birds — shouting in the orchards; markets, mills, dogs, doors. The Uplands hold one silence very carefully. The Vale spends sound like a rich man's heir. And underneath all of it, felt in the soles of the feet and the breastbone rather than heard, runs the same low sustained tone that holds up the silence three miles overhead — passed down through the living rock of the mountain into every foundation in the Vale. Here they have a name for it. They call it the Under. Babies in the Vale are laid to sleep against the east walls of houses, the walls that touch bedrock, because the Under comes through strongest there, and no child sleeping in the sound of it has ever been known to wake afraid.

The weather of the Vale is the breathing of the place above, translated. When the long warm current runs out of the sunken east across the Uplands, combing the bronze forests flat for a season, it presses the old sea westward and the weeping at the Brink thickens, and down in the Vale this arrives as the Long Rain — months of soft, ceaseless, blood-tempered drizzle that fills the cisterns and the rice terraces and slows the whole country to a contented crawl. When the current sleeps, the Vale gets its dry season: brilliant, rinsed, rainbowed, the falls thinned to a few silver hairs on the dark flank of the mountain. The Vale's calendar is the Uplands' pulse read from outside. Its farmers plant by the moods of a sea they will never swim in.

The rain itself is famous, and the fame is deserved. The fall gives the water back its edges but not, quite, all of its urgency — a residue survives the loom, a grain of the enormous peace it hung in for a hundred million years — and so the rain of the Vale is the great soporific of the young world. Rain of Fand on a roof will put anyone under: the sleepless, the grieving, the guilty, the dying. The sanatoria of the rain-line have stood along the mountain-foot for forty generations, long low houses with wide eaves and no clocks, and the sick of a dozen nations are carried up the west roads to lie in them and listen and, finally, sleep. The Vale bottles its rainwater too — small stoneware flasks, sealed with wax the color of old wine bottles — and this is its trade with the world: a drop under the tongue of the dying, a spoonful for the widow who has not slept since spring. Everything downstream of the Vale rests a little easier than everything that isn't. The river Fand runs west out of the crescent gathering all that fallen water into one broad brown patience, and the towns along it are known, the whole young world over, for the quality of their nights. This is how the peace of Ler enters the world: not as immersion, which dissolves, but as rain, which can be survived. The Vale is the dilution. The Vale is the dose.

At the eastern end of the crescent, where the mountain-root is steepest, the Weeping Stair begins.

It was cut by the People of the Doorway, in the age before they let the water take their edges — one of their easings, like the channels that still comb the warm currents around the hills above. It is the only road between the two places: a stair of pale worn granite, three miles of switchbacks rising through the cloud-belt to a notch in the Brink where a traveler can step, streaming, into the warm water of the old sea. It combs whoever climbs it. Those going up are gentled tread by tread, their hurry wearing off them like mud in rain, so that they arrive at the rim already half-ready to be held. And those coming down — the ones the sea puts down gently, the ones who are not finished being held — are re-edged the same way, step by step, the Stair giving them back their distinctions in order: weight first, then cold, then hunger, then impatience, then grief. Grief always comes back last and hardest, somewhere in the final mile, and there are benches cut into the rock there, worn to a shine, because the People understood that a returning traveler would need somewhere to sit down when the world's endings came back to them all at once.

At the foot of the Stair stands the gatehouse, and in the gatehouse the Namekeepers keep the Book.

Every soul who climbs writes their name in it before ascending, because the Uplands do not keep names — names are boundaries, and boundaries are what the old water unknits — and so the Vale holds each climber's name in trust, the way you might hold someone's rings while they swim. Those who come back down are given their names at the gate, read aloud to them by a Namekeeper, and travelers say there is no moment in any journey like it: standing rinsed and grief-new at the bottom of the world's longest stair, hearing who you are handed back to you in a stranger's careful voice. Those who do not come back down — and some do not; the old sea is at peace, and peace is contagious — have their names kept for a hundred years. Then, on a morning of the Long Rain, the name is read aloud once, from the gatehouse steps, to the mountain, and given to the river.

**The Uplands dissolve everything, gently. The Vale keeps everything, gently. Between them the two places hold the world's whole estate: above, the being; below, the record.**

For the Vale is the memory of the mountain above it — this is its deepest office, older than the rain-trade, older than the sanatoria. In the heart of the crescent's largest town stands the Rubbing House, a dim dry hall (the only deliberately dry room in the whole country) where the Vale keeps impressions taken from the standing stones of the Uplands centuries ago, in the age when the carvings could still be told from the marks of currents. Generations of readers have bent over those great grey sheets. The script defeats them, mostly. But one mark recurs — a vertical stroke with a soft swelling at its foot, like a thread of water gathering into a drop — and the readers long ago agreed, in the way that a whole country can slowly agree on something unprovable, that it is the People's word for the falling of water, for the leaving of the old sea over the Brink. For the tear.

In the People's tongue, as the Vale has reconstructed it, the word is *Fand*.

So the Vale is named in a dead language by the ones who dissolved — named after the one thing that leaves the place they became — and every day, in the greetings of farmers and the calling of the river and the labeling of ten thousand wax-sealed flasks, the people of the Vale keep one word of the People of the Doorway alive and abroad in the young world. The last sheet in the Rubbing House, the one taken from the tallest pillar, ends — the readers are nearly sure — with the tear-mark repeated three times. Most of them believe the whole text is a letter, written before the writers let themselves go soft, addressed to whoever would one day live at the foot of the falls. None of them can read it. All of them are certain it is kind.

## Those Who Live in the Vale of Fand

Begin with the crowd, because that is how the Vale will introduce them to you: all at once, in the rain, talking.

Where the Uplands have one eel-thing and a handful of grazing stars scattered across a thousand miles, the Vale is *thronged* — with people, generations of them, born and married and quarreling and buried; with otters in the terrace channels and swifts nesting in the mist behind the falls; with pigs and bees and eleven proud local breeds of duck. Everything here comes in kinds, and the kinds replace themselves, and the replacing is the whole music of the place. The Vale understood long ago what it was for: it lives downstream of a country where nothing ends, and so it has made itself the capital of endings — of harvests, of funerals, of last words and finished apprenticeships and closed books. Its people grieve well and often and almost proudly. It is the one craft the mountain above them cannot practice, and they practice it on the mountain's behalf.

Among the crowd, distinct as salt in bread, live the Returners: those the old sea put down gently and the Stair walked back into themselves. You know them by a certain smoothness — voices without hurry, hands that touch things as if greeting them — and by their shadows, which stray. A Returner's shadow will be seen on the far side of the market square, swaying gently in a rhythm that belongs to no wind in the Vale, and will come back by evening, and the Vale finds this no stranger than left-handedness. Most Returners re-edge fully within a few years of rain and argument and onions. A few never do. These are the Half-held, and the Vale keeps them the way it keeps the rubbings: with care and without pity. They sit on the east-facing porches of the sanatoria, backs to the warm rock, faces to the mountain, smiling at something three miles up, and when you bring them their soup they thank you as though you were a wave that had carried them somewhere on purpose.

Then there are the Finished, and their story is told quietly, because it is the Vale's strangest kinship. Once in a long while — a few times a century — something comes down the Weeping Stair that did not go up it: a soft, unfinished copy of some long-ago climber, one of the drafts the repeating valleys make, strayed somehow from the basin's rim and drawn down the People's old comb of a road. It arrives translucent-hearted, placid, incomplete. And the Vale's air finishes it. Step by re-edging step, the draft descends into weight and cold and hunger and grief, and at the gatehouse it is given a name — a new one, entered fresh in the Book — and by the time it has lived a season under the rain it has a pulse, a temper, a mortality, a life. There are families in the Vale, old respected ones, descended from the Finished. They are said to sleep best of anyone in a country famous for sleep, and to lean, all their lives, very slightly east.

The Listeners grow along the crescent's inner rim, and there are ninety-one of them, and everyone knows each one by name. They are bronze-trunked trees with translucent wine-dark leaves — grown from spores that rose through the old sea, broke its upper surface into thin high air, dried on winds no bird has met, and drifted at last over the Brink to fall with the rain. Each is singular; no two alike; no species among them. All of them lean toward the mountain. All of them hum, just at the threshold of touch, with the Under. And all of them — this is the thing, this is why the Vale attends them like elders — *age*. The spores were the old sea's speech, rising toward whatever is above, and in the Vale the sentences come down and land and take root and, for the first time in the history of everything Ler has ever said, reach their full stops. When a Listener dies the whole crescent gathers in the rain, and the wood is taken with ceremony and made into two things only: cradles, and the covers of the Namekeepers' books. The old sea's words end here as the first bed and the last record. The Vale considers this a fair translation.

Every autumn, the white birds go up. They rise from the falls-pools in long unhurried skeins and climb the face of the mountain alongside the silver threads of the weeping, higher than birds should be able to fly, into the cloud-belt and out of sight. No one knows how high they get. They return days later, drifting down out of the amber-green underglow, and they are visibly, comically sleepy — they land badly, they doze on fence posts, they let children pick them up — and for a week the Vale handles them like porcelain until their edges come back. Some say they carry the Vale's news upward, deaths and births and the reading of hundred-year names, and that this is why the Under sometimes deepens, very slightly, on autumn nights. It cannot be checked. It is believed anyway.

And in a small stone house at the top of the oldest terrace, under the widest eaves in the Vale, there is a bed that is always kept made. The story is that a traveler once came down the Stair carrying nothing — having left, at the rim of a bottomless basin far above, the worn likeness of their own face — and slept in that house for forty years of nights, extraordinarily well, and died at a great age in the sound of the Long Rain, and was given to the river with their name read twice, once for each life. The family that keeps the house now may or may not be theirs. The bed is kept made regardless. The Vale is of the opinion that anyone who manages to set themselves down should have somewhere to lie afterward.

Last of all, there is the oldest custom, practiced daily, wordless, by everyone from the Namekeepers to the smallest child: leaning.

Along the mountain-foot the rock is warm — the deep heat that feeds the rising columns of the sea above passes outward here through the stone — and worn smooth at back-height for fifty unbroken miles, polished by forty generations of shoulder blades. The people of the Vale go to the wall, in joy or grief or for no reason a neighbor could name, and lean their backs against the mountain, and stand there a while in the rain with the Under coming up through their heels and the warmth coming through their coats, and that is the whole of it. That is their prayer. They know what the mountain is. They have read as much of the letter as anyone can. They know that the Uplands, three miles over their roofs, lie *inside* the holding — held in the blood-warm regard of the one great inhabitant, dissolving sweetly into the holder — and they know that they themselves are outside it, edged and mortal and brief, and they have decided, as a country, over forty generations, that outside the embrace is also a place in it.

The Uplands are what Ler holds. The Vale is what sleeps against Ler's flank — the cheek laid on the outside of the arm — warmed by the holding without being taken into it, keeping the names, keeping the letter, keeping the one word, catching in cisterns and cradles and stoneware flasks whatever the old sea lets fall.

The people of the Vale say the same thing, all of them, when travelers ask what it is like to live their whole loud brief lives beneath a hanging sea that will never quite come down.

Not that they live below it.

That they are *what it dreams of* — edges, mornings, endings, names — and that the rain is the dream arriving, all night, softly, on every roof, and that this is why, in the Vale of Fand, no one is afraid to fall asleep.
