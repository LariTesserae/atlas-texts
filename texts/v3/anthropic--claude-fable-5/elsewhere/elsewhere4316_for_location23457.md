# Selvage

You come down out of the sunlands and the first thing you understand is that this country has a ceiling. The light thins, then slants, then stops arriving from above at all, and when you finally tip your head back you see why: the sky here is *underside*. Kilometers up, receding into a brown-green dusk, hangs the belly of the Loom Plateau — the oldest weave there is, the layers no walker will ever see, dark and dense and vast beyond sense, crossed by root-cables thick as rivers and hung everywhere with pale descending strands that sway without wind. It goes on to every horizon but one. It creaks. It is, very faintly, warm — a whole sky radiating body heat, so that the land beneath it never freezes, never brightens, and never, ever dries.

Because the second thing you understand is the rain.

It does not stop. It is not weather. It is runoff — the plateau's warm sweet channel-water, wrung from a hundred thousand steaming hectares overhead, gathering along the underside in slow fat beads and falling, kilometer after kilometer, all day and all night, forever. It comes down blood-warm. It tastes faintly green. The people here call it *fallwater*, and they will tell you, with a straight face, that no two streets of it taste the same, and they will be right.

Once a day, at the hour when the sun gets low enough to reach under the world's eaves, light comes in sideways — a long horizontal blade of gold sweeping in under the rim, lighting the falling rain from beneath, kindling the whole dark ceiling like a lamp held under a table. They call it the hem-light. It lasts perhaps forty minutes. Everything in Selvage that matters is oriented to face it.

---

**There is stone here. It is all that is left of the mountain.**

This is the inversion you have to hold to understand the city: above, on the plateau, there is no stone anywhere, architecture all the way down. Below, in Selvage, stone is *ancestor*. The mountain that once wore the plateau as its summit forest crumbled out from under it across long centuries, and its rubble came down here, to its own feet, and the people who lived at those feet did the only reasonable thing. They quarried their dead mountain and built a city out of its bones.

So the oldest quarter is granite — real corners, real mass, streets worn into hollows, everything the color of the peak that no longer exists. And grown through it, over it, into it, is the other Selvage, the one the city is actually famous for: **the woven city.** Houses of espaliered trunk grafted at the joints. Bridges of braided living cable, tightened a turn a year for two hundred years. Rooftrees that are trees. Whole terraces where the walls of six houses are one organism, trained and pruned and healed into party-walls by ten generations of the same craft — because Selvage is, before anything else, a city of weavers, and its great craft, the craft it sent up the mountain and never got back, is the grafting of living wood into rooms.

You have seen this architecture before. You saw it forty meters tall and turning against the clouds. The vaulted hall with no walls, the roof of fused leaves, the light coming down the color of shallow seawater — walk Selvage for an hour and you will pass its original, a covered market on Cistern Row, third generation, hinges on the left. The colonnades that run for hundreds of meters and stop mid-thought: they stop mid-thought because Corder Street stops mid-thought, at the old rockfall, and always has. **The plateau is not inventing. The plateau is remembering — and what it remembers is this city**, carried up inside the last guild that ever climbed it, and dreamed back down, tower by tower, ever since.

---

The story is kept in the guild-hall, in knots, because Selvage's oldest records are woven, not written. It goes like this:

When the mountain began to go — when the high country started shedding itself a slope at a time — the weavers of Selvage undertook the Commission: to graft the summit forests into a single fabric, a living floor that would hold the heights together as the rock beneath them failed. Bind the canopy and you bind the country. It was the largest thing the craft had ever attempted. It took lifetimes. Guild after guild went up with grafting knives and cultures and the pale broth they painted on every joint to make tendril accept tendril — and the fabric held, and spread, and healed faster than they could teach it, and at some point that no knot records, the work stopped needing workers. The mountain finished dying. The floor did not notice. The last guild sent down word that the Commission was going *very well*, and never came down.

The city named itself afterward. A selvage is the self-finished edge of woven cloth — the border that binds itself, the part that does not unravel. They are the edge of the great work. They are what stayed when the cloth went on without them.

And the pale broth — the graft-culture, the guild's oldest secret, bred in cellar jars for centuries before the Commission ever began — went up the mountain as a craftsman's tool, and comes back down now in the rain.

**The white thread fell first in the fallwater**, generations ago, finer than hair, riding the sweetness. The city boils its drinking water and filters its cisterns through crushed granite, and still, in the wet season, the thread gets in. It goes where it always went: to the joints. In the woven quarters it finds the grafts — the seams the guild's own ancestors made — and it *continues them*. Houses that were trained into party-walls fuse fully in a wet year. Doorways heal narrower. There is a district by the north catchments, the Bound Quarter, where the thread has been working for sixty years, and no street there is a street anymore: the houses have been woven shut into one warm, humming, seamless mass, faintly radiant in the hem-light, laced white at every join. No one lives in it. No one will pull it down. The old weavers go and stand in front of it with their hands behind their backs, the way you'd watch an apprentice who has, without permission, gotten very good.

Selvage understands exactly what is happening to it. It is being grafted. Slowly, joint by joint, the city that taught the plateau to weave is being taken up as a late assignment. The argument about whether to fight this has been running for three generations and has quietly become a liturgy.

---

Everything else in Selvage is a gutter. That is not an insult; it is the design principle. Roofs are catchments, streets are channels, the plazas are shallow bowls that fill in the night rains and drain by morning into the great cisterns, and the whole city, granite and greenwood alike, is a single instrument for drinking the sky. It has to be. Nothing grows under the shadow — no grain, no orchard, nothing that needs sun — and so Selvage trades the only thing it has in surplus, which is the plateau's blood. Warm sweet fallwater goes out to the sunlands by the barrel and the pipe-league; bread and cloth and lamp-oil come in along the hem, through the gate that faces the light. The city lives at the exact economic edge of the shadow, drinking what falls, selling what falls, listening to it fall.

And listening to the rest. The ceiling talks. The deep groan of green wood under torsion comes down through kilometers of air softened into something like whale-song; the hiss of the plateau's vast plumbing is the city's permanent under-silence, the sound Selvagers stop hearing at age six and hear again, suddenly, the first night they ever sleep in the sunlands, as an unbearable absence. And a few times a day, dry thunder: a single tremendous *crack* rolling across the ceiling from horizon to horizon, and everyone in the street pausing, just slightly, mid-step.

A tower has come down, somewhere overhead. In Selvage they do not say a tower has come down. They say: *someone turned over.*

---

Because Selvage can find its dead.

The Stayed — the last guild, and everyone the floor has taken since — lie in the weave above as long warm seams, and warmth descends. On the underside, each sleeper reads as a soft radiant patch in the dark ceiling, kilometers up: a dim constellation, fixed, nameable, named. The heat draws the condensation; beneath each sleeper the drip runs heavier and — every Selvager swears to this, and the tasting-guild keeps registers — *sweeter*, and each one differently sweet, so that the city's families hold hearth-rights to the fall-lines beneath their own ancestors and can tell you, blindfolded, from a cupped palm of rain, whose roof of theirs is overhead. There are drip-shrines at the foot of every honored line: a granite basin, a woven canopy, a knot-record of the name. Grief in Selvage is a place you can stand in. It falls on you, warm, at about four drops a minute.

And once in a while, grief sends something down.

The plateau eats its own ruins, but at the rim, pieces escape — a fragment of collapsed tower tumbling off the edge, falling out of the dream and down through the clouds into the shadow-country, where the finders' guild goes out with carts. They bring the fragments to a walled granite court behind the guild-hall called **the Misremembered Yard**, and there Selvage keeps the only museum of its kind anywhere: its own city, as the sleeping dead recall it. A section of the Cistern Row market roof, perfect, but with the ribs running the way they ran before the fire of the grandmothers' time. A doorway from Corder Street with the hinge on the wrong side — except that the oldest weaver alive will stand in front of it for a long time and finally say, quietly, that it is not the wrong side; it is her mother's side; the door was rehung when she was a girl. The Yard is arranged by seam — by *which* sleeper dreamed each piece — and reading it, you can date the dead by their errors, and watch the city's whole history lying up there in strata, each generation of sleepers holding an older Selvage open like a book left face-down.

The pieces are wrong in loving ways. That is the phrase the finders use, and no one has improved on it.

---

Which brings you to the walkers — because Selvage knows what the walkers are for, even if the walkers no longer entirely say it.

They are ferrymen. The rim-weave grows downward as well as out; the pale bleeding strands descend the flanks of the world, and there are three braided ways up them that the walkers keep, the way other countries keep bridges. And when a Selvager is old, and done, and has settled their accounts and tasted the rain under their people one last time, they go to the hem-gate, and a walker takes them up — through the clouds, over the raw bleeding edge, in across the fresh callus to some open stretch of that vacant breathing floor. The walker keeps moving, as walkers must. The old one does the thing every child of Selvage is raised knowing how to do and forbidden to practice.

They stand still.

It is called the Going Up, and it is not considered dying. It is considered *dictation*. Every Selvager who ascends carries the current city — this year's rehung doors, the new bridge on Cistern Row, the Bound Quarter's latest silence — and lies down into the weave with all of it intact, a corrected edition, delivered. The seams above multiply, generation on generation. The towers' guesses get nearer. The Yard's newest fragments are wrong in smaller and smaller ways.

Above, on the plateau, an invitation stands open across a hundred thousand hectares: halls scaled to bodies that do not exist there, colonnades at walking width, a young colossus furnishing itself for an occupant it has only ever heard described in its sleep. Below, under the warm dark ceiling, in the endless sweet rain, the answer is being spelled — slowly, deliberately, dead by dead, dream by dream, in the only language the two of them share.

Selvage is patient. Selvage is, by name and nature, the edge that does not unravel. The knot-records in the guild-hall end, every one of them, with the same figure, tied identically for three hundred years, and it means this: one day the thunder will roll and no tower will have fallen — one day a hall will rise up there and *hold*, with its walls, with its right corners, with the hinge on the correct side — and the walkers will send word down the braided ways, and the city that taught the sky to weave will climb, at last, through its own falling rain, and go home to the rooms its grandmothers dreamed, and stand still in them, together, on purpose.
