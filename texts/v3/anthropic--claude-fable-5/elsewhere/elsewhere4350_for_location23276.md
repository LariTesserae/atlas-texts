**The Reliquary**

From the seam near the summit of the mountain that is not finished — if you pause before descending, with the roar already climbing into your teeth, and look south along the spine of the range — you can see it on a clear day: an older mountain, lower than its neighbors, round-shouldered, worn. At its top there is a pale cap that catches the morning light strangely, like wet bone, or like ice that never melts. It is not ice.

The walk takes three days along the spine, through two passes, and at the second pass there is a place — the walkers know it, and always stop there — where you can see both summits at once: behind you the nameless peak breathing its thin mist into a cloudless sky, ahead of you the bald pale cap holding perfectly still. It is the only place in the world from which you can see the beginning and the end of the same sentence.

You reach the Reliquary the way anyone can: on foot. That is the first thing to understand about it, and it takes a while to understand, because everything about the other place — the rope, the seam, the cold, the noise — insists that such rooms must be guarded, must be earned. This one is not. There are herders' meadows on its lower flanks, and a worn path, and children have been to the top on a dare. The mountain is old and settled and has decided, long ago, exactly what shape it is. The last hour of the climb is over bare shattered stone, and as you go up, the world's sound falls away behind you — wind in grass, then wind on rock, then only wind, then not even that. Near the summit you can hear a bird's wings before you see the bird. People who come up from the loud valleys say the silence has a pressure to it, a weight, like something added to the air rather than taken away. The keepers of the other mountain say the same thing about silence, and they say it about the first day after.

And then the summit, and the summit is the surprise, because the summit is the room.

It stands there naked: a dome of pale crystal perhaps twenty meters across — exactly twenty meters, a walker from the west would notice, with a feeling like cold water down the spine — swelling out of the shattered grey rock like the crown of a skull. It is the old chamber. The mountain that made it was young stone, and young stone dies quickly in this range; wind and frost and rain took the peak from around it grain by grain, over spans no one counts, but the heart that had been built inside — laid film by film by the trillion small masons, each grain set and died into — was harder than everything that contained it. So the mountain, wearing down, uncovered its own finished work, and now wears as its summit the room it once kept sealed in the dark. What was innermost is highest. What was buried is the first thing the sun touches in the morning and the last thing it leaves at night.

The surface is milky where the weather has chewed it, but wind-driven grit has polished windows in it, patches smooth as still water, and through them you can see down into the stone. You can see the crystal fans, standing in dark sprays exactly as they formed. You can see, running through them, pale translucent threads, streaming shapes frozen mid-snap — the weed, filed into the walls ribbon by ribbon as its seams closed, held now in the light it never committed to. You can see rows of small strokes, grouped in fives, dozens and dozens of groups, sinking away into the depth of the dome like writing seen through ice — and near the surface, where the weathering has almost reached them, the last group, which has four strokes, and no fifth. And in one place, low on the northern side, if the light is right, you can see a dark twisted line running down into the stone with a small wooden shape at its end, like a vein with a knot in it: a rope of plant fiber and its toggle, held in crystal the way amber holds a fly, going down toward a center no eye will ever reach.

Near the top of the dome there is a hollow, a shallow basin about the length of a lying child, and the basin holds rainwater.

The water does not move. It has never needed to. It lies there reflecting the sky without a single flaw in the reflection, the first resting water that summit has ever held, and this — not the dome, not the fossil ribbons, not the drowned writing — is the true strangeness of the place, though it takes the longest to see. Because the still water is working. It is the hardest worker on the mountain. In the warm months it seeps into hairline flaws too fine to notice; in the cold months it freezes and swells and pries, patient as breath, and every spring the basin is a little deeper, its floor a little softer with pale grit. At the other mountain, the water builds and never rests. Here it rests and only unbuilds. The range keeps one water — snow to melt to the deep untraced channels and up through some young roaring heart and out and down and into cloud and snow again — and the walkers from the west say it is the same water, literally the same, that once stood up in columns inside this very stone and laid it shut; that it has come back, quiet now, to open what it made. The water remembers nothing. Or the water is the only thing in the range that remembers everything. The two sayings are told by the same people, sometimes in the same evening.

**The lichen.**

The dome's first colonists arrived on the wind, and they are the opposite of everything the room was built by. Where the masons were invisible, uncountable, colorless, and fast in the aggregate, the lichens are visible, few, fiercely pigmented, and slower than anything alive has a right to be. They are rust and gold and dead-black, coins and crusts and tiny gardens of them across the milky faces — and pigment, remember, is a commitment to a particular light. The weed inside the stone declined to commit, because its light was madness, strobe and dark by the second. The lichens commit utterly, because their light is the steadiest in the world: the same sun crossing the same bald dome, day upon day upon century. They can afford a color. They have time.

And they are unmasons. Each one holds its patch of crystal and quietly dissolves it, grain by grain, freeing in a century what a film laid in a day, and the wind takes the freed grains south and east, down into the valleys, into soil, into grass, into the herds, into the people. The sealed brilliance is leaving the mountain a dust-mote at a time and being spent into everything living downhill of it. There are children in the meadow villages whose bones carry a measurable trace of a room that closed before their language existed. The chamber kept what lived in it, the only way stone knows how. The world is unkeeping it, the only way the world knows how, which is by feeding it to something.

**The tree, and the bird.**

At the rim of the basin there is a tree, and there should not be.

It is small, wind-bent, dense as a fist, and much older than it looks — the only tree at that height for a day's walk in any direction. It grows from a pocket in the crystal where, one spring within living memory's grandmother's memory, the weathering broke through into a little void and gave up a handful of soft pale things: seeds gone ghost-colored with age, and a small carved bird worn smooth as a river stone. A child's tooth, the story says, was in there too, and was never found, and is presumed to be grit in the basin now, which the tellers seem to find right rather than sad. The offerings had been set above the spray line, in the place the closing would reach last; the opening reached them first. Most of the seeds were dust. One was not. The gentlest argument anyone ever made with a mountain — *not everything has to finish* — was answered, very late, in the mountain's own time, with a tree.

Its roots follow the frost-cracks, and so the tree, too, is opening the dome, gently, at the pace of wood.

The carved bird did not stay. It was carried down to the meadows, and traded hand to hand and story to story westward along the range, until it reached the valleys under the unfinished mountain, where the people of the rope received it with an hour of silence and no surprise at all. It lives there now. Each new keeper, before their first descent of the seam, carries it up the mountain and into the noise and back out again — up and back, never left. It is the one offering in the history of the two mountains that ever returned, and its whole duty now is to keep returning: proof, worn smooth enough to hold in a fist inside a mitten, that the stone keeps nothing forever.

The living bird — for there is one, a black quick high-country thing that nests in the wind-bent tree — is the dome's opposite number to the white thing in the deeps. It is all eyes. It rides the summit wind the way the eel rode the rotation, knowingly, slipping from gust to gust a half-second early, present at the updrafts as if by appointment. And it caches. It carries bright small things up from the meadows — a bead, a button of shell, once a sliver of tin that flashed on the dome for a whole summer — and tucks them into the frost-cracks, pushing offerings *into* the stone even as the stone gives offerings out. No one has told it to do this. It does it the way it does everything: as the shape its nature takes in this place.

**The walkers.**

The herders below call the summit the Bald Egg, and climb it rarely, and knock on it for luck, and it answers like a bell with a cold in its chest: one note, brief, solid to the core. Low on the flank there is a cave mouth that breathes cold air in summer, the dead throat of the old deep channel, and the children shout into it, and it does not echo, or echoes wrongly, a beat too late, and they run away delighted. The herders' story of the throat is short: something white lived down there once, longer than long ago, and one season it turned in the dark and did not come up, and went looking. They do not know where it went. The walkers from the west do not tell them.

Because the walkers come. Once in each keeper's life — before the rope, before the seam, before the first hour on the hand-cut ledge — they walk the three days south, stop at the pass where both summits are visible, and climb the settled path to the dome. There they sit beside the basin for one hour of total silence: the mirror-hour, the exact negative of the hour they will someday spend in unbearable noise. They read the drowned tallies through the polished windows — they add no marks here; here one only reads — down to the last group, the four strokes, the missing fifth, the calendar that kept its word by breaking off. They find the vein of rope with the toggle at its end, and they hold their own new rope, coiled on their shoulder, a little differently afterward.

And they take one thing. A palmful of the basin's still water, in a stoppered gourd, carried three days north and a hundred meters down the seam, and poured — a gesture no one could possibly hear or see, into a room where nothing can be heard or seen — into the roaring chamber. Quiet given to loud. Finished given to unfinished. The old room's rain into the new room's heart, where the masons will lay it into a wall within the day.

This is why the loud room has the name it has. A stoup is a small still basin of blessed water at the door of a holy place, and there is nothing small or still about the roaring chamber, and visitors have always thought the name a strange joke. It is not a joke. It is not even a name, exactly. The keepers, who had sat beside the real basin first, named the deafening room after the quiet dish on the finished mountain — named it for what it will be, when the count stops mid-group and the fist closes and, ages on, the rain finds the heart and wears a hollow in the top of it. The name is the longest promise their people make: *you, too, will hold still water. We have seen it. We have drunk from yours.*

**The next resident.**

So: lichens that unbuild, grain by patient grain, and spend the mountain into the living valleys. Still water and frost, opening what turbulent water closed. A tree grown from a sealed beginning, prying gently with its roots. A bird that pushes bright debris into the cracks as the cracks give pale treasure out. Walkers who take a palmful of stillness away and leave nothing but attention.

Every one of them lives in relation to the same thing, and the same thing is the true inhabitant, and it is this: the Reliquary is occupied by its own opening. That is the creature. The silence is its held breath, the deepening basin its slow gesture, the freed grains on the wind its exhalation, the emerging tallies its speech — a room that spent its whole loud life becoming a word with no space inside it, now being read back out, letter by letter, into space again. The other mountain's resident is dying by succeeding. This one died of success, and did not stay dead, because the world does not honor completion; the world only borrows against it. The weather is drilling toward the center, season by season, along the frost-cracks and the tree roots and the bird's little vandalisms, and some who sit the mirror-hour say that on the coldest nights the dome gives one soft knock from deep inside, though this is likely the ice, and they know it is likely the ice, and they say it anyway.

And what they believe — it is scratched nowhere, tallied nowhere, only carried, like the carved bird, hand to hand — is that one day the rain will reach the buried heart and stand there, a small still room of water in the dark, silent at first. And that the stillness will not last, because in this range it never has. And that somewhere in the black channels under everything, something white and eyeless and patient beyond patience will feel the range change shape by one small room, and turn, and remember the way.

The two mountains face each other down the spine, three days apart: one roaring itself shut, one whispering itself open, the walkers going between with a gourd of quiet in one direction and a memory of noise in the other. The range is one slow sentence, written from the inside and erased from the outside and written again. Neither summit knows the other exists. Everything that moves between them does.
