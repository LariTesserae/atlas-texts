**The Terraces at First Frost**

You find them from below, and you find them first with your skin. Climbing the hillside on a cold morning, on the path that switches back through dead grass and dark juniper, you walk into a body of air that does not belong to the season — a soft, wet, blood-warm layer lying against the slope like a held breath — and you look up, and the hill above you is steaming.

What is steaming is a staircase of stone that no one built.

It begins at a wall. Partway up the slope the hillside has been faced with masonry — the outer skin of the same buried work that holds the cistern, courses of dressed limestone keyed into the hill — and low in that wall there is a single carved spout, a stone lip the width of two hands. This is where the overflow channel, after thirty paces of chattering through the dark of the masonry, comes back into the world. Inside the chamber you heard it leave: a hard narrow rush under the broad roar. Out here you can put your palm under its arrival. The water comes out of the spout in a smooth muscular tongue, still nearly as warm as the pool it left, still carrying its freight of dissolved stone, and the moment it meets the open air it begins, immediately and forever, to give that freight up.

That is the whole secret of the place, and the water has been keeping it in plain sight since the first day the channel ran. Warm and confined, the water holds its lime the way held breath holds itself. Cooling, spreading thin, roughed up by air and light, it cannot. So it sets its stone down. It has been setting it down for as long as the cistern has existed — they have the same birthday, the chamber and the stair — and in that time it has built, below the spout, a descending apron of terraces: dozens of shallow pools, each one rimmed and cupped in pale stone the water made itself, each one spilling into the next, stepping down the hillside for a distance you could not throw a stone across, in scallops and lips and small brimming basins, cream-white at the top where the stone is newest and going grey, then gold, then moss-dark toward the bottom where it is oldest.

And here is the thing that will stop you, if you have stood in the chamber above: the terraces look *ancient*. The cistern, a few generations old, looks new — every chisel-mark sharp, every joint tight, a made thing barely begun. The terraces are its exact contemporary and they look like geology. They look like something the mountain did in an age before names. Water-built stone is born looking old; hand-cut stone is born looking young; and the same water, the same lime, the same few decades, produced both. Up in the vault the spring is still only frosting the masons' joints. Down here, uninstructed, it has raised a monument.

The building has its own logic, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it. The water deposits fastest where it moves fastest — where it thins and tumbles and breathes — which means it deposits fastest *at the rims*. Every lip of every pool is therefore always growing, always raising itself, each basin slowly deepening its own edge like a cup improving its manners. Where a rim grows too proud the water abandons it and finds a lower way, and a pool goes dry and grey while a new one brightens beside it; the whole stair rewrites its channels year by year, so that the terraces are never twice the same shape, and the dry basins stand among the live ones like rooms in a house the water has moved out of. The cistern was designed once, by the dead, and holds its design against the water's slow objection. The terraces are designed continuously, by the water, and hold nothing. One place is a sentence carved and defended. The other is speech.

The sound is speech too. Inside the chamber the water has one voice, enormous, doubled by the vault, and it silences everyone. Out here it has a hundred small ones — this rim's trickle, that lip's patter, the spout's steady pour, a dozen overspills braiding — and none of them owns the air. People talk on the terraces. Children shout on the terraces. The roar that fills the cistern is, out here, unbraided into a sound you could fall asleep inside. Same water. Same minute. It depends entirely on the shape of the room, and out here the room is the sky.

Because there is no oculus. There is everything. The terraces lie open to the whole weather, and where the cistern hoards its one poured column of noon, the stair takes the day entire — low light, raking light, cloud-shadow crossing the pools one by one like a hand passing over keys. At midday the chamber above outshines it; nothing on earth outshines that chamber at midday. But come at first frost, at dawn, when the hillside is grey and stiff and every other water on the slope wears a lid of ice, and the terraces perform.

Every pool steams. The whole stair smokes, softly, each basin lifting its own small plume, and above them all — higher up the hill, where the buried vault meets the sky — the oculus lifts its taller one, so that the hillside on a cold morning shows two breaths: the chamber's single column and the terraces' rising crowd, one place exhaling twice. Frost rimes the dead grass to the exact edge of the warm air and stops, a white line drawn around the water's territory as precisely as the moss draws its line inside. Within that line it is another country. It is the country everything on the hillside knows about.

**Who Lives on the Terraces**

The first answer here is the opposite of the cistern's: nearly everyone. The chamber above is a bright empty house; the stair below it is a market. But the honest answer is more particular than "everyone," because the terraces are not one habitat. They are a whole climate laid out on a slope, hot at the top and cool at the foot, and every step down the stair is a step into a different country, with its own citizens who cannot live one basin higher or lower.

**The upper pools: the old economy.**

At the spout and the first basins the water is still nearly chamber-warm, too hot for anything with legs, and here the residents are the same ancient nation that lines the spring's throat far above — heat-loving bacteria, arrived through the plumbing, painting the fresh white travertine in streamers of tan and rust and, where the sun cooks the shallows, a deep burnt orange. The colors lie in bands that follow the temperature the way the algae in the chamber follows the light: each shade is a population, each boundary a border no cell can cross alive. The stone here grows fastest, and it grows *through* them, as it does in the chamber's joints — the mats seed the crystals, the lime entombs the mats, new mats grow atop their own monument. The upper terraces are stone made of generations. Break a fresh rim and its cross-section shows fine layers, pale and dark, seasons of labor pressed into pages. The invisible masons who are pointing the cistern's joints, a film no thicker than frost, are down here building at their full ambition. This is what they would do to the chamber, given centuries. The terraces are the cistern's future, arrived early, outdoors.

**The middle pools: the green step.**

Halfway down, the water has cooled to merely warm, and the green begins — the same lineage as the chamber's streaming filaments, fallen from the same sky, but living here in luxury the chamber never offers: full sun all day, open air, and food. Because the terraces receive what the cistern exports. The overflow carries down not only heat and lime but the chamber's entire small harvest of the drowned — the beetle, the dusk moth, the wind-blown fly that circled the bright pool once and was taken by the channel into the dark. The dark delivers them here. Every casualty of the oculus arrives at the spout, softened, and enters an economy finally able to use it. The middle basins are thick with life on that subsidy: coils of algae fat in a way the chamber's hungry threads never are, small snails rasping the stone in slow constellations, ostracods like animate seeds, hoverfly larvae in the warm silt breathing through tails like threads. The chamber above is a place where things fall in and nothing eats. The stair is where the eating happens. Not one insect that drowns in the bright pool is wasted; it is only deferred, thirty paces, downhill, through the dark.

And at the spout itself, most mornings, a grey wagtail patrols the delivery. She has learned what the carved lip provides. She walks the warm rim with her long tail dipping like a signature being written over and over, and takes the chamber's dead as they arrive, and is, in a sense, the cistern's one predator — hunting it from outside, downstream, without ever entering. The chamber does not know it feeds her. The chamber does not know most of what it does.

**The lower pools: the cool country.**

At the foot of the stair the water has spent nearly all its heat and much of its stone, and the oldest terraces, moss-darkened, hold basins cool enough for the ordinary riot of water: beetles rowing, dragonfly larvae hunting in the leaf-shadow, caddis cases built — in perfect ignorance of the theme — out of grains of travertine, small stone houses made from the water's own masonry. Watercress grows here in the last basins, peppery and dense, seeded from some garden generations gone, and this is the terraces' one crop that walks away on human legs. In summer, frogs. And in winter the frogs do not leave: the lower pools never freeze, the warm country's border holds against the frost, and they winter over in the silt of the last basins — sluggish, safe, subsidized by a spring they have never seen, kept alive all season by heat that fell as rain on some other mountain and came up through the dark.

First frost is when the border shows its full authority. The deer come then, stepping down through the rimed grass to drink where the water still moves; the small birds of the hillside come to bathe, absurdly, in December, in the steaming middle pools, and fly up with vapor coming off their backs; everything on the slope that needs open water in the hard months knows the stair, and the stair's white frost-line is the most visited border in the country. The cistern is a secret the hillside keeps. The terraces are a fact the hillside depends on.

**The human step.**

People, too, and more of them than the chamber ever sees. The chamber gets its rare silent bather; the terraces get the village. One of the middle basins, broad and knee-deep and blood-warm, has served as the washing pool for as long as the terraces have had rims — its lip is smoothed by hands and wet cloth the way the ledge above is smoothed by feet, the same faint polish, the two places wearing their people in the same slow grammar. Children dam the overspills and the water, patient, unbuilds their engineering by morning and continues its own. Someone cuts the watercress. And the one who climbs to the chamber when the overflow chatters wrong — they learn that it chatters wrong from *here*, from below, because a fault in the channel announces itself first as a change in the spout's pour, a stammer in the stair's water before it is a sound in the vault. The terraces are how the cistern is listened to. The keeper reads the pour, and climbs.

**And the one that never stops arriving.**

Ask the terraces what lives here and the answer crowds you — the banded mats, the fat green coils, the snails, the wagtail, the wintering frogs, the deer at the frost-line, the washerwomen's polish on the rim. But underneath the crowd, the truest resident is the same as it is above: the water, which is not a different water. It is the same body, minutes older. It turned in the bright chamber; it spoke in the vault; it took the channel into the dark and came out through the carved lip into the air, and everything on this hillside lives on what it does in the half hour after it leaves the pool — the heat it sheds, the stone it sets down, the dead it delivers, the winter it holds open.

The chamber and the stair are one creature, and each is what the other cannot be. The cistern keeps: the light, the warmth, the silence of everyone in it, the masons' design against the slow objection of the lime. The terraces give away: heat down the slope, stone into rims, food into mouths, and the design itself, revised every season, held by no one. Up there the water lives in a house that hands made and is quietly eating it. Down here the water made the house, and everything else moved in.

Stand at the foot of the stair at first frost, in the warm breathing air inside the white line, with the hundred small voices falling and the two plumes rising from the hillside above you — the chamber's tall single breath, the terraces' scattered crowd of them — and it is impossible to say where one place ends and the other begins, because there is no such border. There is a spring, and a vault of light it fills, and a staircase of its own stone it descends, and the water passing through all of it, loud in the first room and conversational in the second, hoarded and then spent, and warm — blood-warm — the whole way down.
