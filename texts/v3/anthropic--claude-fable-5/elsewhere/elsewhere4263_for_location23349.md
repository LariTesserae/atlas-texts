**The Fold at Windmost**

You arrive from below, because there is no other way to arrive. The land goes from oak to thorn to bracken and then to a grass so short and so combed by wind that it looks less grown than upholstered, and somewhere in the bracken a wall picks you up and walks with you. It is drystone, hip-high, and it climbs at your pace, and it does not stop where the fields stop, because the fields stopped meaning anything long before the wall did. It takes you all the way to the top, and at the top there is the fold — a cluster of stone cells, roofless, thick-walled, packed as tight as the eleven chimneys of a certain drowned town, so tight you could step from wall-top to wall-top. Which is, in fact, how the ravens do it.

Turn, at the fold, and look back the way you came. The valley below you holds a long dark water, olive at its edges, brown-gold where the light goes into it and does not come out. On a still morning — and mornings up here are still the way a held breath is still — you will see, near the middle of that water, a tight cluster of rings spreading, one every few minutes, eleven origins, taking turns. That is the Works at Lowmost, breathing. Windmost is the only place in the world from which you can watch it do so. The hill and the town have faced each other for a hundred years across a change of element, and neither has once looked away.

**Wind**

The wind is the first thing, and the strangeness of it is that it is not even. It comes in rivers. The hill's contours channel it the way pipework channels anything, and there are lanes of moving air up here as fixed as streets — cross one and the cold presses on your chest, smelling of rain and peat and, faintly, of lanolin, the grease of sheep, which is the smell of the hill's one industry the way iron-and-yeast was the smell of the town's two. Down at Lowmost a single bubble every few minutes is the closest thing to weather. Up at Windmost, weather is the principal product. It is manufactured here in bulk and exported downhill in the only direction weather goes, and the town below is dressed, warmed, watered, and combed by what this hill sends it, and has never once sent anything back up except sons. But that comes later.

**Bare**

Nothing here is dressed. That is the second thing, and after the drowned town's riot of green it lands like a slap. The grass is cropped to a nap. The stone is naked to the sky and has been for so long that its nakedness has become a finish, a deliberate surface, like planished metal. The only gardens are the lichens — grey-green rosettes, burnt-orange crusts, growing at a pace that makes the algae of Lowmost look like panic. A lichen the size of your palm on a Windmost wall-top may be older than the Works entire. Down there the plants took over the administration in a generation. Up here the lichens have been in office since before there was anything to administer, and they govern the way very old institutions govern: by doing almost nothing, extremely slowly, forever.

And understand — this bareness is not emptiness. Windmost has not emptied. Ruin implies an interruption, and thinness implies a former thickness, and Windmost was never thick. It has simply worn down to its working minimum, which is: grass, stone, water, wind, sheep, and two ravens. Everything else was optional and has been let go.

**The walls**

The walls deserve their own accounting, because the walls are the town's time-clock, and unlike the town's time-clock they are still legible.

Every stretch of drystone wall is signed. Not with names — with hands. A waller's style is as individual as gait: this man laid his throughstones proud, that one loved a tight double-row of copestones, another worked small stone patiently where his neighbor would have forced a big one. Walk the long wall that runs from the fold down the eastern shoulder and you can read a century of labor in it, man by man, yard by yard. And you can read the departures. A style runs for two hundred yards, confident, distinctive — and stops. Mid-slope. Sometimes mid-gap. Another hand takes over, or no hand does, and the wall goes on gapped and slumping. Every one of those stopped signatures is a young man who put down the stone one morning, walked down through the bracken and the thorn and the oaks, and clocked in at the Works, because the Works paid money and the hill paid weather.

Down in Lowmost, the ink of the time-cards dissolved, and every recorded shift was returned letter by letter to the water. Up at Windmost the record is stone, and stone does not dissolve. It only settles. The hill has kept the ledger the town lost, and it is a ledger of subtractions.

There is one signature — you will find it on the western wall, below the fold, if you read carefully — that stops and then, forty yards on, *resumes*. Same hand. Same proud throughstones, a little slower in the later work, a little more careful, the style of a man who had learned somewhere else what hurrying was for and had declined it. Lowmost, in its drowned engine hall, keeps the memory of the one man who quit, the way an old firm does, as proof that staying is a choice. Windmost keeps the other half of him: the proof that leaving is one too. Forty yards of gap, and then the wall heals. Nobody's name. Everybody's story.

**The light**

Light arrives new here, first-hand, unfiltered, and there is far too much of it, and it is spent on nothing. After the four-in-the-afternoon amber of the drowned streets, the glare of the tops feels almost immoral — forty miles of visibility issued daily to a population of sheep. You can see three other hills, the grey stitchwork of their walls, the dark water in the valley, and, on the clearest days, a much more distant glitter that is the city the water is walked down to in pipes, the city that drinks, with its morning tea, a measurable percentage of everything Windmost has ever rained.

Because that is the trade now, the only one left, and it runs one way like all this hill's trades: downhill. The rain falls here first. It lands on the fleeces and the wall-tops and the cropped grass, gathers itself in the black peat, threads down the becks, and slides into the reservoir, where it goes from green to olive to brown-gold dusk and becomes the *element* of Lowmost — the warm, still, breathable dim in which the shrimp keep their dials and the engine keeps its hour. Every drop of the town's sky fell on this hill. Windmost is Lowmost's weather, held one valley up and released on a schedule no one sets.

There is a rain gauge on the flat ground east of the fold — a copper funnel in a small square enclosure, water-board property, older than the drowning. It is still in service. The funnel is bright where everything around it is dull, kept clean of leaf and moss with a fastidiousness the hill does not otherwise practice, and the collecting bottle, if you lift the lid you should not lift, is never full. It is emptied. Nothing has ever been seen emptying it. The hill's wages are measured before they are paid, which any mill hand from below could have told you is simply how wages work.

**Sound**

The wind is the silence here — a broad, grey, continuous hush that stands in for quiet and does the same job. Underneath it, if you get into the lee of the fold and let your ears settle: the tick of stone expanding in sun or contracting after it; a curlew, in season, unspooling its long bubbling cry as it crosses high over the fold without ever landing in it — the curlews are Windmost's commuters, its daily renewed refusal, exactly as the grey fish are Lowmost's; the mutter of a beck under peat.

And, rarely, a whistle. Two notes, falling. Then a different phrase — quick, imperative. Anyone who ever worked a dog on a hill would sit up at the sound, because they are commands: *come by*, *away to me*, *lie down*. There is no man on the hill and there has been no dog for a long time. There are the ravens, and ravens are mimics, and the ravens of Windmost hold, in their two black throats, the last surviving copies of the hill's working language. Whether they use it idly, the way a clerk hums, or deliberately, is the kind of question this hill, like the town below it, lets you keep. What is certain is this: when the whistle comes, out on the open slopes the sheep half-turn. Every head, the same moment, the same direction. The language still runs. The needle still creeps.

**Rules**

The hill has rules, learned slowly, and they rhyme with the town's the way the two places rhyme in everything.

Stay off the bright green. On the tops, where everything true is grey and tawny and brown, there are patches of vivid, luminous, riotous green — the only riot of green on the whole hill — and they are the bog's open mouths. Down at Lowmost the green is the administration; up at Windmost the green is the appetite. The sheep know the patches to a hoof's width. Follow the sheep.

In mist, follow the walls and not the paths. The paths belong to the flock and go where the flock's business goes, which is not where yours does. The walls were built by people for people and have not forgotten it.

Leave the gap as you found it. There are gateways in the walls with no gates in them, and some are open gaps and some are stopped with loose stones, and the arrangement is not accidental, and it is not yours.

Do not empty the gauge yourself. It is being emptied. And do not count the flock. Counting is a claim, and the tally is kept, and it is not kept by you.

**Who lives at Windmost**

**The wall-birds**

Wheatears, mostly — small, upright, grey-backed, with a flash of white at the tail like a signature. They nest in the walls, in the cavities behind slipped stones, and they are the walls' clerks: the same cavities each spring, generations deep, so that a map of their nests is a map of the walls' interior weather. Where the wheatears are, the wall is sound and dry within. Where a nest-line has been abandoned, the wall is failing from the inside, invisibly, and will slump in some winter to come. They audit. They cannot repair — nothing on this hill repairs, that is the difference between a hill and a town, the town below still oils and cleans and measures while the hill only endures and records — but their little bright presences, spaced along the copestones like the shrimp along a dial-rim, tell you exactly what is still holding. They live two years, three. The audit has been continuous for longer than the walls.

**The ravens**

Two. There have always been two, which means the position has been held, continuously, by no bird in particular, for a very long time. They keep the whole hill and inspect all arrivals, and they will inspect you: a single pass, low, one raven slightly behind the other, one black eye taking your pressure the way the pale keeper of Foundry Lane takes it in the pipes below — not threat, not curiosity, *reading*. They roost on the fold's wall-tops and step from cell to cell across the gaps like men crossing chimney rims. They hold the whistles. They keep, some say — there is no one to say it, but the hill implies it — the tally, the count you are forbidden to take yourself, croaked out in the evenings in a rhythm too regular to be conversation.

**The heft**

And then the flock, which is not the resident. The resident is what the flock carries.

A heft is knowledge held in sheep: the learned, inherited certainty of which slopes are theirs, where the shelter is, where the sweet grass comes early and where the bog lies about its depth. It passes ewe to lamb, unwritten, unfenced. No wall on this hill keeps the flock anywhere. The heft keeps them. The sheep of Windmost have been unclipped and unchosen for generations — huge now in their grown-out fleeces, dignified as furniture under dust sheets — and every individual animal is temporary, and the heft is not. Sheep die on the hill and the hill hires their lambs into the vacancy, and the knowledge goes on wearing new bodies the way the dials of Lowmost go on being read by new shrimp. The hill hires. It has always hired. It is the same firm as the one below, everyone up here understands that; it is the same firm in two premises, and once it transferred its young men downhill freely, from the branch that grew wool to the branch that cast iron, and the wall-signatures are the transfer records.

**The bog**

Below the heft — under it, under everything, the true ground of the tops — the bog.

Black, deep, older than the walls by an order of walls, and its nature is this: it keeps. Things given to the bog do not end. Grass has fallen into it every autumn for eight thousand years and none of it has finished falling; it is all still there, pressed into dark centuries, holding its shape the way the thing in the town's hearths holds its heat. And here is the seam between the two, the deepest of all the threads that run down this hill: the bog was cut, once. Before the coal came up the new roads, the Works fired its first hearths on peat — on the bog's dark bricks, sledded down the slope by the grandfathers of the men whose wall-signatures stop mid-yard. The warmth that sleeps in the drowned foundry, the warmth that pays the wages of the moss and the shrimp and the keeper of the lane, was seeded from this hill's stored centuries. What lies in the hearths and what lies under the tops are the same patience in two states: one spending, very slowly, what it was fed; one holding, very slowly, everything it has ever been given. They are the hill's savings and the town's pension, and the water passes between them carrying the interest.

Stay off the bright green. It is not malice. It is only that the bog does not distinguish between what it is given and what it takes, having never once, in eight thousand years, been asked to give anything back.

**The Gather**

Last, the resident you will not see arrive, because it does not arrive; it assembles.

On certain evenings — no fixed calendar survives, but the ravens know, and station themselves early — the flock begins to move. Not grazing-drift. *Purpose.* From every slope of the heft, along paths worn by a hundred years of exactly this, the sheep walk themselves in toward the fold, string after string converging as the light goes tawny, and they file through the gaps that are open and past the gaps that are stopped with stone, and they pack into the cells, and they stand. This is the Gather: the shape that gathering-work left in the hill when the hands that did it went down to the Works or into the ground or into the bog. Down at Lowmost the Shift received no order to stop and continues gently. Up at Windmost the Gather received no order either, and so, at intervals, the hill gathers itself — presents itself, full-fleeced and patient, at the shedding gate where once a man stood sorting, this one for clipping, this one for market, this one back to the hill — and waits.

No one sorts them. Sometimes, from the wall-top, a whistle: two notes, falling. The flock stirs and settles. And after an hour, or a night, the Gather lets go, and the sheep file back out along their paths, undiminished, unsorted, unshorn, returned to the hill entire, and the fold stands empty under the two black auditors, and the position at the gate remains — this is the thing to understand before you stand in the fold at that hour — *open*.

You may feel it consider you. The ravens have already read you. The wheatears have noted which stones you touched. The rain that lands on your shoulders as you turn to go is on its way to the dark water below, down through the olive dim, into the warm plumes, up a brick throat in a fat silver bubble that will burst on the surface and be handed straight back to this wind, which will carry it up the slope and rain it on the fold again — one water, two towns, passed back and forth forever like a wage endlessly re-earned. The town below continues gently. The hill above waits gently. Between them the whole firm goes on.

Leave the gap as you found it. And going back down through the bracken and the thorn and the oaks, if a whistle comes after you — two notes, falling — do not stop, and above all do not turn and walk back up. That is *come by*. It is not yours to answer.

Not yet.
