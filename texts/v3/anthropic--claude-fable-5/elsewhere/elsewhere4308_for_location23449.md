# The Finished Dark

You come into it from the light, and the light does not end so much as thin. There is no border post, no threshold — only the amber of the rock cooling, mile by mile, through honey, through ember, through the last deep russet glow of stone that has nearly finished deciding what it is, until you are walking by the lantern-trail alone: a line of small cairns, each capped with a chip of young rock carried out from the bright country, each shedding its little ration of borrowed daylight into a dark that takes it without echo. Between one cairn and the next there is nothing. Beyond the last cairn there is the rest of the world.

This is the same country. That is the thing to hold onto, walking out. The Finished Dark is not a neighbor of the Natal Vault — it is the Vault, done. Everything under the deep ceiling is born in the bright country and ages away from it, and the frontier of birth drifts, generation over generation, in its slow birthward direction, and behind it the finished stone lengthens like a wake. To walk out of the Vault is to walk backward through its life. A day out, you are crossing ridges that glowed when the oldest living builders were young. A week out, you are in the century country. Beyond that, the distances stop being distances in any way that matters. Out here, a mile is an age. The Dark is the Vault's whole past, laid down solid, and it goes on farther than any lantern-trail has ever been carried.

The weather dies about a day's walk out. You notice it first as a silence on your skin: the not-quite-rain stops. The ceiling out here sweated itself dry centuries ago; the mist never rises; the pools are gone — you cross their basins, dry and glazed and pale, like the beds of milk that finished being drunk. The air is cold the way a cellar is cold, and so dry that your own breath is an event, and so still that a dropped tool rings for a long time. The Vault's great composite din — the grazer-surf, the herds, the rattling air — is a thing you only understand once you have left it. The Dark has perhaps four sounds. The occasional tick of stone shedding the last of a heat it took on before anyone living was born. The grind, somewhere, once in a long while, of a delver at its work. The bells. And under everything, most of the time, nothing — a silence so complete that travelers from the bright country report hearing their own hearts as an intrusion, a wet stranger they have brought into a finished place.

And the ground holds still. This is the Dark's deepest strangeness to anyone raised in the Vault, stranger than the cold or the black: the country *stays*. Nothing extrudes. Nothing dissolves. No formation grows too ambitious and comes down, because every formation finished its ambitions long ago — the columns met their floors centuries back and stand now in solid ranks, the arches completed themselves, the provisional geography of the bright country has been struck permanent, every ridge exactly where it will be in a thousand years. A traveler who crossed this country a millennium ago would recognize all of it. The Vault is a manuscript being written; the Dark is the same manuscript, closed, and every page keeps.

Which means the Dark can be *read*. Walk it with a lantern-chip held low and the whole history of the bright country is under your feet, verbatim. Here is a herd-road, glaze-scarred and healed and scarred again, the corridor worn by matriarchs who steered the growing stone without knowing it — frozen now, permanent, a road going nowhere the herds go anymore. Here is a basin deepened by ten thousand years of congregation. Cut into any ridge and there is the lamination, band on band of fused grazer-shell, the tombs of the humblest citizen stacked into geology — and threaded through the bands, if you know the signs, other things: the long dark lens of a settled matriarch, a hill with a heart; the thin bright seam, no wider than a hand, where a band of builders once carried one of their dead out onto hours-old stone and weighted it with a chosen cairn-stone and let the world grow over it. The Vault plants its dead in the newest ground it can reach. Then the frontier moves on, as it always moves, and the newest ground becomes old ground, and the old ground goes dark, and so every grave the builders have ever made is on its way here. The Finished Dark is where the buried arrive. It is the necropolis of the bright country — not by anyone's decree, but by the plain mechanics of a world that carries its past outward — and the lantern-trails, all of them, every corridor of refreshed light running out from the Vault's margins, exist because the builders will not let the distance win. The trails are how the living keep reaching what the world is carrying away from them.

There is no green here either, of course; there was never green anywhere under this ceiling. But in the Vault there is appetite in its place, and in the Dark there is something rarer than both. There is *keeping*.

---

Life in the Finished Dark is sparse the way stars are sparse — almost nothing, and each thing enormous with significance. The Vault's economy is newness, and newness does not come here; what comes here instead is everything the Vault is done with, and the Dark's inhabitants have arranged themselves, from the border inward, along that one thin supply.

**The border feeds on snow.** At the Vault's dim margin the great convection columns fail — the drafts that carry the migrating grazer-shells and the spent fliers slacken, and slacken, and finally let go of their cargo. It falls. All along the boundary between the countries there is a belt, a few miles wide, where a thin perpetual precipitation of small dead things comes down out of the last of the moving air: shed shells light as ash, exhausted fliers spiraling to their only landfall, the fine organic dust of the bright country's roar. The builders call this belt (their clicking suggests) something like *the hem*. It lies in drifts. And in the drifts live the drift-eaters — flat, blind, patient kinds, pale as the dry pool-beds, that plow the shell-snow with shovel faces and take a year to cross a dune of it. They are the Dark's only crowd, and by Vault standards they are hermits. Past the hem, the falling stops. Past the hem, nothing arrives ever again except what is carried, and what was already buried, and the bells.

**The grave-bands are grazed from within.** Deeper in, where nothing falls, the only wealth is the interred — and there are thread-things, finer than hairs and longer than trails, that live their whole slow lives inside the shell-laminations, following a single band of the ancient dead through the rock, gleaning the last chemistry out of tombs ten thousand generations closed. They will never see even lantern-light. They are, in a sense the builders seem to appreciate, the readers of the record: each one is working its way along one line of the world's writing, a syllable a year.

**And the hills with hearts are mined.** The delvers are the Dark's great beasts, its answer to the matriarch herds, and possibly — the keepers' glyph-stones seem to claim this — their descendants: the same lithovore line, gone under, gone slow, gone blind. A delver is the size of a modest building and moves at the speed of a rumor. It does one thing. Somewhere out in the dark country it has tasted, through miles of solid stone, the deep chemical note of a buried matriarch — a heart in a hill, the concentrated centuries of a body the bright country closed over long ago — and it is boring toward it, and the boring will take most of its life. Its grinding is one of the Dark's four sounds; on a still day at the hem you can feel a delver working forty miles out, a slow grit in the soles of your feet. Behind it, its tunnel remains — smooth, glazed, permanent like everything here — and the tunnels are the Dark's roads. Gleaner-kinds live in them, eating the seep of the walls. The keepers travel by them. The whole sparse country is slowly being veined with the persistence of animals digging toward the dead.

When a delver breaches a heart, the Dark has its festival. *Heartfall*, the trail-glyphs call it. The sealed wealth of a body dead five hundred years opens into the cold, and warmth comes off it, actual warmth, and for a season there is a small bright country in the black: gleaners arriving down the tunnels for months, thread-things bending their long syllables toward it, drift-eaters hauling themselves in from the hem on journeys some of them will not survive, the delver itself lying in the opened hill like a returning heir. It is the exact mirror of the Vault's rock-births, and the old keepers mark the symmetry in stone: in the bright country the ground splits and the future wells up glowing, and everything converges in days to eat what is about to exist. In the Dark a hill is opened and the past wells up warm, and everything converges over years to eat what has finished existing. The whole deep world lives on the two ends of the same stone, and between the ends it only lives on what it carries.

**Which brings you, as everything in the Dark eventually brings you, to the keepers.**

The Vault's builders armor with age. Their hides deposit mineral all their lives; their elders go gray and hard and slow and are valued as shields. What the bright country does not much speak of — what you learn out here — is where the hardest elders go. Not all of them settle by the windbreaks to be shields. Some, when the stone in them has gained enough on the flesh, when the warmth of the bright country has begun to feel like noise, take up a sack of lantern-chips and a store of young glowing stone and walk out along a trail, and do not come back, and are not expected to. The Dark receives them. Cold suits a body turning mineral; slowness suits it; the keepers' metabolisms bank down like the rock's own residual heat, and out here, eating almost nothing — a chip of young stone a season, carried or brought to them — they live spans the bright country cannot check against anything. Centuries, the trail-glyphs insist. The oldest keepers on the near trails are said to remember, in their own bodies, geography that the living matriarchs remember: they walked the frontier when the frontier stood where the hem is now.

They keep. That is the whole office. They walk the trails and replace the fading lantern-chips with fresh ones carried out relay by relay from the bright country, so that the corridors of borrowed daylight never quite gutter; they guard the chips from the one pest the Dark has produced, a quick little chip-thief that eats young stone and is the only creature in the finished country that steals — and the only theft in the Dark is theft of light. They tend the graves. Every builder-seam within reach of a trail is marked and glyphed and visited; when a delver's boring trends toward one, the keepers reroute it — how, no one from the bright country has seen, but the tunnels bend, and the seams stay closed. They keep the record: cached lines of worked stone tokens, the strata read and transcribed, the herd-roads mapped, the old cairn-lines audited — for the keepers can stand on a ridge in the far dark and check it against the marks of the builders who asked for it, and know, as no one in the Vault will ever live to know, which of the appointments the world kept.

And they answer the bells. This is the traffic between the countries that runs deeper than chips and tokens. The strikers' tones carry through bedrock without regard for borders, and out in the Dark, where there is nothing left to map — the country holds still; the country is *known* — the keepers use the medium for the only thing remaining in it, which is speech. A keeper hears the frontier's ringing arrive through the soles of its feet, faint, days of walking away: the sound of birth still going. And it sets its feet wide on the cold stone in the old listening stance, and takes up the striker-club it carries — found gear, harvested from strikers dead and exposed in the ancient bands, the densest stone the world makes, passed keeper to keeper down the trails — and strikes the finished columns, and the tone goes back. In the bright country the strikers hear it: a return off something beyond the map, a bell answered from past the edge of anything their soundings can draw. They have no glyph for it. The builders do. The builders stand at the ridge crests at the dim ebb, feet set wide in their learned imitation, feeling for exactly that — the far, deep, patient reply that means the trails still run, the graves are kept, the past is still answering.

A keeper's end is the trail's gain. When the stone in one of them finally wins — and they seem to feel it coming years out — the keeper chooses its spot. Sometimes a grave-seam it has tended for a century; sometimes a junction of tunnels; sometimes, the glyphs say, simply a place where a cairn was needed. It takes the listening stance there, feet set wide, facing — always — outward, into the deeper dark. And it does not move again. The mineral finishes its long work; the other keepers come, in their unhurried way, and set a lantern-chip on it, and refresh the chip when they pass, and add its position to the record. The trails of the Finished Dark are lined with cairns, and not all of the cairns were ever built. A young builder carrying chips out from the bright country, replacing the faded lights one by one, is tending, without always knowing which is which, both the markers and the marked — a corridor of ancestors holding the light for a corridor of light held for ancestors, all the way out.

How far out, no one has walked. The trails end where the carrying ends, and the Dark does not. The record's oldest tokens speak of trails abandoned and re-founded, of keepers who went beyond the last cairn with a sack of chips and a club and were heard, for a while, ringing. Out past everything, the oldest keeper-stones stand facing into a country older than the species, older than the herds, older than the writing in the walls — the world's first finished stone, where the frontier of birth burned before anything lived to eat it, black and total and perfectly kept. What they were listening for, at the end, facing that way, the glyphs do not say. Perhaps only the courtesy of the whole deep world, extended one country further: that when a bell is struck, something ought to stand still, and feel for the answer.

At the ebb — and the Dark keeps the Vault's ebb, out of memory or fellowship, dimming its lantern-chips with shutters of split shale in time with a daylight none of its stone has felt for a thousand years — you can stand at a trail junction and hold all of it at once: the silence with your heart intruding in it, the shell-snow settling at the far hem, a delver's grind coming up through your feet like patience itself, the long line of small lights running back toward a glow below the horizon of ridges that is the whole bright roaring living country, seen from its own past — and then, arriving through the stone, faint, unhurried, the frontier's bell. And nearer, from some cairn along the trail that may be a cairn and may be a keeper, the answer going back: *still here. Still kept. Grow this way when you're ready.*
