# Turnwater

The falls never arrive. That is the first thing to understand about the country below the rim: the plateau sends its water down in threads, and the wind unbraids every one of them before they can land, so that what reaches the lowland is not a river's end but a weather. The mist drifts leeward off the cliff-face and settles into a belt of forest at the plateau's foot that has never, in any living memory, been dry. The people downriver call it the Weep.

The Weep is loud in a way the highland above it cannot imagine. Every leaf in it is a gutter. The trees grow broad, sagging foliage shaped by ten thousand generations of catching fog, and the whole forest ticks and patters day and night with the sound of water reassembling itself — drop by drop, leaf to leaf to moss to root — after the wind's dismantling. Ferns stand higher than a rider. The light comes down green and swimming. And here, at last, is the talus the plateau's cliffs refuse to make: the sharp young blocks do fall, a few in a decade, and they land in the Weep's perpetual wet, and the roots and the frost and the seep get into their clean fracture-faces and begin the work the highland has postponed. The plateau does not age. It delegates.

Out of the talus toes the water comes back into the world as springs — not one source but ten thousand, threads of the falls reconvening after their long trip through leaf and stone. They run together, and together, and together, and within a day's walk of the cliff there is a river. It is grey. Not the grey of shadow but of substance: the water is milky with rock flour, the finest grinding of the young mountain, and it keeps that color for a hundred miles. The lowlanders do not call it anything elaborate. It is the Grey, and unlike the undecided braids on the plateau above, the Grey learns its course fast and holds opinions about it — cutting, swinging, undercutting its own banks, carrying the plateau downstream a spoonful at a time.

Days down, where the river throws its widest bend across a floodplain broad enough to hold weather of its own, there is a town.

---

Turnwater is built of the plateau, ground fine and rained on. Its bricks are river mud — which is to say cliff, delayed — dried grey-gold in the sun and stacked into walls nobody expects to outlive them. Everything else is wood: houses up on posts against the floods, framed with pegs instead of nails so that a house can be taken apart in an afternoon and carried, plank by numbered plank, when the river changes its mind. The docks rot in a decade and are rebuilt in a week, farther out or farther in, chasing the channel. The streets are provisional. There are maps of Turnwater, plenty of them, and no two agree, and none is wrong, exactly. Each was true when it was drawn.

The town's one permanence hangs in the air. Turnwater has bells — dozens of small ones and three great ones, bronze, green-shouldered, slung in timber frames that are themselves rebuilt around them generation after generation, the way flesh is rebuilt around bone. When the bend migrates and the town migrates after it, the bells move first, hauled on a groaning sledge with the whole population walking alongside, and where the bells stop, that is Turnwater, and the rest of the town accretes around them like silt around a snag.

The bells are not ornament. Some mornings the plateau's clouds slump over the rim and come down the valley of the Grey as a fog that erases the world to the length of an arm, and on those mornings Turnwater is navigated entirely by ear. Everyone can read the bells the way the highlanders read stone: this pattern for the hour, this for high water coming, this for a boat at the far dock, this for fire, this for a birth. Where the plateau folded itself around silence because sound carries, Turnwater folded itself around sound because the fog eats sight. The town is never quiet. Hammering, hawking, argument conducted at full volume across the water as a form of friendship — a visitor from upriver finds it unbearable for a week, and then finds, to their surprise, that they have stopped listening for anything underneath it. There is nothing underneath it. That is the point.

They count everything here. Floods, seasons, debts, dead, the exact hour. The ledgers of Turnwater go back centuries in a script that has changed four times, and the flood-marks cut into the mooring-stones are obsessively dated and reliably wrong within a decade, so they keep cutting new ones, a ladder of abandoned certainties climbing the stone. Their language runs entirely on motion. It has dozens of verbs for kinds of change — the change of a bank being undercut, the change of milk-water clearing, the change of a voice breaking, the change of a fashion dying — and no comfortable way to say that a thing simply *is*. When they need that meaning, for the bronze of the bells, for the dead, for the mountain on the horizon, they use a loanword, brought downriver long ago and worn smooth in their mouths. The word is *vell*.

Their word for the past is the same as their word for downstream. The future comes from upstream, as floods do, as the water does, as everything does. This means that the plateau — upstream of all things, source of the river, source of the fog, source of the very mud in the walls — sits in Turnwater grammar where the deep future sits. Nobody remarks on this. It is just how the sentences come out.

# The Turnfolk

They are quick people, weathered early and legibly — you can read a Turnwater face like a flood-mark ladder, every year dated. Their hands are never still; stillness in company is considered ominous here, the pause before a flood, and so they fidget as a courtesy, whittling, mending, drumming fingers, to reassure each other that nothing is about to happen. They interrupt as applause. Their tellings are all plot — reversals, drownings, swindles, weddings — and a teller who repeats a story exactly as it was told before is said to be *telling it dead*, which is somewhere between lazy and indecent. A story must change in the mouth or it isn't yours; it's stolen. Somewhere upriver there is a people who would find this monstrous, and the Turnfolk know it, and think it very funny.

Every person of Turnwater has a name that has never been used before and will never be used again. This is arranged by the simplest possible method: at each birth, the child's name is struck, cold, in small letters, into the waist of the greatest bell, among the tens of thousands already there. Bronze fatigues; every ninety-odd years the great bell cracks or sours and is broken and recast, mold of river loam, mold destroyed in the opening, and all the names go into the melt. The bell's voice is, quite literally, everyone. When it rings across the fog the whole dead town speaks at once, and nobody in Turnwater finds this mournful. It is the opposite of mournful. It is roll-call, and everyone answers.

The casting is done by the founders, and the founders, as often as not, are not from here.

They come down the rim-stairs and through the Weep, so their first act in the lowland is to be rained on by their own river. They arrive soaked, grey-clad, in their second decade usually, with pale lake-still eyes and fingertips smooth as river pebbles, and Turnwater knows them on sight. *Rimborn*, the town calls them — upriver, before they left, they were called threads. No one sends for them and no one turns them away; the founders' hall simply keeps a bed made at all times, and it is never empty for long. The rimborn take to the foundry as if it had been described to them in advance. It is the one work in Turnwater that cannot be hurried and must not be repeated: each bell's loam mold is shaped over weeks with a mason's patience, used once, and broken forever to free the bronze — a craft of obsessive fitting in a medium that refuses to be permanent, producing the only permanent thing the town owns. Both of a rimborn's natures are satisfied at once, and the town gets its voice, and neither party examines the arrangement too closely.

During a pour, and for the days of cooling after, the rimborn founder sits beside the buried mold and does not move, and Turnwater — this is the town's only scheduled silence — falls quiet around the foundry in rings, like water stilling. Children are brought to see it: a person being *vell*. It is the nearest thing Turnwater has to what happens, at the same hour, beside a cistern far upstream.

The traffic runs the other way too. In each Turnwater generation there are a few children — two, three, never many — who go still from the inside out. They stop checking the sky. They stand at the upstream edge of town too long, watching the fog come down the valley, and they ask what the bells were before they were rung, and the answer — *ore, mud, other bells* — does not do for them what it does for everyone else. The town calls them *stones*, without unkindness. One morning they walk upriver, through the Weep, up the rim-stairs, and the ledger closes their name with the mark that means *gone upstream* — the same mark used for the year's high water — and somewhere above, a house that was laid out for a certain number finds that it holds that number still. The river runs one way. The people run both. Each place exports its misfits and receives the other's, and each, in its own key, keeps its count without ever keeping it.

---

There is one more traffic, and it is the strangest, and Turnwater depends on it more than it admits.

The town keeps no old form of itself. Its ledgers record floods and debts, not streets; its maps are true for a decade; its houses are carried off plank by plank and reassembled into other houses. But generations ago some traveler out of Turnwater climbed to the plateau and, on a fog evening, seated nearest the warm wall, was asked the only question the highland ever really asks — *describe it* — and obliged, clumsily, with mud and bells and rotting docks. And the highland kept it. Word-perfect. Unworn. So now, in most generations, a few Turnfolk make the climb — *upwalkers*, the town calls them, and in their own grammar they are walking into the future to retrieve their past — and sit through the fog evenings, and come home with an old Turnwater memorized: street by street, dock by dock, a town none of them has ever seen and every one of them is from.

The tellers up there speak of the First Turnwater, and the Third, and the Fifth, as separate places strung along the Grey, each fixed, each fully itself. No upwalker has ever managed to explain, in either language, that these are all one town. The sentence cannot be built. In one grammar a thing changed is a new place; in the other a thing unchanged is a dead telling; and between them the truth just sits, untranslatable, perfectly well understood by both sides.

And every so often the river, swinging in its long slow argument with the floodplain, cuts into a bank and lays open footings — grey brick, post-holes, a mooring-stone with a ladder of dated flood-marks in a script four changes old — and an upwalker, standing over them, recognizes the layout. Matches it, dock for dock, to the Third telling. Because the bend migrates, but the floodplain is only so wide, and the river's meanders wander it in a cycle centuries long, and Turnwater, chasing its bend forever, has been circling. It has stood on this ground before. Its ruins are its foundations. The Turnfolk find this eerie for about a day, and then find it useful — old footings drain well — and build on top.

From the rim, none of it is eerie at all. From the rim you can see the whole floodplain at once: the live channel a bright grey line, and around it every channel the river has abandoned, ox-bows and ghost-braids and the pale rectangles of former Turnwaters, all lying on the land together the way the tellings lie in a Vell evening — simultaneous, set. From up there the town is a bead on a line, and the bead does not visibly move, and the Vell, standing at the edge watching the clouds go out over the lowland, have been watching Turnwater be still for as long as either place can say.

---

At blue dusk in Turnwater, whether or not there is fog, someone rings the small fog bell once. It is not an office and it is not a ritual; there is no priesthood here, and the town's small gods go in and out of fashion like hat-brims. It is just that a day is worth ending, and the note over the water at that hour is worth making, and someone is always there to make it. The sound goes out across the docks and up the valley of the Grey, thinning as it goes, and the distance takes it apart the way the wind takes apart the falls, so that from the rim above, if it could be heard at all, Turnwater would seem to be permanently ringing — one long note answering one long exhalation, neither ever quite arriving, neither ever used up.

The plateau breathes out. The Weep drips. The Grey runs grey. The bell holds everyone's name, and the mountain holds everyone's water, and between the town that is never the same and the city that is never different, the river goes on doing the only thing either of them has ever asked of it, which is everything.
