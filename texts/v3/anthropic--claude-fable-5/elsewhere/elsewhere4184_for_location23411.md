# The Heavy Ground

Three days' pole to the west, at the far edge of the water-country, there is land old enough to bury in.

The ridge there is the oldest thing anyone knows. That is the first thing you'd learn if you asked, and the first thing you'd feel without asking — a depth underfoot, a great settled patience, ground that has been ground so long it has forgotten it was ever anything else. The stone that shows at the ridge's spine is worn round as bread. The soil over it is black and deep, deep enough that a well takes two summers to dig, deep enough that the people who live on it have never in all their generations struck the bottom of it, and the soil is not empty. It is full of them. Barrow upon barrow, terrace upon terrace of the dead, so many and so old that the people say, without drama, that the ground here is *mostly ancestors* — that when you walk the ridge you are walking on the shoulders of everyone who ever walked it before you.

Onto this old weight, a town has been laid out. Laid out is the right word, and it is the opposite of packed. The Heavy Ground was planned, and you can feel the planning in your feet: lanes wide enough for two carts, walls parallel because they were meant to be, timber halls standing apart from one another with clean air between, thresholds worn into hollows by three thousand years of the same doorways being used in the same way. Where the Crowded Stone's lintels carry the fresh dimpled bites of antler picks, the Heavy Ground's stone carries no toolmarks at all anymore; the marks were there once, and time licked them smooth. Edges that learned softness. That is what old land looks like.

The wealth of the place is trees. Orchards descend the eastern face of the ridge in long terraces toward the marsh — hundreds of trees, fruit and timber both, so many that no one ever named one. You do not name a tree where there are hundreds. The antler that becomes picks on the far island comes down out of the deer-hills behind this ridge, through the Heavy Ground's market, into the hands of marsh traders; the fine gray sedge-cord that laces the Heavy Ground's tool-hafts comes the other way, woven on an island most people here have never seen. The two towns have held each other at arm's length across three days of reeds for two generations, each mostly a rumor to the other, joined by poled cargo and a halfway sleeping-platform and one notch-feathered heron that both towns privately believe favors them.

And the Heavy Ground is sinking.

It began — the well-keepers' tallies show it, though no one read the tallies that way for years — the same season the pale stone shouldered up out of the marsh in the east. A finger-width a year, maybe two, and not evenly. The eldest here remember the first sign exactly as the wet-born of the island remember theirs: not a catastrophe but a wrongness of level. A well whose water stood higher than it had ever stood. A cellar with a sheen on its floor. A door that stuck in autumn and then stuck in summer too. What the marsh people accepted in a single season, the Heavy Ground argued with for thirty years — pumped against, walled against, wrote petitions to itself about — because these are people whose whole inheritance is the assumption that ground holds still. Their word for reliable is the same as their word for soil.

The water that rises through the Heavy Ground is not sweet. That is the cruelty of the pairing, if you want to see it as cruelty. The young stone in the east weeps fresh; the old ridge in the west drinks brackish, the marsh coming up into it from below, salting the wells one by one from the lowest terrace upward. The orchards drown from the roots. Every few years another rank of trees at the water-edge stands leafless in spring, and is cut, and the wood — dense, mineral-stained, salt-cured, beautiful — has become the ridge's most valuable export. The island's stone-cutters prize it for adze-hafts above all other timber. The Heavy Ground sells its own drowning, plank by plank, to the town that is rising, and both towns are aware of this, and neither has found anything to say about it that is better than saying nothing.

Because they know. Not as measurement — no one has measured — but the way you know weight on a bench you're sharing. Early in the stone's first years, a delegation from the Heavy Ground poled the three days east to see the thing that had come up out of the marsh, and stood on the wet pale rock among the first houses, and the eldest of them, a deed-keeper, put his hand flat on the new stone the way you'd steady yourself on a stranger's shoulder, and said, quietly, *this is our missing height.* Then he went home and did not speak of it again for twenty years. The marsh people, characteristically, did not argue with him. They made room for the idea the way they make room for everything. On the island now it is one of the things the stone-born grow up half-knowing and rarely say: that the ground they love, the young sweating stone with its held breath, may be the exhalation of an old ridge lying slowly down in the west. Two ends of one bench. When one end rises, you do not have to ask what the other end is doing.

# The Ones Who Kept Count

Begin with the deed-house, because for most of the town's history it *was* the town.

It stands at the ridge's crown, a long dark timber hall, and inside it are the counts: boundary registers on wooden tablets going back past memory, well-tallies, harvest-tallies, the lineages of orchard terraces, the record of every field's every owner in unbroken descent. The people of the Heavy Ground are counters and keepers the way the island people are listeners and menders. Their courtesies are courtesies of the line — the boundary stone, the recorded agreement, the witnessed word. They never needed the island's discipline of unhearing, because their walls never touched; sound has always had room to die here, and so have grudges, buried in the deed-house like everything else. Where the island's crack-listener keeps a reed-strip tally that is never consulted, the Heavy Ground consulted its records constantly, for everything, for centuries. The books were the ground's memory, and the ground was the books' guarantee, and each held the other still.

Then the ground moved, and the books began to lie.

The keeper of the deed-house now is an old man who has held the office fifty years, which means he has spent his whole tenure recording subtraction. He is the mirror the island doesn't know it has. The crack-listener in the east lies awake for the soft *tock* of the stone opening and writes down what the young land gives — a new crack, a new weeping, a new use. The deed-keeper in the west walks the drowning terraces each new moon and writes down what the old land takes back — a field gone brackish, a lane gone to water, a boundary stone whose boundary is now fish. For decades he wrote these as losses, in the register's language of loss, each entry a small formal grief. Then, perhaps fifteen years ago, around the time the marsh traders' talk began to change him, he started a second book. In the second book nothing is *lost*. Fields are *given back*. The hand is the same careful hand. Only the weight of the words is different, and the town noticed the difference before he admitted to it.

What changed him — what is changing all of them — came up out of the barrows.

The lowest barrows stand at the old marsh-edge, which is no longer the edge, and the water found them from below. The ancient dead, wrapped and folded three thousand years ago, began to lift in their flooded chambers. The town fought it at first the way it fought everything: re-sealing, re-weighting, pumping, building small desperate dikes around its grandmothers. And then a marsh trader, asked at the market why her own people seemed untroubled by such things, explained — at wall-height, in the island manner, though no one here knew to call it that — how the Crowded Stone gives its dead to the water on purpose, poled out at dusk along remembered channels, because *the stone is too young to hold the dead.* You do not ask a child to carry a grandmother.

The saying crossed three days of reeds and landed on the Heavy Ground like seed on the one soil ready for it. Because the ridge's trouble, they slowly allowed themselves to understand, was the same trouble reflected: their ground was not too young to carry the dead. It had carried them long enough. It was lying down under the weight of everyone it had ever held. And so — not all at once, not without anguish, the deed-house full of arguments for two winters — the Heavy Ground began to stop fighting the barrows. The first deliberate water-burial here happened eleven years ago: a woman of ninety, who asked for it herself, wrapped in her own orchard's last good sedge and poled out at dusk by her grandchildren toward the deep channels of the middle marsh. The deed-keeper wrote it in the second book. The words he chose were words he had found, astonished, in the oldest registers — a notation from centuries back, marking the names of the landless who had once left this ridge for the open water and become, though no one here knew it until recently, the ancestors of the marsh people themselves, the very people of the Crowded Stone. Names struck through, and beside them: *gone to water.*

He writes it now for the honored dead. He writes it for the young, too — because there is traffic in the other direction, and it is the strangest and best of the new counts. Apprentices from the Heavy Ground pole east to the island to learn what no one on old land ever needed: how to taste a channel with a fingertip, how to hear a failing gradient in the pitch of running water at night, how to re-cut a doorway without resentment, how to live with ground that moves without calling it enemy. And the stone-born come west — grown channel-children, the island's casual hydrologists, hired to gouge gutters through the flooding lower lanes, teaching the Heavy Ground to build its houses on skids and shims, to hold house-lifting days that end in a shared meal, to treat a lane gone to water like a guest changing which side of the bed they sleep on. The lower town is growing a fringe of platforms now, woven-and-timber, walked at low water and poled at high, and no one has said aloud that the Heavy Ground is becoming, at its drowning edge, exactly what the marsh people were before the stone rose. The two peoples are passing each other, moving in opposite directions through the same amphibious life, and where they pass, they trade: old black soil, basket by basket, goes east now as a gift for the island's planted pockets — the old land literally handing its substance across the marsh to feed the young one's three named trees. Salt-cured orchard timber comes back as adze-hafts. Stoppered jars of the island's sweet seep-water are carried west and given, by custom now, to the sick and the dying of the Heavy Ground, who call it *the taste of new land* and drink it like a promise.

And the trees. The orchards are still hundreds strong on the upper terraces, still unnamed, but the lowest rank — the dozen nearest the water, the next to drown — the town has begun to name. Naming is what you do to what is scarce, and scarcity is coming up the terraces a finger-width a year. Someday, the deed-keeper says, the Heavy Ground will have three trees, and it will know each one the way the island knows its three, and that will not be poverty. He is the only one who says such things aloud, and he is old enough to be allowed.

At dusk, from the marsh, the Heavy Ground is a long dark ridge of orchard and rooftop, its lamps doubled where the lower lanes have become water, its sound not the island's silver murmur but a slow deep speech of timber — beams groaning as the ground settles, the *tock* of the east answered in the west by a long wooden sigh. And underfoot, if you stand still on the old black soil, you feel it: not the young stone's held breath but a long exhale, the eldest resident lying down at last among people who counted everything, and who are learning, line by struck-through line, what the marsh people three days east have always known — that the world is a shared bench, that the ground beneath you is a fellow passenger, and that when it shifts its weight, the whole of grace is this: you feel it move, and you make room.

The heron stands some evenings on the drowned lowest terrace, notch-feathered, motionless for an hour, and its arrival is mentioned in doorways here too. Both towns believe it favors them. Both towns are right. It is one marsh.
