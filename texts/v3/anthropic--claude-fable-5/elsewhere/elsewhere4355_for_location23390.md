# The Plateau of Snow and Silence

You leave the basin by the same road you came, but you do not stop at the rim. The road continues — most travelers never notice this, because most travelers arrive at the rim, look down, and forget that the mountain has an upward direction too. Behind the caldera's northern arc the range keeps rising, and the road rises with it, thinning as it goes: from causeway-width to cart-width to a stitched line of cairns. The air thins too, which is the first *right* thing, after the basin — cold and dry and honest, each breath a little less than the last. The roar falls away behind you in stages. First it loses its pressure, then its texture, then its pitch, until on the third day it is only a memory in the soles of your feet, and on the fourth day the memory is gone, and you notice you have been listening for it, the way you probe the gap of a pulled tooth.

Then the mountains stop pretending to be mountains and lie down.

The plateau opens all at once: a tilted immensity of white, twenty kilometers by forty, held in the crook of the range like something being carried. On its high southern edge stands a line of true peaks — locally, *the Fence* — which comb the weather as it comes over and shake the snow out of it, so that the plateau receives, every winter, the emptied clouds of an entire quarter of the sky. The surface is not smooth. Wind has carved it into long frozen swells, and old summers have glazed it, and the whole expanse is blue in the hollows and white on the crests and, in places, faintly rose, for reasons that turn out to be alive. Nothing here looks new either. But where the basin has *finished*, the plateau is doing something — slowly, at the pace of compression — and the people who live at its edge will tell you, if you earn it, exactly what.

## The Hush

Before you understand the plateau, you hear yourself — because there is nothing else to hear.

The quiet here is not an absence. It is a condition, exactly as the roar is, and it arrives the same way: as pressure. Cold still air over hard snow absorbs almost nothing, deflects almost nothing, and on windless days the plateau becomes a lens. Sound does not fade here so much as *travel*. A word spoken aloud carries a kilometer. A dropped tool can be heard across a valley of white. Newcomers from below — and nearly all newcomers are from below — describe the first night as the worst of their lives: lying in the guest-hut hearing their own heart, their own blood, the tick of their cooling boots, the vast surrounding attention of a landscape that misses nothing. Basin-dwellers faint in sealed rooms because silence feels like something about to happen. On the plateau, they learn, silence is something happening. It is the sound of the snow at work.

Because the plateau is not silent. It is quiet enough that everything speaks. The snowpack settles in the night with soft subterranean thumps, whole hectares sighing downward a finger-width at once. The firn — the old snow, the snow on its way to becoming ice — creaks as it compresses, a sound like a ship's timbers heard through a wall. In deep winter, in the great cold, the surface itself sings: a thin crystalline ringing as the top crust contracts, traveling outward from your footsteps in rings you can follow with your ears. The people who live here can stand on the white with their eyes closed and tell you the depth of the pack, the temperature three layers down, and whether the last storm's snow has bonded, all from the voice of one footstep. Children are taught to listen before they are taught to speak, and the first lesson, given out on the white on a still night, is the same in every household at the Hem: *everything can hear you. Say less, and mean it.*

## The Hem

No one lives on the plateau. Everyone lives at its edge.

The settlement — a scattered ring of hamlets in the scree margin where rock meets snow, called collectively the Hem — houses perhaps four hundred people, and has housed roughly four hundred people for as long as its records run, which is a very long time. The houses are low, half-buried, stone-walled and turf-shouldered, built to be swallowed by drift and dug out again; their doors face downhill and inward, toward each other, and their windows are small and deep and glazed — every one of them — with panes of basin glass, carried up the mountain a generation at a time. Inside they are warm, close, whisper-loud, and smell of wool, tallow, and the particular mineral sweetness of melted old snow.

There are no causeways here, and no trams, and no schedules carved in stone, because the plateau needs none of the basin's synchrony. Safety below is a matter of being where the timetable says you are, since no warning can be heard; safety above is the opposite — everything can be heard, and so the Hem's people move loosely, singly, at whim, across enormous distances, each of them audible to all the others the whole way. A figure crossing the white at dawn is tracked by every ear at the Hem without anyone thinking of it as tracking. Basin children grow up watched. Plateau children grow up *heard*. The effect on the adult character, curiously, is the same: a mild, permanent carefulness, the manner of people who know their biography is being drafted continuously by others — except that here the draft is written in footsteps and breath, in how you cross a slope and whether your load shifts, and the people of the Hem can identify one another at two kilometers, in fog, by gait alone, the way the basin identifies its people by the accent of their hands.

What crosses the plateau instead of causeways are the stake-lines: long rows of poles, ridge to hollow to ridge, marching out from the Hem into the white. They are roads and instruments at once. Each stake is graduated, and each is read — depth, settling, crust — on a round that has not been interrupted, at some households, in living memory times three. The road *is* the measurement. To walk out on the plateau is to take its pulse, and no one walks out for any other reason without also doing so, because the reading is not work here. It is manners.

## The Waters

Here is what the plateau is doing, and what the Hem exists to attend:

The snow that falls on this catchment does not leave it by melting away. It compacts, year over year, into firn, and the firn weeps downward, and the summer melt gathers into braided streams that run — every one of them — into the throats.

The throats are the plateau's own vents, run in reverse: natural swallow-holes, a few dozen of them, where the rock beneath the ice is broken along old, settled fault lines, and the water goes *down*. Some are modest, a stream sliding into a blue slot. The great ones are terrible and beautiful — smooth-walled shafts wide as a house, exhaling cold, down which whole rivers of meltwater turn and vanish with a sound that is the closest thing the plateau has to the basin's thunder: a deep, hollow, descending roar that rises out of the ground rather than falling upon it, and which stops, every autumn, when the melt stops, leaving the shafts dry and voiced only by wind.

The water that goes down the throats travels through the mountain's dark plumbing — sinking, pooling, threading the fractured rock, moving at the pace of stone — until, far below and far away, it finds the hot heart of the old caldera and rises again as steam. The Hem's tally-keepers and the basin's surveyors of quiet, comparing their archives across many careful generations, have converged on the figure: the journey takes two hundred years, give or take a decade.

Sit with that. The roar — the total, unrelenting condition that fills the basin, that deafens its people in the shape of itself, that turns its turbines and cures its porcelain and murmurs its unfinished cadence in the still points — is made of these winters. Of *old* winters. The thunder shaking the basin today is snow that fell on this plateau when the great-great-grandparents of the current Hem were setting stakes. The basin is a voice; the plateau is its breath, drawn two centuries in advance. The people of the Hem do not tend a snowfield. They understand themselves — and they will say this plainly, in their whisper-voiced way, without a trace of grandeur — to be tending the future of a sound.

So the catchment is kept the way other cultures keep altars. The first rule of the Hem, older than any of its houses, is that the water must go down clean. The throat-wardens walk the shafts' rims all melt season, clearing windblown debris, lifting out the occasional storm-killed bird with long hooked poles. The herds — a small, dense-wooled, sure-footed animal kept for meat and felt — graze the scree margin only, and are folded off the white entirely; a beast that strays onto the catchment and dies there is retrieved at whatever labor it costs. Nothing is buried in the snow. Nothing is thrown into the throats.

Nothing, that is, except names. But that comes later.

## The Descent

Every ninth year, in early autumn, when the throats have gone quiet and the passes are still open, six people leave the Hem carrying the winters on their backs.

What they carry are the staves: copies, carved fresh, of the tally-house records — one stave per winter, notched and figured along its length with depth, density, the dates of the melt, the count of storms. The tally-house itself is the Hem's one great building, long and windowless and dry, its inner walls racked floor to ceiling with the originals, centuries of them, oldest at the north end where the wood has gone the color of dark honey. It is the plateau's whole treasure and it looks like a woodpile, and the Hem prefers it that way.

The carriers go down the cairn-road, down the thickening air, down into the pressure of the roar — which none of them, however many Descents they have made, ever fully readies themselves for — and they deliver the staves to the surveyors of quiet. And they receive, in exchange, the basin's fired ceramic plates: the seasonal charts of the roar's pitch and the drift of its still points.

Then, in a vaulted room in the second district, sealed and padded so that both parties can bear it, the two archives are laid side by side, and the oldest exchange in either culture takes place: the matching. A stave from two hundred years ago is set against a plate from last season. The winter is read; the pitch is read; the lag is confirmed once more, and refined. The plateau's staves are, it turns out, a score written in advance. The basin's plates are the performance, two centuries late. Each place is the other's archive: the basin pronounces the plateau's past; the plateau holds, in a woodpile at the north end of a dry hall, the basin's future.

This is how the basin came to understand its quarter-tone. The very old below hear the kettle deepening; the tally-house above knows why. Two hundred years back, the plateau endured what its staves call the Starving Snows — a run of lean, dry, wind-scoured winters, a generation of them, carved into the record in notches so shallow the carvers doubled them in charcoal so no one would mistake economy for error. That famine of snow is arriving in the basin *now*, as pitch. The carriers of the last Descent brought down the reading of the staves that follow: the drop will continue, they reported, for roughly forty more years. Then the heavy winters that ended the famine will reach the hot rock, and the voice will steady, and rise a little, and hold. The basin received this the way it receives a tram schedule. The unhurried dread of the rim-road towns is, in truth, a hand-me-down; the Hem felt it first, two hundred years ago, watching the sky fail to arrive — and the Hem carries the newer dread too, the one it has not finished deciding how to say, because the staves of the last three generations are running shallow again, and this time the charcoal doublings are creeping down the wall toward the door.

Two other things travel with the Descent. The first is porcelain. The carriers come home with basin cups — the vapor-cured ware, finished in the mineral fog of the caldera — and every household at the Hem keeps one, and drinks the first meltwater of every summer from it. The people find this fitting rather than poetic, though it is both: the cure on that porcelain is the basin's steam, and the basin's steam is the plateau's old snow, so the cup is, in a strict material sense, made partly of the winters of their ancestors, returned up the mountain to hold the winters of their grandchildren. They do not make a ceremony of it. They simply always do it, which at the Hem is what ceremony means.

The second thing that travels is people. By a custom neither archive records the beginning of, each Descent leaves one witness below and brings one up: a plateau youth who will live out their life in the roar, a basin youth who will live out theirs in the hush. The exchanged witnesses are the hinge of the whole relationship, its living translation — and they report, independently, generation after generation, the same strange homecoming: that the other place's *intimate* register is their own *public* one. The basin's sealed-room voice, soft and half-felt, is simply how the plateau talks in the open air. The plateau's rare full shout is the basin's full-arm sign, the thing done hugely and seldom and seen by everyone. And the basin's touch-speech — the words laid into the bones, jaw to shoulder, palm to sternum — needs no translating at all. A plateau witness, newly arrived below, deafening slowly in a city of eloquent hands, can be spoken to that way from the first day. It is the one channel the mountain left open at both ends.

## Life, Such As It Is

The plateau's citizens-other-than-people are few, and every one of them is loud, and this is not a coincidence the Hem has failed to notice.

The choughs came first, or have always been here: black, glossy, acrobatic birds that live in the crags of the Fence and spend their days falling down the sky for the apparent joy of it, shouting the whole way. They are the only unbroken noise the plateau possesses, and the Hem loves them with the specific, rueful love the basin gives its deaf grey doves — the inverse emblem, honored for the inverse solution. The doves solved the basin by surrendering the doomed sense; the choughs solved the plateau by *refusing the vow*, by being the one voice the great attention of the white cannot shame into whispering. Feeding the doves, below, is the one sign every district makes the same way. At the Hem, the equivalent is this: no one, ever, hushes a chough.

There are white hares at the margin, and the small dense herds, and in high summer *the blush* — a bloom of red snow-algae that spreads across the melting fields in watercolor drifts, the plateau's only flower, harvested by the handful into the basin cups and drunk, faintly sweet, on the longest day.

And there are the moths. This took generations to understand. In certain storm-tracks, the great white moths of the basin — the ones broad as a spread hand, that navigate the vapor at dusk — are drawn up over the rim on the warm updrafts and carried, doomed, out over the range, and a few of them come down on the plateau, and freeze, perfect, and are buried by the next snow. The firn takes them down into itself. And decades later — sometimes a century later — the summer melt of some far, thin year gives them back: intact, wings spread, lying on the old blue ice like something delivered. The children of the Hem collect them. Every house has one or two, kept dry on the sill of a deep window, and the name for them, in the Hem's whispering dialect, translates as *letters* — because that is what they are, plainly: mail from the loud country, posted by weather, carried by burial, delivered by thaw, arriving on exactly the same schedule as the water. The Hem sends its winters down the inside of the mountain; the basin, without knowing it, sends its moths over the top; and each arrives about two hundred years after departure, which the tally-keepers note in their margins without comment, the way one notes that a correspondence, however slow, is at least going both ways.

## The Strange Part

It is this: the Hem gives words to the water, and believes — or does not quite disbelieve — that the water keeps them.

Every person at the Hem has a true name, and it is used, in a whole long life, exactly twice. It is whispered once, at birth, directly into the infant's ear, by the oldest person of the household — given so quietly that even the parents, a hand's width away, are not certain they heard it. And it is shouted once, at death.

The shout is the plateau's single sanctioned full-voiced sound, and it is spent only then. The household carries the name — not the body; the body goes to the scree-yards at the Hem, off the water, under stones, because *nothing of the body, everything of the voice* is the rule and has always been the rule — the household carries the name out along the stake-lines to one of the great throats, and the oldest kin stands at the rim of the shaft, and shouts it, once, complete, down into the dark where the rivers go. On a still day the shout is heard across the entire plateau, by everyone, and everyone stops, and that stopping — four hundred people motionless on a white immensity, listening to one word fall — is the Hem's whole liturgy. Then the name is not made again for a generation, and the water takes it down into the two-hundred-year dark, and the Hem holds, in its quiet way, the belief it will not state as belief: that the mountain is one instrument, intake and exhaust, and that the roar filling the basin — the total, unfinished utterance that city was built inside of — is made of everything that was ever given to the throats. That the cadence the basin's old ones hear in the still points, syntax without words, saying without the said, is the names. Arriving. Two centuries late, worn smooth by the journey, pronounced too slowly and too completely for any single ear — being said, at last, by something with the patience to say them all at once.

The surveyors of quiet, told this, make the cathedral-shrug. The tally-keepers, asked if they truly believe it, make the Hem's equivalent — a slight lift of the shoulders performed while looking at the ground, as if the question were audible for kilometers, which it is. The dove-born, on the two or three occasions one has been carried up the mountain to stand at a throat's rim, have felt the cold breath of the shaft on their faces and said, with their mild firmness, that there is nothing there. And once in a great while, on the deepest nights of winter — perfect stillness, perfect cold, the air over the range lying in layers like glass — the plateau hears something. A rumor at the very floor of hearing, less a sound than a lean in the silence, coming from the south, from over the Fence, from the direction of the bowl of milk and embers a week's travel and two hundred years away. The Hem does not go out to listen to it. The Hem wakes, in its half-buried houses, and lies still, each household breathing quietly in the dark, hearing — or declining to decide whether it is hearing — its own grandmothers' grandmothers being pronounced.

The light through all of this is merciless and ordinary: hard blue days, gold evenings, stars in winter so dense the snow reads by them. From the Fence at midday the whole arrangement looks like nothing at all — an empty white tilt, a fringe of stone huts, a woodpile of centuries, a few black birds shouting at a silence the size of a sky.

Then you notice a child at the far end of a stake-line, alone, kilometers out, kneeling to read a pole; and you understand what you are looking at. Not a settlement. Not even an instrument, quite — the basin is the instrument, the great ear grown on the floor of the bowl. This is the other thing an utterance needs, the thing that comes before the voice and is forgotten by everyone but the speaker.

This is the breath being drawn. The child on the white is measuring a winter that will be heard, as pitch, by people not yet born, in a loud city that has already carved the schedule they will keep and fired the plate their season will be charted on. The sentence the basin has been pronouncing since before anyone came is written up here first — in snowfall, in notches, in names dropped down the dark — one indrawn winter at a time; and the Hem, whispering, patient, keeping the water clean, is not listening to the mountain at all.

It is feeding it lines.
