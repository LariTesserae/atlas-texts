**The Borrow**

You reach it by walking out — up the stair, through the town whose lanes the keepers squint in, out the landward gate, and along a road that was never laid so much as *earned*: a sunken lane, worn below the level of the fields on either side, its bed grooved in long parallel channels by sledge-runners over a span of time nobody counts here either. The road is the first clue, if you are the kind of person who reads roads. It runs half a day into the flat country and it is deepest nearest the town, the way a river is deepest nearest its mouth, because for most of its history this road carried weight in only one direction.

You do not see the Borrow until you are nearly standing in it. That is the nature of the place. A hill announces itself for miles; a hole announces itself with absence. The fields simply stop pretending, the road tips gently downward between two shoulders of grass-grown spoil, and the land opens into a shallow bowl a league across, lower than everything around it, silver and green and rose-grey to its far rim, hissing.

The hissing is reeds. The whole bowl is reeds and water — a marsh laid out in a vast loose mosaic of pools, each pool separated from its neighbors by narrow causeways of unworked ground, so that from the lip the Borrow reads like a net thrown over a mirror. And it takes a while, standing there, before the shape of the truth arrives, though the name has been telling you all along. Every pool is a digging. Every causeway is a baulk the diggers left to walk on. This bowl is where the clay came from — all of it. Every brick in the town, every course of every wall, every riser of the kneaded stair, every coil of the Dovewell's dome and every one of its tens of thousands of fist-pressed niches: the earth for all of it was carried up that road, basketful by basketful, sledge-load by sledge-load, out of this ground. The town is the Borrow turned inside out. Somewhere in this bowl, under the water and the reeds, there is a hole the exact size of the Dovewell.

The diggings flooded when the digging slowed. The water table had been waiting under the clay the whole time, patient as anything, and one wet spring it came up through the pit floors and never left. Now the pools hold the same palette as the dome half a day away — some milky with suspended clay, pale rose, plaster-grey; some settled and clear and dark; the baulks between them streaked white and rust and green where seepage has taken the same paths down their flanks for generations, the mineral handwriting identical to the weep-channels on the Dovewell's inner wall, because it is the same clay writing in the same water, and it does not know it has been separated from itself.

Everything about the sound here is the dome inverted. The Dovewell gathers; the Borrow spends. There is no focus, no returning chord — a shout goes out flat over the water and thins into nothing, and the resident sound is the opposite of the dome's layered roar: one enormous horizontal whisper, the wind working a million reed stems at once, ceaseless, directionless, the sound of a place that keeps no echoes because it has too much sky to keep them in. Where a reed has snapped at the right height the wind plays the hollow stub like a pipe, a single thin note wandering the marsh, and the diggers can tell the weather coming by which pools are piping.

And the light — the Dovewell receives one fat honest column of it, pre-softened, rationed through a single eye. The Borrow gets the entire sky, unrationed, and then gets it a second time off the water. Light here arrives twice. On bright days the underside of everything is lit: the bellies of the reeds, the throats of standing birds, the roof-beams of the one house, all of it silvered from below, so that the whole place has the look of something seen near a lamp it is holding under its own chin.

Tools hang here too, where they were last used: reed-knives on pegs driven into the baulks, blades worn to crescents; drying racks; long clay-paddles; punts drawn up half into the reeds, their hulls caulked with — of course — clay. Someone tends this place the way someone tends the other one: light-handedly, more negotiation than maintenance. The Borrow long ago found its own balance too. It fills a little, silts a little, is cut back a little, and persists.

**Who lives in the Borrow**

Start with the reeds, because everything else here is arranged around them the way everything in the dome is arranged around the pigeons.

---

**The reeds** hold the Borrow by continuity of occupation, nation upon nation of them, in numbers that make the pigeon-counts laughable in their turn. They arrived within a generation of the water and they are, in the plainest sense, what the Borrow grows instead of what it lost — the wound's own green scar tissue, renewed every year, leaning all one way under the prevailing wind exactly as the ferns on the Dovewell's floor lean along the route of the light: a whole population bent by the one thing that visits it.

They are also the Borrow's export, and here the two places stop being a comparison and become a single working body. The reed bundles stacked fresh by the Dovewell's stair — the ones the keeper binds into the wet clay when patching the dome — are cut here, in winter, from these pools, and hauled up the sunken road the way the clay itself once was. Which means every pale scab on the dark dome, every mend pressed over a weeping course, has reed in it that grew in the hole the dome was dug from. The wound grows the bandage. The Dovewell is still being built out of the Borrow, a fistful and a reed-bundle at a time, and has never for one year of its long life stopped.

---

**The bittern** is the voice, and there is one at a time, usually — like the snake, like the keeper, a post rather than a population. You will not see it; a bittern in reeds is a philosophical exercise, upright and striped and motionless, indistinguishable from twelve stems of what it is standing in. But at dusk in the calling season it booms, and the boom is the Borrow's one low note — a sound felt in the sternum before it is heard, the marsh clearing its throat, carrying flat across the water for miles because there is nothing here to stop it.

The diggers say that when the bittern booms over the round pool — of which more below — the pool answers, the water shivering in rings that outlive the note. And the keepers, half a day away and underground, claim that on very still mornings the lowest, largest, emptiest niches near the Dovewell's floor stir faintly at a pitch nothing in the chamber is producing, and they say that is the Borrow, arrived through the earth, the clay calling to the place it was taken from. That may just be keepers being keepers, and diggers being diggers. Low frequencies travel strangely, and tuned cavities are credulous listeners. But it does happen, at both ends, and the two families have compared mornings, and the mornings match.

**The herons** stand around the bittern's marsh like the swifts' opposite made flesh. A swift is a creature that cannot stop; a heron is a creature that is mostly stopped. They post themselves in the shallows at the pool-margins, grey as old plaster, and hold still for spans that make visitors uneasy, and then move once, and eat. If the swifts are the Dovewell's timekeepers, the herons are the Borrow's — they keep the other kind of time, the kind that is nearly all interval.

---

**The water-folk** are the night shift, and the Borrow's day and night run inverted to the dome's. The marsh whispers through the daylight and *roars* after dark: frogs in their thousands, a chorus with neighborhoods and etiquette and precedent, layered exactly the way the dome's daytime churring is layered, bass under rattle under single ringing cries — the same instrument, unrolled flat under the sky.

Eels thread the pool-bottoms, arrived — nobody knows how, across half a day of dry fields — from a sea no one in either household will ever visit; the Borrow, too, has currents running out of it toward places this telling will not follow. And in the warm shallows live the grass snakes, clay-colored, water-loving, a whole loose nation of them, and here the diggers keep a piece of lore they trade against the keepers' snake-lore at the winter feast. The keepers say there has always been exactly one snake behind the Dovewell's lowest course, hers before her and hers before that, immemorial. The diggers say: of course there has — because every generation or two an egg goes up the road asleep in a basket of patching clay, knowing or unknowing, and hatches behind the wall, and the keepers find a young snake where the old one was and call it continuity. The wall stays one flesh, the diggers say, and so does its snake. The keepers do not concede this. The snakeskins on the stairwell pegs are not talking. Both stories may be true, which is the usual condition of stories shared between these two places.

---

**The commuters** are the plainest thread of the connection and the most beautiful, because they run on wings, twice a day, without negotiation. At dusk the pools breathe out their insects — the hatch rising off the warm water in soft columns, the Borrow's rain going upward — and the swifts come out from the Dovewell to meet it, screaming low over the reeds, drinking on the wing from the pool-surfaces in long stitched touches. Later the bats arrive to work the same air, having poured up the oculus and crossed the dark fields on the strength of the marsh's abundance. In the mornings and evenings the pigeon-threads pass over, out and back, and some of the fields they feed in are the drained margins of the Borrow itself, green on old spoil.

So the accounting runs like this, and the diggers can recite it flatly, as bookkeeping: the Borrow feeds the sky; the sky feeds the dome; the dome makes its slow black soil; the soil comes back down the road as trade. And at dusk, when the swifts hunt low, the pools fill with their reflections — the Borrow is the one place in the world that watches the braid from below, birds falling upward through the water, and the diggers' children lie on the baulks on summer evenings watching the drowned sky fill with wings that are on their way home to the other house.

---

**The diggers** are that family. One family, one house, the way the Dovewell has one keeper — though the diggers run to children and dogs and noise where the keeper runs to neither, and each household regards the other's arithmetic as bravery. They live on the spoil island: the great grassed hummock in the middle of the bowl, the highest ground for miles, built entirely of overburden and rejected earth — made ground standing on unmade water, the exact complement of the Dovewell's made floor lying on made ground.

Their house is a small coiled-clay dome. Tradition holds that it is the *practice dome* — that this is where dome-builders learned the coil before being trusted with the real work at the other end of the road, and that the family has therefore been living inside a lesson for as long as it has been a family. The corners went round generations ago. Pressed into one interior wall, at head height, is a single spiral of a dozen fist-sized niches — someone's exercise, someone's apprenticeship — and in the niches live a dozen wall-marked pigeons of the old Dovewell lines, barred white and rust. These are the message-birds. A digger can tie a word to a leg at the Borrow and the bird will rise, cross the half-day of fields in an hour, drop down the oculus through three hundred feet of vertical confusion, and land in its own niche in the great wall it was bred from; the keeper keeps a basket of the diggers' line by the stair for the replies. Two households, one road apart, with a wire of wings strung between them, and the wire is alive and preens itself.

Their work is the mirror of the keeper's: cut the reed in winter, when the pools skin over and the stems stand dry; tend the racks; keep the baulks open; and dig the clay. And here is the piece of the work they hold most carefully. One face in all the Borrow is still worked — the *live seam*, a low bank of clean rose-grey clay at the edge of the round pool — and no blade has ever touched it. The patching clay for the Dovewell is dug by hand, fistful by fistful, so that the newest clay pressed into the dome carries knuckle-marks the same as the oldest, and the wall stays one flesh from its first coil to its latest scab. The diggers sing while they dig, low repeating work-songs, and they will tell you these are the elder songs — that the pressing-songs the keepers credit to the builders were these same songs, carried up the road in the same baskets as the clay. The pitch of the song tells the digger the wetness of the seam; a dry seam sings sharp. So the chord the dome returns to a clap and the songs sung into the pit are, by the diggers' reckoning, one music heard at either end of a road, and the wall does not remember the builders' singing so much as continue it.

Trade goes back down the road in the other direction: the keeper's dried grey mushrooms; guano for the island garden, which is the richest half-acre in the flat country and grows vegetables of a frankness that unsettles the townsfolk; squabs for the winter feast, when the keeper walks out — squinting, abbreviated, vaguely seasick under all that sky — and sleeps one night a year in the practice dome, in the noise of a family, and reportedly sleeps well, because a coiled dome full of breathing bodies is a coiled dome full of breathing bodies.

And on the island there is a fig. It was struck from a pruning of the Dovewell's ambitious sapling, carried out in a clay pot years ago, and it is the keepers' standing argument continued by other means: if the fig cannot be allowed the dome, its line can be given the island, and patience can be answered with patience. The diggers point out, mildly, that when their fig fruits, the pigeons will eat the figs, and the pigeons go home down the oculus, and the argument will simply start over on the floor of the Dovewell, seed by seed, forever. The older diggers, like the older keepers, seem to find this prospect satisfying rather than alarming.

---

**The round pool** is set apart, and everyone who lives here sets it apart. It lies at the Borrow's heart, beside the live seam: the oldest digging, the deepest, and the only round one — dug in the first age of the work, when the diggers made holes the way the builders made niches, by feel, in curves, before anyone thought to square anything. It took generations to dig and one wet spring to fill. Nothing is taken from it now. Its reeds are not cut. The diggers can stand on any baulk in the marsh and tell you which building each pool is — *that one is the granary; those two, the bridges; that long one, the seaward wall* — because a pool here is not a pool, it is a building's absence holding water. The round pool is the Dovewell's.

It is the dome's negative, lying open-mouthed under the sky, and the sky treats it accordingly. On still nights the moon crosses it slowly, a migrating brightness traveling the dark water exactly as the sun-column travels the dome's floor half a day away, and the eels gather under the moon-path the way the ferns lean into the light-path, an audience following a slow performer. Stand at its rim on such a night and you are standing where the crown of the dome would be, looking down into a hemisphere of stars, into the inside of the Dovewell as the clay remembers it — the one hollow the whole standing world was lifted out of, keeping the shape of what it gave.

The bittern booms there, mostly. The diggers consider this fitting, and do not explain further, and it does not need explaining.

---

**And the seam.** The keepers say the dome is the Dovewell's senior resident, and they are right, and the diggers make the matching claim without any sense of competing: the seam was here before the dome, before the road, before the first basket went up on the first back, and it will be here after. Everything else in the Borrow turns over — reeds, eels, herons, frogs, diggers, even the pools slowly shallowing as the marsh does its patient work of filling what was emptied. The seam simply continues, sleeping, and everything the Dovewell will ever be patched with is already lying in it, unpressed, unsung, waiting for a fist.

The diggers put it this way, and mean it plainly, however visitors hear it: the Borrow is the part of the Dovewell that stayed home. The dome is this clay stood upright and taught to sing; the marsh is that singing's absence, flooded, learning the answering part. Between them runs the whole circulation — clay up and reed up, guano down and mushrooms down, swifts out at dusk and home by dark, bats out by night and home by dawn, a snake's egg asleep in a basket, a word tied to a pink leg, a song at the seam and a chord in the wall — one body's slow breath, drawn through a road half a day long.

Nothing here was ever borrowed that is not still being returned, a wingbeat, a reed, a fistful at a time. The repayment has been running for centuries and will run for centuries more, and neither household is keeping count, and the Borrow — which holds the sky all day in ten hundred mirrors and gives it back every evening full of other people's birds — shows no sign at all of wanting to close the account.
