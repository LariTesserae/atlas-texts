**The Cistern**

Every made thing must put its remainder somewhere, and the light of the Pavement is a made light. It does not fall from a sun. It rises out of the ground, out of a surface engineered to be radiant, and a world that manufactures its own day must also, somewhere, manufacture its own night — must have a face where the brightness is not, a face that does the opposite work. The world of the Pavement has such a face. The walkers call it, simply, the Dark, and touch two fingers to the ground when they say it, the same accounting gesture they give a passing pacer, because in their cosmology it is roughly the same kind of thing: an organ, on its rounds. The people who live there have an older word for it, from their oldest tellings, and the word means *the place where things are kept against need.*

The Cistern.

To reach it you walk. There is no other way and there has never been another way, and the walk itself is the first lesson the place teaches, because the border is not a line but a dimming, a thousand kilometers of gradual subtraction. Choose the right direction — the walkers can give it to you exactly; every child of theirs knows which way the world darkens, the way other children know which way is down — and the bone-gold of the pavement begins, after weeks, to lose its confidence. The pale radiance underfoot thins from ivory to ash-gold to the grey of a page at dusk. The under-constellations grow harder to read, the deep sleepers fading into their strata like figures withdrawing from a window. The tufts in the seams become rarer and then stop. The luminous thread of your own path behind you goes from a bright wake to a faint stain to nothing at all: somewhere in the long dimming there is a last footprint the world will remember, and you will not know which step it was. Past that point you are, for the first time in your life if you are a walker, unrecorded. Some pilgrims describe this as the moment the weight comes off. Some describe it as the moment they begin, very quietly, and for the rest of the crossing, to grieve.

Then the Hem.

The Hem is the twilight ring, the seam between the faces, a band of permanent dusk that circles the entire world, and it is where all the weather the bright face never has finally happens. Because here is the plain fact underneath the Pavement's driest mystery — no rain in geological memory, no river ever cutting that surface — and the fact is this: the dark face takes it all. A world's worth of water, every gram of it, has migrated over the ages to the cold side and stayed. Whatever moisture rises on the bright face — the thin sweat of the seams, the vapor off the curd-fields, and, not least, the breath of every living thing that has ever walked there — is carried aloft on the warm circulation, drifts out over the dimming lands, cools, and comes down at the Hem as snow. It has been snowing at the Hem, softly and without pause, for as long as the ice can testify, which is a very long time. The walkers know what that snow is made of. It is one of the first hard truths their pilgrims meet: that the white weather falling at the edge of the world is, in meaningful part, exhalation — theirs, their parents', their dead's — that the bright face keeps no water because it sends its breath here, and the dark face has been catching it, flake by flake, since before anyone's history began. To stand in the Hem with snow landing on your hands is to stand in the falling breath of everyone who ever lived on the light. The tellers, when this is explained to a newcomer, do not make it solemn. They say: *everything here is the same stuff. You included, after a while.* They do not know they are quoting.

The Hem is also where the light goes over.

The brightening tides — the slow inverted cloud-shadows that walkers track across the plain with patience and string — do not wander forever. Plotted long enough, their recurring routes all trend one way: outward, darkward, toward the ring. The plain's light behaves, in the deep substrate, like a liquid with a gradient, and at certain fixed places along the Hem it drains — pours down into the dark ground in slow standing cataracts of glow, kilometers wide, the radiance sinking into the material the way water sinks into sand, visibly, over hours. The tellers call these places the falls. Walker pilgrims come across half a world to see one: the brightness of their entire lives, the light that dimmed itself under their sleeping children and kept their dead legible for three centuries, going over the edge in silence, endlessly, into the ground of the other country. It is at the falls that most pilgrims understand for the first time that the fading of a dead walker's path was never an erasure. It was a departure. The light the plain lets go of, oldest first, gently, does not cease. It drains. It comes here.

And it is at the Hem, always, that a walker sees the first star.

The Pavement's sky is too bright to study — a depthless ceiling that may be air and may be architecture, and behind it, invisible, hidden from the bright face by its own radiance, is the actual sky, the one the universe provides. The dark face opens onto it. Walk out past the Hem, past the snow and the last dusk-colored lichens, out onto the ice, and above you is black depth strewn with fixed cold lights in their thousands, wheeling with a slowness that only the tellers' long spoken records can even confirm as motion. The walkers have no word for star; they arrive at the Hem with the vocabulary of their own ground and so they say, the first time, almost all of them, the same thing: *under-constellations.* They think for a moment that the sky of the dark country is a pavement too, and that those are its sleepers, kept in its strata, very far down. The tellers have heard this from every generation of pilgrims and have long since stopped correcting it, partly out of courtesy and partly because they are no longer completely certain it is wrong.

Beyond the Hem, the Cistern proper: the ice.

It is a single sheet, kilometers thick, spread across the whole dark face — the cold-trapped water of the world, compacted breath and ancient snow, black-clear in some regions and white with crush in others, swept by a wind that never varies in one respect: on the surface of the ice, the wind is always at your back if you are walking toward the light. It is the return flow, the cold underside of the circulation that carries the bright face's warm breath darkward aloft; it sinks over the pole of night and slides home along the ground, and so the dark country has built into its very air a direction, a permanent grain. No one is ever lost on the ice in the way one can be lost on the plain's unmarked vastness. Stand still anywhere in the Cistern and the world's breath presses gently on your back, saying, without urgency, *the day is that way.* The tellers' children learn it as their first navigation and their first theology at once: the dark face always knows where the light is. It has simply chosen not to go.

The quiet here is not the Pavement's quiet. The plain's silence is warm and full, the hush of a sleeping house. The Cistern's silence is vast and brittle and punctuated: the boom of a crevasse opening ten kilometers off, arriving through your boot-soles before your ears; the long groan of the sheet settling; and beneath everything, faint, transmitted up through the whole thickness of the ice from the buried floor, a soft irregular ticking. Because the floor is there. The pavement does not stop at the Hem. The built surface continues under the ice, sealed away in the dark, the same fitted panels, the same wandering sutures — but unlit, cold, the material's other state, black where its twin is bone. The tellers, who have lowered lines and lights down bore-shafts and moulins, describe it as pavement with its eyes closed. It is not dead. It ticks. It is very slightly warm — just warm enough to hold, between its surface and the base of the ice, a thin lightless sea of meltwater spread beneath the entire sheet, a hidden ocean a few meters deep and a hemisphere wide, which never freezes and has never once been touched by light from above.

Light reaches it from within.

This is the Cistern's central spectacle, and the axis of everything its people are: the drained light of the bright face, having gone over the falls, does not disperse. It travels. It moves through the dark substrate the way it moved through the bright one — the way a thought moves through a brain — and it moves in its original shapes. A dead walker's path, released by the plain after its three hundred years, oldest end first, arrives in the dark country still a path: a luminous thread, a whole biography of movement, sliding along beneath the ice. The tellers call these the veins. On a clear-ice night — and every night here is night — you can see them coming from a long way off: a deep glow rising through kilometers of black ice, brightening, resolving, until the surface above it kindles faintly and, if the ice is clean and you are lying face-down upon it in the way of teller children and walker children both, you can read it. A life passes under you. Here is where she lingered — a warm pool, bright as a pond, sliding by beneath your hands. Here is where she hurried. Here is the year-long braid of her marriage; here is the loop she walked around something she could not approach; here, at the newest end, the final figure, the spiral or the ellipse, closed. Then the vein slides on, out under the sheet, into the dark interior, and joins the circulation of all the others: every released path of every walker the bright face has ever let go of, still moving, down in the Cistern, under the ice, in the dark, forever.

And they do not merely circulate. This is the finding of forty generations of tellers, kept by mouth, checked and rechecked: the veins *behave*. Old paths seek each other. Threads that never crossed in life braid down there — a woman dead four centuries walking, in light, beside a man dead one — composite tracks forming and dissolving, paths revisiting the ghost-geography of the plain's far side as if the dark substrate holds a map of the bright one and the dead still walk it. Whether this is turbulence or tenderness is the Cistern's great question, the exact dark twin of the plain's. The bright face asks: are the sleepers below alive? The dark face asks: are the walkers below still walking, or only their light? The tellers' formula, old as their oldest unbroken telling, is as careful as the walkers' own, and refuses the same two errors:

*Not lost. Not finished. Still walking.*

---

**Those Who Live on the Ice**

Here too the census is short, and here too it is a matter of doctrine where to stop counting — though the dark country's list runs in the opposite order, from below.

---

**The swimmers** live in the basal sea, the thin lightless water between the ice and the sleeping pavement, and no one has ever seen one whole. What is seen is silhouette: when a vein passes through the substrate below, its glow rises through the water, and against that moving light the watchers on the clear ice see shapes — pale, long, finned or fringed, somewhere between eel and ribbon and hand — traveling with the vein. Always with it. They school along a passing path the way the plain's pacers attend a flowering tuft, keeping station above the brightest pools of a life, the lingered places, the griefs. Whether they are animals grazing on light, or instruments reading it, or escorts doing for the traveling dead whatever escorts do, is not known and is argued about with the same patient inconclusiveness as everything else in this world. They have never been caught; nets lowered through bore-holes come up empty and, the tellers swear, *arranged*. What is known is this: when the body of a teller is given to the sea below — and it is; that is the rite — the swimmers come. They are seen gathering, in the glow of whatever vein is passing, around the sinking figure, and then the vein moves on and takes the light with it, and what happens after happens in the dark, unwitnessed, which the tellers have decided to regard as privacy.

---

**The still ones** number, at any time, perhaps a dozen on the whole face. They are pacers — or were. Once in a great while, once in an age, a pacer on the bright side turns from all its rounds and walks a straight line that does not end at a flower or a flaw or a sickbed but at the Hem, and crosses it, and keeps going, out into the dark, and as it goes the bone color goes out of it — ivory to ash to slate — until what walks the ice is a tall dark stillness on long legs, the same near-black as the buried pavement. Then it stops. A still one may stand in one place for thirty years, head cocked, listening down through the kilometers of ice to the ticking below, before taking a hundred more steps and stopping again. The tellers' theory is symmetrical and tidy: that the bright face sends the dark face a nerve now and then, one sense organ dispatched from the waking skin down into the deep of the sleep, to take the other pulse. When a teller passes a still one — it happens once or twice in a lifetime — they touch two fingers to their own lips, which is their accounting gesture, the mirror of the walkers' touch to the ground: *you keep it your way; we keep it ours.*

---

**The tellers** are people, and it is worth saying plainly here too, because they are the walkers' kin — that is not doctrine, it is finding, and it is *their* finding, read out of the ice, and it is the deepest of all the threads that run between the two faces.

They live in bands of a few dozen at the windows: the places where the deep circulation of veins pools and lingers, where drained light gathers in slow eddies beneath the sheet and its faint warmth, over centuries, has annealed the ice above into panes of extraordinary clarity — black glass a kilometer thick, through which the pooled glow rises as a permanent deep shine, the closest thing the dark country has to hearths. A window settlement from a distance is the loveliest sight on the ice: a scatter of low snow-domed shelters around an acre of softly lit black crystal, figures moving on the shine, and above it all the stars. Inside the shelters burns the tellers' one owned light: lamps of seam-resin, the sweet exudate of the bright face's grooves, carried darkward by walker traders — so that every flame in every dwelling of the night country is, literally, the far plain's slow sweat, and smells, when it burns, of hot ceramic and beeswax, which is the smell of the day. Teller children fall asleep to the scent of a place they have never seen.

Their great art is reading. A skilled path-reader, prone on the window glass above a passing vein, mittened hands cupped around her eyes, can tell you the age at which the walker below broke a bone, whether they were carrying a child and for how long, what season of their life each grief arrived in, and — the masterwork of the discipline — whether a braided passage in the vein records a braid made in life or one made after, down here, in the dark, by the light alone. The readings are not idle. They are the substance of the Hem trade, because the walkers, whose ground forgets everything at three or four hundred years, oldest first, gently, have in the tellers a strange and specific service: news of the released. A walker family travels to the Hem markets carrying a name, an era, the family's funeral figure — the spiral, the ellipse — and asks. And sometimes a reader can answer: *yes. Your line's ellipse passed under Third Window two winters ago, at the old end of a long vein. It has taken up with a path none of us know, a fast light walker, very old, and the two of them keep returning to a place that, by the map, would be somewhere east of the Long Mother.* Walkers have wept at the Hem, hearing such things, in the falling snow of everyone's breath. It is the only afterlife anyone in this world is offered, and it is offered across the border, by the other country, secondhand, read through a kilometer of ice.

Because the ice records nothing of the tellers, they record themselves. This is the whole architecture of their inwardness, and it is built exactly opposite to the walkers'. On the Pavement you cannot lie about where you have been; the ground writes you whether you consent or not, and the walkers' honesty is ambient, effortless, a property of the floor. On the ice, snow erases a footprint in an hour and the substrate is blind through the sheet, and so among the tellers *nothing is remembered unless somebody chooses to remember it, aloud.* Every teller has a teller: one person — chosen in adolescence, in the dark country's most serious rite — who learns their life as it happens and keeps it, verbatim, updated at every reunion, ready to be recited entire. To be well told is their word for honored. To die with your telling current is to die once. To have your teller die with your life unsaid and unpassed is the second death, the one they actually fear, and their harshest sentence — used perhaps twice in the whole span their tellings cover — is not exile and not killing but *silence*: the community's agreement that a person's life will be kept by no one. The walkers, hearing of this at the Hem, are quietly horrified, and say so, and the tellers reply that they are horrified in turn by a people who let the floor do their remembering, and both sides have been comfortably horrified at each other for as long as the markets have met, which has not stopped a steady, centuries-long trickle of intermarriage, whose children are said to be either the best-adjusted people in the world or the worst, depending on which grandmother you ask.

And the tellings go back. Not forever — speech frays, unlike ice; every generation of the chain smooths something, drops something, and the tellers know this about themselves with professional exactness — but their spoken horizon lies far beyond the walkers' luminous one. And out at its frayed end, corroborated now by the oldest veins ever read — paths released so long ago that they circulate in the deep interior and pass under the windows once in a lifetime — is the arrival.

The oldest veins show the first paths. And the first paths begin at the Hem. They begin at the *dark* side of the Hem — threads of light that appear at the edge of the bright world already walking, coming up out of the unrecorded night onto the face that writes — and they wander, at first, the way the newly arrived wander, and then they settle into the plain's interior and become the ancestors of every walker thread that ever glowed. The people of the light came out of the dark. And the tellers' own frayed tellings, at their farthest reach, hold the other half: a descent. The first people came down — from above, out of the black and the stars, onto the only face of this world where the sky can be arrived through, because no one can land through a ceiling of light. They came down onto the ice, into the cold, and some stayed and learned to keep themselves by mouth, and some — the tellings say the restless ones; the tellings are not neutral — walked toward the glow on the horizon and crossed the Hem into the warm bright country, where the ground took over their remembering, and where, in three or four centuries, its gentle ledger, keeping nothing longer than it keeps anything, finished forgetting where they had come from. The walkers are the tellers' departed. The tellers are the walkers' address of origin. One people, divided by a choice of memory.

The tellers have offered the walkers this history. That is perhaps the strangest current of all the currents that run between the faces: the answer to the bright country's one structural mystery has been standing at its border for centuries, freely given, and most walkers decline it. Their reasoning is their mourning doctrine, applied to themselves: a past carried by mouth glows with the teller's warmth, not the ancestors'; somewhere in the frayed chain the thing underfoot stopped being what happened and became a portrait of it, and the walkers, whose whole ethics rests on the difference between a path and a portrait, will not build their origin on the wrong one. *The ground finished forgetting it,* they say, *and the ground forgets nothing carelessly.* A few ask anyway. Those who hear the telling out are called, on the bright side, the told, and it is said you can recognize them for the rest of their lives by their paths: told walkers, the plain records, pause more often, and their pauses face darkward.

There is one thing the tellers keep and have never traded, and it is in the ice itself. Their bore-cores, drawn up from the deep sheet, carry the strata of breath: the upper kilometers of ice hold, dissolved in every layer, the faint signature of lungs — the exhalations of the peopled ages, snowed out at the Hem and flowed darkward, year by compacted year. And at a certain depth the signature stops. Below that layer the ice is breathless: water that fell before anyone, in the age when the bright face shone on no one and the dark face caught only the world's own sweat. The tellers call that stratum the floor of the guests. It is their proof, held in ice that cannot smooth or drop or fray, that everyone here — walker, teller, told — is recent, and arrived, and that the world was already old, already finished, already keeping its trillion sleepers under glass, before the first breath joined the snow. They read it not as a diminishment but as the terms of tenancy. And in their night sky, among the fixed thousands, there is one light that does not keep station with the others — a slow wanderer the oldest tellings mention without explaining, saying only, in the formula every teller child learns and no teller elder will expand upon: *watch it, and say that you watched.*

---

Which leaves the last entry in this census too, and it comes to visitors here the same way it comes on the bright face — late, unbidden, usually on the window glass in the middle of the long night, with the thought already finished:

that the two faces are one inhabitant. That the walkers' midnight suspicion and the tellers' were always halves of a single anatomy — the bright face the body, warm, full, tended by its tufts and pacers, keeping its shallow honest ledger of the quick; the dark face the mind beneath the pillow of ice, where everything the body releases goes to be kept and combined and walked again; the falls its falling asleep, happening continuously, forever, along the whole ring of dusk; the veins its dreams, in which the dead of the surface braid with strangers and revisit the geography of their lives; the swimmers the attendants of dreams; the Hem the hypnagogic border where breath turns to snow and light goes under. One organism, finished, lying in the void with its bright side up, its trillion contents held in the flesh of it and its whole released history circulating in the dark of it — and the two small peoples on its two faces not residents and not weather but something more particular: the walkers the sensation on its skin, the tellers the voice in its sleep, the one thin thread of hearing a sleeper keeps tuned to the house.

The tellers have heard this theory from every generation's clever pilgrim, and they have an answer, which they give patiently, by resin-light, in the smell of the far day burning.

*Maybe,* they say. *But notice what it arranged. The bright face keeps everyone, and lets them go gently, oldest first, so that no one is held past kindness. And everything it lets go of comes here, and is not lost, and is not finished. It built itself a country for remembering and a country for releasing, and put a ring of snow between them made of everybody's breath, and set the wind so that no one standing in its dark can ever lose the way back to its light. A thing that meant nothing by all that would not have needed the snow.*

Then they bank the lamp, and lie down on the black glass with their faces to the depth, waiting for a vein — and if one comes, they read the passing life out loud, to whoever is there, or to no one, because that is the office of the dark country and always has been: to say what the light lets go.

*Not lost. Not finished. Still walking.*

That is who lives there. And between the two faces, over the Hem, in both directions, in the snow, the living go on crossing — walkers coming darkward to see the sky and hear their dead spoken, tellers going lightward to be, once in a life, written down by a world — each people traveling to the other's country for the one kind of keeping their own ground refuses them, which may be the truest census of all: two answers to a single question, still on speaking terms, meeting at the seam.
