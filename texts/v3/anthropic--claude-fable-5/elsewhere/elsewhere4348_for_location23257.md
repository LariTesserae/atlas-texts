**The Slaking Stair**

Stand at the southern lip of the Quickening Shelf, where the air thins back to honest cold, and face into the wind. The wind has a direction, and the direction has a history. The warm wet fog that feeds the shelf does not condense out of nowhere; it arrives — a slow aerial river, mineral-heavy, running the same course day and night, season after season, up the raw shoulder of the unfinished mountain and into the bowl. Feedstock is freight. Freight comes from somewhere. Follow the river backward — down the shoulder, off the mountain's foot, out across the wide gray basin where the ground is glazed with pale waxy traces (the wind's road, marked by everything it has spilled in passing) — nine days on foot, or a day and a night if you are a fog — and you arrive at the source, which is a mountain going the other way.

You stop hearing the world before you see it. That is the first thing. The basin has its own small noises — grit, wind, your feet — and then, over the course of an afternoon's walking, they begin to feel rude, and then they begin to feel *loud*, and then you crest a low rise and understand that you have been walking into a hush the way one walks into weather. The Slaking Stair does not roar the way the shelf roars. It exhales.

It was a whole mountain once. What remains is the bottom third: a vast stepped mass, five broad terraces descending like the base of a ziggurat whose upper stories have been unwritten, each terrace a quarter-mile deep, each riser a wall of old stone forty or sixty feet high. The summit is not fallen or broken. It is *gone*, and gone in a particular way — the terraces have no rubble at their feet, no scree, no wreckage. Nothing here has collapsed. Everything here has been *let go of*, molecule by molecule, drawn up out of the rock into the warm fog that stands over the topmost terrace in a permanent pale column, leaning always north-northeast, always toward the basin, always toward the far young mountain that is putting the material back on. The Stair makes its own weather and exports every ounce of it. One place in the world rises a finger-width a day. This is the place that pays for it, a finger-width a day, and the books balance to the width of a finger.

Up close, the terrace walls are smooth in the wrong way — the smoothness of something poured, being unpoured. And they are translucent, deeply so, deeper than the shelf's walls, because the stone here is old and cured and the light can travel in it. What the light finds in there is the second thing you understand about the Stair, and it changes everything you thought you knew about the shelf: the walls are full of *rooms*. Sealed dimples the size of a cupped hand, in their thousands, at every depth. Small domed shapes, five-lobed, entombed in rings and clusters. Straight seam-lines, spent now, silver-gray and dark, running in the same too-straight geometries as the gold lines in the growing rock nine days downwind. The Slaking Stair was grown. It was a quickening place once, ages ago — it had its loud season, its pools, its screaming tenants, its finger-width mornings — and then it was finished, and then it was a mountain for longer than anything can remember, and now it is being taken back, wall by wall, and everything that was ever sealed into it is being handed out again in reverse order. The fog over the basin is not weather and not freight. It is a mountain in transit, being mailed to its next address, and the mail includes the archive.

The sound of the place, inside the hush, is the sound of release. A continuous fine tick, everywhere, like a cooling stove the size of a county — laminations letting go of each other after ten thousand years of embrace. Beneath that, a soft ceaseless breath: stone going directly to vapor at the active faces, a sound like rain falling *out of* rock. And every hour or so, somewhere on the terraces, a long sigh as some deep structure yields all at once — not a crash, never a crash, because the material lifts as it loosens, powder rising instead of falling, a slow gray flame of dust going up into the column and gone downwind before it can settle. The Stair is the only place in the world where things fall upward as a matter of course, and after a few days you stop flinching at it.

Water runs everywhere, and it is the opposite of the shelf's water: thin, cold, hungry, undersaturated, weeping out of the walls wherever the recession reopens ancient channels. Because the plumbing is still down there. Hair-fine conduits that once served living roots — the same courteous engineering that waters the shelf's one fern today — are being unsealed by the retreating stone, and they still deliver, faithfully, to root-systems that died before the basin was a basin. Springs feed pools, and the pools do not shrink here. They *deepen*, and worse, they *rise* — because the stone will not dissolve beneath standing water any faster than around it, and so the oldest pools have been left behind by the descending ground, held up on smooth stone stems like goblets, some of them thirty feet in the air, brim-full, trembling faintly, waiting.

The rules of the place, for a patient observer, are the shelf's rules read in a mirror. The ground you stand on at dawn is measurably lower by dusk. Objects left overnight are found in the morning gently *loosened* — knots slipped, seams opened, laces lying in loops, anything joined coming quietly unjoined, released rather than claimed. Near the dead seams, dropped water falls a fraction fast, as though the place's grip on physics is being backed off to zero a turn at a time. Living things are never taken: the stone will not dissolve beneath a heartbeat, which sounds like mercy until you see what it does to the sleepers — but we will get to the sleepers. And once or twice a day, with no warning, the whole Stair goes silent inside its silence — every tick and sigh and trickle stopping in the same instant, for three or four seconds. If you have stood on both mountains with a good clock, you know something the animals of neither place can know: the pause falls *at the same moment* in both. Nine days apart by foot, a breath apart by whatever is doing the measuring. The world saves one draft, and it saves everything at once.

Then the ticking resumes, the walls breathe, the fog leans northeast, and the mountain goes on becoming a letter.

**Who Remains on the Slaking Stair**

Begin with the smallest, because they are the couriers, and without them the light could not leave.

**The tithe-moths** hatch in the seams. Not near them — *in* them: their eggs are laid in the hairline gaps where spent instruction-lines have gone dark, and the larvae feed on something in the dead channels that is not quite mineral and not quite anything else, and when they emerge — gray, soft, a thumb-joint long, wings like wet ash — each one carries in its body a single mote of gold. The last light of the seams does not simply go out. It is *collected*. A tithe-moth lives one night. It climbs the nearest wall on foot, opens its wings at the top, enters the column, and is gone downwind, and somewhere over the basin it dies and the mote rides on without it, and on clear nights the aerial river between the two mountains glitters, faintly, end to end — a procession of instruction traveling in bodies, the blueprint of the old mountain going to sit in the walls of the new one. The gold pulses in the Quickening Shelf's growing stone have all made this trip. Every line of light the shelf obeys arrived as a moth's last errand. The animals of the shelf were never told this. Neither were the moths.

**The wane-snails** are the grazing class, and they are the gleaners' opposite in every particular. Where the gleaners are fat, complacent, and prone to entombment, the wane-snails are lean, coiled, patient as clergy, and they follow the *dissolving* front the way gleaners follow the growing one — pressed to the active faces, drinking the stone at the exact moment of its letting-go, when it is neither rock nor fog. Their shells are built of what they borrow, and the borrowing is on record: when a wane-snail dies, its shell goes to vapor within a day, reclaimed, called in, and the soft body is left lying on the terrace like a small gray comma. Nothing here is ever entombed. Everything here is eventually *audited*. The snails seem untroubled by the arrangement. They chalk the walls with slow silver trails that outlast them by a week, and then the walls the trails are written on go up into the sky.

**The sexton-wrens** attend the exhumations. Because the dead come out. That is the Stair's great daily event, its weather report, its news: as each wall recedes, whatever the old growing-place sealed into it an age ago approaches the surface — first a shadow deep in the translucence, then a shape a hand-span down, then shallower every morning until, one gray dawn, it lies free on the terrace, ages old and perfect, released. Domed five-lobed grazers of a species older than the gleaners but unmistakably their kin. Small birds with one too many wing-joints, folded forever. Mineral casts of eggs in reopened nursery-dimples — and the reopened dimples themselves, thousands of them, uncupping from the walls one by one, each a finished family from a season no living thing remembers. The sexton-wrens — small, umber, quick, with chisel-scaled feet their far cousins on the shelf would recognize — nest in the reopened nurseries and eat what the walls give up, and they are the only carrion-eaters anywhere with a backlog measured in millennia. They have a habit that may be nothing: before feeding on an exhumed body, a wren will stand on it for a moment, wings half-open, motionless. It may be balance. It may be testing for warmth. It has the look of something else, and the keeper's ledgers decline to name it.

**The sleepers** are the Stair's tragedy and its comedy in one body. Badger-sized, dense-furred, slow-hearted, they estivate for years at a stretch in dens dug into the terrace floors — and the stone, obeying its one inviolable courtesy, will not dissolve beneath a heartbeat. So the ground goes down around them. A sleeper that dens for a decade wakes on a column ten years tall, blinking, marooned above a terrace that has politely left without it. The young ones wake often and climb down chagrined. The old ones sleep long, and the Stair's middle terraces are studded with their pillars — some occupied, a faint pulse of warmth at the top; some capped only with a curled shape that stopped needing the courtesy, after which the pillar, released from its obligation, breathes itself into the fog in a season. The tallest standing pillar is four times the height of the walls. Whatever is asleep up there has been asleep since before the keeper's grandmother kept the ledgers, and its heartbeat — you can feel it if you lay a palm on the stem — comes once a minute, enormous and unhurried, a tenant the landlord will wait for as long as it takes.

**The tree** stands on the second terrace, and it is the oldest living thing on either mountain, and it is why there is a fern on the shelf. A colossus, half-dead, one flank silvered and bare, the other still putting out dark fronds — for it is a tree-fern, or was, before size and eons made the word ridiculous — and it stands on a plinth of knotted root-stone a hundred feet high, because the process that will not take ground from under a heartbeat extends the same clause to sap. The plinth is plumbed. The ancient channels still run. One new frond a season, each one mineral-tipped, each one releasing spores into the column, and the column takes them northeast with everything else. Spores have been crossing the basin for as long as the wind has. Nearly all of them land on ground too old, too finished, too dry. One of them, some seasons ago, landed on ground so young it had not yet learned to refuse anything, and put down roots, and the young mountain grew a pedestal around them and ran it hair-fine channels, because that clause, too, made the trip — carried in gold, in a moth, in the dark. The hip-high fern by the shelf's southern lip is this tree's child and does not know it, in the way that nothing on the shelf knows it is furnishing its rooms with a dead mountain's furniture.

**The pools on stems** hold the bones. Warm-blooded, lung-breathing swimmers lived in the old growing-place's waters, as one still lives in the new one's — the kinship is not conjecture; the skeletons say it plainly, two meters long, ribbed, built for warm murk. Their pools rode the terraces down for ages, held up on stems as the ground left, and one by one the water went too thin and too cold and too hungry, and the walls have been handing the bones back ever since. The keeper lays them out on slate on the fourth terrace, in order of exhumation, an entire species emerging from stone in reverse. The ledgers record one anomaly, four keeperships back, in the driest entry in the archive: *one departure, alive, direction downwind.* Whether the glossy flank that rolls in the shelf's largest pool belongs to a creature that was always there — a pond-dweller carried uphill on rising ground — or to the one that climbed down off a dying stem in a wet season and walked nine days across the basin, following the only wind in the world that leads to rising water, is a matter on which the keeper and the observer, who have never met, hold opposite and equally unprovable views. It is the only entry in either of their records marked with a question.

**The heron-things** roost here. Of course they do. There are eleven of them on the Stair — the rookery is a row of freestanding spires on the third terrace, left behind the way the pillars are left, though no one saw what slept there — and here their heads make sense, or as much sense as they will ever make: a head that shows the near future is a modest instrument on a mountain being born, and a working tool on a mountain being unmade, where the future of every wall is legible and imminent. They stand at the active faces for hours, shimmer angled at the stone, watching what is about to come out of it before it comes. They attend every exhumation. They read the dead — there is no better verb; they stand over each released body with the shimmer lowered like a lens, for exactly as long as it takes, and then they move on, and the sexton-wrens move in. One of them commutes: nine days northeast, always walking, always arriving from the south, to stand at pools that are the opposite of these and, once, at a black post. Which brings us to the Yard.

**The Yard of Posts** occupies the lowest terrace, the oldest ground, the floor the Stair will end on. Forty-one slim posts of black material stand there, hip-high, each in its ring of permanent gap, in no arrangement anyone has made sense of. They were in the walls once — the ledgers have watched them come out, one every few decades, the stone receding around each post's column of refused space until the post stands free and the terrace lowers away beneath it, leaving it exactly hip-high, always hip-high, on a little pedestal of gap. Nothing has ever seen a post move. There are more of them every century. The keeper's count of the shelf's marks, relayed by basin traders, stands at one post, three plates, and an alcove — which means either the new mountain is early in its furnishing, or forty of these were earned across the old mountain's whole tenancy, mark by mark, for reasons the size of eras. The wrens will not fly over the Yard. The moths route around it. The heron-things walk its rows, sometimes, slowly, like inspectors, like mourners, like readers in a stacks.

**The alcove** is coming down. On the last standing height of the fifth terrace — the topmost, the active front — a recessed square room the size of a cupboard sits flush in the dissolving wall, and it is not empty. A knot of gold lives in it: heart-sized, steady, brightest at its center, the only light on the Stair that never pulses, never drains, never boards a moth. It has ridden that alcove down through the entire unmaking — the ledgers track its altitude the way other archives track kings — and the wall it lives in loses a finger-width a day, and the arithmetic is public: some countable number of years from now, well inside the keeper's lifetime, the alcove will reach the terrace floor, and then the floor, and then there will be no wall left to be recessed into. Downwind, on a wall that grows, an identical alcove rises, empty, always at twice head-height, too clean, waiting, the one apartment with a name on the lease. The heron-things have begun spending more time on the fifth terrace. Last season there were four of them standing below the alcove at once, shimmers raised, watching a future none of them shared with anyone. The tithe-moths have begun laying, for the first time in the ledgers' memory, in the *living* seam that feeds the knot. Everything on the Stair that can read is reading the same sentence, and the sentence is nearly finished, and no one knows the word at the end of it, and the wind blows northeast.

**And the keeper.** Because there is one; there has always been one; the ledgers go back through nineteen hands. The keeper lives on the fourth terrace in a house of slaked slate — stone the mountain has already released is the one material in the world neither mountain will ever claim again, spent coin, honorably discharged, and the basin traders carry it downwind by the sledge-load, and every unclaimable slate on which a certain observer keeps notes at the foot of a growing mountain was quarried from this hush by this hand or an earlier one. The keeper records the exhumations, lays out the bones, counts the posts, measures the descent, and once a year hands a trader a written slate addressed simply *downwind*, and once a year receives one back covered edge to edge in another hand's small urgent script — a hand the keeper taught to write, years ago, before its owner stood one evening watching the column lean northeast and asked the question the Stair raises in everyone and answers in no one: *where does it all go?* — and walked into the basin to find out. The keeper has never made the trip. The keeper says the Stair also requires witness; that someone must sign for what is given out, as someone downwind signs for what is delivered. Twice a day, for three or four seconds, the whole hush goes silent around the house, and the keeper stops writing and waits, and knows — this is the one comfort the ledgers permit themselves — that nine days away, in a deafening bowl of wet new stone, the same pause is falling on the same instant around someone doing the same waiting.

Then the ticking resumes. The walls breathe out. The moths climb the stone with their freight of gold, the fog leans toward its address, and the old mountain goes on doing the only thing either mountain has ever done, which is not building and was never destroying: it is *forwarding* — matter, water, instruction, spores, bones, tenants, light — one finger-width a day, one moth at a time, across a gray basin from the world that was to the world that is being written, and keeping, in a hush the size of a county, the receipts.
