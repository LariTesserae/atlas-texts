## The Mere at the Wall's Foot

There is a place at the bottom of the same sky that is the size of a parish.

You reach it — anyone can reach it — by a cart track along the valley floor, rutted and easy, through hay meadows and over a plank bridge, and that is the first difference. No stair, no cloud to surface through, no test. The place lets you in the way flat country always does. What it asks of you comes later, and it asks it of the neck: because from the moment you cross the bridge you are walking toward something your eye keeps refusing, the way the eye at the top refuses the void — a presence instead of an absence, a fullness where the shelf hangs over an emptiness — and you find yourself looking at your feet, at the ruts, at anything near and small, rather than at the thing that owns the eastern half of the world.

The people who live here do not call the mountain Vantar. Vantar is a name used two ridgelines away, by folk who can stand back far enough to see a summit, a shape, a whole. Here there is no whole. Here the mountain goes up out of the meadows and does not stop, and you cannot see around it or over it or, on most days, even to the top of it, and so it is not a mountain at all. It is the Wall. You name a mountain when you can see all of it. You do not name the side of the world.

**The Wall.** Two vertical miles of stone, standing on the edge of the parish like weather that has decided to stay. A belt of cloud lives on it, roughly two-thirds of the way up, renewing itself all day out of the air the face forces upward — and this is the same white that lies below the shelf at Vantar's Notch like a floor. The floor of the high place is the ceiling of this one. Neither has ever seen the other. The shelf stands on that whiteness and looks out over nothing; the mere lies under it and looks up at everything; and between them the one cloud does both jobs at once, floor and roof, without effort, the way a door is two rooms' wall.

Below the cloud-belt the face is legible, and the mere-folk read it the way farm people elsewhere read sky. The stone is grey-black with age except where it is pale, and every pale patch is a wound — a scar where the Wall has shed itself, fresh rock showing bright as a snapped bone. The oldest scars have half-darkened; the newest are white as chalk and everyone knows their birthdays. There is a scar shaped like a hound that fell the year of the great snow. There is a long pale seam called the Ladder that no one now alive saw made. The face is a page and the falls are the writing on it, and the parish has been reading the same page for as long as it has existed, watching new words appear.

Above the cloud, nothing — or almost nothing, or almost never. Perhaps once in a generation, on a rare hard winter day, the air goes clear from the valley floor to the top of the sky, and the whole Wall stands out at once, every mile of it, and the parish stops work. On those days you can see, near the very crown, a small dark interruption in the summit ridge — a bite, a gap, as if something long ago took one mouthful out of the top of the world. They call it the Gable-Bite. It is the size, from here, of a missing tooth. Once, on one of the clearing days, an old man with a long-glass held it steady on a fence rail for an hour and then put it down and said the bite was *green* — green, at that height, above the ice-line, above everything — and had smoke around it, a fine turning smoke of black flecks. He was not believed and he did not care. It is in the fall-book, in a hand four keepers of the book ago: *the Gap is green, and something lives in it.*

**The shadow.** The Wall stands east of the mere, so morning here is not a sunrise; it is a rumor of one. The sky lightens, the far western hills go gold, and the parish stays in a blue half-day, waiting, while the sun climbs behind two miles of stone. Then, sometime toward the middle of the morning, the shadow's edge appears at the western fences and begins to come home — a straight line of light crossing the meadows toward the Wall, and the parish tells its time by where that line lies. There are no hours here. There is *sun-at-the-bridge*, *sun-at-the-mere*, *sun-at-the-graves*, *sun-at-the-fan*, and last of all, brief and doubtful, *sun-on-the-stone*, when the light finally reaches the foot of the Wall itself and warms — barely, briefly — the lowest yard of it. In midwinter the line never gets past the mere. For five weeks the Wall keeps the sun entirely, and the parish lives in the blue, and lights lamps at noon, and says, without bitterness, that the mountain is drinking the light. In those weeks the only things that come down from above are the two the Wall never withholds: the water and the sound.

**The still-rain.** Here is the small wrongness this place deals in, the mirror of the water that falls at the notch and comes back up: at the mere, it rains out of empty sky.

Not often, and only ever on windless days. The Wall weeps along its whole height — every seam, every moss-choked crack, every hidden shelf and pool of it letting go its water continuously — and in any wind at all the falling water is torn to mist within yards and driven back up the face, drunk and re-drunk by the heights, never arriving. The Wall keeps its own rain aloft the way a juggler keeps his rings. But when the wind fails — certain heavy summer afternoons, certain enormous still evenings in autumn — the whole suspended freight of it comes quietly down. A fine, cold, mineral rain, out of blue air, tasting of stone, falling on sunlit hay. The mere-folk call it the *letting-go*. It is the softest rain anyone here has ever felt, because it is the oldest: water that left the top of the world hours ago, that has been falling and rising and falling again all day, arrives finally with no force left in it at all, like a message worn gentle by distance. Some of it — though no one at the mere knows this, and no one at the notch either — left a black pool the size of a sleeping ox that morning, went out through a slot in a lip of stone two miles up, and has spent the whole day finishing the fall.

When the first letting-go of spring comes, the parish walks outside and stands in it. All of them, wordless, faces up, until it stops. This is not called anything and is not discussed. You do not worship your neighbor's roof-water. But you can stand in it, once a year, and be counted.

The letting-go sometimes carries passengers. Shreds of moss of a kind that grows nowhere in the valley. A black feather with a green sheen. Once — this is in the fall-book, dated, witnessed by three — a newt, alive, gold-eyed, sitting in the wet grass where the rain had set it down as gently as a hand would, having fallen, if it fell from where the newts come from, two vertical miles in mist, slowly enough to live. It walked to the mere. Of course it walked to the mere. Everything here does.

**The mere.** At the outermost edge of the parish, farthest from the Wall, in the lowest ground: black water, bitterly cold, deeper than any sounding-line the parish owns, fed by no stream anyone can point to. The water comes up through the broken ground from under the great fan of fallen stone — all the seepage of the Wall's foot gathering in the dark and rising here — so the mere is the Wall's water twice over, the letting-go from above and the ground-water from below, and it holds its level to the finger's-width in flood and in drought, a fullness that never overflows, which is a family trait, though neither pool knows it has family.

The mere is where the gold eyes are. Newts — black, cold-eyed, uncountable, thousands upon thousands where the pool at the top of the world keeps its strange seven — rise to the surface at dusk across the whole width of the water, sip air, and sink, and on still evenings the mere shows more gold eyes than the sky shows stars, and shows the stars too, between them, so that the parish's children grow up unable to say with confidence which lights are above and which below. Char live in the deep cold and are the parish's silver: fat, slow, sweet-fleshed, netted at dawn. And the mere does one more thing, which is the parish's proof that the mountain has a voice. On certain nights — north-wind nights, the nights when folk two ridgelines away say the mountain is humming — the note comes down through the stone itself, too low almost for ears, and the mere *shows* it: the black surface breaks out in fine standing rings, patterns of trembling that hold their shape for as long as the note holds, the whole lake shivering to a sound made by wind crossing a gap two miles overhead, the way wine in a glass shows a voice in the room. On those nights the char will not bite and the newts stay down, and old people go out and put an ear to their doorsteps — and the doorsteps matter, as you will hear — and listen to the top of the world talking in its sleep.

**The stone.** The Wall sheds. This is the parish's weather of weathers, its one violence, the tax it pays for living where it lives.

Between the meadows and the face lies the fan — a half-mile apron of fallen rock, boulder heaped on boulder, pale stones on dark, everything the Wall has let go of since before there were people to watch. The fan is growing. It has always been growing. The parish has moved back from it three times in its history, and the three abandoned town-sites lie in a line marching outward from the face, the oldest nearest the Wall and mostly swallowed now, only its corbelled tower-tops still standing up out of the talus like the fingers of a buried hand — and those corbels, note, are laid in the same patient stone-on-stone manner as a certain cairn two miles above them, work of the same nobody.

A fall begins with a crack. The crack comes down faster than the stone does — sound is the Wall's one act of mercy — and every child of the parish is taught the count before they are taught their letters: when you hear the crack, count. A stone letting go from high on the face gives you a count of thirty between the sound of its leaving and the sound of its landing, and in thirty counts a person who knows the ground can be out from under anywhere. The counting is done aloud, calmly, by whoever hears it first, taken up by the next voice and the next across the meadows, so that a rockfall at the mere sounds like this: a distant snap, and then the whole parish numbering together in a spreading ring, and then, somewhere on the fan, the arriving roar, and then everyone finishing their sentences. No one has been killed by the Wall in four generations. The Wall is not gentle. The parish is simply fluent.

**The road.** Under the hay meadows, striking dead straight from the mere's shore toward the face, runs a paved way. Pre-iron work: you can see, where the turf is stripped, the peck-marks of stone-on-stone all along its kerbs, thousands of small patient bruises. It is a fine road, finer than anything the parish could make, and it goes nowhere. It runs to the fan and under it — under a half-mile of fallen mountain — and where it finally reaches bare standing rock at the foot of the face, it becomes a stair.

One hundred and nine steps survive at the bottom, cut into the living gneiss, each tread worn into a shallow spoon by feet and by the water that now uses them, climbing the first pitch of the Wall — and ending in air, at the raw edge of a scar, where the face long ago shrugged and took the rest away. Above that: nothing but the page of the Wall, its scars and its seams, up into the cloud that never opens. The parish's flat statement about the stair, delivered to every child and every stranger in the same words, is this: *it goes to heaven and it is broken.* They do not know — how could they know — that the stair's crown survives; that far above the cloud its last forty-one steps still climb out of the white into clear cool light; that something the size of a room waits at the top of the missing middle, green and loud and dripping, with the stair's other broken end resting at its lip. Two ends of one work, each ending in air, each certain it is all that is left.

The middle of the stair is not gone. It is *in the fan*. Every so often, working the talus edge or clearing a field, someone turns over a flat stone with a spoon of wear in it — a tread, unmistakable, a step from somewhere in the lost two miles, carried down inside some ancient fall — and there is a law about these, the parish's oldest. A found tread may be used for one thing only: a doorstep. Every old house at the mere has one, a stair-step for a threshold, its worn hollow catching the letting-go rain in a small bright spoonful by the door, so that the parish crosses a piece of the way to heaven every time it goes in to supper. Nobody roofs with them, walls with them, or breaks them. You may stand on the old work. You may not make it serve.

## What Comes Down, and Who Keeps It

The parish has a theology and it fits in one sentence: *the mere keeps what the Wall lets go.* Water, stone, sound, birds, bones, oddments — everything the height releases arrives here eventually, and everything that arrives must be kept. Nothing is refused, nothing is thrown back, nothing is left to lie. A boulder through a byre roof is built into the byre's new wall with its scar showing. It is not gratitude, exactly, and not fear. It is the manners of living downstairs from something enormous: you accept what comes through the ceiling, and you keep the account.

**The book and its keeper.** The account is literal. In the meeting-house at the mere lives the fall-book — a ledger, generations deep, in dozens of hands, some of them beautiful and some barely writing at all — and the office of keeping it passes by teaching, sometimes along blood and sometimes not. The keeper of the book records the falls and their scars, the letting-goes and their dates, the finds and their finders. The green Gap is in it. The fallen newt is in it. The clearing days are in it, all nine of them in living record. The book's rule mirrors, without knowing it, a rule kept two miles up — where the office is *take nothing*, here it is *keep everything* — two halves of one commandment that no one ever issued, holding at both ends of the same fall.

**The plunder-days and the sky-finds.** On certain days — the parish can feel them coming, though no one can say how — black birds come down the Wall. Strings of them, shrieking, riding down out of the cloud-belt in long tumbling skeins: slate-winged, metal-voiced, conversational, insolent. They raid the drying racks and the middens, and they steal — anything bright, anything twisted, buttons and buckles and once the sexton's small shears — and they carry it all *up*, back into the cloud, to a market the parish cannot imagine: a weeping wall of inherited holes, and a cairn where such things get wedged into crevices beside offerings left by human hands, in an arrangement no party has ever acknowledged. The mere-folk call the birds the *tithing-birds* and let them thieve, because of what the birds pay.

For sometimes, out over the void above the cloud, in a certain turning game where objects are passed down a hanging string of birds and dropped for the next to catch — sometimes a catch is missed. And a missed catch above the notch has two miles to fall.

These are the sky-finds, the treasures of the parish, entered in the fall-book with more ceremony than births. A polished pale pebble of no local stone. A feather bound with a wisp of green-rotted cord. And once, found by a girl on the fan and carried home at a run: a disk of slate, polished, bored clean through the center — hours of work, a whole winter of somebody's evenings, made by hands, made *carefully*, fallen out of an empty sky. No one at the mere could say who made it, and no one two ridgelines away, where a keeper of a different office once climbed forty-one steps and wedged a winter's work into a cairn, ever learned where it went. It hung on a nail by the meeting-house door for sixty years. Then, on a plunder-day, it was gone — and the fall-book holds both entries, the finding and the taking, and beneath the second, in the driest hand in the whole ledger, four words: *taken back. fair enough.*

So there is a trade, three-cornered and wordless, that no party has ever negotiated and every party keeps: the high keepers give to the cairn, the birds carry between, the mere receives and is robbed in turn, and small made things circulate forever through two miles of vertical country between people who will never meet.

**The swifts.** Every summer dusk, screaming lines of them go *up* the Wall — up and up, drinking the rising spray as they climb, until they vanish into the cloud-belt toward an evening's hunting at a lip of stone they never touch. At the top of the world they are the ones who never arrive. This is where all their arriving happens, such as it is: they breed in the drowned towers of the oldest town-site, in the corbelled fingertips that stand up out of the fan, and even here they barely consent to the ground — the only use a swift has for the earth is a hole in it, sideways. The parish does not go among the old towers, except in one hour of the day, of which more below; so the swifts nest in the one place at the mere where no one walks, tenants of the buried town, commuting nightly to the roof of the sky.

**The falcon-graves.** Twice a day, a knife goes up or comes down the face — one falcon, always one, holding the whole vertical country as her territory, her nest on a ledge of the lower face that the parish roughly knows and has never closely looked for, out of something like manners. High above, an office of keepers declines to name her. Down here they hold the other end of that arrangement: when a falcon's time ends, the height lets her go like everything else, and she comes down to the fan or the meadows or once, feathered and terrible, into the mere itself, and the parish buries her. There is a row of small mounds at the fan's western edge — eleven, at the last count in the fall-book — each capped with a single Wall-stone, none marked further. They do not name the falcons either. They only see them home. Between the death of one and the arrival of the next, the parish notices what the shelf notices: the black birds come down bolder and louder, the plunder-days grow frequent and sloppy, the whole sky above the Wall gets faintly careless — and then a new knife takes the office, and the height snaps back into the shape fear gives it, and the mere feels the change in the manners of the thieves.

**The held breath.** And then there is the half hour before dawn.

In all the generations of the fall-book — in thousands of recorded falls, in stone-weather of every season and size — no stone has ever fallen from the Wall in the half hour before dawn. Not one. The book's margins carry the tally, checked and re-checked by skeptical keepers who each set out to find the exception and each ended by adding their hand to the count. The wind drops. The Wall's ceaseless trickling seep goes quiet, or seems to. Even the letting-go, on the stillest nights, holds off until the light comes. The parish calls it the *held breath*, and it is the one promise they trust absolutely without any idea of why it is kept — because the why is a room two miles above their roofs where, in that same half hour, the ticking wall slows and the birds say nothing and the whistlers go silent and something that may be the whole mountain stops speaking and listens.

The held breath is the walking hour. It is when the fan is crossed, when the falcon-graves are dug, when the fall-book's keeper goes out to read the face for new scars by first grey light. It is when children, on a dare as old as the parish, run the buried streets of the oldest town among the sleeping swift-towers, out to the foot of the stair, up the hundred and nine steps to the broken edge — and lay one palm flat against the standing stone of the Wall itself, the only touch the parish ever gives it, and feel the chill come up into them from somewhere very deep. Two miles above, unknown and unknowing, a keeper asleep on borrowed sedge is waking with the certainty of company, and finding none, and not being afraid.

Then, far up beyond the cloud — too far, you would swear, for any voice to carry, and yet it carries — a single harsh call comes down the face.

And the Wall exhales; and somewhere on the page of it a crack snaps out like a first word; and across the blue meadows the counting begins, calm as a psalm, one voice and then a ring of voices, numbering toward thirty while the day's first stone comes down the side of the world to be kept.
