# The Tableland of Old Light

There is light here. Not daylight — daylight implies a sun somewhere above, renewing the account each morning. This is a place where light is among the materials, but the supply stopped being replenished long ago. What lies over the country now is a remainder: the last hour of an afternoon that has lasted, by the count kept in the villages, some nine hundred years, and is measurably lower in every one of them.

The light is horizontal and thick, amber going brown at its edges, and it is cold. It gives no heat at all. You can stand in a full flood of it, gold to the waist, and your hands stay chilled; warmth here must be gotten from fires, from bodies, from the south walls of old barns — from anything, in fact, except the shining. Heat and light have been separated in this world, decanted into different vessels, and everyone on the tableland knows where the heat went. It went where the rivers go.

And the light pools. This is the thing no description prepares you for. It behaves like a supply, not a condition — it drains off the heights first, so the hilltops have been dark for generations, bald and blind under a brown sky, while the valleys still brim. You walk *downhill* into brightness here. In the lowest meadows the light stands so deep it has surface tension: shallow lakes of it, gold and cold and knee-high, lapping very slowly at the grass, and around every one a tide line of dimness recording where the light used to reach. Children wade in it. Old people sit at the edges with their feet in it, the way you sit with your feet in a warm sea, except that the sea is sight itself, and it is going out.

Because it is going somewhere. That is the tableland's one article of certainty, held by everyone from the surveyors to the orchard-keepers: the light is not fading. Fading is what light does when it is spent. This light is being *drawn down* — through the floor of the country, patiently, at one long sip per minute — and it is arriving, or it will arrive, or it is being held against arrival, in the low warm dark country that the rivers already know the way to. The tableland is not dying of its dimness. It is being decanted.

---

**The ground** is old. Not historically old — *finished*. Erosion completed its sentences here long ago and has nothing left to say. The hills lie like knuckles under a wool blanket, every edge gone, every fracture face weathered to leather; you can run your palm along a ridge of stone and find nothing that would cut butter. The paths are worn in so deep they cannot be unfollowed — sunken lanes with grass at eye level, polished by feet into a kind of horn. Nothing here is sharp. Travelers who have been down to the new country come back speaking of stone fins with glassy exact edges, of rain writing its *first* thin channels, and the tableland people listen to this the way you listen to news of a grandchild's first words. They know those channels. They have the finished versions underfoot.

The rivers run at grade. No falls, no rapids — water moving with the exhausted competence of something that has done this forever, sweet-smelling, orchard-colored, warm only at the very end. Because the rivers end *inside* the country. Each one, after its long flat miles, arrives at a throat: a smooth-lipped hole in the floor of a valley, wide as a barn door, down which the water turns without hurry and goes. The lips of the throats are warm. Lean over one — the people do it without fear, it is like leaning over a well in a farmyard — and a soft heat comes up at you, and a smell of iron, and, very far down, a sound like slow industry: a continuous low manufacturing, patient as a tide.

The land is subsiding to follow its water. Every generation's survey finds the country slightly smaller, the hills slightly settled, the distances a little shorter than the fathers' pace-counts recorded — not folded, not confused, simply *reduced*, the way a stock is reduced, the way a candle is. The material of the tableland is being drawn down through its own floor and poured again below as new ground, warm and tacky and sharp-edged and set last week. The old people say you can feel it if you lie flat in a pasture at the dim end of the light: the whole country, very gently, being spent.

---

**The works** here are cold. Scattered across the country — never clustered, always one at a time, a quarter mile apart or more — stand riveted iron towers, house-tall, streaked with nine centuries of rust, and they are running. But where the engines below exhale, these inhale. Put your palm on a flank and you feel it: one long breath drawn in per minute, the same tempo exactly, the other stroke. The pipes at their bases run down into the ground and the pipes are cold, and they pulse *inward*. The old surveyors — heretics up here too — maintain there is exactly one machine in the world, and the tableland stands at its intake and the basin at its delivery end, and everything between is plumbing.

On the flank of one dark hill there is the mirror field: some hundreds of tarnished plates on seized iron mounts, all angled toward the last light, all pouring what they catch down a single shaft. It was bright-work once. Now it moves perhaps a hand's width a year, still tracking, still decanting, doing at the end of the world's afternoon exactly what it did at the beginning.

In a pasture near the eastern villages an iron stair descends into the ground, and its first eleven steps are missing — sheared off long ago, rusted stubs in the turf, so that the stair now begins below reach and goes down into the dark of the shaft. Travelers back from the basin describe a stair of eleven steps rising out of a flooded mere and stopping. The tableland people nod at this. The bottom of the stair arrived, they say. The rest is in transit. At the head of the shaft, on the last remaining step, there is a seat worn glossy by long sitting — the smooth deep polish of centuries of one patient occupant — and it is empty, and no one sits in it, and everyone knows who did.

And a day's walk apart, two cut ends of cable stand up out of the ground, humming faintly, connected to nothing at this end either. The span between them runs below. The people press their palms to the cable at the dim hour and speak the names of their dead into it, quietly, one name per visit, and travelers report that along a humming wire in the dark country something warm travels back and forth, back and forth, at the speed of a slow walk, drinking.

---

**The animals** are the last editions. Where the country below is crowded with drafts — ten thousand creatures and no two alike, no species, everything a print run of one — the tableland has arrived at the opposite condition by the opposite road: every species here has narrowed, over the long afternoon, to a final individual. There is one hare. There is one heron, one ox, one fox, and they are old, and the villages keep the list. The animals know their standing and carry it gravely; the last hare will sit in a lane and let you pass at arm's length, unbothered, the way a retired official watches traffic.

Their eyes are enormous and open and dimming — eyes that have seen everything the light ever showed and are running out of light to see it by. They spend the deep hours standing at the edges of the light-pools, heads lowered into the gold, drinking sight while it lasts.

When one dies, it is carried to the river. Not buried — *shipped*. And this is where the two countries stop being neighbors and become one operation: travelers back from the basin, describing by touch what bumped their shins in the warm dark, are listened to up here with a terrible attention, because the tableland people recognize the pieces. That fur that changes its grain mid-back — the hare's coat, spliced to the fox's. The cool pleated hide. The fifth leg folded against a belly like a proposal — an amendment, they say, to the ox. The maker below is not inventing. It is *re-drafting*: taking delivery of the tableland's finished animals one by one, at the tempo of one death per long year, and issuing them again below in variations, sealed-eyed, warm from the mold, ten thousand proposals for what the hare might be next time — because next time there will be light to select by, and this time it is still deciding.

The catalogue in the dark is the tableland's fauna, remembered forward.

---

**The surplus.** Every country has one thing too much of. The basin's is dark; the tableland's is memory. Sound here carries absolutely — the air is so old and so still that a dropped tool in one village rings faintly in all of them, and the whole country is audible at once, which is why everyone whispers, and why the tableland at the dim hour is a single held breath miles wide. And the ground replays. The sunken lanes give up old conversations at certain hours — worn voices rising from the horn-polished dirt, mid-sentence, in dialects two centuries gone, saying ordinary things: a price, a name, a remark about weather when there was weather. Nobody is frightened by this. The land is not haunted; it is *retentive*. Erosion finished, and now nothing wears away, including the said.

Even the colors are leaving in order. Blue went first — four generations ago, out of the sky, out of the flax, out of the last jay's wing, all in the space of a year — and the tableland has been an amber-and-brown country since. But travelers report that down in the dark the oldest stayers use the word *blue* with evident meaning, for the feel of the fine warm drizzle on the back of the slower hand. The tableland people were glad to hear it. The blue arrived, they say. It fell as rain.

And at the dim end of every stretch of hours, over the western hills where the dark is oldest, the strays appear: small flames, sourceless, cold, at about the height of a raised hand, each burning for the length of a match and going out. They drift a few steps sideways while they burn, as if carried in a walking hand. The tableland people count them from their doorsteps. Each one, they hold, is a match struck in the basin — the shining, which cannot arrive down there, surfacing up here where light still knows how to land — and each one means the same thing: *someone new has come into the dark, and has not yet learned.* Three or four strays in an evening, then a long gap. You stop striking matches after the third or fourth. The tableland watches you learn.

# Who Lives on the Tableland of Old Light

**The rememberers.** The people of the tableland farm a little, in the bright valleys, and keep the last orchards, and whisper, and their true occupation is the one their name says. They are the memory of the world the way the stayers below are the memory of the basin — but where a stayer's registry lives in the hands, in dents and weld-lines and fracture faces, a rememberer's lives in the ledgers: long calfskin books, kept in the light-pools where they can still be read, recording everything that has gone down. Every animal shipped on the river, with its description, so that the drafts below can one day be checked against the originals. Every subsided survey. Every color, with attempts at its definition made while it still existed — the entry for blue runs eleven pages and ends *cold, but the way water is cold, not the way iron is; ask the rain.* Every name spoken into the cable.

They answer the strays. This is their tenderest custom and they perform it nightly: for each small cold flame counted over the western hills, something goes into the river before the light-pools are left — a windfall apple, a wrapped packet of seed, a tool, a letter sealed in wax and addressed to no one. *So that whoever struck the match finds something against their shins by morning.* The orchards exist for this now. Nobody presses fruit anymore; the whole last thin vintage of the tableland goes down the throats sweet and dissolving, and the travelers' reports confirm the delivery: the warm water below tastes of iron and of something sweeter underneath, like sap, like the inside of a green stem. The tableland people, hearing this described, look at each other with the satisfaction of cooks whose dish has reached the table.

**The ones who go down.** A rememberer's memory eventually fills. There is a recognized fullness — when the ledgers are internalized, when a person can recite the country — and at that point, by long custom, they walk to a throat. They go at the dim hour, carrying nothing, and they step down into the warm and are not grieved, because they are not considered dead. They are considered *shipped* — freight of the most valuable class, an archive delivered to a country that cannot keep records in ink. Travelers speak of the oldest stayers below, the ones whose speech runs at one stroke per long minute, whose cheeks feel poured rather than grown, who are said to have arrived by the rivers rather than the roads. The tableland keeps their names in a separate ledger, the ledger of the delivered, and speaks them into the cable on their birthdays.

**The one who went ahead.** The seat at the head of the broken stair is empty because its occupant finished waiting up here. It was the oldest of the last animals — the great sitter, pleated-hided, patient past all the others, the first entry in every ledger and the only one with no species recorded, because no one had ever seen another. It sat at the stair for longer than the villages have stood, facing west, watching the light go down. And one dim hour within living memory it rose, and walked to the stair, and descended past the missing steps into the shaft, and the tableland people understood: it had gone down to wait at the *other* end — to take the delivered eleven steps as its post and face outward over the dark meres, watching, in a country with nothing yet to watch with, for the thing it had spent nine hundred years watching leave. Travelers who have touched the warmth on the top step of the basin's stair are asked, up here, only one question: *is it still facing west?* It is always still facing west. The rememberers find this a great comfort. Somebody is at the window of the new house, watching for the movers.

**The light.** Last, and largest, and leaving: the light itself, which the tableland people no longer really consider a condition of their country but its senior resident — an old tenant giving notice, moving out one valley at a time. They live inside its departure the way the basin's population lives inside an arrival, and they know, because the ledgers run the numbers, roughly how the end will go. The pools will withdraw to the lowest three valleys, then to one, then to a bright puddle the size of a threshing floor with the whole remaining population — one hare, one heron, a handful of unfilled rememberers — sitting at its rim with their feet in it. And then the floor of the world will take its last long sip, and the tableland will be finished the way its stone is finished, dark and smooth and fully spent, a country that can finally lie down.

And below — this is the entry the ledgers save for the last page, written in every generation's hand, recopied like a vow — below, all at once, after the warm dark centuries, the delivery. The shining will arrive. The seals will lift from ten thousand stored eyes with a sound, the rememberers imagine, like a country of tiny films peeling, and the drafts will look at each other for the first time and the maker will begin to choose among them, and the drizzle will be *seen* to be blue, and the one the room was prepared for will stand in it, lit.

The tableland does not grieve this. Or it does, quietly, at the whispering pitch everything is done at here. Its people stand at the edges of the shrinking gold in the one long afternoon, around them the last of every kind of animal drinking sight while it lasts, under them the patient inhalation of the intake towers and the slow settling of a country being poured into its own child — and they keep, together, the old occupation, the one that needs no future to practice and no hope to time:

they are remembering.

Which is, the surveyors say — the ones who hold that there is only one country, met at both ends — the same act as expecting, performed from the other side of the dark.
