**The Old Bed**

You find it first by its bridges. The road north crosses three of them in an afternoon — good timber bridges, tarred and maintained, humped over the land the way bridges are — and under each one there is no river. There is the *shape* of a river: a broad channel of sorted stone, gravel bars still lying in the teardrop curves that current builds, boulders polished to a gray sheen and standing in arrangements that only moving water arranges. But the water is not there. You look down from the bridge rail into forty years of dry, and the first thing you understand, the thing you never stop understanding, is that this country is not a valley. It is a riverbed. Everything in it was put where it is by a hand that then let go.

The bed runs for sixty kilometers across the same boreal plateau, from the intake weir in the north — where the river is taken, folded into its penstocks, and sent underground — down to the outfall in the south, where it is given back to daylight already spent. Between those two points the channel lies open to the sky and empty, and it has kept its shape with an exactness that unsettles people the first time they walk it. The waterline is still on the canyon walls: a pale band, a bathtub ring on the landscape, below which the rock is scoured clean and above which the lichen begins, so that you can stand on the dry bed and read exactly how deep you would once have drowned. Rapids are still rapids — chaotic gardens of tumbled stone, now climbable, now quiet. Pools are still pools, deep bowls ground into bedrock, some holding rain, some holding nothing. The gravel still grades itself finer downstream. The bed remembers everything about being a river except the river.

It is quiet. That is the country's true ruler, and it is not the ordinary quiet of the north. Wind moves in it; ravens work the canyon rim; the willows tick and rustle. But under all of that there is a specific missing frequency — the broadband, ceaseless, chest-filling voice that a river of this size puts into its valley, the sound the valley's whole acoustic was built around — and its absence has a texture. Old people from the hamlet say the quiet took years to stop being frightening. Some say it never did. Dogs brought into the bed grow uneasy and stay close.

And in one stretch, the quiet lies. Midway down its length the bed crosses over the buried rift — passes, though you cannot see it, a few hundred meters above the vaults where its own water is being worked — and there, on still days, the stones hum. The hamlet children know the place; everyone knows the place. You lie down on the dry channel floor, put your ear against a boulder polished by the very water in question, and you hear it: faint, deep, directionless, the river going by underneath, through rock and root and the frost-dark chambers where it shakes an entire buried country on its way. The river haunts its own bed from below. There is a hand-sign for the stretch, learned from the crew families and passed to everyone: a flat palm pressed to the ear, then pointed at the ground. *Listen down.*

The centerpiece is the falls, or what the falls left. Two-thirds of the way along, the bed drops ninety meters in a great black staircase of columnar rock — the head of water that made the river worth taking, and the reason the pipes exist. Dry, it is architecture: terraces and chutes and enormous potholes bored by ten thousand years of swirling stones, some of the potholes deep as wells, smooth as poured glass, a few of the grinding stones still lying at the bottom of them like eggs in cups. The plunge pool below survives as a spring-fed pond, dark and cold and much diminished. The permanent mist that once climbed the gorge is gone, and you can read its ghost on the walls: a wide band of dead and dying moss, the drowned-air forest that lived on spray for centuries browning back year by year, a rainforest in the process of forgetting why it grew.

The rest of the bed is greening from the edges in. Willow and alder have come down off the old banks and advanced onto the gravel bars, a few meters a decade, patient as anything in this country is patient. But the center of the channel stays bare — clean stone, open water-shaped ground — and it stays bare for a reason, which is the one fact you need in order to understand everything that lives here.

Once a year, for eleven days in late summer, the river comes back.

They call it the Loan. The company calls it something with more words in it, a scheduled environmental flow coinciding with the annual maintenance shutdown, but the hamlet has called it the Loan since the first one, and the name has the right accounting in it: lent, not returned. The penstocks are closed. The turbine galleries fall still. And the river, having nowhere else to go, pours over the intake weir and comes down its own bed in daylight for the first and only time all year. People walk up the channel to meet it. The front of the water arrives as a smell first — wet stone, crushed green, something mineral and old — then as a sound coming around the bend, then as a low tumbling wall of foam and sticks and forty years' patience, and behind it the bed fills, and for eleven days the country is what it was: the falls thundering, the mist climbing the gorge and wetting the dying mosses, the pools joining hands, the quiet gone. On the twelfth day the gates open below, the pipes drink, and the river drops out of its bed over the course of an afternoon — visibly, foot by foot, pools stranding, gravel emerging steaming into the sun — and the Loan is called in, and the quiet comes back down over everything like snow.

It is the Loan that scours the channel and keeps the willows at bay. It is the Loan that keeps the bed a bed. And it is the Loan, as you will see, that keeps most of the residents alive.

Winter is the bed's other tenderness. Snow fills the channel bank to bank and lies there smooth and white and river-shaped, and from the canyon rim in February you would swear the water had come home. Once a year in water, all winter in snow, the Old Bed gets to be full. It spends the rest of its time being remembered at.

**Who Lives in the Old Bed**

Begin with the eldest, because they were here before the taking and intend to be here after.

In a spring-fed side channel below the falls — a few hundred meters of clear, cold, permanent water, all that groundwater can afford — lies a colony of freshwater pearl mussels, black, wrist-long, half-buried in the gravel in the postures they have held for longer than the pipes have existed. Some of them are over a century old. They were adults when the river ran; they were adults when it stopped; the taking, to them, was a bad decade in a long life. Out in the main bed their kin did not manage it — you can still find the shoals of dead, drifts of paired shells lying open in the dry gravel like spilled coins, forty years weathered — but the side-channel colony held, and holds, and here is the thing about them: they cannot continue alone. A pearl mussel's larvae must spend their first winter clamped to the gills of a salmon or a trout. No fish, no next generation — a rule as absolute as gravity. The colony has therefore compressed its entire reproductive existence into eleven days a year. They spawn to the Loan. When the water comes down the bed and the side channel joins the river, the mussels release, all together, a single annual cloud of larvae into the one brief week when there are gills in the world to catch — and how there come to be gills in the world at all is a human story, and it is coming.

In the spring pools of the upper bed, in water that never quite freezes and never quite dries, live blind amphipods — small, colorless, deliberate. They are the same stranding as the ones in the black seeps of the vaults: one river's worth of small lives, orphaned in the same year, divided by the taking into an upstairs and a downstairs and left in the leaks. The two populations lie in places a few hundred meters apart, sundered by solid rock, and have not met in forty years and will not meet until the pipes fail. The hamlet uses the crews' sign for them, learned from crew families at kitchen tables — two fingers wriggling, a fist closing slow. *The ones the water left.* It is the only phrase the two countries fully share, and each uses it about its own.

The potholes of the dry falls hold the bed's strangest nations. When the river was taken, char were stranded in the deepest of them — a dozen fish here, twenty there — and their descendants live there still, pool-locked, dwarfed, each pothole a sealed world with its own diminishing strain: pale in this one, fine-boned in that one, in the deepest and coldest a stock so small and dark and slow they are hard to see at all. For eleven days a year the Loan joins the potholes into a single roaring staircase and the nations mix, trade genes, redistribute themselves, and are then dealt out again at random when the water drops — the same fish, or their children, sorted into new wells for another year of separate becoming. The children of the hamlet know the potholes by name and feed the fish in the hungry months, which the biologists officially discourage and unofficially fund.

The dippers are the mystery no one has solved. A dipper needs running water the way the beetles below need the roar — it is a bird made entirely of current — and there is no current here, and so there are no dippers, three hundred and fifty-four days a year. On the first or second day of every Loan, they are on the falls. Not eventually; immediately, as if they had been waiting in the willows, though no one has ever found them waiting. They work the whitewater for eleven days, bobbing on the wet stones in the resurrected mist, and when the water drops they are gone, to wherever a river bird goes to spend a year being patient. An osprey checks the plunge-pool pond a few times each summer, out of thoroughness or habit or lineage, finds it too small and too dark, and moves on. The old tern islands, mid-channel gravel once ringed by water, are fox-walked now and bare.

Then the hamlet — nineteen houses at the foot of the falls, on the old bank above the waterline scar, built by generations who netted the salmon run at the staircase and who now live beside its dry monument. The company's compensation keeps the houses heated. What keeps them *inhabited* is harder to name, and takes two forms.

The first is the carrying. The salmon did not stop coming. Every summer they arrive from the sea and run upriver as far as the river will let them, which is the outfall — the tailrace pool where the pipes give the water back — and there they mill, thousands of them, nosing a current that comes out of a wall. During the Loan, when the old bed runs and its long-dead scent goes down to the sea-reach, some of them turn into it and come up the channel — the first wild ascent in a year — and hit the falls, which even full are barely passable and which a fish gets eleven days to solve. So the hamlet solves it for them. They net salmon below the staircase and carry them up it — in tanks on trailers where the old portage road allows, and by hand, in wet canvas slings, two people to a fish, where it doesn't — and release them into the running water above, where the char nations are mixing and where, in the side channel, a colony of century-old mussels has just released the only children it will have this year into the path of the only gills it will see. On paper the carrying is a fish-passage obligation, a line item. In practice it is a procession, and everyone involved knows it: the whole hamlet on the wet rocks in the reborn mist, passing the river's silver upward past the place the river was broken, eleven days a year, forty years running. Children are given their first fish to carry at about the age they are trusted with anything else that is alive and heavier than they are.

The second is the counter.

There has been a fish counter at the falls for over a hundred years — a ledger kept daily through the great runs, a column of numbers that was once the hamlet's whole economy and calendar — and when the river was taken the counter of that era did not stop. She ruled the ledger the same as always and wrote *zero*, and dated it, and wrote zero the next day, and the next, and the practice has outlived her twice over. The current counter is an old woman who walks the dry staircase every morning of the year, in frost and in fly-season, looks at the empty potholes and the springs and the pond, and records what passed: nothing, nothing, nothing — and then, for eleven days in late summer, real numbers again, the count of the carried, entered in the same columns her great-grandmother used, so that the ledger reads as one unbroken record in which the river is simply having a long bad century. She counts the mussels in the side channel each autumn, one by one, on her knees in the cold water. She knows the pothole nations individually. It was she, decades ago, who fought the timing of the Loan out of spring, where the engineers wanted it, and into late summer, where the mussels needed it, and it is not clear the company ever fully understood what it agreed to.

She has never been down the seam. She and the keeper of the seventh chamber have never met. But once a year, in the maintenance crews' hands, two messages cross. When the Loan ends she sends down the year's count — fish carried, mussels living, which pothole nations survived the winter — and up from the dark comes his: the surveyors' number, how many centimeters the chambers widened, how many rings the columns put on, how much closer the buried hand has closed. Two ledgers, one river, two numbers passing each other once a year in a crewman's jacket pocket. Neither has ever added a personal word, the crews say, except once: the year the eldest mussel she monitors turned one hundred and thirty, she wrote the age at the bottom of the count, and the paper that came back up had beneath the survey figure a single line — *the columns are older. tell it to keep going.*

And the two countries touch one other way, which everyone in both has felt and no one in either says much about. During the Loan, the vaults fall silent. The penstocks stand empty; the roar — the oldest resident, the landlord below — stops. For eleven days the crews do the year's deep work in a stillness that veterans describe as worse than any noise; the pale beetles cluster motionless on the columns, waiting for the wood to speak again; the bats fly out into evenings where, for once, their own voices would work below and are not needed; the fox travels her route with her ears up. The trees on the plateau stop humming. And the keeper, they say, does not sleep at all — he sits in the seventh chamber inside the only silence of his year, enduring it the way the owl endures the roar, and it is the hamlet's belief, held quietly and never tested, that the owl and the keeper have the same arrangement in opposite directions: each goes once a year to the one place where the thing they are made of is impossible, and calls it rest, and the world permits it. For eleven days the whole system runs inverted — river in daylight, silence underground, the water loud and *seen* — and then the gates open, the pipes drink, the roar stands back up in the vaults like something stretching, and the counter, on the thirteenth morning, walks the wet-then-drying staircase and writes zero.

Last, largest, least separable: the absence itself. Down in the vaults they will tell you the oldest resident is the roar, and that everything below has shaped itself around it. Up here the oldest resident is the same river in its other form — the absence — and everything in the Old Bed is shaped around that: the mussels' eleven-day lives, the pothole nations, the carried fish, the ledger of zeros, the willows held at the channel's edge, the stones that hum when you lie on them, the quiet that took years to stop being frightening. One river, split by the taking into a sound and a silence, a presence nothing can escape and an absence nothing can fill, each with its full complement of tenants arranged around it like iron filings around the two poles of a single magnet buried in the plateau.

And each country keeps its own prophecy about the lease. Below, they say that when the machines stop and the pipes fail, the river will come back into its bed *in the dark* — that the vaults will flood and seal, and the forest will inherit a black loud country no one hears again. Up here they know the other ending: that a failed intake spills over its own weir, that water finds its oldest work first, that the bed has been rehearsing — once a year in water, all winter in snow — and that when the hand below finally closes on whatever it closes on, the river will come down the staircase in daylight for good, and the mussels will still be there to meet it, because they have outwaited worse and are, by their own reckoning, only partway through the bad century. No one alive will see which ledger closes the account. In the meantime the two numbers cross once a year, the salmon go up the falls in canvas and wet hands, the beetles wait out their eleven days of terrible quiet, and the river runs on between its two shores — one of stone and daylight, one of root and roar — belonging entirely, and equally, and insufficiently, to both.
