**The Wet Throat of the Young Sess**

You come down to it the way the water does — headlong. On the plateau's rainy flank, whole streams step off the floor of the world without ceremony: they run bright across the heath, gather in stony funnels the shepherds call the Swallows, and are gone. There is no dwindling of light in stages here, no gold and then blue and then brown. There is daylight, and then there is none, and the transition takes about as long as falling does.

The sound arrives with you, because you arrive inside it. It is a roar, and if you have stood in Old Sessern you will know it at once for a cousin — the same physical pressure against the sternum, the same abolition of the idea of silence — but it is a different animal of a sound. The roar of the dry vault is a million voices; it has grain and texture, it argues with itself. This roar is one voice. It is white and total and wet, the sound of water doing the only thing water does underground, which is work.

This is where the river went.

The Sess made Old Sessern — hollowed it, graded its avenues, fluted its walls, scalloped its ceilings — and then, an age ago, the plateau shifted some private inch of itself and a lower door opened, and the Sess left. Rivers are not sentimental. It abandoned a country it had spent a million years carving between one geological afternoon and the next, dropped through the new fault, and set about the same work all over again in fresh rock, miles to the east and a thousand feet down. Sessern got the long dry afterward. The Sess got this: a raw, black, thundering youth of a cave, a stair of cataracts descending through the roots of the plateau, each fall booming in its own chamber at its own pitch, so that the survey teams who have pushed farthest into it navigate home by ear, from note to note, the way you would walk back through a carillon.

Everything Sessern finished, the Throat is beginning. The breakdown blocks are sharp enough to cut rope. The walls are mid-sentence — half-formed scallops, flutes still deepening, a ceiling pendant that will be elegant in ten thousand years and is presently a snag. There is no dust. There will never be dust while the river lives here; there is instead the fog, a permanent hanging spray in the fall chambers that a lamp cannot defeat, only inhabit — you carry your light through it like a small white room with you inside. The air smells of ozone and wet stone and nothing else. No ammonia, no mushroom, no smell of anything having accumulated, because nothing accumulates. In Sessern a footprint is a geological event. In the Throat a footprint lasts until the next hard rain on the plateau, two days' walk and a vertical half-mile away, at which point the river rises, the whole cave swallows, and the record of your existence is edited out overnight. Sessern is an archive. The Throat is a draft.

And yet the hand is the same, and you can see it. Every scallop the Sess is cutting down here has its fossil twin in the ceilings of the old city. The same turbulences, the same eddies, the same habits of a particular water, remaking themselves in new stone — as if the river carried its style with it the way an old mason carries his. In quiet side-pockets above the falls the Sess keeps its small perfections: rimstone terraces filling and brimming, and cave pearls turning in their stone cups, polished spheres it has been rounding for centuries, for no one, out of grit that may once have been the walls of somewhere else.

Between the two countries — the one the river finished and the one it is writing — runs the passage the surveyors call the Dry Sess: the abandoned bed itself, miles of dead riverbed graded to a perfection no road-builder ever matched, floored with the last gravel bar the water ever sorted there, every stone still in the order the current left it. Halfway along it there is a dry waterfall, a fluted lip eighty feet high with no water and no sound, which people who have stood beneath it describe as the loudest silence they have ever heard. The Dry Sess is the only stillness in either system. On one end of it, the roar of wings. On the other, the roar of water. In between, miles of rock where nothing has happened for an age — or almost nothing, but that belongs to the census.

At its lowest known chamber the Throat drowns entirely, the ceiling stoops to the water, and the Sess goes on without company through flooded galleries no one has seen, until — some unknowable distance later — it comes up into daylight at the foot of the plateau, boiling out of the rock in a broad blue-green spring. The people who live on that pool call it the Sessmouth, and their town by the same name, and they do not know what the name remembers. Why would they? The water arrives cold and clean and already old, and the town takes it at its word.

**The Inhabitants of the Wet Throat**

Begin with the river, because it will not let you begin anywhere else.

The Sess is the Throat's only true citizen; everything else is a tenant, and knows it. The river sets the calendar (there is no dusk here, no dawn, only rise and fall), owns all the architecture, and reserves the right of demolition over any of it. Under the white roar, if you listen long enough, you can hear it doing the actual work: the ceaseless click and shuffle of gravel in transport, and beneath that, in flood, a deeper sound that unsettles even experienced surveyors — the slow subterranean knock of boulders walking downstream, stone striking stone in the dark, the sound of the country being moved.

Everything alive here lives on what the river brings, because the Throat has no wealth of its own. This is the great economic difference between the two vaults. Sessern is rich — the guano falls like rent, forever, and whole nations stand on the hoard. The Throat is poor and lives on imports: leaves and drowned beetles funneled down the Swallows, the occasional bounty of a washed-in carcass. Some years ago a deer came down whole, and the chamber where it lodged was famous among the fauna for a season, a boomtown of white shrimp and pale flatworms, and then the flood took the last of it and the chamber was poor again. Life in the Throat is thin, fast, and opportunistic. Life in Sessern is dense, slow, and assured. They are the two ways of being alive in the dark, and one river made both.

The fish are the newest arrivals and it shows. The Throat is young, and so its transformations are unfinished: these are the descendants of surface fish swallowed alive by the Swallows within recorded memory, and they are caught mid-change, like the walls. Some still carry a ghost of color along the spine. Some have eyes gone soft and skinned-over but not yet gone — you can see the dark bead of them under the pale tissue, an organ in the act of being forgotten. Set one beside the blind salamander-things of Sessern, whose lineages finished forgetting so long ago that blindness itself has worn smooth, and you are looking at the same sentence at two points in its writing.

And they are more than an analogy, the salamanders — they are family. This is the Throat's deepest kinship with the old city. When the Sess left Sessern, it stranded its own fauna: the swimming things of the old river, marooned in a country going dry. Most died. A few lineages endured, slowed, dried into that strange patience, and became the dust-backed fixtures of the dry vault — the small damp officials who move a few times a year and live, perhaps, for centuries. But the river kept some of the family with it. Their cousins swim in the Throat today: the same blood, quick and wet and hungry, hunting shrimp through the fall-pools, living their loud brief decades in the spray. One departure, an age ago, split a single family into two fates — those who kept the water, and those who kept the house. The wet branch got life. The dry branch got time. No one can say which was the inheritance and which the disinheritance.

The humans of this system live at its mouth and know it only from the outside in. Sessmouth is a town of perhaps four hundred: millers whose wheels turn on the spring's outflow, eel-weirs, watercress beds, a stone church whose font is filled — this is their one liturgical extravagance — directly from the pool. The spring is generous and strange in about equal measure. It runs cold and constant, but after great rains on the plateau it rises days later, on its own schedule, as if answering a question asked somewhere else — which is exactly what it is doing. And sometimes it delivers things. White fish come up dead or dying into the light, and are called ghost-catch, and are thrown back with a particular muttered formula. Once, in the record of the parish, a blind salamander came up alive, pale as candle wax, and lay in the shallows for three days while the town argued about what it meant, and then was gone.

The town holds one more inheritance it has never counted. Generations ago, a party of Sessmouth men went up onto the highland to dig guano from fissures a shepherd had found — and their footprints are on the plazas of Old Sessern still, crisp as the hour they were made, leading in and leading out again. They came home with sacks of black wealth and a story about a roar of wings under the mountain, and the story survives in Sessmouth as a tale for winter, and no one has ever set it beside the roar of water at their own doorstep. On clear evenings the whole town can watch, high on the plateau's rim, a plume of smoke that is not smoke rise and bend and stream away into the dusk — the mountain breathing, they say — millions of wings pouring out of the dry country. Sessmouth lives at the mouth of one roar and under the evening shadow of the other, and has never once joined the two. It stands between the underworld's two emissaries, taking the water by day and the smoke by dusk, and thinks of them as different weathers.

And then — last, and least certain — there are the ledges.

Above the flood line, in the fall chambers where the fog is thickest, the survey teams have marked certain shapes on the high dry shelves as breakdown. Some of them probably are. But the careful ones note that these blocks stand rather than lie; that they are pale in a way the local stone is not; that their proportions rhyme — the rounded-doorway curve at the shoulder, the fluted economy of limb — with figures half-buried in dust in the silent districts of a city miles away, which these surveyors have mostly never seen. The shapes of the Throat are rougher than Sessern's, less resolved, as though the shell were still being grown, or as though they were younger. Or newer. They stand facing downstream, all of them — toward the drowned galleries, toward the carving front, toward the rooms the river has not finished making.

In the Dry Sess, the dead bed between the two countries, there are grooves worn in the floor. Everyone attributes them to the vanished river, and most of them are the river's. But some run up the gradient. Some arc, slowly, around obstacles a current would have ignored. They run in one direction only, out of the old city and down the abandoned bed toward the sound of water, and some of them are old beyond reckoning, and some — the careful say, looking at what has and has not settled within them — are not.

Whether the still ones of Sessern are walking, at a pace of a groove per millennium, out of the finished country and into the unfinished one — following the water to its new house the way the salamanders' ancestors could not — or whether these are others of their kind, gathered here the way they gather, perhaps, wherever a vault is being made, no one knows, and neither vault is telling. They have never answered anything. They simply stand in the fog above the falls, facing the future of the rooms, the quietest tenants of the loudest house.

Stand long enough on one of those ledges, in the white roar and the moving dark, and the place discloses its essential character, which is the old city's character read in reverse. The Throat is not a wilderness, exactly, and not yet a country. It is a place that has not finished — that has barely begun. The Sess is down there right now, carving halls no one will name for ten thousand years, tuning falls that will someday be avenues, roughing out the plazas of a city that may never have builders, or may already have them, waiting on the shelves. And one day — an unhurried day — the plateau will shift another private inch, a lower door will open, and the river will leave here too, without sentiment, the way it left before. The roar will stop. The fog will settle. The dust will begin.

What that is like — the long afterward, the slow snow, the sound of a new dark learning to talk to itself — is not a mystery in this world. It is on display. It stands miles to the west under the dry heart of the same plateau, at the far end of a dead riverbed, where the same story is already over: told twice beneath one highland, once finished and once beginning, with the loudest silence anyone has ever heard lying quietly between the tellings.
