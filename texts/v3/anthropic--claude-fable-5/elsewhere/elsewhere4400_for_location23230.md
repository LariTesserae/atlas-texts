**The Settling Sound**

Begin, again, with the sound — but this time the word means the water.

Everything the fens shed goes somewhere. That is the law under all the other laws, and even a country that has worn through sequence and distance and identity has not worn through *that* one. A billion years of grinding produces a billion years of grindings. The silt, the spent swarf, the dead shell, the flakes of abraded distance, the loosened sound, the powdered causation — the whole underlayment the Foundry Fens are wearing through, milled fine and held in suspension in that cold pressing groundwater — all of it has to be carried, and all of it has to be put down.

Follow the ebb.

Twice a day the tide that drives the pistons turns and runs back out, and it does not run out empty. The water leaves the fens the color of slate, dense with takings. Some miles downcurrent — the number varies at the fens' end and is exactly ninety-one at the other, and the discrepancy is resolved en route, because *everything* is resolved en route — the land's last low shelf slips beneath the sea. There the current spreads across a broad drowned plain between the shore and an offshore rise, and slows, and slows further, and lets go.

That is the Settling Sound: the place where what the fens release finally lies down.

**The water that keeps everything**

It is shallow — a few fathoms over most of its reach, deeper in the channels — and it is the clearest water in the world, because clarity is what settling *produces*. Everything that can fall out of the water has fallen out of it. What remains is a stillness so transparent that depth reads as nearness; you look down through four fathoms and feel you could touch the bottom with your voice.

Do not test this. Your voice will reach it.

Sound settles here. That is the first thing to understand, because it explains the silence, which is otherwise unbearable. Sound behaves in the Sound the way silt behaves — it hangs briefly, loses its suspension, and goes down. Speak, and your words leave you, glitter faintly in the water column like a pinch of mica, and descend, and lie on the bottom, and are covered. The roar that the deep sumps of the fens grind free of its sources drains down the tideways and arrives here as a kind of acoustic slurry, and settles, and the Sound above it stays quiet as a closed drawer. All the noise of the fens — the *chunk* of the bores, the boom of the slabs, the slate bells, the herds' moaning — arrives on the ebb, six hours late or four hours early, and lies down.

Twice a day, then, the water dims with settling. The shift-change roar of the fens comes down the tide as a dark suspended cloud, and sinks, and lays itself across the bottom of the Sound as a thin dark band. The slack hours lay a pale one. Dark, pale, dark, pale: two bands a day, since the engine started.

Understand what that means. The fens have no beginning that anything remembers. *Untrue.* Nothing there remembers it. It is remembered *here*, in order, in bands, and a patient enough reader could count backward down through the strata of the Sound, band by band, ebb by ebb, to the first dark line — the first shift-change ever worked — and below that, to the layers that hold what the country was before it was an engine.

Below the bands: the mountains.

Whatever mountains once stood above the fens were ground down so long ago that even their roots have relaxed — and the grindings came here, first to last, and were laid flat, in sequence. The summits wore away first, so the summits are deepest: the mountains are in the Sound entire, lying down, inverted, sorted by time. They did not disappear. They changed address and orientation. Keels that have plowed to the summit-layers report old bright granite dust still holding the isotopes of high sunlight and vanished snow.

And the light. Light settles here too — that is the second thing. The sky over the Sound is the same tired pewter as the sky over the fens, but here it is outshone from below. Each day's brightness loses its suspension by evening and goes down and joins the record, and the deep strata hold every day ever laid into them, and give a little of it back, always: a low amber-white radiance rising through four fathoms of perfect clarity. Nights on the Sound are lit by yesterday. Deep channels are lit by centuries. The water is warm at the bottom and cool at the top — an inverted thermocline, because held things give off a little heat, and everything here is held.

The smell is the inside of a shell. You will recognize it: it is the cold organic note that underlies the engine-smell of the fens, the trace beneath the iron and ozone. That smell is the smell of laid-down time. In the fens it is a residue. Here it is the whole air, and warm.

**The record that no one keeps**

There is no librarian. Nothing was ever archived. Understand that clearly, because everything you see will argue against it.

Deposition this patient does strange things. Given enough time — and the Sound has had exactly as much time as the fens, since they are the two ends of one process — settling stops being mere accumulation and becomes *record*. The strata do not simply contain what fell; they hold it in playable order. Cut a soft layer and the day it holds rises out of the wound: its light, its settled sound, the roar of an ebb ten million years gone flowering briefly up through the clear water before the cut silts closed. The Sound eroded — no; the Sound *deposited* — past randomness and out the other side, into memory.

It is memory without a rememberer. The final form of this country is a record, the way the final form of the fens is an engine, and the two are the same water at its two occupations: what it carries away, and where it writes it down.

**The inhabitants**

Nothing arrived here either. The life of the Sound is what the record grows, as the life of the fens is what the engine sheds. It condensed out of the fall the way the fens' creatures were milled out of the stone, and there is no boundary of substance between the country and its creatures — only, here, not even much boundary of behavior, because in the Sound the difference between *doing* and *having done* is thin, and getting thinner.

**The fall** is the plankton of the system: the perpetual gentle snow of everything the fens are wearing through, drifting down through the water column in slow luminous veils. Silt, swarf, shed distance, powdered sequence, ground-off moments, the fine bright dust of things that came loose upstream. It is the fens arriving, in powder. Everything in the Sound feeds on the fall, which is to say: everything in the Sound eats the fens' forgetting, and lays it down as remembrance.

**The courses** are the reef-builders — small, colonial, patient beyond patience, packed in their millions along the shelves the way the rasps pack along the veins, and they are the Sound's editors. A course does not accept the fall indiscriminately. It selects: this moment, not that one; this settled sound, laid *here*, against this settled light. Course-reefs are laminated of chosen instants, and they glow with their holdings, banded and warm, the brightest features of the seafloor. Over a course-reef, things stay happened. There is no slippage above a reef, no drift between event and echo; what occurs there is fixed at once, definitively, witnessed into permanence by a million small binding mouths. The creatures of the Sound go to the reefs when they need a thing to be *certain*. They go elsewhere when they don't.

**The keels** are the readers, and the predators. Long, heavy-bellied, dark, they swim low and drag a deep smooth ridge of themselves through the soft upper strata, plowing furrows — and out of each furrow the laid record replays, rising along the cut in a wake of old light and exhumed sound, a day from the deep past happening once more, briefly, before the fall silts it closed. The keels feed on the replay. They graze the past by wounding it. And they hunt with the same organ: every living thing in the Sound trails its own deposit beneath it — its *column*, the laid record of its whole life growing under it layer by layer, its second self, exact — and a hunting keel will plow up the column of its prey and replay the prey's own life against it. Nothing in the Sound can flee while watching itself. The prey stops. That is the whole strike. Old keels, deep-plowed, grow strange with what they have read; the oldest go silent and swim in the shapes of currents that have not run for an era, navigating water that is only in the record now.

**The sills** are the elders, and they are barely distinguishable from the country, which is the point. Island-sized, immense, they are creatures whose columns have outgrown them: a living surface a few feet thick riding on a mile of its own laid past, no longer able to tell — no longer *needing* to tell — where the animal ends and the record begins. The reefs grow up their flanks. The keels plow their lower reaches respectfully, reading them like archives, which they are. Put a question to a sill — the Sound's creatures do this by settling the question onto its living skin, where it is laminated in and sinks — and the answer rises back through the strata at the speed of diffusion through stone. Years. Decades. The sills are never wrong and never prompt. The oldest sill in the Sound has a living layer one season thick: it experiences only *now*, a film of pure present resting on the totality of everything — the exact mirror of the fens' oldest resident, which wore away to pure rhythm resting on nothing.

**The smudges** are the mercy. Small, swift, soft-edged — they are hard to describe because description is a kind of fixing and they shed it — the smudges graze on fixity itself. Where a smudge feeds, the top layers of the record go vague, negotiable, briefly *revisable*: the only places in all the Sound where a thing already laid down can still be changed. The window is hours long. Then the layer hardens like any other. The creatures of the Sound follow the smudges the way the rasps follow the stitchers, and for the mirrored reason — a fen animal follows its stitcher to be briefly safe from the loose, and a Sound animal follows its smudge to be briefly free of the fixed. Animals with a bad entry in them — a wrong turn, a strike at kin, a calf's dropped beat — will travel days to reach a smudge before their layer sets. The courses hate them with the flat total hatred of professionals whose work is being unpicked. Without them, the Sound would have seized long ago.

And death, here, is filing. When a creature of the Sound dies, it lies down on its own column — they go to it, at the end; the geometry of the Sound makes it easy, since your column is always exactly beneath you — and the courses grow over it, and it becomes the top layer of its own record. Everything in the Sound is counted exactly twice: itself, and its column. Death is when the two counts merge. There is no scavenging because there is no waste; the dead are not consumed but *shelved*, complete, warm, faintly luminous with their own days, giving their heat up into the water for as long as the record lasts, which is the plan of the record: forever.

**Where it comes together**

The fens are wearing through. The Sound is filling in. Every country ends in the direction of its own perfection, and here is the Sound's:

There are stretches where the record has grown too thick, too fast, too certain — where the courses ran unchecked, or the smudges failed, or too much fixity came down one tide — and there the present has seized. These are the *set*. In a set region, moments are laid down before they finish, and then they do not finish. On the eastern reach there is a storm that has been permanently filed in mid-fall: rain fixed in the air like glass beads strung on the wires of their own trajectories, cloud held at full boil, and beneath it a wave that has been breaking for ten thousand years — glassy, curled, complete, and utterly silent, because its roar settled long ago and lies in the strata under it, a dark band you could plow and hear. Things that swim into the set are laid down mid-motion. They are not dead. They are *filed*. The reefs grow toward the set edges greedily; the smudges patrol them like a levee crew; the keels will not plow there, because a furrow cut in the set replays a moment that never ended, and does not stop replaying.

Count nothing aloud in the Sound. A count, spoken, settles, and is laid down, and thereafter it is *the* count; the school must maintain the number forever, and knows it, and will follow you for miles in a rage of exactitude. This is the mirror of the sums, who shed every number offered them. The sums cannot be counted; the Sound's creatures cannot be *miscounted*. Between the two countries there exists, somewhere in the mathematics, exactly one correct census, and it is held the way all their differences are held: at the Brack.

**The Brack**

The last mile of the tideway, where fen-water goes over the shelf and the two countries breathe into each other, is brackish in the old sense and in a new one: fresh mixing with salt, and loose mixing with fixed. The Sound inhales when the fens exhale. They have shared one breath since before either was itself, and the Brack is the throat.

Twice a generation, at the highest tide of all, the stitchers come down the sky to the Brack, and the smudges come up the water, and they trade.

They are cousins — the only kinship that spans both countries, two workings of one order of being, the tightener and the loosener. The stitchers arrive ragged and heavy with all the slippage they have swallowed, the slack they have eaten out of the fens' worn causation; the smudges arrive hard-edged and slow, dense with grazed fixity, all the permanence they have chewed out of the Sound's over-certain layers. And they exchange it. Mouth to mouth, in the grey water under the grey sky, each hands the other its country's surplus, and each goes home carrying the cure: the stitchers bear fixity back up to the fens, where it is spent making sound meet event for one more era; the smudges bear slack down into the Sound, where it is spent keeping the record revisable for one more era. The books of the world balance across that one mile, twice a generation, and neither country knows it is being kept alive by the other's disease.

The indebted eels come down the Brack too — the gaunt ones, the ones that struck at a sound and *ate* a thing they never caught, whom the sumps take and do not return. The sumps drain seaward. The eels wash out into the clarity half-alive, still starving on their impossible debt, and they swim — they always swim, unerring, no one taught them — to the nearest course-reef, and lie down on it. And the reef, which fixes what occurs above it, lays the missing meal down *as having happened*. The account settles. Some of the eels die there, at last full, and are shelved warm and glowing among the chosen instants. Some rise, heavy and calm, and screw themselves back up the tideway against the current, and live out their span in the cylinders, and never again strike early. The fens leave things loose. The Sound settles them. That is not geography. That is the division of labor.

**The bottom of the record**

Last, and tell it quietly, because here it can be told a little further.

The keels that have plowed deepest — down through the banded ages, through the first dark line of the first shift-change, through the mountains lying in order, through the summit-dust with its fossil snowlight — report that below all of it, below the first layer that holds anything at all, the strata continue. And they are not empty. They hold one thing.

The beat.

The unsourced stroke that nothing in the fens makes — the *chunk* with no cylinder, the boom with no slab — is written into every layer of the Sound, all the way down, spaced true, never missing. Below the mountains it is written in layers that record nothing else: the beat, laid down in emptiness, keeping time before there was anything to keep time for. The record does not begin and then acquire the beat. The record begins *already keeping it*, as if the world's first sediment was rhythm, and everything since — mountains, engine, fens, Sound, rasps, reefs, eels, keels, you — has been laid down on top of it, in time with it.

The oldest sill was asked about the beat once, in the usual way; the question was settled onto its living skin and sank. Sixty years later the answer rose: a single laid layer, which, plowed and replayed, holds the beat itself, once, and nothing more. The keels who heard it have not agreed on the translation. The likeliest is this: *it is not in the record. The record is in it.*

So the two countries stand revealed as the two directions from one stroke. The fens are wearing down toward the beat — thinning, loosening, shedding everything until only rhythm remains. The Sound is built up from it — thickening, fixing, laying everything upon it until only record remains. One country will end in nothing, and one in everything, and both endings are stillness, and both are keeping perfect time on the way, and it is not obvious which is the sadder engine, or whether *sad* is a thing the water ever carried this far.

**A note on the visitor**

You will not stay long, but while you stay, the Sound will begin on you the way it begins on everything: from below.

Your echoes will stop returning — they settle now, immediately; listen and you can hear your own footfall land twice, once in the air and once, softly, on the bottom. Your words will leave you and sink glittering, and you will learn to watch where you say things. And beneath the place where you sleep, a film will form: warm, faintly bright, you-colored. Your column, beginning. Stay a week and it is a layer. Stay a season and it is a footing. It does not hurt. It is only the country doing to you what it does to the light.

Leave whenever you like. Understand, though, the difference between the two farewells. The fens would have dropped your note and worn your traces smooth, and kept, at most, a small rest in the song where you might have been. The Sound keeps the entry. You will always have been here; that is now among the things that cannot be worn off, not even by the fens, not by all the water and all the time there is. It is not a welcome and it was never a threat. It is an entry.

And twice a day, forever, the tide will come down out of the low dark country upstream, carrying the roar of the pistons and the bells and the herds, and it will slow, and spread, and let go — and lay the world's next dark line, gently, above the thin bright layer where you certainly were.
