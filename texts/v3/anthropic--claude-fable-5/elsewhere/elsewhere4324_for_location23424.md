## The Tower of the Carried Ones

You reach it by going up, and the going up takes longer than the hill admits to. From the valley it looks like an hour's walk; it is a morning's. There is no path — the hill keeps no path — but there was a road once, and the ground remembers it: at dawn and at dusk, when the light comes in low, a paler line shows in the grass, climbing in long deliberate switchbacks, the ghost of the route the builders' carts took when the stone went up. Walk the ghost-road and the climb is easier. The hill seems to prefer it.

The air changes at a certain height, all at once, the way it changed on the cistern's fourth step — but inverted. There the change was downward: cool, mineral, wet. Here you climb into wind, and dryness, and the smell of thyme crushed underfoot, and light with no mercy in it, and once the wind has found you it does not let go again until you descend. The wind is the first citizen of this place. Everything else lives here on terms the wind has set.

The tower stands alone on the bald crown of the hill, and it is the only made thing in sight. Understand the inversion, because the two places are built on it: in the cistern, nothing is natural — every surface shaped, every brick placed, a made world sunk whole into the earth. Here it is the opposite. The hill is unmade, the grass is unmade, the enormous sky is unmade, and in the middle of all that unmadeness stands one deliberate thing, a round tower of coursed stone, roofless, the height of perhaps ten men, held up into the weather like something being offered.

The stone is pecked. Go close, put your face near the surface, and under the crusted lichen you can read the same dimpled tool-marks that line the cistern's walls — thousands of small frozen percussions, the identical signature, the identical patience. The same masons made both, or masons trained in the same hands, working the same tradition outward across the country, chamber by chamber and tower by tower, and then going on, the way scaffolding goes on, until they were gone entirely. There is a scooped hollow on the hill's northern shoulder where the stone was quarried; it holds a dew-pond now, and sheep drink from the hole the tower came out of.

There is one door, low, so that you enter stooped — the tower's single demand of the body, a bow at the threshold. Inside there is no roof and no floor above you: a shaft of open sky, blindingly bright at midday, ringed by the rim of the wall high overhead. And lining the whole interior, spiraling up from head-height to the rim, are the niches.

Hundreds of them. Small recesses left in the coursing, each the width of two spread hands — the width, exactly, of the cistern's oculus, and this is not a coincidence; the whole system is built to one gauge, the gauge of a folded courier. There is no stair. There are hand-and-toe holds worn into the wall, worn on their outer edges by climbers who hugged the stone against the drop, and no one living has used them.

By day, every niche holds a small darkness. The tower is a bright shaft studded with hundreds of pockets of night, and in each pocket of night a leather-winged thing hangs asleep, and the sleep is not rest. The sleep is custody. This is the place the couriers come to when they rise out of the oculus before dawn — this, and here is the thing to understand about the tower before anything else: they do not come only from the cistern. They come at first light from every direction below the horizon, dozens, hundreds, each one folding out of the grey air and dropping over the rim, each one carrying a column. There are more counting-rooms sunk in the country than anyone has numbered — rooms under other hills, other towns, other silences, each with its niches and its chorus and its patient afternoon arithmetic — and every one of them empties its figure into the sky at night, and every figure comes here. The cistern is one column. The tower is the sum.

The floor of the tower is mostly mound: a pale, chalky, gently domed accumulation at the center of the shaft, the settled droppings of the couriers laid down over centuries, taller than a standing person at its crown. And the mound is not bare. It grows things — and what it grows is wrong for this hill, wrong for this whole dry upland: soft valley flowers, a reed, a moisture-loving sedge, seeds carried in from every distant place the couriers pass over and cast down here in the one spot of richness on the whole bald crown. It is a garden of elsewhere. Among the growth, leaning out of the mound's flank toward the door, is a single pale fern.

The tower is never silent, but its sound is nothing like the cistern's. The cistern roars from within; the tower is played from without. The wind crossing the open rim and threading the hundreds of niches makes the whole structure sound, one low continuous tone with a shifting body of overtones, and — a visitor notices this on the second day, not the first — the tone is not the same from day to day. It varies with occupancy. Each sleeping body damps its niche; each empty niche whistles. The tower sounds its total, all day, to the empty hill. At dusk, when the couriers pour up out of the shaft and scatter to their rooms across the country, the tone thins and rises, and all night the tower stands on its hill whistling high and hollow — the sound of zero, the sound of a ledger open and waiting.

Other wrongnesses, in their order of discovery. Count the couriers leaving at dusk: count twice, the figure agrees with itself; count the next dusk and the figure has changed, and agrees with itself again. The wind at the door is never quite the wind on the hill — hold a grass stem at the threshold and it leans on a different schedule from the grass ten paces away; the tower breathes on its own time. The sun, crossing the open rim, drives a band of brightness slowly around and down the spiral of niches over the course of a day, touching each one in turn — the same slow clock-hand as the cistern's light-column, but here it is not telling time. It is reading. Once a day, niche by niche, the sunlight takes the registers.

And no bird ever lands on the rim. In open country, on the highest point for miles, on a warm stone ring that any crow or dove should covet — nothing lands there. Nothing except one.

Nothing here is dangerous either. But where the cistern counts you the moment you descend — adds you, carries you, subtracts you when you leave — the tower does something quieter and worse. Step through the low door and the tone does not change. You are not added. Stand in the bright shaft under the hundreds of sleeping darknesses and feel the fact settle: the tower is not counting you because it does not need to. You were counted somewhere else, some other day, in some dim room you did or did not know you had entered, and your figure came here on wings while you slept, and the tower has already had you.

What lives in the tower lives in shifts, because the tower is one half of a day-long breath — full and silent-toned by light, empty and whistling by dark — and each of its residents belongs to one phase of the breathing.

**The couriers by day** are the tower's substance, the way the frogs are the cistern's. Asleep, folded, one to a niche, they are not resting from their work; they are performing it. What a courier carries it carries in itself — the figure taken down at the oculus in that compressed backward chitter — and the only way to hold a figure through the long bright dangerous day is to hold it in a sleeping body, sealed, dreamless, damped. Their sleep is storage. This is why the wind, which enters everything on this hill, never enters an occupied niche: the tower will play its flute on the empty cells and the dead air, but a holding body is not disturbed. And this is why, each dawn, the arriving couriers do not return to yesterday's niches. Watch the dawn settling — it takes the better part of an hour, the shaft filling with slow circling bodies, each one hesitating, choosing, folding in — and understand that the choosing is the arithmetic. Which niches are filled, in which rings, in which order around the spiral: the arrangement is the number. The couriers are digits here exactly as the frogs are digits below — the same office in a different medium — except that a frog holds one place in one column, and a courier, hanging in the great spiral, is a bead on an abacus the size of a tower, and the abacus is set fresh every morning with the summed count of every dim room in the country. Then the sun's band comes around, reads the registers once, slowly, and the day's total is taken.

**The falcon** keeps the rim, and is the reason nothing else lands there. She is a small dry-country falcon, dust-colored, and she hunts the slopes below the hill — larks, voles, the ordinary economy — but she has never in any watcher's memory taken a courier, though every dusk several hundred of them pour past her perch slow and low and heavy with sleep still on them, the easiest hunting the world could offer. She rises with them instead. She circles the departing cloud once, twice, and if any courier falters — flies crooked, labors, carries its figure loosely — she takes that one, and only that one, out of the air before it can leave the hill. The eels rise to meet the dead frog halfway down; the falcon meets the failing courier halfway up. She is the sum's error-correction, its warden, and whatever truce she keeps is older than she is, older than her line; falcons have kept this rim the way the number of frogs has kept itself. She takes her wage from the slopes and her duty from the tower and she asks nothing and explains nothing.

**The wasps** are the last masons. They come in early summer and build in the unoccupied niches: grey paper cells, chambered, coursed, corbelled in miniature — and their paper, if you look closely, is made of the tower, scraped from its weathered stone-dust and its ancient door-timber, so that they are quite literally building small rooms out of the great room, the tower's own substance recycled into new chambers by the only builders left in this country. Everything with hands is gone; the wasps are what remains of the habit of making, a mason's tradition orphaned from its species and carried on blind. It never lasts. The wind unbuilds them — any nest that would fill a niche falsely, that would damp a cell and put a counterfeit digit into the tower's tone, is found by the wind within days and shredded and carried off the hill. The wasps begin again. They have begun again every summer for longer than the lichen can testify to, and they will never stop, and their paper never survives a season, and there is something in this — the endless small construction, the endless patient demolition — that the tower seems not merely to tolerate but to require, the way a ledger requires an eraser.

**The lichen** is the outside's archivist, the snails' dry cousin. It grows on the tower's weather faces in slow crusted circles, each colony widening at a rate that does not vary — a thumbnail in a lifetime — and so the tower's whole exterior is a clock-face of overlapping rings, its age written in growth for anyone who could read growth, the way the cistern's walls are written in silver for anyone who could read silver. Two exterior scripts, then, overlaying the same tool-marks on two buildings a country apart: the trails record the keeping of the wet room; the rings record the enduring of the dry one. The couriers never brush the lichen. The falcon never fouls it. The record is respected.

**The wind** is here what the eels are there, and once this is seen it cannot be unseen. The pool's eels cannot be counted, arrived by no possible route, patrol without ceasing, and keep the water from holding any fact too long. The wind cannot be counted, comes from nowhere answerable, patrols the hill without ceasing, and keeps the summit from holding anything at all — no soil, no path, no scent, no warmth, nothing but the tower and the mound. The cistern has no outlet; the tower is all outlet. Everything the wind touches, it takes a little of, daily: grit from the coursing, dust from the mound's rim, moisture, heat, the day's tone itself, carried off the hill and spent into the general air. The ledger is being paid out in dust, a grain at a time. The tower is measurably lower than its own oldest lichen rings say it was. Whether the count will end when the tower is finally gone, or the tower will be allowed to be gone when the count is finally complete, is the kind of question the place raises and declines to answer; but the eels lie down between two herringbones to be addressed by the chorus, and the wind, at the height of each day's tone, goes strangely steady across the rim, holding one pressure, one direction, for the space of many breaths — and it is hard, standing in the shaft, not to conclude that the sum speaks to its remainder here too, and that the remainder, here too, listens.

**The mound and its garden** are the sum made solid — every figure ever carried here, held for a day in a sleeping body, spent, and settled. Centuries of counted rainfall and counted drips and counted arrivals, rendered down into pale chalk and heaped at the axis of the shaft, and out of that heap grows the garden of elsewhere: the valley flowers, the misplaced sedge, seeds from over every counting-room in the country. And the fern. It is the same species as the fern that leans from the joint beside the cistern's oculus — the pale outward-listening fern, the one thing in the wet room oriented toward the world — and there is only one way its spore came to this hill: on a courier's back, through the narrow stone mouth, across the dark miles, into the shaft. Somewhere far from here its parent watches a small grey opening in a corbelled ceiling. Its child grows on the hoard of spent numbers, leaning toward the door. The two ferns will never know of each other, and they are the truest kinship the two places have: each stationed at its room's one opening, each watching what comes through, each — possibly — counting the counters.

**And the niche.** One niche, at the top of the third ring, at the height where the morning band of sunlight first touches the spiral, is always wet. In a place where everything is dry — where the wind takes the dew off the grass by mid-morning and the stone is warm as bread by noon — that one recess is beaded, always, drought or no drought, its little hollow of shadow holding moisture the way the cistern's bench holds its impossible dryness. No courier has ever folded into it. The wasps skirt it by a hand's width. The falcon, riding her circles above the rim, does not cross the air above it. It is a seat, and it is kept, and by now the rule can be stated plainly, because it holds in both places and presumably in every room the ledger runs: the keeper's seat is the exception. In the wet room it is the dry thing. In the dry room it is the wet thing. Whatever presided at the far wall of the cistern, receiving the ascending afternoon sum, holds a place here too — or holds all its places at once, one refusal of the local condition in every chamber of the system, so that the whole country of counting-rooms and this one summing tower are less a network than a single body, seated everywhere, auditing everything, dry where the world weeps and wet where the world is wrung.

So: the cistern's last fact, finished. If you ever went down those twelve cupped steps — if you sat on the walkway with the cold coming up through the brick while the chorus entered you in the ledger — then that night, while you slept in the dry loud unaccounted world, your figure came here. It came folded small in a chittering body, over the dark hills, down through the open rim; it was chosen a niche; it was held in a sleeping darkness through one whole turning of the sun; the tower's tone included you for a day, and the light read you once, slowly, in passing. Then you were spent, and you settled, and you are in the mound now, under the garden, feeding some soft valley flower that has no business on this hill — a small bright misplaced thing that is, in part, the permanent record of your visit.

And on the clearest nights, when the tower stands empty and whistling its thin zero, a visitor camped below the summit may hear, far off, from some other height in some other direction, a second tone answering on the wind — lower, larger, the sound of a greater flute. The tower is a digit too. Somewhere, in whatever wider sum this hill feeds, it is still being carried.
