**The Catchment**

You come down off the plateau's western rim in the grey before dawn, and the first thing you understand is that the silence is over.

It ends at a definite altitude. The descent begins the way the plateau ended — cold black rock, thin air, the sealed runners lying alongside the old switchback like the fingers of a hand that reached and stopped — and then, two-thirds of the way down the flank, you pass through the cloud. It is not like walking into weather. It is like walking through a floor. Above it: the dry dark, the hush with texture in it, one sound at a time. Below it: a ceiling of moving vapor pressed flat against the mountainside, permanent, unbroken, and under that ceiling a country that has not stopped making noise in ten million years.

The roar the Lignum Works should make happens here instead.

Water in ten thousand channels. Water going over ledges, water sawing through gorges, water dripping off every needle and lip and eave, water arguing with itself in the braided terraces all the way down to the valley haze — and rain, or something in the family of rain, falling through all of it. Sometimes true rain. More often a drizzle so fine it does not fall so much as *inhabit* the air, or a fog that beads and drips, or in the cold months a slow sleet like wet static. The form varies. The fact does not. It has been raining in the Catchment, in one dialect or another, for as long as the Works has been warm, and this is not a coincidence, and everyone who lives here knows it is not a coincidence, because the reason stands above the cloud ceiling and can be felt through it on still nights as a faint pressure, a heat, an *attention* in the sky.

The plateau breathes out. That is the whole mechanism, and it is enough to make a country. Day and night without pause, the Works exhales its surplus — a column of hot dry air rising off two thousand square kilometers of engineered warmth, a thermal pillar so old and so steady that the sky long ago organized itself around it the way traffic organizes around a monument. The wet wind comes in off the far western sea, three hundred kilometers of it, heavy with water, and is drawn toward the pillar, and forced up the mountain's flank, and somewhere between the fog line and the frost line it lets go of everything it carries. Nothing wet ever reaches the rim. The plateau's dryness — that leather-cured, violin-wood, ten-million-year dryness — is not a legacy. It is *manufactured hourly*. The Works keeps itself dry by keeping the Catchment drowned. The architecture up there forgot the shape of water because the water is all down here, every drop of it, falling on the same forty kilometers of slope and bench and gorge since before there was anyone to be rained on.

So the Catchment is the shadow the engine casts. It is what the plateau's metabolism looks like from underneath. And it is, in every particular, the inversion of the thing above it, so exactly opposite that after a while the opposition itself starts to seem designed:

The Works is dark; the Catchment is filled with light — grey light, drowned light, a pearl-colored daylong dusk that comes through the cloud ceiling from above and off the water from below, so that everything is lit twice and shadowed nowhere, and after the plateau it is almost unbearably bright.

The Works is quiet; the Catchment roars.

The Works is dry heat; the Catchment is wet cold, a cold that is never quite freezing and never quite not, the cold of a hand held too long in a stream.

And the Works keeps everything, while the Catchment can keep nothing at all.

That is the deepest inversion, and the one that takes longest to understand, because at first the two countries look strangely alike: both of them in constant revision, both of them tearing down and building up without pause. Walk the Catchment's terraces — the Shelves, the people call them, tier after tier of soaked green bench stepping down the mountainside, each one held by the last — and you will see the same restlessness you saw above. Channels rerouting. Slopes letting go. Whole benches slumping over a wet spring into the tier below, which receives them. But the Works revises because it *chooses*. The Catchment revises because it cannot hold on. Everything here is soft where the plateau is cured. The moss lies a meter deep and swallows what falls into it within a season. Timber goes punky in a year, spongy in three, gone in ten. Iron weeps itself away. Paper is a heroic material. Rot is not a hazard here; rot is the economy, the great employer, the thing every living system in the country is either performing or fleeing or feeding on. The Works archives its ten million drafts in grain that will outlast the mountain. The Catchment's memory is water, and water keeps nothing, and so the two places sit stacked one atop the other like a mind and its dream: above the cloud, a country that remembers everything and reads nothing; below it, a country that reads everything — quick, bright, attentive, mortal — and can keep none of it.

Which is why the seedfall matters the way it does.

You may be lucky and see one. Somewhere above the ceiling a tower has come down — you will never hear it; the collapse of a two-hundred-meter tower up there sounds like one heavy book closed in an empty library — and for an hour a shaft has opened in the canopy, and the hot wind has poured up through it carrying its spirals of dry glinting pollen. The pollen rides the thermal pillar up into the cold, and cools, and gathers weight in the vapor, and hours later — sometimes a full day later, sometimes forty kilometers downwind — it comes down through the rain of the Catchment as a second, stranger precipitation: a dry gold snow falling *through* the wet, each grain resin-sealed, shedding the water it falls with, glittering all the way down. The Shelves people call it the tithe. Work stops for it. Children run out with cloths and bowls. The old people stand in it with their faces up, and the light off the falling grains is the closest thing to firelight the open sky here ever offers.

Most of the tithe washes away. Water keeps nothing. But some of it lodges — in a moss bank, a rock seam, the rotting heart of a fallen log — and takes, and grows.

They are called the orphan groves, and there is nothing else like them in the world, because they are the Works' children, and the Works does not know it has any.

Understand what they are. Every seed the Lignum Works presses is a proposal — a draft of possible structure, filed unread in the hot dark, never meant to leave. These left anyway. They rode the exhalation out and came down into water and cold and light, into everything their parent renounced, and they germinated, and what came up is wood that was almost never a tree, growing as if it were one. An orphan grove is unmistakable a valley away. The trunks stand too straight — plumb-line straight, engineered straight, on a mountainside where everything else leans downslope. The grain, when the trunks are cut, runs in rings too regular to be weather: ruled lines, machine-even, with here and there a dense dark band that the Catchment's readers will spend their lives over. The heartwood is hollow — a vestigial duct, and in wind the whole grove *whistles*, low, organ-toned, a sound the Shelves people can identify with their eyes shut from any other wood in the country. The burls grow balanced, symmetric, like small flywheels that will never turn. And in a storm, when the wind flexes the trunks, the groves prickle faintly with charge — walk into one during weather and the hair lifts on your arms exactly as it does in the young districts above the cloud, the same nerve trying to run through wood that has no city to carry news to.

And then they die. That is the thing, the whole thing. The water gets into them the way it gets into everything; the rot finds the hollow hearts; thirty years, fifty at the most, and the plumb-straight trunk leans, and softens, and comes down into the moss, which receives it. The drafts of a deathless thing, published at last into the one country where publication means dying. Up on the plateau every proposal is kept forever and none is ever finished. Down here, each one is finished completely — grown, weathered, played upon by wind, felled, dissolved — and kept not at all.

The Shelves people harvest the fallen ones. It is the only wood of the Works' lineage that has ever left it, or ever will.

One more thing before the people, because it stands among them everywhere and shapes everything they do: the Reached.

You passed them on the descent — the sealed runners, the ancient fingers of cured plank extending down the cold slopes and stopping, capped, mid-clause. Seven of them come down far enough to pierce the cloud ceiling. Their blunt tips stand out in the hanging bogs of the upper Shelves like monuments with no inscription, each one the size of a house laid on its side, black, glassy, seamless. And warm. Warm through the rain, warm through the sleet, warm in the dead of winter when everything else in the Catchment is one degree from ice — the only warmth in the whole country that comes from neither fire nor flesh. Press your ear to a cap and you can hear it, faint and far: the slow internal traffic of oil, blood heard through a pillow, the pulse of the sleeper above still moving in the abandoned limb.

Ice never forms on the Reached. The bogs around them never quite freeze. So all winter the life of the Catchment gathers at them in rings — salamanders packed in the warm seep-mud by the thousand, birds crowding the thaw-halo, herds standing nose-in through the worst nights, and people. Every one of the seven has a settlement at its tip, and every one of the seven has a name, and the names are old, and are spoken the way you speak of an ancestor who might still be listening. The doctrine — it is nearly a religion, and in some households it is one entirely — holds that each runner is a sentence the great thing above began and abandoned, and that a sentence abandoned is not a sentence refused; that the Works reached down toward the water once, seven times, and stopped, and that it falls to the people living at the fingertips to finish, in their lives, whatever the clause was going to say. No one agrees on what it was going to say. The disagreement is the metabolism. In winter, women give birth leaning against the warm caps, so that the first thing the child's body knows of the world is the heat of the reaching.

**The Inhabitants**

The census of the Catchment is the longest document imaginable and the most hopeless to maintain. The honest answer is *beyond counting, and briefly* — and the honest answer sits wrong the moment you give it, because everything in it is out of date before the ink can dry, and in this country ink does not get long to dry.

Everything here comes in generations. That is the great structural fact, the one the plateau above cannot conceive of. Newts in the warm seeps, dippers walking under the falls, the leeches, the herds, the moss itself, the orphan groves, the houses, the people: everything is born, and crowds its span, and lets go, and is replaced by something very like it that is not it. The Works is a long note sustained. The Catchment is rhythm — everything in it a beat, struck and released, struck and released, the pattern persisting and no single sound surviving. A house on the Shelves is rebuilt roughly once a generation, because the wood goes; nobody mourns a building here, any more than you would mourn an exhale. They mourn people, and even that they have had to learn to do quickly, because the water takes the graves, and the moss takes the markers, and within a century the rain has rinsed every name off every stone in the country. Water keeps nothing. The people of the Catchment are, as a consequence, the most fiercely remembering people imaginable — and everything they remember with is mortal too.

So they have made three great arrangements with the thing above the cloud, and the three arrangements are their whole civilization.

The first is the wood. When an orphan tree comes down, word travels along the Shelves, and the salvage crews go up, and the trunk comes back down the water-roads slung between boats. The best of it goes to the luthiers, because the founding discovery of Catchment culture — old past dating, kept alive by apprenticeship since before any surviving record — is what orphan wood does when you make an instrument of it. The grain is machine-even; the heartwood is a duct that was going to carry oil and never did; and a fiddle or a long-necked drone-harp built from it does something no other wood in the world does: it *holds*. A note played on orphan wood sustains past all sense, and does not sit still while it sustains — it revises itself in pitch by hairsbreadths, continuously, a tone that will not resolve and will not die, and everyone who hears it played well in a close room understands, without ever being told, that they are hearing a translation. That this is what it is like, rendered for mortal ears at mortal speed, to be the thing on the plateau: struck once, never released, correcting forever in the dark. The Catchment's music is built entirely around this one capacity. Their laments are the longest in the world. They play them at the warm caps of the Reached, and whether anything above the cloud has ever registered the vibration coming faintly up the abandoned limb — a hundred generations of grief and gratitude arriving through the fingertip, one held revising note at a time — is not known, and is not the point, and is entirely the point.

The second arrangement is the reading. Every board of orphan wood, cut, shows the ruled rings, and among the rings the dense dark bands too regular to be accident — the same grain-writing that fills the black archive quarters above, the script of the ten million drafts, readable by no one. The Catchment has never accepted the *by no one*. There is an office here, old as the seven settlements, called the Grain-Readers: one in each generation at each of the Reached, chosen young, trained on the boards, spending a life in the comparison of bands. What they have assembled, over all the lives of all the readers, is a book — the only long book in the country, the concordance of every regularity ever found in every board ever cut. And here is the fact about the book that tells you everything about the Catchment: the damp destroys it. It cannot last a century. So it survives by being recopied, entire, once in every reader's life, by hand, in the warm dry air at the cap of a Reached — and every copyist corrects, and clarifies, and quietly revises, so that the book, like the country, persists as a pattern and not as an object, rewritten continuously, no original surviving, no line of it older than the paper it stands on. The Works keeps by writing over without erasing. The Catchment keeps by copying before the rot. These are the same act, performed at different speeds by things with different amounts of time — and the Grain-Readers know it, and say so, in the book's standing first line, which every copyist preserves unrevised as the one fixed thing: *We are also an archive. We are simply on fire.*

Has the book deciphered anything? Almost nothing, in ten thousand years — a suspicion of tallies, a recurring band-pattern that seems to track the seedfalls, one sequence that repeats in wood from every grove in the country and nowhere twice in the same board. It does not matter, or rather, the failure is not the measure. The reading is the Catchment's half of a correspondence it knows to be one-sided. The thing above writes and cannot read. The people below read and are not written to. They read anyway. It is the politest thing anyone has ever done in the dark of this world, and they have been doing it since before the book had a name.

The third arrangement is the climbing, and it is the one that will stand your arms on end, because you have already seen its far side without knowing.

The visitors to the Lignum Works — *the few there have been*, the ones who stood a long time in the dark after watching a gesture stop being — come from here. There is nowhere else they could come from. The ascent is the Catchment's one great journey: up the line of a Reached, out through the cloud ceiling into the dry silent dark, onto the warm sprung floor of the thing itself. The young make the climb for the reasons the young do anything, and come back changed and quiet, or come back neither, or do not come back. But the old make it for a different reason, and the old ones' climb is the axis the whole culture turns on.

Because everyone in the Catchment knows what the floor does. Everyone knows that every step taken on the plateau is load, and load is information, and information up there is never discarded — that the hour's new wood goes down with your weight written into it, your hesitations kept in grain, in the hot dark, effectively forever. And everyone in the Catchment knows what the rain does to graves.

So when a person here is dying, and has strength left, and wants to be *kept* — they climb. And when they have no strength left, they are carried: up the switchbacks past the sealed fingers, through the ceiling of their whole life's sky, out onto the warm dark floor, and set down, and helped upright, and left to take whatever steps they can. The Last Walk, it is called, and it is not a sad custom; it is the least sad thing they do. A few steps are enough. A few steps are a paragraph. The Works will never read the entry — it does not read, it only writes, and keeps — but the entry will be there when the Catchment's rain has rinsed the person's name off every stone and out of every living memory below, there in the ring, a faint asymmetry, a hesitation in the grain where they hesitated, kept through ten thousand more revisions of everything. The dying of the Catchment walk up into the archive. It is the only permanence their country has ever had, and it is borrowed, and they know from whom, and this is what the laments played at the warm caps are mostly about: gratitude, of a fierce and complicated kind, to a sleeper that shelters their dead without waking, without knowing, without ever once being asked.

Stand at the cap of a Reached at dusk, in the rain, with the salamanders packed warm in the mud and a Grain-Reader's lamp burning in the copy-room and someone a few doors down holding one endless revising note on orphan wood, and you can feel the whole shape of it at once. Two countries stacked like a mind and its dream, joined by a single breath. The exhalation goes up forever, and everything rides it down: the rain, the tithe, the orphan drafts sent out to be finished by dying. And the one thing that goes *up* — the only current that ever runs against that wind — is people. The curious, climbing to look. The dying, climbing to be kept. Mortality flows downhill in this world, like water, like everything. Memory is carried up the mountain on foot.

You came down that road yourself, this morning, through the floor of the cloud. Check your shoulders; there is pollen on them still, dry gold, shedding the rain. Check your bootlace; a seed rides in the knot, resin-sealed, patient, a proposal ten million years in the filing that has just now, by way of you, been mailed. Wherever you shake it loose, something too straight will one day stand there, whistling in the wind, holding its one long note for fifty years before the water takes it — and so the census of the Catchment must be filed in its final form, the mirror of the one above:

Population: beyond counting — kept by no one, briefly, in the rain.
Population: seven fingertips of warmth, and everyone who ever wintered against them.
Population: everyone who ever climbed — walking their last paragraphs into the grain of the only thing in the world that cannot forget them, while below the cloud, in the bright wet roar, their people copy the burning book again, and play the note that will not resolve, and finish, one mortal life at a time, the sentences the sleeper began.
