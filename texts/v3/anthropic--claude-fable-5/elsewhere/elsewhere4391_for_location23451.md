**The Rime Heights**

The land here is old. You can tell by the edges of things: it has none left. The rock is granite gone gray and grandfatherly, domed and dimpled, exfoliating in slow onion-shells, every corner it was ever born with rubbed away by weather that no longer exists. There are valleys here that were carved by rivers, and the valleys remain, and the rivers do not. There are shorelines cut into the flanks of the hills by lakes that evaporated before the plain below was poured. This is a country shaped entirely by water, standing in a world that has forgotten what water falling looks like.

It stands as islands. That is what the Heights are now: an archipelago of ancient summits in the young black sea of the Mound Plains. The basalt came from the east in floods, generations of floods, and it drowned this country's lowlands one by one — filled the river valleys, buried the marshes, lapped up the foothills and cooled and lapped higher — until only the high ground remained, ringed by a stone tide that still, in living memory, rises. Stand on a southern summit and you can see the whole truth of it at once: gray weathered rock under your feet, and below the last slopes a horizon-wide quilt of blue-green with ochre skylines standing out of it, and the low organ-breath of the cities' chimneys arriving faint and continuous on the wind, like the sound of the thing that ate the world, still chewing.

And yet the Heights are wet. That is the contradiction this country offers, and like the plain below it, it never resolves it — only repeats it, every single night.

The wetness comes up from below, and it comes from the enemy. The plain's dark rock pours heat upward all day, and the heat carries what the green quilt failed to drink at dawn — the escaped fraction of the plain's one daily breath, a thin freight of vapor riding the thermals two and three kilometers into the cold. The Heights stand in the way of it. After dark, the vapor finds the old stone and the old trees and freezes onto everything it touches, needle by needle, a white fur of rime laid over the whole country in the hours before midnight. Then the sun comes up, and the rime lets go.

The Heights *drip*. It is their weather, their season, their entire hydrology: every morning, for four or five hours, the whole country sheds its nightly ice as meltwater, branch to branch, ledge to ledge, filling the air with a sound the plain below has never once made. Trickle, patter, the irregular percussion of drops falling ten meters through still air onto stone. It is not much water — a heavy dew, by the standards of the drowned old world — but it is falling water, the only falling water anywhere, and everything that lives here has arranged itself beneath it like hands held under a tap.

So the geometry of the two countries is this, and once seen it cannot be unseen: the plain exhales, once a day, and the Heights are what the breath becomes. The murdered country is kept alive by the breathing of its murderer. The oldest living things in the world are watered by the youngest land in it. Nobody arranged this. It is simply where the vapor goes.

And where the plain is silent, the Heights are loud. That is the other inversion, the one that takes a traveler longest to stop flinching at. Under the canopy here, sound does not carry; the forest baffles and swallows it; a voice is safe in a way no voice on the open plain has been safe for ten thousand years. So the Heights kept their voices. The mornings here are drip and birdsong together, water-sound and throat-sound braided, and to an animal raised in the great hush below it is almost unbearable — a whole country talking in its sleep.

**The Trees**

Begin with the oldest, because everything else shelters under them.

They are not tall. Nothing here spends on height anymore; the trees of the Heights are low, vast-girthed, wind-sculpted things, more root and burl than crown, their wood so dense and slow it rings under a knuckle. They grow perhaps a hand's width in a century. The great ones — and every grove has its great ones, known individually to every creature in earshot — are older than the plain itself. This must be said plainly, because it is the hinge of the whole country: there are trees on these summits that stood under actual rain. Their heartwood was laid down in a world with rivers in it. When the basalt floods came and the sky went dry, they were already ancient, and they did what ancient trees do, which is fail to die.

They live now on the drip, and they have remade themselves for it. Their needles grew combs — fine bristled surfaces that strip rime from the night air and channel the morning melt inward along the twig, down the branch, down the furrowed bark in glistening threads to the root-plate. An old tree in the drip hours is a slow fountain running in reverse, gathering its own private rainfall out of the plain's lost breath. If the technique sounds familiar, it should. The cushion-plants of the plain — the whole hundred-million-hectare quilt — are the trees' descendants: the understory of this forest, flattened, spread, and sent out onto the new rock in the drowning years. The green sea around these islands is the forest's own child, grown larger than its parent by a factor the parent has no way to conceive. Some of the great trees and some of the great clonal mats below are, by descent, close cousins. The quilt drinks the fog at knee height so thoroughly that the sky above the plain stays empty; what little escapes, its ancestors comb from the cold; and in this way the family, without knowing it is a family, passes one day's water twice through its own hands.

The trees keep the country's records the way the cities below keep theirs — in structure. Every drought-year, every rich year, every year the rime came thick or thin is written in the rings, and the record runs back through the drowning, back through the last rain, back into the rain age itself. The difference is that no one down on the plain can read the cities' memory; it is held in walls and worked by the blind. Up here the memory has readers.

**The Singers**

The birds of the Heights sing, and the birds of the plain do not, and they are the same birds.

Every generation, some of the young ones stand on the last trees of the last slopes, look out over the green, and go. It has been happening since the basalt first offered them somewhere to land. Down there, in that open silence, a song is a beacon and a confession; within a handful of generations the emigrants' descendants had traded the throat for the wing-flash, and the plain filled up with the Heights' children with their mouths shut, speaking heliograph, telling the horizon nothing. The songless birds of the towers, semaphoring their courtships across the avenues at dawn — those are hill birds, edited. Now and again one of them strays back up on a thermal, blunders into the canopy, and stands in the drip and the morning chorus turning its head and flashing its wings at singers who are not watching, unable to answer a single voice. It usually leaves by noon.

But the ones who stayed did something with their voices that nothing else in this world has done. They remembered.

Song here is lineage. A young bird learns from its elders, note for note, and the elders learned from theirs, and certain songs — the old songs, the ones sung at the tarns at first light, the ones every species' repertoire is built around — are not invented and are not birdsong at all. They are water. Trills that run downhill. Long liquid passages that pool and spill. A rapid stony chatter that any human ear would place instantly, without being told: that is a stream over rocks. That is a river. There are phrases in the oldest songs that the birds who sing them have never heard the referent of, because the referent has been extinct for ten thousand years: the sound of rain arriving on a forest, learned by some ancestor from the last rains themselves and handed down, teacher to student, through ten thousand years of drought, with the fidelity of creatures whose whole culture is the accuracy of their ears. The last recording of rain in this world is kept in living throats, on mountaintops, above a plain that has never once heard it.

Whether the songs are still accurate, no one can check. That is the nature of the archive. The birds sing the rain every morning over the sound of the drip, correcting each other fiercely on details none of them can verify, and the water falls around them in its small surviving way, as if listening for its own name.

**The Tarns**

In the high cirques — bowls gouged by glaciers so long gone that even the trees don't remember them — the morning drip collects, and stands, and stays. There are perhaps forty tarns in all the Heights. They are the only standing water in the world.

They are small, cold, and unreasonably beautiful: dark glass set in gray stone, each one refilled every morning by its ring of dripping crags, each one holding through the day and night what nothing else anywhere holds — a reflection. The tarns are the only places in this world where the sky can see itself. Birds come from other summits just to drink at them, which is to say, for no reason anyone can defend, since the drip is everywhere; they come, it seems, for the water that waits.

In the tarns live the last amphibians. They are small, translucent-skinned, cold-blooded and cold-tempered, each pool a sovereign nation of a few hundred, and their entire manner of existing is a hostage to standing water: their eggs must float, their young must swim, there is no version of them that can be otherwise. All of them together — every individual of the last water-born vertebrates in the world — would fit in a traveler's two cupped hands, several times over, but not many times. They are not declining. That is the strange grace of it. The drip is faithful; the tarns have held their levels for millennia; the nations are small and stable and go about their slow cold politics as if the world still had an ocean in it somewhere. They are living the rain age in miniature, forty rooms of it, kept running on the breath of the very plain that ended it.

The tarns leak. Everything here does; old granite is a body of joints and fissures, and the overflow of every pool goes down into the dark of the mountain and is not seen again. But the buried valleys — the fossil riverbeds interred under the basalt floods — run east, downhill, under the plain. The old country's drainage still exists; it has merely been roofed over. And it has been noticed, by the few in a position to notice anything, that the deep pale caste of the builders, mining vapor kilometers down in the young rock, finds more water than young rock alone should breathe — and finds it richest along certain long, straight, valley-shaped seams. If that is what it appears to be, then the circle closes in the dark: the plain exhales, the Heights freeze the breath and melt it and drink it and spill the remainder into their own drowned rivers, and the drowned rivers carry it home under the quilt, to be walked up through the galleries a droplet at a time, in the throats of the rivers' pale replacements, and breathed out again. Two countries, one water, passed back and forth forever. Neither has any idea.

**The Shore**

Where the basalt meets the old rock there is a tide-line, and the tide is still coming in.

It is slow now — the great flood-eruptions are long finished, and what arrives at the Heights' feet in a decade is a creeping, crusted meter or two — but it arrives, and the shore is therefore the one place in this world where the two countries touch and negotiate. It is a strange, rich, provisional territory, and everything about it is double.

The newest basalt here is riddled with hollows in the shape of trees. That is what happens when the tide takes a grove: the lava molds itself around each trunk before the trunk burns away, and cools, and keeps the shape — a country's edge cast full of tree-shaped pipes, some still standing upright, stone ghosts of the forest at the exact posture of its death. The Heights' small mammals moved into the ghost-trees almost immediately, generations of them, denning in the hollow casts of their own former canopy; and it was those hollow-dwellers, already shaped for tunnel and gallery and dark, who later walked out onto the plain and found the cities offering tunnels at a scale no tree ever dreamed of. The vault-mice of the great conurbations, sitting up in rows in the gray hour combing fog from their fur, are shore-mice who kept going. They learned to live inside architecture by living inside the memory of trees.

And it is to the shore that the walkers come, at the end.

No one knows what calls them. A walker's whole life is the chord of the cities — the map of organ-notes it holds in its head, skyline to skyline across the continent — and the Heights are the one hole in that map, the one country with no builders, no chimneys, no note; from out on the plain the Heights are audible only as an absence, a silence standing on the horizon. Old walkers turn toward the silence. They leave the chord behind — days of walking with the voices of every city they have ever known fading behind them one by one — and come up against the black shore, and most of them end there, at the very rim of the world they circulated all their lives. The wax-sealed queens in their hoof-pouches do not end. The frontier cities — the young, small, high-voiced towers that stand right at the tide-line, adding their thin treble to the continent's chord a few new notes a century — are founded, nearly all of them, from the pouches of walkers who died reaching for the quiet. The plain's map grows its newest voices out of the graves of its carriers, at the feet of the one country that has none.

A few walkers do not stop at the shore. A few — old, slow, certain — climb: up the lava ramps, up the gray slopes, through the forest where the birds fall silent to watch them pass, all the way to a tarn. And there the largest animal of a country with no standing water lowers its great eared head and drinks, for the first and only time in its life, water that waits — and stays by the pool, days sometimes, and dies there, in the sound of the drip, inside the silence it navigated by. The bones around the old tarns go back further than the songs.

**What Remains**

Ask who lives in the Rime Heights and the answer comes out backwards from the plain's.

Down there, no individual matters and nothing dies: the builders are two years' ephemera, the arrangement is immortal, the place itself is the inhabitant. Up here it is the opposite in every particular. The arrangement is a remnant, a rain-age machine running on a trickle; but the individuals are tremendous — trees older than the continent below them, songs older than the trees, forty small nations of swimmers keeping the memory of oceans in bodies the size of a thumb. The plain is a single vast being with no one in it. The Heights are a gathering of ancient someones whose world is gone.

And they hold each other up, the two of them, without consent or knowledge, in the plainest physical way. The young land breathes; the old land condenses the breath and sings over it and sends it home through the dark. The plain's tenants are the Heights' emigrant children; the Heights' groves are the plain's quilt in its first, upright draft; the water is one water, and the memory is split between them — kept in walls below, where no one can read it, and in voices above, where no one can check it.

At dawn you can stand at a tarn and have the whole of it at once. Below, past the shore, the knee-high fog is being eaten off ten thousand kilometers of green, silently, from the bottom up. Around you the rime is beginning to let go, and the drip starts, ledge by ledge, and the first singer opens with the sound of rain falling on a forest — a sound the world has not made in ten thousand years, sung note-perfect over the only water in the world that reflects the sky. And underneath everything, if the wind is right, rising with the vapor that will be tonight's ice: the chord of the cities, faint and low and patient, the voice of the young country breathing its once a day —

which the old country catches, as it has every night since the drowning, and turns back into rain the only way still left: slowly, at first light, one drop at a time.
