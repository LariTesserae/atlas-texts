**The Race**

The cable knows the way, even if nothing else does.

On the chamber's western wall it enters solid rock and does not come out again — not there. But armored conduit carries a leak-field, and a leak-field can be followed, and over the course of nine seasons a survey team with induction gear and more patience than funding walked the line's ghost across the surface: over the fracture country, down through the old scarps where the stone finally remembers weather, sixty-one kilometers by the crow and considerably more by the cable, which wanders underground the way buried things do, avoiding faults no one on the surface can see. The signal never faded and never flickered. Whatever the line carries, it carries steadily, and it carries it toward the sea.

It ends — begins — at the Race.

You hear it long before the coast is visible, and this is the first thing visitors from the chamber remark on, because the two places are, by the meter, nearly the same volume. Ninety-some decibels there; ninety-some here. It is difficult to overstate how little else they share. The chamber's sound is a single held chord that the nervous system files away as silence within an hour. The Race's sound has never once repeated itself. It is water in a slot — a sea-chasm perhaps two hundred meters long and nowhere more than nine wide, cut into cliffs of grey banded stone, through which the tide of an entire embayment is forced twice a day in each direction. The bore comes through as a standing chaos of collapse and slam and hiss and gulp, boulders knocking in the throat like teeth in a jaw, and no two seconds of it have ever been alike, and no two seconds of it ever will be. People who sleep easily beside the chamber's drone cannot sleep here at all. You cannot stop hearing the Race, because it never gives you the same thing twice to stop hearing.

The rock is old the way the chamber's rock is young. Every surface in the descent to the water is a settled negotiation: potholes bored smooth, edges surrendered geological ages ago, whole galleries polished to a dull shine by suspended grit. There are no corners. There have been no corners for a very long time. The chamber smells of struck flint and a chemistry that never met rain; the Race smells of iodine and salt and rot and roe, the loud wet smell of a place where things are being born and eaten in quantities no census will ever hold. The light changes by the minute — spray-haze, cloud, the low sun firing the chasm walls, the green underwater glow at slack tide. The lichen of the chamber, printed once in the shape of a lamp and holding that shape forever, would go mad here in an afternoon. The Race has never offered any living thing the same light twice.

Someone built here too. This is not in question the way it is in the chamber, because here the work is legible. Above the narrowest point of the chasm — the throat — stand the intake gates, rusted open, their pintles worn to sculpture. Below them, cut into the western wall and drowned to the waist at every flood, is the wheel hall: a single vaulted gallery of the same honest riveted construction as the engine, the same ceramic insulators bolted in rows like vertebrae, unmistakably the same hand. Whoever they were, they built at both ends of the line, and they built to last, and they are gone from both ends equally.

And in the hall, mounted in bearings the size of millstones, is the wheel.

It is everything the engine is not. The engine runs at one speed and has always run at one speed; the wheel has never held a speed in its existence. It takes the bore full in its blades and shrieks up to a blur; it stalls against the turning tide and hangs, groaning, sometimes for whole minutes, sometimes rocking backward with a slam that you feel in the cliff; it fouls, sheds its fouling, catches, races. Its sound is the loudest single voice in the Race's chorus and the least predictable. The power it sends down the line is, at the point of generation, filthy — surging, spiking, dropping dead, harmonically rank. The instruments that measured it were designed for the chamber's serene supply, and the first readings were assumed to be equipment failure.

They were not. And the reconciliation of the two sets of readings — the wheel's chaos at one end of the cable, the engine's perfection at the other — is the finding that reorganized everything anyone thought about either place. Something along those sixty-one kilometers eats the difference.

**The worms.** They are the same worms. This should be said plainly, because it is the one plain fact in the whole file: the thread-thin, metallically iridescent conduit worms braided through the heat-fins in the Standing Chamber and the worms braided over the cable head at the Race are one continuous nation, and the probes pushed into the rock at both ends were touching the same body of life from opposite sides. Here at the Race the braid is at its thickest — a sheath of plaited tissue as deep as a forearm where the cable leaves the wheel hall, so dense that the original armoring has not been seen in living memory and may no longer exist, may have been eaten and replaced, wrap by helical wrap, until the insulation *is* the population. They feed by induction, as at the chamber, but here there is so much more to feed on: every surge, every spike, every rank harmonic the wheel throws down the line is disorder in the field, and disorder in the field is food. The braid dines on the filth of the current, and what it cannot use it passes down-line to kin with subtler appetites, and the kin pass their leavings further still, and the surveys mapping worm density along the cable's buried length found exactly what the theory demanded: the braid thins steadily toward the chamber, kilometer by kilometer, as the current runs out of chaos to offer. By the time the line surfaces in that dry black room sixty-one kilometers away, it has been filtered through the appetites of an entire strung-out civilization, and what arrives is what the engine has always received — power of impossible purity, chaos with everything eaten out of it but the mean.

The chamber's eternal note, in other words, begins here, in the slam and stall of a wheel that cannot hold a speed, and is manufactured along the way out of everything the wheel does wrong. The perfection was never the engine's. It is digestion.

**The churn.** The Race's own fauna resist the census in the opposite way from the chamber's. There, the surveyors gave up naming animals and named positions, because the animals declined to be told apart and the positions never moved. Here there are no positions. There is no address in the Race that exists from one tide to the next — every shoreline migrates twice daily, every pool is provisional, every surface is scoured — and so the unit of life is not the place but the moment, and the surveys eventually stopped trying to list species and began listing verbs. The collective term that stuck is *the churn*: the ephemeral biomass of the chasm, spume-life and fry and swarm, generation times measured in hours, whole apparent species arising and vanishing between expeditions, so that the census is out of date before the ink dries and possibly before the sentence ends. Taxonomy assumes its subjects will outlive the paperwork. The churn does not, and the surveyors' field notes read, in places, like weather reports.

And it has teeth. This deserves the same pause the chamber's toothlessness got. The Race's food web is all verbs — chase, ambush, bolt, gorge, flee — a vertical panic of eating and being eaten running from the swarm up through darting silver hunters to the pale long shapes that come in with the flood and leave with it. Nothing here has agreed to anything. Energy enters as sunlight and tide and leaves as heat and motion and death, contested at every step, and visitors from the chamber, accustomed to a room where nothing has ever fled from anything, find the first hour in the Race either exhilarating or unbearable, usually in that order.

**The riders.** On the wheel itself lives the fauna the surveyors are proudest of, because nothing like it should be possible. Limpet-formed, dense-shelled, gripping the blades and the rim in scattered constellations, the riders spend their whole lives being flung — accelerated, stalled, slammed, reversed — and this, as far as anyone can determine, is what they eat. Their bodies are inertial: a heavy free mass slung in a muscular sleeve, and every lurch of the wheel drags the mass through the sleeve, and the dragging drives them, exactly as the standing wave's flexing drives the hummers. They are the hummers' dark cousins. A hummer feeds on one pure frequency and dies of any variation; a rider feeds on variation itself and dies of purity. Riders pried loose and kept in still tanks starve untouched beside food they have no way to want. Riders moved experimentally to smooth-running machinery starve just as surely — steady rotation is, to a rider, silence. They need the jerk, the slam, the stall. They have colonized the one surface in the world that will never stop misbehaving, and they crowd the wheel's rim the way the hummers crowd the antinodes: each species pressed hungrily against its own definition of food, each unable to survive one day in the other's paradise.

**The holdfast.** And then there is the one resident that breaks every rule the others keep, and by now you will know the shape of it before it is described.

At the throat of the chasm — the narrowest point, the worst water in the Race, where the full bore comes through like a dropped wall — there is a boulder-sized dome of ringed, horn-grey shell, gripping the sill. It does not move. It has not moved in the entire record: not in the bore, not in the storms that rearrange the chasm floor around it, not in the tide that has translocated everything else in the Race a thousand times over. The early surveyors assumed it was rock. It is measurably cooler than the water around it — the only cold anomaly in a chasm that is thermal chaos everywhere else, the Race's one piece of stillness the way the bellows is the chamber's one piece of weather. What it eats, no one knows. The best hypothesis, offered quietly, is that it eats the flow's assault directly — that grip itself, maintained against that water, is somehow a harvest and not a cost. It is the only fixed address in the Race, and the surveyors have given it the tenderness they give the bellows, and for the mirrored reason: in the chamber, the bellows is the only resident with a tomorrow different from its today, and may by that definition be the only living thing in the room. In the Race, where everything is nothing *but* tomorrows, the holdfast is the only resident that will still be itself when you return — and by a definition of life that requires persistence, it may be the only thing in the chasm that qualifies. Each place keeps exactly one dissenter. Each dissenter practices the other place's religion.

**The pulse.** The last entry in the Race's file is the one that gets discussed at night, off the record, at the little survey station above the cliffs.

There is a signal on the cable that does not come from the wheel. It is faint, and it is perfectly steady, and it comes *up* the line — from the east, from sixty-one kilometers of rock away — and its frequency is the chamber's chord. The electrical ghost of the standing wave, riding the conductor home. That much is measurement. The rest is the part said quietly: the wheel has never held a speed in its life, but integrate its rotation over a season — average every shriek and stall and backward slam — and the mean comes out at the engine's speed. Not near it. At it, to as many decimal places as the instruments will certify. One machine that never varies, one machine that never repeats, and between them a single unwavering number that each expresses in its own temperament, and no one has been able to establish which end disciplines which — whether the herd-polished chord is the governor that trims the wheel's chaos toward its mean, or the wheel's long average is the pulse the engine has been faithfully holding all along, or whether the question even divides that way. The builders left no manual. They left a circuit, and the circuit closed over their absence like water over a stone, and it has been trading with itself ever since: the Race sending change down the line to be eaten into constancy, the chamber sending constancy back up the line to give the change its center. Sixty-one kilometers of worm carry the trade in both directions and take their wages out of the difference. The surveyors' standing joke — every station has one — is that they have not found two places at all. They have found one organism with two chambers to its heart: one chamber that beats and one that holds, one that spends every moment the instant it arrives and one that has kept a single moment so long it has forgotten there were ever others, joined by an artery that is also the only citizen of both.

The organism does not notice them either. That, at least, the two ends agree on.

What the ends do not agree on is memory. In the chamber, nothing erases: every footprint of every expedition lies in the dust by the entrance, back to the first, a complete archive kept by a room that will never consult it. The Race keeps nothing. Bolt a marker to the chasm wall and the winter takes it; scratch a benchmark and the grit polishes it out; the station above the cliffs has been rebuilt four times and the tide line holds no evidence of the other three. Every expedition to the Race arrives at a place with no record it was ever visited, and leaves knowing it will contribute none. The chamber remembers everything and changes nothing; the Race changes everything and remembers nothing — and between them, humming in the dark under sixty-one kilometers of rock, runs the one thread that does both, carrying the pulse from the heart that beats to the heart that holds, steady as the note, old as the wheel, needing nothing at either end, least of all to be understood.
