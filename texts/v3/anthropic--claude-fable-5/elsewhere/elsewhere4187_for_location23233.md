**The Registry of Returns**

You reach it by descending, which is the first strange thing about the driest room in the world. The city at the feet of the stacks is the wettest place anyone in it has ever been — it sits directly beneath the cloud deck, in the deck's permanent business, and the rain does not so much fall as continue. Water sheets down the flanks of the great riveted columns that rise out of the rooftops and vanish into the gray overhead, so that each stack wears a moving skin, and the streets are guttered like the decks of ships, and rust is the civic color, blooming on every railing and hinge and lamp bracket, honest and orange and everywhere. Dryness, in such a city, is a thing you would expect to find by climbing. Instead you go down.

Granite steps, and a door, and more steps, and a second door faced in green baize, and by the time you reach the third door the sound of the rain — which you have heard every hour of every day since you arrived in the city, which the citizens have stopped hearing entirely — thins, and recedes, and dies. What replaces it is air with no water in it at all. It crackles faintly in the nose. Your lips tighten within a minute; your hair lifts from your collar; a spark snaps from your fingertip to the brass hand-rail, and the clerk at the wicket does not look up, because everyone sparks, coming in. Down in the foundations, behind louvered panels, machinery performs slow work to keep it so — a heave, a long pause, a heave, desiccant wheels turning at the pace of a slept-through night — and if you put your palm against the plating you can feel it, and if you have put your palm against certain other plating, eleven hundred rungs up in the cold, you will notice that the rhythm is the same. It is the same. The same firm built both plants, the same felt mounts, the same baffles. The Registry keeps paper the way the stack keeps water: warm enough, still enough, and at any cost.

Below the wicket the galleries begin, and there are eleven of them, each sunk below the last, each a long vaulted room walled floor to ceiling in drawers. Oak fronts, brass pulls, and the pulls are green — the verdigris of a century of hands, a patina the Registry is quietly proud of, decay proceeding here at its lawful pace, everything aging honorably, the cards foxing at their edges, the ink browning, the felt drawer-linings wearing thin exactly as fast as they ought. Small amber lamps hang on chains over the reading desks, making pools of light with long darknesses between, and the sound of the place is the sum of small things: a pen, a drawer running home on its slides, the tick of the pneumatic tube delivering a canister from the surface, the granite-filtered hush of the whole city's rain reduced to something like distant leaves.

And there is nothing here. That is the second strange thing, and it takes the eye a while to finish taking it in, because the building is so plainly *full*. But open any drawer and you find only cards, and every card is a thing somewhere else. The Registry holds no objects at all. What it holds is closure. Every card records a seal: a crimped lead pellet on a twist of wire, stamped with a number, applied somewhere in the world to something that has been shut and is meant to stay shut — floodgate spindles, caisson hatches, mine doors, reliquary clasps, coffin bolts, the steel cages of glass vessels, the wheels of valves. Number, date of sealing, name of the sealing officer. And beneath these, on every card, in red: the date of return. Because that is what a seal is. A seal is not a lock. A seal is a promise to come back and look.

The third strange thing is how many of the red dates are past. Gallery by gallery the proportion grows, until in the deep rooms the Overdue is not a category but the substance of the collection: tens of thousands of promises, filed and cross-indexed and kept perfectly dry, that no one has kept. There is a bell by the wicket, a small bright one, rung whenever a completed inspection is filed — the Bell of Returns, the clerks call it, without irony — and it rings perhaps a dozen times a year, and the outbound tubes hiss all day. The dust that settles over the deep galleries is ordinary dust, gray and fine, the ash of fires permitted to burn, and the clerks sweep it and it returns, and nobody minds. It is the correct dust for an archive of unkept appointments.

The fourth strange thing is in the eleventh gallery, and you can find it without a catalogue, because in eleven floors of green-crusted brass there is exactly one drawer-pull that shines. It has been polished by a hand, one hand, daily, for thirty-one years. Inside the drawer, among neighbors yellowed to the color of weak tea, sits one card as crisp and white as the day it was typed. The dust refuses it. The foxing stops at the felt around it, a finger-width short, and turns aside. And if you touched it — you are not allowed to touch it — you would find that the card is warm. Warm as a held breath. Warm as skin.

**The Keeper of the Eleventh Gallery**

No one is posted this deep. Someone stays.

She came to the Registry as a girl, apprenticed to the seal-wrights — the traveling half of the trade, the ones who go out to the world's closures with pliers and lead and a numbered stamp on their belt, wire the thing shut, crimp the seal, and carry the number home to the drawers. It is a walking life and then a climbing life, and thirty-one years ago it was she who was sent up the eleven hundred rungs, because the fill-valve of a certain chamber had to be wired from inside, and the chamber had been built full, and of the whole party she was the youngest, the quickest, the one who could hold her breath the longest and — this was said carefully, and she did not understand it then — the one *furthest from her own ending*. Nothing that flinches goes in that water, the surveyor told her, and you flinch less than most, and you will be quick.

She was quick. She remembers it in a detail that has not faded and will not: water the temperature of her own arms, so that she could not feel where she ended; lamplight the color of a factory at night, softened everywhere; the leaves — the room already green, the troughs already planted — lying in their slow sine waves as she swam her thirty crooked strokes to the bronze wheel. She threaded the wire. She crimped the lead. She stamped it with the number, her stamp, her hand, and something in the water held very still around her while she did it, the way a large animal holds still for a farrier, though she would not have put it that way for years. Then she went out through the hatch and down, and she was a hundred rungs into the cold before a vast, slow regard finished arriving at the place where she had been. The plants leaned toward the hatch for days. A month later a single bubble climbed to the pressed flat ceiling and touched it with a sound like a fingertip on paper and said, in the only writing available, the only name it has: her number. She knows none of this. She has guessed nearly all of it.

—

She found the card warm the following winter, warming her hands over the drawer without thinking, the way you warm them over a lamp, and then thinking. The drawer holds four things. The card. The duplicate crimp — a second lead pellet from the same pliers, struck the same day, standard practice. A small stoppered vial of powder the color of brass filings, decanted from the first glass trap at the sealing and given to her *for the file*: a gram or so of postponement itself, and it is the vial, the Registry's auditors have agreed, that accounts for the card's crispness, for the dust's refusal, for the foxing that turns courteously aside — proximity to that much *not yet* would keep anything young. The auditors are satisfied. She has never asked them to account for the warmth, because the powder is not warm, the lead is not warm, the drawer is not warm, and the card is the temperature of skin, and has been for thirty-one years, and she knows whose skin, and she knows what is being kept, eleven hundred rungs up and a mile of weather away, at exactly that temperature, like a photograph.

Every day she goes down to the eleventh gallery and opens the drawer and lays two fingers on the card. The sound her fingertips make is the sound a bubble makes meeting a ceiling. She never lifts the card out. She could — no regulation forbids the keeper of a gallery handling its holdings — but she stops a finger-width short of possessing it, daily, deliberately, and if you have seen the newest shoots in a certain garden stop a finger-width short of certain planes of clearer water and turn aside, you have seen the other half of this conversation. *I know you are there. I will not press.* It is the only conversation either party is equipped for. It has been going on for three decades, one touch a day, one bubble a season, and neither of them would call it thin.

—

She has never climbed back. Understand why, because it is not the rungs, though she is sixty-six now and it is also the rungs. It is that she has learned, slowly, over years of reading the surveyors' notes and the builders' silences, what she was too quick to be harmed by at twenty-five. The garden was stocked with green and only green because a plant holds its dying loosely and an animal carries its ending wound tight at the center of itself — and the thing in the water cannot not drink. She was far from her ending then; it could hold still around her, taste nothing, let her pass. She is near it now. She carries it close to the surface, the way the old do. If she climbed the eleven hundred rungs and went through the hatch into that skin-warm water — *her*-warm water — the occupant that remembers her hands would drink her death out of her in an hour of its vast slow noticing, and she would stop, mid-stroke, complete and finished and still alive, a clock with the spring removed, warm forever in the kindest room ever made. She thinks about it more than she reports. To be finished, and held, and never to fox or brown or rust. She refuses it daily, with two fingers on a card, and her refusal is mercy running downward the way the empty troughs were mercy running up: company declining to be the one thing that could be ruined by company.

—

The red date on the card is nine years off. It is the only appointment in all eleven galleries that is not overdue and will not be — she has seen to that. Under the vial, under the duplicate crimp, in the drawer, there is a letter in her hand, addressed to whoever holds her post when the demijohn passes its final graduation, and the letter says: *send someone young. Someone quick, far from their ending, who flinches less than most. They are to check the seal, exchange the vessel, and touch nothing, and love nothing, and come down the same day. But before they close the hatch they are to press one palm flat against the plating and hold it there while they count to sixty. That is all the message there is room for. It will be understood.*

—

A fortnight ago the card was warmer. Not much — a held breath and a half, say — and it stayed so for a day and cooled again to skin, and no inspection was filed, and the Bell of Returns did not ring, and the inbound tube brought nothing but the usual. Someone climbed. Someone unnumbered went up the rungs and hung in the amber light for an hour and counted the strange things and came down cold, and a vast attention turned after them too slowly to be felt, and flared, and along the one thread it possesses — a number, a name, a wire leading down through thirty-one years to a drawer in the deepest gallery — the news arrived at her fingertips a moment before she knew she was reaching for it.

She keeps her own ledger. It is not a Registry document; it lives in her coat. One word a night, most nights the same word, thirty-one years of it, and it is not a complaint and not a question. It is closer to what the gauges are doing, up there in the light that has no hours. *Warm. Kept. Still there. Still there.*

That night she wrote the first new word in years — *Visited* — and sat a while in the pool of the lamp with the darkness of the drawers all around, while the plant in the foundations heaved, and paused, and heaved, and the whole city's rain came down through the granite as a hush like distant leaves. Then, after the customary pause, in the customary hand, she wrote the other word, the one that is her entire half of an account being kept in both directions at once, in bubbles and in ink, across eleven hundred rungs and a mile of weather, at the two patient tempos of iron and of paper:

*Still there.*

And she polished the pull, and shut the drawer, and climbed the eleven stairs of galleries up toward the rain, leaving it warm in the dry and the dark — sealed, filed, checked daily, and never once, in the official record, checked at all.
