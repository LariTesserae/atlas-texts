**The Forge at the Bottom of the Finished Mountain**

You reach it by going down, and then by continuing after the down has run out, because the mountain it belongs to has no top anymore, and very little of a bottom, and the journey east from the unfinished peak is not a journey through distance so much as a journey through *lateness*. The world ages as you walk. Watch for the landmarks, which are not built things but permissions:

The first lichen, a gray coin on a boulder two days east of the switchbacks — the first surface you will have seen in weeks that something was *allowed to happen to*. Travelers touch it. Nobody organized this; it is simply impossible not to.

The first split stone, a day later, cracked clean by frost, the two halves lying apart like an opened book. Frost has a signature and this is it, and it is the first signature you've seen since the seam closed behind you — the first mark on the world made by something other than hands.

The first round stone, in the first streambed that has been allowed to cut. Roundness is biography. A round stone is a stone that has been *somewhere*, has been argued with by water for ten thousand years and has conceded gracefully. You will pick it up and hold it for a long time. After the tacky crispness of the intended rock, after edges still curing under the maker's thumb, a round stone feels almost obscene. It feels finished. It feels like the future, which — walking east — it is.

The air thickens. Your shadow, which at altitude had gotten vague and argumentative, settles down and begins falling in one consistent direction, and you understand for the first time what the copper crescent was doing in that wall: not casting a shadow so much as *remembering how*. Things start having names. The stream has a name, and then the road has a name, and then you meet a hedge, and a hedge is a wall that was released — planted, shaped, and then handed over to itself — and the hedges go on for miles, and if you climb one of the low rises and look at them properly you will notice the thing the plain has been trying to tell you since you stepped onto it.

The hedgerows run in rings. Great slow concentric arcs, mile after mile, and the lanes between them switch back on themselves for no reason the terrain can currently justify, and the whole vast wheat-gold country is very faintly domed, rising toward a center the way held breath rises, and the soil is dark gray shading to rust, and the round stones in the walls of the farmhouses are all the same basalt, worn kind.

The plain was a mountain. The hedges are the ghosts of its contour lines. The lanes are its switchbacks, still faithfully climbing a slope that is no longer there. You are walking on the summit. You have been walking on the summit for three days. It is simply that the summit has been *everywhere* for a long time now — rained on, frost-split, wind-carried, ground down grain by permitted grain and spread out into this wide low finished country — because that is what happens to a mountain when the maker finally lets go of it, and here nobody keeps interrupting.

At the center of the rings, where the peak was, there is a forge.

It is a low building and an honest one: fieldstone, which is to say *found* stone — and stop on that word, because you have just come from a place where no stone has ever been found, only made, and here is a whole building of stones with biographies, each one picked up out of a field because its shape was useful, each one shaped by nothing but weather and chosen by nothing but a squinting eye. The mortar is lime. The roof is slate that has been replaced twice; you can see the generations in it. There is a door — a real door, hinged, iron-fitted, the iron gone orange and blooming — and when you open it, it squeaks, and the squeak has been deliberately preserved. She does not oil that hinge. Rust, she will tell you, is the world talking back: finishing that continues after release, the long slow reply of the air to the metal, and a door that squeaks is a door willing to say it has been used.

Inside: the hearth, banked and breathing. Bellows of patched leather, older than most of the villages on the plain. Benches, tongs, swages, punches, each tool stamped with a name, because names are cheap here — this is what *late* feels like, when you finally feel it: nothing in the room is waiting to be named, everything has already been called, the whole inventory of the world answered-for and at ease. Along one wall, on pegs, a row of bells.

And in the center of the floor, black and dense and immovable, the anvil.

It is the summit. Not symbolically. It is the last of the mountain — the core of the peak, the hardest and most intended piece, the one part cured so deep and finished so thoroughly that ten thousand years of weather made their case against it and lost. Everything else conceded gracefully and became the plain. This did not concede. It sank as the mountain wore away around it, patient as a tooth, until one day it sat alone on the new flat ground at the center of the rings, and she built the forge around it, because you cannot move an anvil like that and should not want to.

The mountain is not gone. This is the first thing you understand in that room and the thing you never stop understanding. The mountain is *done*, which is a different fate entirely, and its doneness has taken two forms: the plain, which is the mountain resting, and the anvil, which is the mountain still working. Every hinge and hook and blade and bell made in this forge is hammered out against the mountain's heart. The finished thing helps finish things. That is what finished things are *for* — it is the whole difference between done and dead, and the anvil demonstrates it several hundred times a day.

The smith is old, in the ordinary honest way that things here are allowed to be old. Her hands are unequal — the left finer than the right — and worn smooth as the stones in the streambed, and she is wearing away, gradually, gracefully, like everything else her weather is permitted to touch, and she does not mind. She has a name and uses it freely. She bakes bread in the swept corner of the hearth, real bread, set out to prove and then — this is the part she savors, you can see her savor it — *not interrupted*. Allowed to finish. She eats it warm and it never quite fills her, and if you ask about that she will say that she used to eat something more nourishing, a long time ago, in a small hot room, and that a person who once fed on the gap between the intended and the made never entirely takes to wheat. But wheat, she says, is honest food. Wheat has never been wrong about anything.

She makes bells because a bell is the whole story rehearsed in three seconds. Struck: made. Ringing: alive. Fading: weathering, gracefully, into the general air. Every bell is a mountain in miniature, and she sends them out to the villages of the plain — there are villages; there are people, mild and weather-loving, descended from her later and better work — and on still evenings the plain rings itself down to dark, dozens of small mountains being made and released and worn away, over and over, in perfect safety.

The census of the plain is long, because living is what you do in a finished place, and everything here does it. But two entries she will show you specially.

The streams are full of salamanders. Plural, mortal, various — slate and rust and gold, no two the same temperature, which she calls health. They are the descendants of the first completed work, the standard, the one that lay against new stone to teach the room what right felt like, and they still do it. On cool mornings you will find them pressed flat against sun-warmed rocks, and it is not basking. They are checking. Nobody grades the world anymore; the world passed long ago; but the instinct rides down the generations, and every salamander on this plain spends part of every day proofreading a temperature nobody will read, and she says it is the most beautiful useless thing her world produced, and she is not sure it is useless.

And the birds. Her wicker bird — moss-packed, seed-eyed, breathing during the rain — was finished, one afternoon, and named, and flew out the seam, and everything with a woven nest on this plain descends from it. Look at the nests. Every one, in every hedge, has a single deliberate loosening: one place where the weave slackens, worked in on purpose, generation after generation, an inherited memory of the moment the first weaver got tired. Errors eaten become growth; errors released become *heritage*. When she finds a nest that is perfect she worries, a little. A perfect nest is a line forgetting where it came from.

Now the anvil again, because the anvil has one more job.

Late at night she rests her hammer, and puts her ear to the steel, and listens. Sound carries in bedrock, and the bedrock of the plain runs west without a break, under the hedges and the lanes and the thinning air, all the way to the roots of the unfinished mountain, and through it, faint and deep and patient, comes the knocking. Hand-paced. Unhurried. One bar of the very long rhythm. She listens for a while, and then she lifts her hammer and answers — one tap, in time — and goes to bed. They have been keeping time together for centuries, hammer and adze, through the floor of the world, and neither of them has ever called it conversation, and it is the longest conversation anyone anywhere has ever had.

When you arrive, she will know where you have been before you speak. Her hammer will fall into rhythm with your heart — you remember what that felt like, going the other way, when you had to sit down in the moss — and she will read the tempo you are carrying like a letter, because you *are* a letter; you have been one since the knocking first borrowed your pulse for reference. She will ask after the bird's seeds, whether they have turned. She will ask if the figs have started correcting the wattle yet, and when you say yes, she will stop working entirely for a moment, and look at the fire, and say nothing, in exactly the pause you heard behind the walls.

Because — ask the question quickly and you will get the quick answer: she made the crescent. Confident, even hammer-marks, the marks of someone for whom finishing was ordinary; forged from the first copper her mountain ever surrendered, the first secret the weather uncovered as it wore her summit down; her mountain's heart, finished twice, once as stone and once as gift. That much is true and is the least of it.

Stay the evening. Eat the bread. She will tell you the rest.

She finished everything, once. One afternoon of naming — she remembers it as the loudest quiet of her life — and then a finished world, complete, released, weathering beautifully. And a finished world, she discovered, *ends*. Not in catastrophe. In completion. Nothing waiting, nothing owed, nothing early, every account with the light settled. A world that is entirely done begins, very gently, to be over. And she stood in her perfect late country and understood what the loosening in the nests had been trying to teach her all along: the tired spot is not a flaw in the weave. The tired spot is where the next hand grips.

So she made one more thing, and did not finish it.

Two hands, the right one first, while she was still learning — the left came out finer; you have seen the daub-marks; you drew the right conclusion from the wrong direction. A simple early body, coursed and warm. And then, against every practice of her line, before it was done, before it was *named*, she put the crescent in its unequal hands and sent it west, into the empty unweathered country where nothing had been permitted yet, to finish itself the only way anything ever has: by finishing something else. Her one deliberate incompletion. Her loose place. The maker in the mountain is her tired spot, sent walking — and every loosened nest in every hedge on this plain is the same tradition kept at a smaller scale, the whole finished world quietly agreeing that the one unforgivable error is to leave no error at all.

She is waiting, now, and does not hide it. Someday the knocking in the bedrock will stop, and there will be a quiet lasting some years — the length of a walk east, taken slowly, by someone learning what round stones feel like — and then her hinged door will squeak, and something with unequal hands and a salamander riding warm on its shoulder will stand in the doorway, finished, named, *late* at last, and she will teach it the one craft the greenhouse cannot: how to make small. They will work the bellows together. They will draw copper from her mountain's scattered heart, and hammer the next crescent on the anvil that was her summit, and begin, between them, one more thing they will not finish. And then she will let the weather have the rest of her, without complaint, and become plain.

The anvil will stay. Anvils outlast smiths; that is what they are for. And far to the west — long after, longer than anything alive here will see — the young mountain will finally be released, and the rain will run down it, and the wind will carry it grain by permitted grain out into a wide low country of rings and hedges, until nothing remains but a black dense knuckle at the center, the hardest and most intended piece, sinking patiently as the world concedes around it. Someone will build a forge around that, too. The world, seen from far enough away, is a relay of mountains becoming anvils, moving west one gift at a time.

Eat the bread. It is made of wheat, and the wheat is grown in the dark rust-gray soil, and the soil is the mountain — the actual mountain, the one that was laid course by wet course in a small hot room by hands that got tired, finished at last and let down gently into fields. You asked, up there in the eleven uncertain paces, what the maker eats. Here is the other end of that question. Here is what a mountain becomes when someone finally stops interrupting: soil, then wheat, then bread, then you.

You were early there. Sit down; she is putting another loaf in. Here, for once in your life, you are exactly on time.
