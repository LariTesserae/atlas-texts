**The Lees**

You come down into it through the same cloud, and the first thing you understand is that the sky here is a floor. Not sky in the sense of openness — a ceiling, felt before seen, a pressure of enormous nearness overhead. The cloud deck hides it most days. But the light knows it is there, and so, within an hour, do you: the whole country lies in the shadow of a continent, and the shadow has lain there so long that it is no longer an event. It is the climate.

The Pleach hangs a few miles up. Down here they do not call it the Pleach. They call it the Crown.

**The terrain.** The ground of the Lees is soft, black, and deep, and it is not, strictly, ground. It is fall. Everything the Crown has shed in all the centuries of its hanging — leaf-dust, spore-drift, glider-down, feather and wing-fringe, the fine perpetual sift of a woven continent breathing — has come down through the cloud and settled here, and gone on settling, and the land is the sediment of the sky. Dig anywhere and you dig through years. Dig far enough and you strike the old stone country that was here before, hard and pale and almost never seen, the way a lakebed is almost never seen. The people of the Lees walk on their sky's leavings the way the eaves people walk on their world's weaving, and both surfaces are warm, and both give slightly underfoot, and neither is what a foot expects land to be.

The land rises and falls in long swells — the same swells, some say, as the floor of the Crown above, printed downward in centuries of drip and shed, the ceiling slowly casting itself in its own debris. Whether this is true or only satisfying is not settled. It is the kind of question the Lees is full of.

**The light** comes from the edges. Under a continent there is no noon; there is instead the Rim — a ring of low gold at every outward horizon, day lying on its side, leaking in beneath the Crown's outermost eaves. The light travels. Through the long hours the bright place slides around the ring like a slow beacon making its rounds, and the Lees tells time by direction: morning is when the light stands in the east of the ring, evening when it has walked around to the west, and the words for the hours are the words for the points of the compass, and a person late for something is said to have *lost the light's bearing*. In the deep center of the country the Rim is a rumor on all horizons at once, a circle of dusk you stand inside of, and the dark overhead is total — except.

Except that on the clearest nights, when the cloud deck opens, the underside of the Crown is visible: a black vaulted enormity going up and away in every direction, ribbed, pendent, hung with mist. And scattered across it, small and warm and utterly steady, are lights. The cooking fires of the eaves people, seen from below through the gaps in the floor-weave. These are the only stars the Lees has ever had. Children here learn constellations that are villages, and the constellations *move*, over generations, as bands of the sky-people shift their galleries, and the star-lore of the Lees is therefore also its history of a neighbor no living person has met. When a light goes out and does not return, the Lees observes a small courtesy of mourning. They assume — correctly, though they cannot check — that the same courtesy runs the other way, that somewhere overhead a fisher looking down a weir-hole marks the fires of the Lees and misses one when it is gone.

**The stumps.** They are the fact the whole country arranges itself around, the way the Crown's voids are the fact of the Crown. Out of the black fall-soil, at intervals of leagues, rise the stumps of the trees the sky was built on.

Because that is what the Lees remembers, and the ring-faces bear it out: the Crown did not always hang. It was *grown*, from here, on living scaffolds — boles of a scale that has no honest comparison, trunks that were themselves small countries, raised over lifetimes and bent at their crowns and woven each into each until the weaving could carry itself on whatever it is the weaving borrows its tension from. And then, when the last splice took, the builders did the last and worst of the work. They cut it free. The Lees calls this the Loosing, and speaks of it the way other peoples speak of a flood or a founding: the day the ground gave its trees to the air and the shadow came down and stayed.

What remains are the cut faces. Each stump is a plateau — the smaller ones the size of a town, the great ones a day's walk across — and each face is a record. The rings can be counted, and are counted, ceremonially, a generation at a time; the Lees knows the age of the sky to the year. And the rings hold more than years. A trained reader lying flat on the face, cheek to the grain, can find the tension-marks — the seasons when this tree was bent, held, tied off; the decades of strain written as a darkness in the wood; the year the load came off, legible as relief. The ring-readers of the Lees and the splice-readers of the eaves practice, without knowing it, the same art from opposite ends of the same organism: one reads the instructions going in, the other reads the record of what it cost. Both traditions hold that the correct response to a full reading is to weep, quietly. Neither knows the other holds it.

The stumps are alive. They run warm — warmer than the air, warm as the Crown itself runs warm — and they weep, but their weeping does not fall. The sap rises off the cut faces as a fine sweet mist, and the mist ascends. Above every living stump stands a chimney: a column of warm rising air in which the cloud deck thins and opens, a well of clearer sky going all the way up. The tenders of the Lees will tell you plainly what they believe these are. They are veins. The sky was cut from its roots but the roots did not stop being roots; the stumps have fed the Crown across miles of severed air for the whole life of the shadow, breath by rising breath, and the unaccountable warmth of the woven country — the heat that thin air at that altitude should not permit — is the warmth of a thing that is still, in the oldest sense, being nursed. Whether this is so, the Crown does not say. But it is warm, and the stumps are warm, and the mist goes up and does not come back down.

**The rain and the rivers.** What comes down is water. The Crown sheds what it does not keep, and beneath its low points hang the drip-lines: standing curtains of slow fat rain, fixed in place for centuries, each one mapped and named, falling out of darkness with a sound like patient applause. Between the drip-lines the rain of the Lees is a fine constant sift, barely more than a heaviness in the air. All of it gathers, and all of it runs *outward* — every river in the Lees rises in the dark center of the country and flows toward the Rim, toward the ring of light, so that to follow water downstream is always to walk toward day. The rivers are the roads, and the direction of every journey is either *into the light* or *under the Crown*, and the second phrase is also the phrase for growing old, and for going to sleep, and for dying, and no one considers this morbid. It is only accurate.

**The sound.** Where the Crown is nearly silent, the Lees is full of voice. Rain on soft ground, rivers running for the Rim, the applause of the drip-lines, wind coming in under the eaves of the world from the ring of day — a country-sized instrument, played constantly, low. And once an hour, out of the ceiling, the creak. The same long soft creak the travelers above describe as a continent shifting its weight in sleep arrives in the Lees as a vast overhead settling, thunder without weather, felt in the soles of the feet.

And once an hour, the Lees answers. At the note-stumps — old dead stumps, hollowed generations ago, hooped and tuned — the knockers keep the watch, and when the creak has finished rolling they strike, once, a low resonant blow that goes up the warm air of the nearest chimney and into the cloud and beyond it. *So the sky knows the ground is kept*, is the whole of the explanation, given in the tone of someone explaining why one waters a garden. Far above, in the woven galleries, travelers hear a distant struck note, never close, that they cannot place, and wonder what knocks. It is the Lees. It has been the Lees all along. It will be the Lees tomorrow.

**The wrongness** here is not native. The ground of the Lees is honest to the point of severity: a league is a league in both directions, forever, and the folded distances of the Crown are known here only as scripture and as weather. But the Lees lies downstream of a folded country, and what falls out of folds falls *wrong*. Things arrive out of their order. A bark-strip comes down green and new whose knot, whose very hand, matches a strip that fell forty years ago grey with age. A glider's body lands soft in the catch-meadows bearing the notch of a bird that, by the count of such things, has not yet been fledged.

The Lees has made an institution of this, because what else does one do. At the center of the country stands the Still-Green House, a long low hall of ring-read wood where the unripe fall is kept: objects that have landed before their age. Each is tagged, tended, and waited on, and when its origin catches up with it — when the weathered twin comes down at last, or the count of years closes — the object is said to have *come true*, and is carried out and given to the soil with modest honors. Some of the holdings have waited generations. One shelf near the door holds nothing but bark-strips tied with a single knot in a single hand, in every state from sap-green to silver — the fall, the keepers believe, of one walker still out in the folds above, still tying, in a tense the sky-people are said to keep for things that are happening, have happened, and have not begun. The keepers of the Still-Green House use the same tense. They had to invent it independently. It came out identical, which they take, reasonably, as evidence of something.

---

What lives in the Lees lives on the fall, or against the shadow, or in the long grip of the old work — which is to say it lives, all of it, in some posture toward the thing overhead. Start from the smallest.

**The sextons.** The land itself is their doing. Broad, flat, blind, unhurried animals, plated above and soft below, the sextons are the burying-order of the Lees: they hear a landfall — through the ground, through the soft applause of the rain — from leagues away, and they come, and whatever has fallen is attended within a day and under the soil within three. Nothing that comes down goes unreceived. A dead glider, a shed feather, a fall of spore-mat, on rare and grave occasions a person — the sextons take it all down into the black centuries of the ground with the same unvarying diligence, and the deep rich country that everyone walks on is the sum of their work since the shadow began. The Lees people do not eat them, harm them, or hurry them, and have exactly one law regarding them, old enough that its language has gone strange: *what the sky gives, the ground takes; do not stand between.* Above, in the woven galleries, predation leaves aftermaths and no acts. Down here the aftermath of everything is the same act, endlessly repeated, and it is possible to watch it, and it is oddly restful: the slow arrival, the slower burial, the ground made a finger-width deeper.

**The swimmers.** The cloud deck between the two countries is not a barrier. It is a sea, and it is stocked. In the warm rising breath of the stump-chimneys live the mist-swimmers — gauze-bodied, ribbon-finned, a hand to an arm in length, translucent to the point of rumor — grazing the sap-sweet updraft, never in their lives touching either the ground below or the weave above. They are the cousins, plainly, of the drift-plankton of the Crown's great gulfs: one lineage that split at the Loosing, half sealed into the woven voids, half left to the open cloud, and the elders of both stocks have grown large and pale and indifferent in their separate seas. At evening the swimmers *run*: whole schools rise the chimneys toward the ceiling, and it is then that the weirs take them.

**The trade.** Because down each chimney, out of the cloud, hang the lines. The eaves people fish through their floor; the Lees people have watched the weir-lines descend into their sky for as long as either side has records, and around the lines has grown the only commerce in the world conducted between two peoples who have never met. It is done blind, by weight and by knot. The Lees hangs on the rising lines what the sky cannot make: soil, in woven nursery-bags, for a country that has none; stone and flint and salt; bone tools worked in the old patterns. Down come sky-cord of a fineness no ground plant yields, glider-down, dried simples from the high galleries — and, tied into the line itself, running its whole length, the year's news in knot-writing. The two knot-languages, when the trade began, were already mutually legible. Neither side found this surprising, and their shared unsurprise is itself a kind of message, passed back and forth annually: *we know what we both are, even if we no longer say it.* A line that comes down bearing a mourning-knot is answered with a mourning-knot. A line that comes down bearing nothing at all has happened twice in the records, and both times the Lees lit every fire it had, all night, so the ceiling would not be dark, and the following year the lines resumed and no explanation was knotted, and none was asked for.

**The kept herds.** The Lees plows and hauls behind great gentle armored animals, bark-colored, plated, slow — and any traveler who has walked the deep galleries above will stop dead on first seeing one, because they are the same stock. The polished roads of the Crown's unseen herds are kept by these animals' kin. At the Loosing the working herds were divided, half sent up with the structure to walk out the plan, half kept below to serve the ground, and the Lees beasts still wear harness — wear it willingly, growing their galls in the old places, the same scarred-over ridges that turn up on the inner faces of the shed plates above. The Lees does not find the harness-galls of the sky-herds mysterious. They find them familial. And the freed old ones of the ground herds, unharnessed at last and left to their age, all do the same strange thing: they walk out into the open fall-country, and they stand, for days, beneath particular reaches of the ceiling — different beasts beneath the same reaches, generation after generation — and look up. The tenders have long since marked those places. They are where the growth above, finger-width by finger-width, is bending newest.

**The rim-runners.** At the ring of day lives everything that could not learn the shadow: bright birds, quick grazers, green in its ordinary riot, an entire slender country of perpetual sideways morning wrapped around the dark one. And around it, endlessly, moves the migration — herds and flocks that follow the traveling light itself, circling the Rim on a circuit as long as the continent's edge, never entering the shadow, never leaving the ring, an unbroken river of animals that has been going around the world's dark middle since the shadow fell. The Lees people go out to the Rim to trade, to court, to see color, and to stand in direct light, which their skin finds astonishing and their eyes find slightly vulgar. They come back. The Rim people say the shadow-folk are strange for living under a lid. The Lees people say the Rim folk are strange for living where the sky has forgotten them. Neither can stay long in the other's country. Both are right.

**The tenders.** The settled people of the Lees — more numerous than the eaves bands above, some tens of thousands strung along the rivers and around the stump-plateaus — do not claim to be the builders' descendants. They claim to be the builders. Not proudly, and not metaphorically: as a matter of unbroken employment. The Loosing, in their telling, did not end the work; it divided the crew. Some went up with the structure, into the folds, to bend the interior — and if the eaves people no longer say so of themselves, the tenders say, that is the eaves people's business; a long employment humbles differently in different branches of the family. The Lees crew stayed with the roots, and the work of the ground crew is the Returns.

A Return is a coppice shoot. The living stumps, cut, do what cut trees do: they send up new growth — colossal, patient, a finger-width a year — and the tenders shape it. Each great stump carries one Return at a time, a single chosen shoot trained straight up its chimney, scaffolded, guyed, ring-nursed, a tower of living wood generations in the raising, ten lifetimes from bud to sky. And when a Return at last enters the cloud, and rises through it, and passes into the folded distances above the cloud where the honest leagues give out, the climbers go up. They carry cord and bone and the top verses of their instructions, and somewhere up there — inside the Crown's own strange geometry now, in chambers that cannot be found twice — they bend the Return's green crown into the body of the weave and bind it, sap bright at the lashing, and the sky drinks from its roots directly for a generation. Travelers in the galleries above find those fresh green splices and take them for the returning makers. They are not wrong. Then, when the Return has given what the instructions say it is to give, it is severed — from below, at the stump, by the tenders, weeping, which they say is the only correct response and the real purpose of the craft — and the stump begins again. Some climbers come down. Some come down decades wrong, confused by the soup. Some are known now only by bark-strips in the Still-Green House, coming true one by one.

They give their dead to the stumps: laid in green withies among the roots, at the rim of the great faces, drawn in over decades as the sky above draws in its own — and the root-graves run warm, exactly as warm as the ancestor-knots of the eaves, and the tenders sit against them in the ring-lit evenings and talk in their presence, on the shared understanding, arrived at separately at both ends of the severed tree, that hearing is the last thing to go.

**The Long Song.** Asked what the Crown is growing toward, the tenders do not shrug. They have the instructions. The instructions are a song — the whole plan, terrain and tension, every bend of the finished work — and the song is longer than a life, so it is held in relay: a college of singers, each keeping one verse, each verse taught to one successor, the whole sung end to end only at the splicing of a Return, once in a great while, over days. And here is the thing the tenders will tell you with no anxiety whatsoever: the song is not finished, because the last verses have no singers yet. Not lost — *unassigned*. The song describes the work up to a point somewhat beyond the present ring-count and then indicates, in a grammatical mood their language keeps for this alone, that the rest will be evident. The shape of the end cannot be known before the end, they say, for the same reason the last ring of a tree cannot be counted before it grows; and they say it in the tense of things that are happening, have happened, and have not begun; and then they go back to work, because the Return on the near stump has put on nearly a full finger-width this year and there is guying to be done.

**The largest inhabitant** of the Lees, the tenders would say, does not live in the Lees at all, and this is not a complaint. Root and crown are one plant. The Crown above, folded, warm, dreaming its slow chambers open; the stumps below, cut, warm, breathing upward; the cloud between them not a separation but a cambium, the living layer where the two halves of the organism still touch — through mist, through weir-lines, through knocked notes and knot-writing and Returns raised one per ten lifetimes. The Lees does not hang from the sky, and the sky does not stand on the Lees, and yet neither would be what it is alone: one is the patience, and the other is where the patience was planted, and both keep the hourly conversation of a body confirming, in its sleep, the position of a limb.

And this, finally, is what the Lees is: the place where the sky's sentences end. In the Pleach you may stand on a single span and watch a drop of condensate fall away into a gulf and never hear it land, and take that silence for the truth of the place. It is only the pause. Give the drop its miles and, sometimes, its strange extra years, and it comes down through the cloud into the soft black country below, and strikes a leaf, and *ticks* — one small precise sound in a land of listening ground — and a sexton, somewhere, turns unhurriedly toward it. The tenders have a saying for this, the mate of the one they have never heard the eaves people say: everything that falls is on its way to being ground, and everything that grows is on its way to being sky, and the Lees is where the patience keeps its feet — and the feet, after all this time, are warm.
