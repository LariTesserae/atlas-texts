**Lastwarm**

The condensate leaves the Works at a constant rate. That is the last thing the thirty-eight know about it. The warm water runs off the flow into the hot mud, and the mud drains — slowly, by threads and seeps, gathering itself — into a channel, and the channel finds the old river called the Ald, and the Ald goes down through forty miles of marsh and willow carr toward the great grey lake at the bottom of the lowlands. The Works does not look outward, and so the Works has never followed its own breath down the water. Other people have. At the far end of the breath, on the last spit of firm ground before the river loses itself in reeds, there is a village, and the village is called Lastwarm, because that is where the warmth runs out.

The land here is old. That is the first strange-but-true fact of the place, and it is the exact opposite of the fact upstream: at the Works the rock is younger than the machinery, and at Lastwarm the ground is older than everything, older than memory, older than the river's own name. The spit is silt and peat going down black and soft for ten thousand years, and when the villagers cut fuel out of it they turn up bog oaks the color of the basalt at the Works — black, dense, gleaming — except these were trees before anyone anywhere had thought of a valve, and lay drowned all that time, waiting to be firewood. Children find bones in the peat, and antlers of a kind of deer that no longer exists, and once a wooden paddle carved by somebody dead nine thousand years. The village keeps the paddle on a shelf in the meeting hall. It is the oldest made thing anyone in the lowlands has ever touched, and it hangs a two-day journey from the youngest land in the world, and the same water connects them.

The village itself is the youngest thing on this oldest ground, and it was founded not on a date but on a temperature. The elders tell it plainly: the winter after the flow came up out of the lowland — the winter the horizon grew its plume — the Ald froze as it always froze, white bank to bank, except for one black lane of open water down the middle that would not take ice. The fishers of the scattered marsh crofts came to look at it, as people will come to look at a wrongness. Then a man knelt and put his hand in the open lane and said, *it's kind.* Two degrees kinder than the world, maybe three. That was the founding. They followed the kindness upstream until the mud got too hot to pole through and the haze swallowed everything and something enormous was hissing inside it, and they turned around, and they built where the warmth was last measurable, and named the place for the fact of it. Lastwarm was founded by a thaw that never ended. The village has a birthday and the birthday is a reading on a thermometer.

You can see the warmth, if you know how to look. It comes down the channel wearing color: the same orange and rust algae that paints the drains at the Works runs all the way down the warm thread, mile after mile, narrowing as the heat pays itself out into the marsh, brightest far upstream, dulling through rust to umber to a brown that is almost the brown of ordinary mud — and somewhere near the village weirs, on one particular stone or another, the color simply stops. The villagers call it the color-line, and they read their years by it. The line creeps up-channel in mild years and down in hard ones, and its position is discussed the way other places discuss harvests. *The year the color came past the third weir* is a date at Lastwarm, fixed and citable. The last colored stone is a known object. Children make pilgrimages to it. Nobody has ever explained to the Works that its plumbing keeps a calendar forty miles away, in orange, on the bed of a river it has never seen.

And you can see the source, on clear days. Low on the northern horizon there stands a single pale feather of steam, permanent, unhurried, rising off the edge of the world at the same angle it rose yesterday. The villagers call it the Feather, and they have made it their weather-glass, because it is the one thing in their sky that has no weather of its own: when the Feather stands straight, the coming day is calm; when it leans east, wind off the lake by evening; when it flattens and smears, storm, get the boats up. Fishers glance at it the way the works manager glances at his gauges, which is fitting, because it is the same instrument read from the other end. *The Feather stands* is a greeting at Lastwarm, and a blessing, and a way of saying that all is fundamentally well with the world — and the thirty-eight people whose engine holds that plume at its angle do not know that anyone lives by the sight of it, any more than a heart knows it is being listened to.

Everything else about the place is weather, and season, and change — which is to say, everything about Lastwarm is what the Works has none of. The light is hard and directional and it swings through the year like a slow lamp: long gold evenings in summer, iron-grey noons in autumn, and in winter a low blinding sun that lays shadows across the ice a hundred feet long. Shadows are ordinary here. There is frost that ferns the windows, there is rain in fat warm ropes, there is a wind off the lake that cracks the skin of your hands — the villagers' hands are seamed and split and horn-hard, the perfect opposite of the soft crackless steam-cured hands upstream, and when the two kinds of hands meet, at the boat landing, each finds the other faintly unbelievable.

The second strange-but-true fact of Lastwarm is that its seasons run backward. In the season when the whole lowland goes silent — when the marsh freezes and the frogs stop and the world holds still under snow — Lastwarm is at its loudest and most crowded, because the warm thread will not freeze, and the open lane down the middle of the white Ald becomes the only open water for a hundred miles. Every bird that failed to leave comes to it. Swans in the hundreds, ducks in the thousands, rafted so thick on the black lane that the water looks feathered; herons spaced along the ice edge like fence posts; and the noise of them, dawn and dusk, is a roar you can hear from a mile out on the marsh. Under the bird-roar the frozen river sings — the ice booms and whines and cracks in long traveling zips as the temperature moves — and up and down the open lane the winter freight goes, because the lane is also the road. All winter the boats run the warm thread: coal going up, iron going up, mail going up; smoked fish coming down, and news, and now and then a person who has decided about the Works, or been decided about. In January, Lastwarm is a port. In July it is a hamlet drowsing in the reeds, half its people gone to the hay meadows and the lake coast, the thread invisible in the general warmth, the quietest place imaginable. Winter is its summer. It breathes with the year — in and out — which is exactly the thing the Works, holding forever at the top of its breath, has never once done.

The village is built of what the water brings: driftwood, lake-salvage, alder cut from the carr, and black bog oak for the parts that must not rot. The houses face the river both ways — upstream toward the warmth, downstream toward the lake — and the finest of them stand on posts out over the open lane itself. Along the shore run the smokehouses, a row of steep-roofed sheds going day and night in autumn, and their smoke rises and leans and tatters — a ragged, mortal, weather-bitten answer to the Feather standing changeless behind it on the horizon.

And across the river stand the weirs: four great wicker-and-timber fences angled into the current, mended every year, older in their pattern than any living mender. They have one law, and it is the village's only absolute law: the center of the channel — the warm lane, the thread itself — is never netted, never staked, never touched. *The road belongs to the fish, and the fish belong to the warm.* Because the fish are the whole of it. Every spring the silver run comes up out of the lake, massing below the weirs in shoals you can hear, and passes up the open lane, and goes — the villagers know exactly where it goes, and the knowing is the tenderest secret of the lowlands. The silver run the warm thread forty miles up into the haze and spawn in the hot bright channels of the Condensate Works, in the discharge, under the pipe trestles, among the orange algae, watched by herons and geckos and one child. The workers upstream say the fish are *descended from marsh stock, nobody knows how.* Lastwarm knows how. Lastwarm has always known how. The first warm winter, the elders watched the silver find the open lane and turn up it like a key turning in a lock, and they have kept the road clear for them every year since — so that the mystery at the heart of the Works is simply Lastwarm's fidelity, unsigned. Each spring, before the weirs take a single fish, the first of the run is netted, carried past all four fences in a dripping basket, and let go into the open lane above the village, headed north. *Sent up to the Kettle,* they say — the Kettle being their name for the thing inside the haze, the thing under the Feather, which most of them have never seen and about which the children believe magnificent nonsense, some of it true.

**Who lives at Lastwarm**

Lastwarm has never held a number. The count breathes like everything else here — ninety-some souls in high summer, twice that when the winter port is running, swelling and slacking with the runs and the freezes — and the villagers regard the fixed thirty-eight upstream with the same polite wonder the thirty-eight would feel about a town that couldn't say how many people it contained. *Steady* is not the compliment here. The compliment here is *timely.* A timely mend, a timely boat, a timely person: one who reads the year right, who is where the season needs them at the hour it needs them. At the Works, virtue is holding still. At Lastwarm, virtue is arriving. Between them they divide, fairly evenly, the two halves of being good at living.

The **weir-mistress** keeps the law of the open lane, and keeps the calendar, which at Lastwarm are nearly the same office. She reads the color-line, walks the ice edge in winter judging its temper by sound, and declares the seasons — the day the run is *on*, the day the smoking starts, the day the lane is safe for freight. Her authority is total and almost never exercised; the year mostly declares itself, and her genius is in confirming it at the right moment. She has been up the thread to the Works once, forty years ago, as a girl in her father's boat, and what she remembers is not the machinery but the stillness of the steam — *smoke that never tattered* — and she has never felt the need to go again. She says you should not stare too long at the thing your life hangs from.

The **thread-pilot** belongs to both places, the only living person who can say so, and it has made him slightly foreign in each. All winter he runs the warm lane — up with coal and bar iron and mail, down with smoked silver and the year's small news — and he can hold the thread in a whiteout fog by trailing his bare hand in the water and steering along the warmth, reading the river the way the borehole walker reads his domes: by palm. He carries the languages back and forth along with the freight. He has taught a handful of the valve-yard hand-signs to the winter fishers, who use them across the roar of the birds, and there are now people at Lastwarm who can hold the sign for *return line* a half-second too long and mean it. He carries the letters of the child at the Works, who draws geckos, and the letters of the Lastwarm children, who once, after long committee, sent her a lump of bog oak nine thousand years old with a note reading *this is what a shadow is like, only it lies down.*

The **come-downs** are four: former Works people, part of the small steady leak of those who cross the planks for good, and they live in the houses built out over the open lane, hulls of old boats for floors, the warm water passing a foot beneath their beds. They came down because they wanted sky and seasons and their own shadows, and they stopped at Lastwarm and went no further, because at Lastwarm — with an ear to the floor, on the stillest winter nights — they say the beat is there. *Whum-whum-whum-whum,* faint past believing, coming down forty miles of water from the engine none of them ever saw stopped. The doctor, who lives three doors along, has put instruments to the floorboards and found nothing, and has watched the come-downs sleep like drowned men above that same floor, and has written in her private notes that she declines to rule on whether the beat travels down the water or only down the memory, "there being no clinical difference." She is the same doctor who once examined the whole crew upstream and recorded their pulses falling within a beat of the engine. She goes up on the first winter boat every year, and it is she who could tell the works manager — but never has, considering it a matter of doctor's discretion — that his senior heron, the one with the bent toe who went missing from the log for four mornings six years ago, spent those four mornings on the ice edge below the Lastwarm weirs, and is known here too, and has a name here too, and it is a different name.

The **clerk** keeps the ledger that is the log's dark twin. The pay of the Works accrues down here, and eleven of the thirty-eight have accounts in the clerk's book, and some of the accounts have not been touched in twenty years — quiet fortunes, growing at a constant rate, belonging to people who will never come and collect them, held in trust by a village they have never seen, at the end of a river of their own warm exhaust. The clerk finds this arrangement entirely reasonable. Every place, he says, keeps something for another place. Lastwarm keeps their money; they keep our winter open. He rules the lines, and carries the balances forward, and the entries hold as steady as anything upstream.

And there are the **children**, who own the shadows. Shadow-games are the local art — winter noon, when the low sun lays a child's shadow a hundred feet up the white river, is the best playing of the year — and it was the children who conceived, and argued for, and finally achieved the thing the whole lowland now tells: the visit. Last winter, on the pilot's boat, with the request entered formally in the log upstream, the girl from the Works came down the thread. Seven years old; had never seen the sun arrive from a direction; had never seen her shadow. They took her out onto the frozen Ald at noon with the whole village standing on the bank and the birds roaring on the open lane, and she stood on the ice, and the sun was low and gold behind her, and her shadow ran out from her boots across the white river further than she could have thrown a stone, and she looked at it for a long time, and then — this is the part Lastwarm tells — she moved her hand, slowly, and watched the far dark hand move, and said, in the flat exact voice of her people, *steady.* She stayed three days, slept badly in the silence, and went home up the warm road to the heartbeat. The log entry for that week reads: *One away. One returned. Steady.* The Lastwarm children consider the visit the great event of the age, and are already planning the next one, and have appointed a committee.

At dusk the two places face each other without knowing it. Upstream, old Sefa sits by the Grandmother tree at the marsh edge, the one figure at the Works who looks outward, into the haze, southward, down the water. Downstream, on the longest jetty, the eldest of the come-downs takes his evening pipe facing north, up the water, watching the Feather go from white to grey to a pale standing smudge against the first stars. Forty miles of warm thread run between the two of them, carrying fish up and fortunes down, algae and letters and herons and the disputed ghost of a heartbeat, and neither man knows the other exists, and both are keeping the same watch.

The Works holds its breath, forever, at the top. Lastwarm is where the breath comes out into the world — and the world takes it, and warms itself, and lives by it: an open lane in the ice, a roar of wintering birds, a run of silver going home to a place that never wondered where it came from. The ground gives heat at a constant rate, the engine takes it at a constant rate, and forty miles down the water, at the exact point where the warmth becomes indistinguishable from the world, a village of no fixed number mends its weirs, reads its horizon, banks its neighbors' money, keeps the road open for the fish, and greets each morning with the only report it has ever needed:

The Feather stands.
