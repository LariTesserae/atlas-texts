**The Spring at the Bottom of the Weather**

There is no stream out of the cirque. This has been said, and it is true, and it is also the beginning of a lie of omission, because the water does not stay. Watch one drop: it forms in the dark hours on the flank of a cushion-dome, swells until dawn, catches the doubled light for one whole silver second, and falls into the gravel. And then it is gone — not evaporated, not drunk, but *admitted*. The mountain is so young that it is fractured through and through, all clean cracks and settling seams, a vertical labyrinth of passages that have never carried anything but darkness, and the bowl's dew goes down into it the way a letter goes into a slot.

It goes down for a very long time. The maze is deep and it branches, and the water feels its way, pausing in hairline chambers, threading fractures the width of a leaf-stem, descending a mountain that is still flinching and shifting around it, so that the path itself changes while the water is on it. No one has measured the journey and no one could, but the people at the bottom have done the only arithmetic available to them, and by their count the drop that falls into the gravel at dawn will come out into daylight again in about the time it takes a child to grow old. The water is a slow letter. The mountain is full of mail in transit — a vertical mile of dark rock holding, at any given moment, seventy or eighty years of dew, all of it falling, none of it arrived.

Where it arrives is the seam.

Below the cloud-sea the world is another world. The light down there is grey and sourceless and gentle, the underside of that same white floor the cirque stands above — from below it is a ceiling, low and luminous and always moving, and the country beneath it lives in a soft permanent brightness without sun, where shadows are as weak as they are on the summit, though for the opposite reason. And through this grey country runs the scar of the mountain's birth: the seam, where the new rock came up through the old. You can see the whole violence of it written in a single cliff — pale blue-grey stone, the same knife-fresh stone as the summit, riding up over rock of an entirely different age, dark and rust-red and rounded, the flesh of the plain that was here first. The mountain did not rise out of this country. It rose *through* it, and the seam is the wound, forty generations old and ferned to the lip.

At one place along the seam, the wound weeps.

It is a low cliff, perhaps forty paces of standing stone, and it runs with water from hundreds of mouths at once — cracks, lips, hairline seams, some pouring a steady rope of water, some releasing one drop at a time with the patience of the bowl's own basins. The whole face glistens. The whole face *sounds*. This is the first thing the spring does to you, and it does it before you see it: a wall of small voices, trickle and patter and gulp and drum, so dense that at three paces you cannot hear your own footsteps, so layered that no single drop can be picked out of it. It is the exact inverse of the bowl. Up there, one falling drop carries thirty paces through the stillness. Down here, thirty thousand drops fall together and cancel into a single continuous grey roar, soft-edged, endless, the sound of the mountain emptying itself of decades.

The first strangeness of the spring is that the noise has grain.

Stand at the wall long enough — an hour, a day; the people will tell you it takes a season — and the roar stops being a roar. Underneath the water-sounds there are other sounds, harder ones: knocks, ticks, dry splitting cracks, arriving through the rock itself. The cliff is a drumhead stretched over the fracture-maze, and every settling of the young mountain, every knuckle-crack of stone a mile overhead, comes down through the seam and sounds here, instantly, mixed into the water's voice. The wall is where the mountain can be heard from inside. And the two channels run at two speeds: the sound arrives *now* — that tick was struck somewhere in the dark this very moment — but the water arrives *late*, carrying the cold it was given a lifetime ago. Cup your hands under any mouth of the wall and drink, and you will taste it: metal and snow, the taste of air at the top of the weather, the cold of a dawn seventy years gone, delivered. Sometimes one mouth runs suddenly, viciously colder for an hour or a day, and the old listeners nod at it: some particular storm, some one great snow that fell on the bowl before any of them were born, arriving at last, still cold.

The second strangeness is the green, which is the mountain's other answer. The cirque grows on light and almost no water; the seam grows on water and almost no light. Everything at the spring is dark and lush and dripping — ferns in ranks up the wet stone, liverworts like spilled dark oil, mosses again in colors moss should not have, but here gone the other way: bottle-green, black-green, a green that is nearly blue in the grey light. Alders lean over the water with their roots in the noise. Nothing here grows in rings. Nothing here needs instruction; there is too much of everything. It is the same mountain keeping two gardens, one on each side of the weather — the starved bright one above, the drowned dim one below — and a botanist could spend a life on either and never guess the existence of the other.

The third strangeness is the coin.

Below the weeping wall the water gathers into a pool the size of a small room, and the pool is as loud and busy as everything else — roiled by the falling mouths, dimpled by drips, crossed constantly by rings of disturbance. Except in one place. Near the center of the pool, a little off toward the old red rock, there is a patch of water perhaps two cupped hands wide that lies perfectly flat. Ripples cross the whole pool and die at its edge. Rain stipples every inch of the surface and leaves it untouched. It is not sheltered — nothing shelters it; you can watch a wave go around it the way the wind goes around the lip of the bowl, not *cannot* but *will not*, the way you do not jostle someone sleeping. The still patch is exactly the size of the deepest dew basin at the top of the weather, and it is stillness of exactly that kind: not absence but occupancy. This is where the letters arrive. The bowl's quiet comes down drop by drop, a lifetime late, inside the water it silvered every dawn, and here at the bottom of all that noise it pools — a small flat coin of the summit's silence, held open on the surface of the din.

The people found the coin before they found anything else. That is their oldest story, and their whole vocation grew from it: someone kneeling to drink, generations ago, noticing one hand's-breadth of water that the roar could not touch, and understanding — slowly, the way everything is understood at the spring — that the mountain, which never stopped talking, must have somewhere a place where it kept its quiet. And so they went up through the cloud to look for it, and found the bowl, and cut the steps.

At the pool's edge there is a slab of the old red stone worn to a gloss in its center, stained deep with ochre, twin to the slab half-buried in cushion plants a vertical mile above. There is always someone sitting on it.

**Who Lives at the Spring**

Visitors first, here too, because the spring is the bottom of the same breath the bowl is the top of. The choughs roost in the seam-cliffs — this is where the ink-feathered birds that wheel the cirque spend their nights — and every grey morning they pour upward off the rock and vanish into the cloud-ceiling, going up to be received, one at a time, by the quiet above. Deer come out of the fern-country at dawn to drink at the pool's loud edge. Salamanders live in the wall's cooler mouths, black and gold, motionless for hours in the falling water. At night, moths in hundreds drink at the wet stone. And in the pool itself lives the dipper — a dark round bird that walks *under* the roiled water as though the noise were solid ground, and that sings, all day, every day, directly against the roar of the wall, a song no one can hear more than a body's length away. It sings anyway. There has always been exactly one dipper at the spring, and it is never the same dipper, and no one has ever seen one arrive, and the people long ago stopped remarking on this, because they live beside the pattern's other half and know an office when they see one.

The hares are theirs too — this is the answer to the question the bowl cannot ask. The meadows along the seam are full of brown hares, ordinary ones, drumming and boxing and fleeing as hares do. But every few years one of them turns white out of season, and stops its games, and one evening is seen crossing the lower scree, going *up*, at a steady unhurried pace no hare uses for anything. The people do not follow it and do not speak of it above a murmur. *The quiet has sent for its hare.* None has ever been seen coming back down, and none has ever needed to be.

The people themselves number fewer than a hundred, a hamlet of turf-roofed houses a few hundred paces down the seam, far enough from the wall to hear each other. They keep goats on the fern-slopes and gardens in the grey light, and within earshot of the wall they wrap the clappers of their goat-bells in felt — partly courtesy, so as to add nothing to what must be listened through, and partly, the old ones say, so as not to teach the mountain the wrong words. Because that is what they believe they are attending: an infant learning to speak. The wall is its mouth and its crib-babble, the ticks and cracks its first syllables, and the people keep a watch at the ochre-stained slab the way other villages keep a fire — always someone sitting, in turns, day and night, listening through the water for the grain.

They keep records. When a great crack sounds — one of the rare deep ones that stills the whole hamlet, that the salamanders feel and the dipper answers — the watcher on the slab takes a flat pebble from the pool and drills it through, and threads it onto a cord of twisted grass. The cords hang in generations from the rafters of the watch-house, hundreds of beads long, a library of everything loud the mountain has ever said. The single drilled pebble on the bleached cord wedged into the cairn at the top of the weather is one such record — the oldest they had, carried up whole and left there so that the ear at the summit could hold a transcript of the mouth at the foot. *Here is what you have said so far.*

And they climb. Once a century, more or less — they do not count years; they count *rings*, because the first climbers, standing in the bowl, read the arcs of the cushion-domes for what they were and understood they were looking at a record kept by someone else. There were eleven rings then. They cut eleven steps, one for each, guests announcing that they had read the guest-book. Every new ring earns a new step, cut with a hammerstone and patience by a climber chosen in childhood and prepared for nothing else — which is why the tool-marks in the notch are crisp; the eleventh step is not ancient at all, only kept perfect by the cold, cut by a grandmother whose granddaughter now takes her turns on the slab. The twelfth ring is nearly closed. The twelfth climber is alive, and knows it, and is learning to grind ochre.

Because that is the offering. The red earth of the seam — the old land's flesh, turned up at the mountain's birth — is carried to the summit, and there, beside the deepest basin, the climber grinds it on the smooth slab for a day and a night: a long, low, even sound, stone circling on stone, the most patient sound a human being can make, given to a being that lives on attention the way a song is given to a child. The cairn is re-seated so it still rings. The powder is carried back down and pressed onto doorposts and newborn foreheads: red from the bottom, ground at the top, returned. And the climber comes down changed — quieter, everyone agrees, for the rest of their life — and sits their watches at the pool, and the people say the still coin lies a little wider while that one sits there, and no one has ever measured it, and no one ever will.

But the people are not the oldest thing at the spring. They keep the watch; they did not invent it.

Under the pool, in the seam, on the old land's side of the wound, there is a presence, and it is everything the resident of the cirque is not. It has no body unless the noise is its body, no face unless the pool is its face — and it is *old*, old the way soil is old, older than the mountain by an unthinkable margin. It is the settled patience of the plain that was here first: the residue of a hundred million uneventful afternoons of flat country under weather, attention worn so wide and so thin that it covers everything and grips nothing — the exact opposite of the child's fierce one-thing-at-a-time stare a mile above. It has no rings, because it finished growing before the mountain's rock existed to record anything. When the mountain tore up through its country — the worst noise this land will ever make — the old one was not destroyed and was not surprised. It moved aside the way water moves aside, and settled again at the foot of the new thing, against the seam, where the wound wept. It has been there since, doing what the very old do with the very young.

It lets the child talk.

It lives in the roar as comfortably as the young one lives in the silence — noise is only weather, to something that old — and it receives everything at once, the ticks and the cracks and the seventy years of falling water, the deer and the dipper and the watcher on the slab, all of it held loosely, none of it missed. And it keeps the coin. That flat hand's-breadth of water is not the old one's stillness; it has none of its own to spare. It is the *child's* stillness, arriving in the mail, a lifetime late, drop by drop — and the old one holds the surface of the pool open there, unbroken in the middle of the din, the way you leave a sleeping child's hand lying where it fell on your arm. The two presences have never met. They are in constant touch. The mountain's quiet reaches the old land by gravity, without the child ever knowing it writes; but nothing runs uphill, and the old land can send no answer by water — and so the answer goes up by hand. That is what the people are, though they would say it more modestly: the return post. The cut steps, the ringing cairn, the drilled pebbles, the ground ochre, the century rhythm of the climb — all of it is one long correspondence between the oldest attention in the world and the youngest, carried on the backs of the only creatures at the spring with legs and intentions and a reason to be brief.

On the rare days when the cloud-sea lifts entirely, the whole hamlet stops work and looks up. The summit stands impossibly high and pale against a blue no one down there ever breathes, and somewhere just under it, invisible, is the notch, and the steps, and the green eye lying open in its cradle. The people look for as long as the lifting lasts. Then the ceiling closes, and the wall goes on talking, and the watcher goes back to the slab.

It will not always be this way. The glaciers are coming, in their time; they will gnaw the knife-edges round and swell the spring to a torrent, and the wall will roar for ten thousand years, and the child above will attend to being carved the way it attends to everything, one winter at a time. And after that the mountain will grow quiet the way mountains do, its babble slowing, its cracks spacing out to one a decade, one a century, and whoever keeps the watch by then will drill fewer and fewer beads. Some at the spring say that near the end of all the settling the mountain will finally say its first word, and that it will be a name. Some say a mountain only ever learns to say *water*. But the oldest listeners, the ones who have sat longest on the red slab, say the word has been coming through the whole time — that the mountain has been saying it since the first dawn dew fell into the gravel, one drop at a time, and that it simply takes a lifetime for each syllable to arrive, and that if you drink from the wall and hold the cold of it on your tongue, metal and snow, you already know roughly what it means. Something like: *still here.*

And at the bottom of all the noise, in the middle of the loud grey pool, the small flat coin of quiet lies open — kept, held, wider on the days the climbers sit near it — the oldest thing in this part of the world doing the youngest work there is: keeping a place still for a child who does not yet know it is loved from below.
