## The Stair of the Wakeful

Begin with the basin, because that is where the hollow ends and everything else begins. Watch the drip strike the black water for as long as you can stand the stillness, and notice what does not happen: the basin never rises. It never overflows. Somewhere beneath the open ring of pale crayfish there is a way down, and the water takes it — out of the hollow, out of the cold light of the larvae, into the body of the mountain.

What happens to it in there, nothing living witnesses. The limestone is honeycombed to its roots, and the water goes down through it in the dark, thread finding thread, seam finding seam, in no hurry at all — it cannot hurry, because it has already been metered. Nothing enters the mountain faster than a cloud can be drunk one bead at a time. No one has measured the crossing, but the water carries its own testimony: it comes out the far side tasting of stone dissolved so patiently that the taste is almost a temperature, and it comes out *old*. The water leaving the mountain today fell as cloud before the oldest person now drinking it was born. When someone in the valley dies, the saying goes, the mountain still owes them water; their share is in the dark somewhere, on its way down, and will arrive in its own time, for someone else.

It leaves all at once.

At the foot of the mountain — below the last of the fog, below the pines, where the valley opens out into weather and distance and ordinary daylight — the rock face splits, and out of the split the water comes white. Not a stream. An arrival. It sheets from the broken stone with a sound you hear a mile off and feel in your sternum from ten paces: the whole patient accumulation of the heights, every metered bead of a hundred years of cloud, delivered at last and all together. The local word for a spring like this is a *rising*, and it is the right word. Everything about the place goes up. Spray goes up. Mist goes up off the pools in the cold of the morning — the water handing the cloud back to the sky it was borrowed from. At midday the mist carries rainbows, small and low and constantly rebuilt. At night the moon comes apart on the moving water into more pieces than you can count and none of them holds still.

And below the rising, descending the slope in wide, brimming steps, is the stair.

No one built it. Pool after pool of pale stone — cream, honey, bone-white where the water runs thinnest — each one rimmed with a rounded lip of lime, each one full to that lip, each one spilling in a continuous fringe into the pool below, forty pools or more, down and down to the river at the valley floor. The stone looks poured, and it was: the water made it, and is making it still. Everything the mountain dissolved — the softened walls of the hollow, the cast shells of the crayfish, the glaze drawn slowly over the lowest salamanders, whorl upon whorl of the buried snails — the water carries down through the dark, holding all of it in solution the whole way, letting none of it go in the lightless seams. And then it meets sunlight and open air, and it lets go. The stair is built out of the hollow. Every step of it is the sleepers, laid down again in the light, a hair's thickness a year. You are standing on them the moment you arrive. They make good footing. They are warm in the sun.

It is the loudest place for miles. The rising roars, the forty lips hiss and chatter, and beneath both there is a deep, braided churn that never resolves into anything you could call a rhythm. You cannot hear your own footsteps at the stair. You can barely hear your own thoughts — which is one of the reasons people come. The other reason is thirst.

Because the water is faithful. That is the valley's word for it. The river below floods brown in spring and shrinks to a rope in the dry months; wells fail; rain forgets whole seasons. The spring does not vary. The same measure comes out of the split rock in drought as in deluge, year upon year upon lifetime, and no one at the stair knows why. The reason is thirty paces across and holds its darkness like a cupped palm, far above them in the fog, and it never changes; and a place that never changes cannot send changing water. The drip does not hurry. Therefore the spring never fails. The valley built nothing at the rising — no cistern, no channel, nothing that presumes — but three footpaths converge on it from three villages, worn as deep as the snail-roads of the hollow, and where they meet there is a flat washing stone polished by generations of cloth, and a tin cup on a chain, its rim worn thin and bright by more mouths than anyone could name.

## The Wakeful

Begin with the smallest, because the stair does, and because they will not wait for you.

The mayflies live a day. They rise off the pools toward evening in columns that catch the low sun through their wings, and for an hour the air above the stair is full of moving light — a sky of sparks that climbs and falls and climbs, dancing out the whole of a life between one bell of dusk and the dark. By morning they are gone, all of them, and the pools carry their spent wings down over the lips like a scattering of mica. And the next evening the columns rise again, the same figure in the air, made of entirely different lives. The hollow hangs its constellation fixed on the ceiling of the world and never lets one star go out. The stair assembles its constellation fresh each dusk out of creatures with a single day in them, burns it, loses it, and does not grieve. Between the two of them the mountain keeps both kinds of sky: the one that never changes and the one that never repeats.

The caddisflies are the masons' apprentices. In the shallows of every pool, small armored figures inch along the pale stone, and their armor is the stair itself: each larva builds a case around its body out of the travertine sand — grain by grain, mortared with silk — which is to say each one clothes itself in the sleepers, in crumbs of dissolved snail and shed crayfish carried a hundred years through the dark. And here is the difference that tells you where you are: when the caddis is finished growing, it leaves. It splits its case, rises through the skim of the water, and flies — and the little abandoned house rolls into a hollow of the pool, and the lime seals it in, and the step grows over it. Break open the stair's stone anywhere — you would not — and you would find it packed with tiny vacated dwellings, thousands upon thousands. The hollow's walls hold shells that are always occupied again, tenancy without end. The stair's steps hold houses abandoned by the living, every one of them a departure honored in stone.

The trout keep the pools, and they have solved stillness from the other side. They hold their stations in the current — under the lips, in the seams where pool spills into pool — perfectly fixed in place, and they achieve it by never once ceasing to swim. Watch one long enough and you understand it is working every second of its life to stay exactly where it is; the moment it rested it would be swept down the stair and out of the story. All of them face upstream. All of them face, though they cannot know it, the mountain — nosed into the arriving water their whole lives, swimming steadily toward a hollow they will never reach, going nowhere with all their strength. In the heights, the salamanders lie motionless and are slowly carried somewhere — into the shelf, into the stone. Here their pale opposite hangs in the bright water, all motion, going nowhere. The mountain apparently required both.

The dipper is the answerer. A round, sooty bird, it stands on the wet lips of the pools and bows — constantly, compulsively, a bob of the whole body every few seconds, courtesy without end — and then steps off the edge and *walks under the water*, down the face of the stone, wings half-spread against the current, hunting the shallows on foot as though the roar were a meadow. It is the only creature at the stair with a voice pitched to be heard over the water, and it uses it all year, even in ice, a long bright thread of song laid over the churn. The crickets of the hollow listen to the seething of the moss and say nothing, forever. The dipper has never listened to anything in its life. It sings *back*. If the water is carrying a message down out of the dark — and it is — the dipper is the only one who has always answered it, cheerfully, without once asking what it says.

And the stone itself is grown, not laid. The pale film that skins every surface of the stair — slick, faintly golden, alive — is the counterpart of the moss above, and the inverse of it. It is one continuous tissue, pool to pool, lip to lip; touch it at the rising and at the river and you have touched the same individual. The moss drinks the cloud and swallows what it is given — the axe, the light, the hours. The film drinks the water and *gives out stone*: it is the mason, coaxing the mountain's dissolved dead out of solution, laying them down in courses, raising the lip of every pool a hair's thickness a year. And because the highest pool grows fastest, where the water arrives richest, the stair is climbing. Slower than anything alive can witness, lip by lip, the stair is building its way up the slope, back toward the fog, back toward the place the water comes from. The hollow sends itself down in the dark; the stair rebuilds it upward in the light; and the distance between the two places closes by a hair a year, patiently, as though somewhere it were understood that they will meet.

The people are many, and they do not stay — that is their way of belonging here. They come down the three paths with vessels and washing and infants and grief, and the stair takes what each has brought. The vessels it fills. The washing it takes the dirt from. The grief it takes the *sound* from — this is known in all three villages, that the stair is where you go to weep, because the water is louder than any sorrow and will carry the noise of it down over forty lips and away to the river, so that you can come back up the path lighter, having been, for an hour, inaudible even to yourself. The tin cup on its chain passes from mouth to mouth to mouth. It is the opposite of the axe in every particular: made to be used, used by everyone, surrendered by no one, worn thin not by water but by lips. The hollow holds one made thing, given up forever by a single hand. The stair holds one made thing, held onto forever by all hands at once.

And the stair keeps an archive, because the people found that the growing stone would take an impression and seal it. At the lip of one wide pool, generations have pressed things into the soft new lime — an oak leaf, a coin, a braid of hair, the sole of a baby's foot — and the mason has grown over each one, and the water has kept them, sharp, under a glaze you can see through. It is a wall of small farewells. Whoever leaves the valley presses something in before they go. The hollow's archive is of those who stayed past staying; the stair's is of those who went, held crisp in the stone while their originals scattered into the world and softened and were lost. And low in the oldest terrace, deeper than any living memory, under glaze gone amber with age, there is one impression that the valley can no longer explain: the outline of a hand axe, flaked chert, pressed into the wet lime edge-down, every facet of its knapping distinct. The edge in the stone is still sharp. It will always be sharp. Far above, in the wet grey light, the thing itself lies in the moss with a snail asleep on its face, its edges long since taken by the water — but the stair remembers exactly what it was, the way it remembers everything: at the moment of departure, and forever.

Once a year — the first cloudless dusk of autumn, when the mayfly columns are thin and the air over the pools has gone cold and clean — a single bat comes down off the mountain, circles the mist above the rising once, drinks from the highest pool on the wing, and is gone back up into the dark. No one at the stair knows what it is a delegate of. The children call it the first bat of winter and are not wrong, exactly.

And once in a great while — once in a lifetime, perhaps less — someone standing at the rising with the cup in their hand hears something inside the roar. Not over it; inside it. Underneath the churn and the hiss and the forty spilling lips, a single interval — one beat, unvarying, patient — as though all that white tumult were only the noise a clock makes when it is poured through a mountain. The water has carried the time of the drip down through a hundred years of dark without spilling a second of it, and this one person, for whatever reason, can hear it. They come back the next day to be sure. They stand longer each time. They stop weeping at the stair, or stop needing to. And one morning they fill the cup, drink, set it down on its chain for the next mouth, press something small into the wet lip of the pool — and take the path that goes up, past the washing stone, past the last of the pines, into the fog.

The stair does not mourn them. The stair does not keep anything except the shapes pressed into it; that is its whole nature, and its gift. It takes a century of held cloud and gives it away in an afternoon. It takes the mountain's dissolved dead and hands them back as footing, warm in the sun. It takes grief and makes it inaudible, takes thirst and fills it, takes its own children — one in an age, called by a heartbeat hidden in the roar — and gives them, too, up the hill, and never runs dry, and never runs over, and is never for one instant silent or still.

Everything the hollow keeps, the stair releases. Everything the stair releases, the water carries. And between the seat that waits and the cup that is passed, the mountain stands with its two mouths — one drinking the sky a bead at a time, one giving it back all at once — while the pale stone climbs, a hair a year, toward the fog.

The water knows the whole road.

It has never once told, and it has never once stopped telling.
