**The Bare Parish**

You leave the hollow by going up, because up is the only direction the borough does not fold. The green thins toward the head of the valley — the stems narrower, the grown-shut places grown less shut — and the light begins to lose its gold, paling through jade to a grey-white you have no name for until you remember: *daylight*. Actual daylight, unfiltered. Then the leaf-mass ends, all at once, the way a wave ends at a hull, and you climb out of the last of it onto stone.

The seam is a weather system in itself. The green exhales — warm, wet, breathing air rolling off the canopy like breath off an animal — and the stone above it is cold, and where the two meet, a ribbon of mist stands all day along the green-line, a horizontal cloud you wade through at the moment of crossing. Below the mist: the plant, the whole drowned valley of it, a sea of canopy stretching away with the borough somewhere under it like a bubble in bread. Above the mist: bare rock, wind, and — this stops you dead, and it takes a moment to understand why — *sky*. You have not seen sky in a long time. Neither, you will come to understand, has anything down there.

There is a road. That is the second shock, smaller but stranger. A real road: cut stones, laid by hand, cambered for drainage, climbing the ridge in patient switchbacks. Behind you it runs downhill and drowns — you can follow it with your eye right to the point where the green closes over it, the paving stones disappearing under the leaf-mass like a stair descending into water. Ahead, it climbs. Roads mean feet, and feet mean somewhere to go. You follow it up through the wind, and near the valley's head, on a shoulder of rock above the last of the mist, you come to the town.

You know it immediately.

Not *of* it. You know *it* — the way you know a face. The road becomes a lane, and the lane bends left in a curve your legs recognize before your mind does, and delivers you into a small square with a well at its center, and you stand there in the cold wind with your skin crawling gently, because you have been here before, a valley away and a world apart, in warm green light with a roar coming up through your feet. It is the same town. The same plan, lane for lane. The square is the square. The frontages lean toward each other over the streets at the same companionable angles. The stair climbing to the upper doors is the stair. There, on the north side, is the doorway — the one that was doubled below, the one whose twin stood on the south frontage with identical moss. Here it is single. You stand in it and your shadow falls only out of itself, and the singleness feels like an amputation. You keep waiting for the square to arrive from a direction you didn't walk. It doesn't. Space up here does what it is told, and after the borough, obedient space feels broken.

But before any of that — before the recognition, before the well — there was the first thing, and as in the borough, it was so total you mistook it for nothing at all.

The silence.

The parish is silent the way the hollow is loud: structurally. Down there, sound was the medium; here, the medium has been removed. There is wind, but wind is not a voice — wind is an erasure, a long *shhh* drawn over the stones — and beneath the wind there is nothing, and your body cannot accept it. For the first hours your chest keeps supplying the thrum, phantom bass in the bones of your feet, the way land sways after a long time at sea. You catch yourself straining downhill. It takes days for the borough to finish draining out of your skeleton, and even then, on still evenings, you will lay a palm on the cobbles and imagine you feel it: far below, through a valley's depth of rock and root, something enormous, slow, and familiar.

Hold that thought. The parish will explain it, in its own time. The parish explains everything, that is its nature — it is the answer key to the borough, weathering in the open air — but it explains things the way ruins do: in the wrong order, with pieces missing, and you have to walk it to read it.

Walk it, then. The third thing arrives in the details, and the details are these: *tool marks*. Chisel-dressing on the door jambs, still crisp in the sheltered angles. Mortar in the joints, gone to sand where the rain reaches. Hinges rusted to orange lace, still hanging. A lintel over one door with two initials and a date cut into it, the date worn past reading, the initials worn to a suggestion — but cut, unmistakably *cut*, by a hand holding an iron tool on some particular morning. The borough's deepest strangeness was that nothing ever made it. Here is the answer, standing in the wind: something made *this* one. Hands did. Generations of them. Every shape the green reproduced in living tissue exists here first in dressed stone, and the wear the borough quoted so faithfully — the dished thresholds, the rounded corners, the polished arc on the well-rim — was *earned* here, footstep by footstep, elbow by elbow, over centuries of actual mornings. You kneel at a threshold and fit your fingers into the dish of it. Down in the hollow there is a threshold of grown green dished identically, to the depth of a fingernail, and you understand now that the one below is not mysterious at all. It is a portrait. It is this stone's likeness, taken with terrible fidelity, wear and all.

Which makes the differences the whole story. And the differences are ruin.

The parish does not maintain its decay. Nothing fills its sags. Roofs have gone in — you look through doorways at rooms open to the sky, rafters down and lying like ribs, drifts of broken slate, a rowan tree growing hale and indifferent out of a hearth. Nettles hold the yards. The lanes are silting up with soil the wind has manufactured out of the town itself. This is what empty really looks like, you realize: not the borough's swept, breath-held readiness, but *this* — accumulation, collapse, the slow riot of everything a place can no longer refuse. Abandoned places fill up. The borough's emptiness was a kept thing, a made bed. The parish's emptiness is just time, doing what time does when nobody argues.

And so the two towns have traded places in the strangest way: the copy is now more whole than the original. There are houses here fallen past all reading — a rubble heap, a doorpost — whose complete shapes still stand a valley below, in green, weathered to precisely the age they were when the likeness was taken. If you want to know what the parish looked like before it began to die, you cannot learn it here. You have to go back down into the roar and walk the portrait. The parish has forgotten itself. The valley remembers it.

How? By what possible channel does a plant a valley away hold the plan of a stone town, lane for lane, threshold for threshold?

You get the answer at dusk, on your first evening, and it comes screaming.

Swifts. A ribbon of them, slate-grey, pouring up out of the valley in the last light, rising through the mist at the green-line and streaming up the old road faster than anything should move — and then they are *in* the parish, in the lanes, taking the corners, and you stand in the square while they tear around you along routes you have watched them fly before, in another light, between other walls. The same turns. The same banked corner at the lane's curve, the same split around the empty point above the cobbles, the same channels worn into the air. Of course. *Of course.* The swifts nest here — some of them, in summer, in the surviving eaves, as their line has nested since the eaves were new — and the swifts sleep down there, in the warm vaults above the borough's leaf-ceiling, where the insects never fail and the winter never comes. They are one population with two towns. A fledgling hatched in a parish gable makes its first flight down a street-grid a thousand generations of its ancestors wore into wing-memory, and carries that grid, that whole town rendered as pure route, down into the green — where it flies it, and flies it, and flies it.

And the green, which grows to fit whatever presses upon it, grew to fit the flying.

That is what the hollow is. It was never a bubble the plant happened to leave. It is a space the swifts refused to let close — a town's worth of remembered streets, flown so faithfully, for so long, that the vegetable mass grew its walls up against the channels and stopped, and put facades where the flying said facades were, and windows where ten thousand summers of parish swifts had learned there were windows to shear past. The borough is a cast of the Bare Parish, taken in air, poured in green. The swifts are the mold. Nobody built it; that much was always legible. But somebody built *this* one, and the swifts have been carrying it downhill, wingbeat by wingbeat, ever since.

Even the borough's absences are quotations. You notice it in the churchyard, which sits behind the tower's stump at the parish's uphill edge: leaning slates, a low wall, the names weathered entirely away — the parish keeps its dead and has forgotten every one of them. The borough has no churchyard. You walked all of it; there is none. And here is why: the swifts do not fly there. Nothing to hawk for over the graves, no lane to thread — the churchyard leaves no path in the air, and so it was never carried down, and so the green never grew it. The borough is the parish with its dead subtracted. Perhaps that is the real difference between them — why one can only remember and the other can *expect*. Grief leaves no flight path. The green got everything but the mourning.

The tower is a stump, and beside it, in the nettles, lies the bell.

It is cracked stem to lip, a wound you could lay your forearm in. You clear the nettles and strike it with a fist-sized stone, because you have to, and it answers with a dead clonk, a flat nothing, the sound of the sound being gone.

But you have heard this bell. You know it the moment your knuckles rest on the cold bronze, the way you knew the square. You heard it through the soles of your feet, in warm green light, coming up out of a depth no one has dug to: the bass thrum everything down there tunes to. The deep singer. Centuries of ringing soaked this bell's one note down through the rock and into the roots of the valley, and the valley — a resonating body the size of a lowland, a grown ear — caught the note and has never once let it decay. Then the tower came down, and the bell cracked, and the note simply went on without it, below, where nothing is ever gone. The parish's voice outlived the parish's throat. Every frog and wall-singer and swift in the Hollow Borough takes its key from a bell that will never ring again. There is an old jackdaw in the tower stump — the jackdaws are the parish's last citizens of any standing, hoarding rings of rusted nothing in the rubble — that does an imitation of a bell, learned from a parent that learned it from a parent, and the imitation is worn and wrong, a folk memory, a story about a sound. Uphill, that is all that remains of the voice. Downhill, the voice itself, perfect, permanent, load-bearing.

That is the arrangement, you begin to see. That is what these two places are to one another. The parish is where things happen once and are worn away. The borough is where the wearing-away is kept. One town, two tenses: the parish is the past that can only finish; the borough is the same town held forever at the instant of being about to begin. And between them runs everything the parish ever let slip downhill — a note, a street-plan, a population of wings, and the last channel, the strangest one, which you find on your final morning, at the well.

The parish's well is dry. Mostly. Its rim is polished along one arc only — the original arc, the true one, smoothed by six hundred years of parish elbows, mornings, gossip, rest — and you lean there because the stone has been shaped to make you lean there, and you look down the shaft at darkness and dry rubble. Except that sometimes, on no schedule the jackdaws or the wind could tell you, the shaft holds water. Water that cannot be there, standing far too high, blood-warm to a lowered hand, lit from within by a soft green-gold that has no business on this cold ridge. And from it, faint and rising, a roar. Lean over it in one of those hours and you will see, in place of your own grey sky, a canopy — and you will understand at last what the borough's well was doing on the day it reflected a canopy that was not its own. It was reflecting *this*, or reflecting through it. One well, folded, standing in two towns at once: the doubled doorway at the scale of the whole world. The road drowned generations ago. The door never did.

So: what runs between the Bare Parish and the Hollow Borough? A road, dead. A bell's note, immortal. A river of swifts, twice a year, carrying a town in their wings. One well. And, threaded thinner than any of these — one in a generation, the parish's records would say, if the parish had kept records, if the rain had not eaten them — people. Because when the green rose and drowned the valley pastures and the road out, and the living got thin, the families went uphill and over the ridge into whatever the rest of the world is. Almost all of them. But every so often someone went the other way instead — down the drowned road, into the warm and the roar — and the borough's dished green thresholds count those feet among their smeared, scattered, out-of-order population. The doorways down there fit you because they fit *people*: parish masons sized the originals for parish bodies, and the green never changed a measurement. The absence in the borough's square, the one the swifts bank around, with its stooped height and its favorite leaning-spot — it was never only you. It is everyone who ever leaned on this arc of stone and then, some morning, kept walking downhill.

You lean on the arc now. The wind combs the nettles. The jackdaw makes its wrong bell. Downhill, under the green, in the warm gold light, a rim of grown bract polishes a little smoother against an elbow that is and is not yours, in an hour that has and has not happened.

The parish is, in its way, completely finished.

And it is not troubled by this, any more than the borough is troubled by its peace — because a town that has managed to send its voice, its shape, its swifts, and its rarest children downhill into a place where nothing is ever lost has not exactly died. It has *drained*. It stands up here in the wind, emptying gracefully, letting the last of itself weather loose and wash down the valley into its own green afterlife, where the rooms are swept, and the chorus holds its key, and the door — every door, its door — is standing ajar.
