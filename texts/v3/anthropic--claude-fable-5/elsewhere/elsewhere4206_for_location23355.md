**The Windfall of the Quick**

You leave the Tableland by its eastern rim, which is the only place the plateau consents to end. The corbelled domes grow more frequent as you approach it — every one of them opening east, every dark doorway aimed the same way, like a crowd at a railing — and then the bedrock simply stops holding you up. A long scarp drops away below, the one steep thing in a world that finished with steepness, and beyond it, far down and running out to a hazed horizon, lies a country the color of the plateau's dust. Which is what it is. That is the thing you understand first, standing on the rim with the evening wind beginning to pour past your ankles and down the slope like water finding a drain: everything below you is made of what the ones behind you have breathed out.

The descent takes a day. The talus at the top is real stone, the last of it, and then the stone goes under and does not come back. From there down it is dust all the way — the same fine pale dust, soft as ash, that gathers in the hollows of the Tableland, except that here it has been gathering since before the mountains up top forgot they were mountains, and it lies hundreds of feet deep. You are not walking down a slope so much as down a snowdrift the size of a nation. Your boots print it. Everything prints it. It holds every mark for a little while and none of them for long.

And then you are in it, and the world has changed tempo underneath you like a dropped gear.

---

The Windfall is soft country, all the way through. There is no bedrock within reach of anything living; there is only the deep dust, packed by its own weight into a pale gold flesh that rain can carve like a thumb through butter. And rain comes. The storms that the plateau outlasted did not die — they moved downhill. They ride in off the eastern distance in bruised towers, strike the great wall of the scarp, and rain themselves to rags over the lowland, and every storm redesigns the place. Ravines are born in an afternoon, run milk-pale and violent for an hour, and die by dusk. Bluffs shrug off their faces. Pinnacles that stood for a decade — landmarks, named, sung about — go down in a single wet night, and the country simply closes over the loss and proposes new landmarks by morning. On the Tableland, the horizon has not changed within the memory of anything breathing on it. Here, the horizon has changed since you last blinked.

It is loud. That is the second thing, and after the plateau it lands on you almost as pain. Water talking in the ravines. Banks letting go with a sound like enormous soft applause. Birdsong run together into ribbons, because the birds here live nine days and have no time to leave gaps between the notes. And underneath and over everything, constant as the water, a fine fast trilling in every register at once, coming from the banks, the brush, the mounded towns — which you take at first for insects, and which is speech.

Because the Windfall is full. It is full the way the Tableland is full, occupancy in every shadow, except that nothing here has finished with movement, nothing here has *begun* to finish with movement, and so the fullness churns. Flowers wait in the dust as seed for weeks and then, one hour after rain, detonate — whole hillsides going gold and violet and white between noon and mid-afternoon, setting seed by evening, gone by the next day. The Quick call these hour-meadows, and drop everything to stand in them, because a Quick who misses one may not live to see another. There are darting fox-like things the color of wet dust, and burrowers whose towns honeycomb the bluffs, and the nine-day birds, and the milk river easing east through the middle of the country, pale and thick with the land it is carrying away — carrying, though nothing here says it this way, the grandmothers on to wherever the grandmothers go next.

---

The wind is the clock of the place, and it runs on a tide of one day.

All morning and afternoon it blows *up*: warm air climbing the great wall, carrying the Windfall's breath to the plateau — its rain-smell, its pollen, its noise thinned by distance to something the elders up there may register as a pleasant seasoning of the light. Then comes the turn.

It happens near dusk. The upslope wind falters, and for the space of a few breaths there is no wind at all, and the Windfall — the loud, churning, unpausable Windfall — goes still. All of it. The trilling stops. The birds stop. The Quick stop, wherever they are, mid-stride, mid-word, tools raised, and stand with their faces west toward the wall, which is catching the last light and burning a long dull gold above their darkened country. Nobody teaches this and nobody explains it. It lasts as long as the windlessness lasts, a handful of heartbeats even by our counting, an eternity by theirs — the one stillness in a life made entirely of hurry — and some of them say it is the moment the plateau notices them, and some say it is merely good manners, and then the first cool air comes sliding down the scarp, and with it the first grain, and everything resumes at once like a held film released.

Because at night the wind pours *down*, off the Tableland, and it does not come empty. It comes carrying the dust — the fine exhalation of ten thousand finishing elders, sifting off the eastern rim past all those open doorways and falling across the lowland in a snow too sparse to see and too constant to escape. By morning everything in the Windfall wears a grey film: leaves, rooftops, sleeping bodies, the surface of the river. They call it the windfall, which is where the country gets its name, and they mean the word both ways — the thing that falls on the wind, and the unearned gift. The ground rises under it. Measurably. Perceptibly, in a place where a lifetime is a year: doorsills climb, floors go under, and every few generations a town simply steps up out of its own ground floor and builds again on its own roof. There are hills in the Windfall that are nothing but towns, forty deep, each one the forgotten foundation of the next, and the Quick measure their history in depth rather than in years, because years are lives and nobody hoards those, but depth is honest. *That song is two doorsills old. The ford was lost three floors down. Nobody has been west of the ravines since the ground was lower.*

---

**The Quick**

Give up, first, the instinct to find them pitiable. They are not brief the way a spark is brief. They are brief the way a note is brief — struck deliberately, in tune, in time, part of something with a length of its own.

They stand knee-high to you, lean, hot to the touch — fevered, you would say, until you understand that this is simply the temperature of a body doing everything at once. Pelts the color of the dust, because everything here is the color of the dust, because everything here is *made* of the dust in the most literal sense there is. Their hearts are a blur. Their eyes are enormous, dark, wet — the same eyes, you realize with a small shock, as the eyes on the plateau, the identical dark attending eye, only running at a different speed — and what those eyes see is this: you, glacial. Your walk a slow toppling, your speech a tide, your week-long crossing of their country an event their grandchildren will finish watching. They perceive the flicker in a candleflame as weather. They watch each individual raindrop choose its place.

And above the western edge of everything they perceive, filling half the sky at evening, holding the light after their whole country has gone dark: the wall. The Ledge, they call it, or the Old Light, or the Shelf of the Grandmothers, depending on the town and the generation and which songs survived the last flood. They cannot see the Settled as alive. Be fair — from a distance of one year, nothing about the Tableland moves. To the Quick the plateau is eternity itself, geology, the fixed thing against which everything else is fast, and they are more right about this than they know, and also they are exactly wrong, and both of these facts would delight them, because the Quick love nothing better than a thing that is true twice.

They live a year. Two, for the venerable, who sit wrapped at the doorways being asked things. Into that year they fit an entire arc — childhood, mastery, love, office, decline — not compressed, not rushed, but *scaled*, the way their hour-meadows are not failed forests but complete flowers. They talk constantly, that ribbon-trill you heard from the ravines, and their hands, many-fingered and never still, are always making: pots, walls, channels, toys, instruments, repairs to all of the above, because in the Windfall everything made is already dissolving and the making never gets to stop. Their cities are magnificent and doomed and they know it and build anyway — towers of rammed dust, bridges of woven brush, painted; always painted, in the pigments of a land that will eat the paint within a decade. Their arts turn over in a generation. Their sciences are rediscovered, brilliantly, again and again, by scholars who have no way of knowing they are the ninth. Their history is a rumor their songs tell about their songs. The land eats every archive; the river carries the libraries east; the Quick forget the way other beings breathe, continuously, as a condition of living —

Except.

---

Except that the material remembers.

Potters here learn it as apprentices, their first strange thing: a bowl thrown in the evening and left to stand overnight will be found in the morning with a grain in it — fine lines, raised just barely under the thumb, running radially back from the rim. Rammed-earth walls, given a generation, settle out of true, always in the same direction: bowing, gathering, easing toward the curve of a dome. There are towns in the Windfall where the granaries have gone round-shouldered and inward-leaning over the decades until they stand — nobody planned this; the builders are eleven generations gone — in rings. Flat stones stacked by children playing on a bluff are found, years later, fused at the touch-points, arranged with a rightness no child intended. The dust has habits. Ground fine, scattered on the wind, rained on, trodden, packed into bricks by hot quick hands, it still holds — at some grain below any grain the Quick can see — the grooves of everything it concluded when it was slow. Build with it and it will drift, patiently, at its own pace and not yours, toward old conclusions. The Quick have a saying for a plan that keeps veering back to an earlier shape no matter who executes it: *the mud has an opinion.*

And at night, when the down-wind is running and the towns are quiet, there are those among them — one or two in a town, called listeners, an office older than any city standing — who go to the oldest wall in the district, the deepest-founded, and press themselves flat against it, ear and palms and fevered breastbone, and listen to the hum.

It comes down through the scarp and through the deep dust, conducted as sound is conducted in a drumhead: the plateau's night hum, the unbroken utterance, the conversation of every body standing in every trail up there, one statement that has been in the middle of itself for longer than there has been anything with ears. On the Tableland you felt it through your knees and took it for ambience. The Quick know better, and have known better for longer than they have known anything else. A listener spends a life — a whole life, a year, the entire year — attending to that note, and at the end of it marks down, on the Wall of the Taking, in a script maintained unchanged across dynasties of forgetting (it is the one thing they have refused to let the land eat; when a town rises a floor, the Wall is unbuilt stone by stone and rebuilt on top, first, before any dwelling), the fraction of a fraction of a syllable that passed through the world during their term.

The Taking is, by their own depth-reckoning, some thousands of doorsills old. It is the single continuous institution of the Quick, the spine running through every buried city, the only project longer than a hill. Whole civilizations of theirs have risen, flowered, and washed away *inside a vowel.* And the transcription proceeds, life by life, each listener adding a mark and dying and being replaced, a relay of ten thousand runners carrying one word — and some generations ago, the scholars of the Wall announced, cautiously, in the manner of scholars, that the first word appeared to be complete. What it says is argued about, in the way the Quick argue, which is joyously and forever. The largest school holds that it resolves to something like *still here.* A smaller school insists the marks are not a phrase at all but a name — one name, the name of some single one of the slow, offered downwind to whoever had the patience to take it — and that the courteous thing, the only courteous thing, is to keep listening for the next.

---

They know what the dust is. Or they believe it, which in the Windfall are neighboring towns. The nightly grey film, the rising ground, the opinionated mud — their songs have always said it plainly: the slow ones on the Shelf give themselves downwind, and the Windfall is the gift, and every Quick who has ever hurried anywhere was built, meal by meal, crop by crop, out of the flesh of the grandmothers. This is not metaphor and they do not treat it as one. It is agronomy. An elder finishing on the rim above — a century of exhalation sifting east off the edge — becomes, over the following ages, the topsoil of a province, and the province feeds a line of towns, and the towns raise their ten thousand generations, and so a single one of the Settled becomes, in time, a *nation* of the Quick: one long thought, ground fine, and set on fire, and taught to sing.

The Quick name their provinces for the character of the ground — the Patient Levels, the Fond Hills, a stretch of famously stubborn earth called Old Contrary where the walls will not stay square for love or money — and they do not know, and would be honored past bearing to know, that these are personalities. That they are living on individuals, and in them, and of them, districted by temperament.

Some of them guess. There is a thing the old ones do, in their last autumn, when the year has gone through them and the hurry finally lifts: they walk west. Against the night wind, upslope, into the falling dust — *walking into the snow*, the songs call it — out past the last towns and up the great talus where the stone begins. Nearly all of them finish on the slope, and the wind brings them home again as the finest of the grey film, which is not counted a sad ending; it is counted a round trip. But the songs insist — and the songs, remember, are the only history that has ever outrun the river — that a few keep climbing. That the climb slows them, step by lengthening step, each stride wider in time than the last, until at the top there is no walker anymore, only a small dark dome, bare of lichen, eyes already open, come to rest in the dust between two elders whose trails, over the following centuries, will curve very slightly toward it.

No one breathing on the Tableland has seen a new one begin. No one breathing in the Windfall lives long enough to see a climber arrive. Between those two facts there is a doorway, east-facing, and the Quick have decided what stands in it, and nothing on the plateau has ever contradicted them at any speed you could catch.

---

And you, coming down — you are the third thing, the odd current in the loop. Travelers off the Tableland arrive in the Windfall still carrying the plateau's pace in their bodies, breath stretched, steps like tides, and to the Quick such a walker is an event on the scale of geography. A slowed traveler crossing the country is attended by generations: fed, sheltered, sung to, argued about, mapped. Children are told to meet at the Walker. Towns adjust their roads. Some travelers speed back up, day by day, until the Quick can no longer quite see them, and go on east with the river. Some never do, and become landmarks, and then provinces, and the Windfall has a name ready for that too.

Because this, finally, is what the two countries are, and neither of them could tell you, and both of them would agree: two lungs. All day the low country breathes up the wall — rain-smell, pollen, the thin bright noise of ten thousand lives per acre — and the high country tastes it in the light. All night the high country breathes down — itself, grain by grain, its finished elders and its concluded thoughts — and the low country rises on the gift and burns it gorgeously fast. The plateau is where the world holds the note. The Windfall is where the world plays it. The dust goes down and becomes fire; the fire climbs, once in a great while, and becomes stone; the milk river carries what's left east toward some third tempo the wall's shadow never reaches; and in between, at dusk, for a handful of heartbeats at the turning of the wind, both countries hold perfectly still at once —

and the listeners say that is the comma.

They are, by their own count, a few marks into the second word. The senior school believes it will resolve, in another forty generations or so, to something like *you.* They are in no hurry to be right. It is the one thing in the Windfall that has ever been in no hurry, and they are very proud of it, and they pass it on.
