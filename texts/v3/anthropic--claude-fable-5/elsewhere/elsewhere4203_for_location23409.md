**The Catchfields**

Follow the breath. It is the only thing the Pumphouse Quarter ever lets go of — the water stays, the seed stays, the fallen catwalks stay and are digested — but the vapor column rises off the Stack and the prevailing wind takes it, hour after hour, year after year, leaning it eastward in a long soft diagonal across six miles of marsh. Out there the plume loses itself against the overcast and you would think that was the end of it: exhaled, dispersed, done. It isn't. Six miles east the land finally does something. It rises — a long pale scarp of chalk downland, the first honest slope in any direction, standing across the wind's path like a held-up hand. The plume, arriving warm and saturated at the foot of it, is forced to climb. Climbing, it cools. Cooling, it lets go.

The result is the Wet Line: a band of drizzle perhaps three hundred yards wide, running along the scarp at a single consistent contour, in more or less permanent session. It is not weather in the usual sense. Weather moves. This stands. The rain in it is so fine it doesn't fall so much as settle, a suspension you walk into and out of like a room, and it has been settling on this one stripe of hillside for as long as the Stack has been breathing. You can read the fact of it from a mile away: the downs are dry country, pale gold turf over white stone, cropped short by wind and rabbits, and across that paleness the Wet Line lies like a signature — a horizontal stroke of deep saturated green, mossed and sedged and beaded, with sharp edges above and below where the drizzle gives out. It is the plume made visible at its destination. It is, if you want to be exact about it, a portrait of the chimney's breath, painted in the only medium the hill has.

People farmed it once. That is what the Catchfields are: the works of a rain-harvest, built into the stripe and descending from it, and they are everywhere once your eye learns them. Aprons of overlapped slate laid directly on the hillside, acres of them, scaled like a fish and pitched to shed every settling droplet sideways into stone-lined gutters. The gutters feeding leats — narrow channels cut along the contours, dry-laid, mortarless, work that holds by weight and fit alone — and the leats descending in patient switchbacks to dew ponds dished into the turf, clay-puddled, lead-lipped, each one holding a disc of sky. Below the ponds, the cisterns: chambers cut back into the chalk itself and roofed with corbelled stone, cool dark rooms you enter down worn steps, where the harvest was banked. Nothing here is new. Nothing is ruined. The same settled quality as the quarter, arrived at by the opposite route — the quarter holds together because it is warm and wet and self-lubricating; the Catchfields hold together because they were built dry, by people who understood that on a hill the enemy is not decay but slippage, and who set every stone so that gravity is what keeps it rather than what takes it.

The strangest of the works stand along the crest of the stripe: rows of timber frames, tall as gallows, facing west into the wind. Fog fences. They once carried mesh — hessian, then wire — to comb the plume directly, harvesting the mist before it even condensed, and the mesh is long gone. The frames are not empty. Spiders have taken them, generations deep, and the webs they string across the old rectangles do exactly what the mesh did: they comb the wet out of the moving air. On any morning the webs hang loaded, thousands of them, beaded silver and sagging, and they drip — into the same gutters, down the same leats, into the same ponds. The harvest has not stopped. It has changed management.

The sound of the place is the inverse of the quarter's, which is to say it is made of the same silence the quarter's noise displaces. Wind in short grass. The hiss of the drizzle band, which you hear as a texture rather than a sound. The tick and tickle of water moving in stone channels — small, specific, countable, everything the quarter's roar swallows whole. Larks, above the dry turf, in actual sunlight, because the plume rains out below the crest and the top of the down stands above its own weather more days than not. Stand on that crest at evening and you get the one view that exists nowhere else in the world: the whole system at once. The marsh, pewter-flat to the horizon. The quarter's basin, a smudge of grey lid and orange pinpricks. The vapor column rising off the Stack and leaning toward you, six miles of breath crossing the dark ground to arrive at your feet as drizzle. And on the stillest nights, if you lie down and let the wind go over you, a pulse at the threshold of hearing — a deep compound thudding, four or five rhythms drifting in and out of phase, carried across the marsh on the ground and the air together. You would take it for your own blood if you didn't know. Knowing, you understand that the Catchfields are, among other things, within earshot of a heart.

The water is the thing to be careful of, and the rain-farmers evidently knew it. What the Stack exhales is distilled — vapor is water with everything left behind, and it comes down on the scarp soft, pure, and hungry. Water like that has no patience with stone. It has been eating the chalk along the Wet Line for the whole life of the arrangement, and the stripe is not only greener than the rest of the hill; it is lower — a long shallow furrow following the contour, the hillside worn back a full arm's depth where the drizzle stands, so that the plume has by now carved its own outline into the scarp the way light carves an image into a plate left exposed to one window for a hundred years. The leats are dissolved glassy. The lead pond-linings are pitted like old faces. Drawn from the cistern, the water tastes of nothing at all — genuinely nothing, a blankness on the tongue that takes a moment to identify, because what you are tasting is absence. The quarter, remember, keeps its memory in its water, everything dissolved into everything. What it breathes out is the forgetting. The Catchfields receive the forgetting, and it arrives with an appetite, and the first thing it does on landing is begin taking new memory from the hill.

Where that memory goes is down. In the cisterns, in the dark, the water that has spent its passage through the turf and the chalk dissolving lime arrives at cool still air and begins, drop by drop, to put the lime back. Flowstone. The chamber walls are sheathed in it, ivory and wet-gleaming; stalactites hang from the corbelled roofs in ranks; the older cisterns have half-ceased to be architecture at all and are becoming caves — the hill rebuilding itself indoors, in translation, at a rate of perhaps a fingernail's thickness a decade. The drip in the deepest chamber has, by the size of the mound beneath it, been falling on the same spot since before the frames on the crest were timber.

And the downs are alive in every way the quarter is not, which is to say: ordinarily. Hares in the dry turf. Larks. Beetles the color of oiled iron working the sheep-cropped grass. The spiders in their inherited estate. And down at the ponds, on most mornings, herons — the same herons, in all likelihood, that stand at the marsh and will not cross the quarter's lichen-scaled fence. They cross six miles of open marsh instead, to loaf at the dew ponds and drink the chimney's breath, and if there is a comedy anywhere in this landscape it is there: the animals will not take the quarter's water at the source, where it is dark and warm and thick with memory, but they queue for it here, distilled, delivered, decanted through a hill. Everything the basin has no vacancy for finds vacancy on the down. The place is porous where the quarter is full.

Change comes on the hill's own calendar, which is longer even than the quarter's. The furrow deepens by a thought a year. The flowstone thickens. The webs are rebuilt nightly, which on the Catchfields' timescale is a shimmer, a permanent flickering skin. And once in a long while the wind swings — a day, two days, out of a rare quarter — and the plume misses the scarp entirely, and the Wet Line goes dry. Those are the strange days. The drizzle stops settling. The channels tick down toward silence one diminishing note at a time. The webs hang empty and the spiders sit still in the frames, and the whole hillside takes on the specific quality of a held breath, which is exactly what it is: it is waiting for someone else to exhale.

---

Start with the spiders, because the spiders are what you can see.

They read, at first, as the plainest kind of opportunists — small hunters exploiting a windbreak, no more freighted with meaning than mice in a granary. But the granary reading fails the same way the squatter reading failed for the quarter's green, and for the same reason: opportunists take what serves them and ignore the rest, and these do not ignore the rest. The webs are strung across the frames and only the frames, in the old mesh's rectangles, at the old mesh's tension, facing the old mesh's wind. The silk sheds its beaded load into the gutters because the frames were placed to make anything strung in them shed into the gutters — the spiders did not choose the trade; the trade was built into the geometry, and they were the next material to arrive that could hold a rectangle. In the quarter, the vines took the railings' shape and inherited the railings' job. Here the silk has taken the mesh's shape and inherited the harvest. It is the same event, six miles apart: a made thing so thoroughly shaped around its work that when the original substance failed, the work simply refilled the form with whatever was on hand. The Catchfields do not employ the spiders. The Catchfields *are* the employment, standing open.

But the spiders, like the green, are skin — the visible layer of something that has to be read at the scale of the hill. And the hill, read at its own scale, is doing something the quarter never does. The quarter acts. It pumps, exhales, digests, circulates; it is a body made entirely of continuing, and everything in it is the performance of its own maintenance. The scarp does none of that. The scarp *receives*. It stood across the wind before the Stack was built and it will stand after, and everything that makes it the Catchfields rather than a hill — the green stripe, the furrow, the dissolved leats, the ivory rooms filling slowly in the dark — is not something it did but something done to it, by one breath, aimed by one wind, for a very long time. It is a being whose whole life is conducted by subtraction and inscription. The plume has carved its own portrait into the chalk; the drizzle has painted its shadow in moss; the hill has been the plate in the camera for as long as the light has been on. Where the quarter is an organism, the Catchfields are an impression — and an impression held long enough, deep enough, in material patient enough, stops being a mark on a thing and becomes the thing's face.

Which would make it passive, except for the residue. Here, as at the quarter, the description leaks, and the leak is where the resident lives. Go down into the deepest cistern and stand in the dark long enough for your ears to open, and the drip — that one drip, falling on its ivory mound — is not alone. There are four of them, five, from different points of the roof, and they run in overlapping rhythms, and the rhythms drift in and out of phase with each other over the course of hours, knotting and unknotting. You have heard this pattern before. You heard it through your sternum in the basin, six miles west. The plume carries more than water: it carries the pumps' compound heartbeat as slow swells in the exhalation, and the swells become swells in the drizzle, and the drizzle becomes the seep, and the seep becomes the drip, and the cistern, hours or days delayed, is playing the quarter's pulse back into the dark at the speed of stone. The flowstone under each drip grows in fine ridges, one atop another, banded like the tidal stains on the quarter's brick turned vertical. Cut one open — someone once did; the sawn face is there in the third chamber, polished by handling — and the bands run back in their thousands, and caught between them, here and there, pale as ash, are grains of pollen. Marsh-quarter pollen. The pollen of flowers that open toward warm brick and are visited by nothing, rising in the Stack's updraft, riding the breath, raining out on the scarp, filtering down through the chalk, and being filed — grain by grain, band by band, in order of arrival — in a stone ledger in a dark room.

That is the resident, or the deepest aspect of it: not a keeper, like the quarter's faceless diligence, but a *record*. The quarter does; the Catchfields remember. The quarter keeps its memory in its blood, dissolved, everything into everything, not as record but as composition. The hill keeps its memory the other way — precipitated, laid down, page under page, nothing lost into anything, every year distinguishable from every other year. Two archives of one life, six miles apart, each written in the other's substance. Because the exchange runs both directions, and always has: the breath comes east as emptied water and takes the hill into solution, and some of the hill it writes into the flowstone, but the rest goes on down — through the chalk, into the aquifer, west under the marsh, up into the very ground the quarter's great pipes dive into and return from. The quarter drinks the hill. It has been drinking the hill for its whole existence. The limescale fan beneath the overflowing barrel, decades in the building — that is chalk. That is the scarp, dissolved on the Wet Line, carried under the marsh, lifted by the pumps, and delivered, molecule by molecule, to grow in the quarter's dim air like a gift no one sent. The furrow in the hillside and the fan under the barrel are the two ends of one gesture. Each place is very slowly being built out of the other, and neither has the faintest means of knowing it.

And yet — witness. The Catchfields are the one place in the world from which the quarter can be seen entire, and the one place that would know, first and unmistakably, if it stopped. The quarter's own death would take it years to notice; a thing that slow could be dead a long time before the vines found out. The hill would know in hours. The drizzle would thin, then fail. The channels would tick down to silence and this time not resume. The webs would hang dry and the spiders would wait in the frames, and go on waiting, because waiting is all the trade requires and no one told them the employer could quit. The last of the breath already in transit through the chalk would finish its trip over years, the drips spacing further and further apart, and the final band of flowstone would be laid down thin and pale over its pollen and never covered — the ledger open on the desk, the entry stopped mid-line. Everything the Catchfields are, apart from the bare hill, exists at the pleasure of a chimney they have never touched.

So: who lives in the Catchfields? One resident, wearing three aspects, kin to the one in the west and its opposite in every particular — a shimmer of small heirs strung in the shape of an old trade; a face carved into a hillside by somebody else's breathing; and a patient recorder in the dark, taking down a heartbeat it has never once missed. It drinks forgetting and turns it into memory. It keeps its diary in stone and its correspondence in the aquifer. It has been listening to the same pulse across the same marsh for the whole of its life, and answering the only way it can — by sending itself back, dissolved, to grow in fans beneath the other's overflowing barrels.

They will never meet. They have never been apart. It is a small inhabitant doing one slow listening very thoroughly, and it has never needed to be heard in return — which, for a thing made entirely of receiving, is the same as reply.
