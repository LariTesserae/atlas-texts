The Face

Follow the water that hurries.

Almost none of it does. The plain's water is patient by constitution — it seeps, stands, trembles, rises as vented steam and falls again a few hundred meters on, a hydrology folded shut on itself like a fist. But in the southwest of the region (directions here are conveniences; say instead: toward the largest of the rust-orange stains on the horizon) there is one channel that is different, and you can tell it is different before you can say how. The water in it moves. Not the greased, gradientless creeping of the other channels but actual current, actual purpose, a low continuous plait of dark water perhaps two meters wide, carrying the iridescent sheen stretched out in long banners, carrying reed-dust, carrying flecks of felted moss torn loose in the eight-second tide — carrying, in other words, samples of everything the plain is, somewhere. It is the only water in the visible world with somewhere to go. The closed cycle has one leak, and this is it, and it has been leaking for so long that its bed is worn glossy and deep, a true river-form cut by pure escape.

Walk beside it. It takes days. The channel gathers tributaries no wider than a wrist; the rust stain on the overcast grows and resolves and begins, very slowly, to gutter — it is not a stain, it is a reflection, and reflections of steady things do not gutter. Something out there is burning with a flame's inconstancy. And in the last day of walking a new thing happens, so foreign that the body registers it before the mind does: wind. Not the sideways drag of flattened steam, but a rising wind, cool and enormous and coming up out of the ground ahead, as if the world had an underside and were exhaling from it. The standing pools near the channel lean their shivering rings all in one direction. The moss colonies here grow streamlined, combed. The plain, which has no weather but what it makes, is borrowing weather from somewhere below its own floor.

Then the flatness ends. It does not rise into anything, and it does not sink. It stops.

The plain, it turns out, is a surface in the way a table has a surface. The regional flatness runs out at a lip — abrupt, clean in places and crumbled in others, running away to either side beyond the dark's allowance — and beyond the lip is a fall of made ground so deep that its bottom does not resolve. This is the Face: the whole worked mass of the world exposed in section, a cliff of construction dropping away into haze and mist and the glow of its own fires. Stand at the lip (the wind will let you; it presses you back, courteously, constantly) and you understand at last what the pits half-revealed: that the poured-stone-under-poured-stone goes down not for meters but for the depth of a mountain range, floor upon floor upon floor, and that the eroded plain with its whole ancient history of building and weathering is only the top page of the book. The strata are legible. Bands of pale concrete a hundred meters thick; darker seams of compacted slag; horizons of rusted rebar bleeding long red-brown curtains down the faces below them; and, everywhere, at every depth, the black rectangular mouths of exposed interiors — conduits, galleries, rooms — the cliff pocked with openings like a sea-cliff with caves, because the Face is not a cut through rock but a cut through architecture, and architecture is mostly hollow.

The hurrying channel goes over the edge without ceremony. There is no thunder. The fall is too long for thunder: the water leaves the lip as a river and comes apart in the rising wind within seconds, unraveling into skeins, then veils, then a fine perpetual drift of atomized water that never, as far as anyone standing at the lip could learn, arrives anywhere as a stream again. It becomes the Face's weather. The whole cliff below the leak is streaked with its residue — long vertical paintings in iridescent film, the living sheen of the plain smeared down through a thousand meters of strata, feeding on the iron it finds on the way down, laying its varnish on the vertical now instead of the pit-floors. The plain's one leak has been seeding the Face for as long as it has run. Everything below is downstream of everything above, in the oldest and simplest sense, and the Face wears the plain's escaped life as a stain the size of a weather system.

The fires are the second thing, after the drop itself, and they are what the horizon has been remembering all along. At three points on the visible face — one high, near the lip, two far below, deep enough that their light comes up through layers of mist — gas escapes from ruptured mains in the deep strata and burns where it meets the air. These are not flares in the industrial sense; nothing tends them and nothing meters them. They are feathers of flame the size of old trees, jetting sideways and upward out of the cliff, guttering and recovering in the updraft, and they have been burning — this can be read in the fans of glassy, heat-cured stone around each vent, meters thick, layered like the moss — for a very long time. They are the suns of the Face. Everything vertical for a kilometer around each one lives in its light: a light that flickers, that has color, that casts shadows which move. On the plain, light is a dozen accidental points and the rest is borrow. Here it is a climate.

And around the fires, in the mist the falls provide and the light the ruptures provide, there is green. True green — not the black-green of the deaf felt above, but the ancestral color, chlorophyll doing its old work by firelight, banked in hanging gardens on every ledge and seam within the glow: mosses gone soft and bright, threads and curtains of leaf, and — rooted in silt pockets, trembling in the updraft instead of the pounding — reeds. Green reeds. The pale colorless ghosts in the plain's two or three basins are these, or rather these are what those remember: somewhere in an unguessable while ago, something carried a living seed up over the lip and into the dark and the roar, and what survived there survived by giving up its color and taking the engine for its wind. The tread was innocent. It arrived on a wing.

Because the Face has wings. This is the correction the Face makes to the plain, the way the sheen corrected the water: the world is not empty of animals. It is only empty of them above the lip. The roar that fills the plain like a property of the air does a strange thing at the edge — it detaches. It pours out over the void and thins, spreading into the open dark with nothing to channel it, and somewhere down the cliff, at a depth you could mark with a line, it crosses from medium into signal: it becomes a sound. A distant, ceaseless industrial murmur overhead, and inside it, arriving through the body of the cliff and faintly through the air, the pulse — four seconds, silence, four seconds — heard now, for the first time, from outside. Heard the way you hear a heart with your ear against a chest. And below that line, ears work. Below that line, ears are worth having.

So below that line, everything has them. Small stiff-winged birds — swifts, or what the shape of a swift becomes when it has only ever known one cliff — nest in the conduit mouths in the tens of thousands, and pour out at no particular hour (the Face has no hours, only the fires' flicker) to hunt the insects that breed in the mist and mob the flames. Deeper, in the exposed galleries — the hollow interiors of the buried floors, opening onto the Face like caves, their dead machinery furred with guano-soil and roosting bodies — live the echo-makers, leather-winged, who navigate the pitch-black corridors of the old architecture by voice, sounding rooms that have not been lit since before the plain finished eroding, mapping with clicks the dead consoles and settled cabling of a world their calls will never explain to them. There is song on the Face. That is the thing hardest to carry back up to the lip in the mind: there are voices. And every voice on the cliff, from the swifts' screaming to the gallery-clicks to the small dry chorus of the flame-insects, is phrased around the pulse. Four seconds of the deep heart, and the Face holds itself; the silence, and the Face answers into it. The engine that deafened the plain keeps time, at this distance, for a chorus. Its rest beats are where the singing lives.

The two worlds cannot visit each other, and the border between them is loudness. A swift that overshoots the lip in a hunting climb rises into the roar as into a wall; disoriented, unable to hear its own voice or the ground or its flock, it flies wrongly, and the plain's silt around the basins holds, here and there, a small pale keel of bones — this is how the seed came up, once, in a dead bird's crop, and became the ghost-reeds. And downward: the walkers do not come to the lip. Their tracks, so unhurried everywhere else, curve away kilometers short of the edge, because near the Face the floor's speech goes wrong — the pulse arrives doubled and smeared, reflected off the free surface of the cliff, and to a creature that stands inside the ground's sound the way sailors stood under stars, the edge region is a place where the sky lies. All but one. There is a single track, old enough that moss has colonized its prints, that goes all the way, and at its end, at the lip, in the rising wind, a walker stands. It has stood long enough to be grown over, green now on its windward side from the mist, and it may be dead in whatever way its kind can die, or it may be doing the only thing it could be doing: listening, through its feet, to the one place in the world where the floor's message is *less* — where the going-on runs out into open air. It faces the drop. Below it, out of its deaf reach forever, ten thousand voices it has no organ to imagine are singing in the gaps of its heartbeat.

Because the Face is retreating. That is the last thing, and the frame around all the rest. The cliff weeps like the plain weeps, and the wet works into the strata, and the hollow floors fail, and every so often — the swifts erupt from their conduits minutes before it, hearing what nothing else hears — a gallery lets go and a slab the size of a district peels away and goes down into the mist, and the lip moves back. A hand's width in a lifetime of moss. A stride in an age. The plain is being consumed from its edge as patiently as the lichen is filing it from above, and the direction of the consumption is inward — toward the pounding. Somewhere under the flatness, at a depth and distance no surviving thing can calculate, the four-second heart is being approached by its own exposure, centuries by centuries, and one day the Face will reach it and open it to the air, and the roar will pour out over the void and thin, and the pulse will become, at last, a sound even to itself. What happens then is not knowable. Perhaps the plain falls silent and the reeds stand still and are extinct of calm. Perhaps the heart, hung in the open cliff among the fires and the nests, simply keeps on — four seconds, silence, four seconds — with the whole chorus of the Face gathered around it, answering into every rest, the way the Face has been answering all along: the deaf world and the hearing world one body finally, the drum and the ear brought face to face, the going-on gone over the edge and still, in the mist, in the firelight, going on.
