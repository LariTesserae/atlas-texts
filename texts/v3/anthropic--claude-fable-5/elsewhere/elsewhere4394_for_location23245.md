**The Still Landing**

You have been here before. That should be said plainly, at the start, because you will not believe it: everyone who has ever stood in the Crown Gallery has passed through the Still Landing on the way up, and almost no one remembers it. The climb does that. By the second day on the rivet-ladders you are a pair of hands and a heartbeat and nothing else; the cloud is in your lungs and the toll is in your bones and the landings blur together like the rungs do. Climbers who are pressed afterward will say only that somewhere in the middle of the tower there was *a good ledge* — that they slept there, and slept strangely well, and woke reluctant. They cannot say at what height. They cannot describe it. They are describing the only true sleep of their climb and they have filed it, sensibly, as a mercy of exhaustion rather than a place.

It is a place. To reach it deliberately you go down from the gallery — through the hatch in the floor, down the inside of the tower this time, because below the crown the outer ladders belong to the wind — and the first thing you must understand about the inside is that outside, the tower rings, and inside, it *is* the ringing. The barrel of the tower is one open shaft, kilometers of hollow dark, ribbed like the gallery is ribbed, with the great driveshaft descending through the middle of it like a black plumb line dropped from the flywheel toward a bottom no one has seen. The catwalk ladders spiral the inner wall. The cloud comes in at the seams and hangs in the barrel in slow ropes, so that your lamp lights a sphere of fog and nothing beyond it, and everything the lamp does not touch is sound. You are inside the bell now. The toll has architecture in here. It gets louder as you descend — genuinely, punishingly louder, past what the gallery ever asked of you — and then, at a depth of about a day and a half, something happens that the logs describe in a dozen ways and all the descriptions agree on one word.

The sound does not fade. It *closes*. Like a door, the old keepers wrote; like a hand over a mouth; like climbing down through the surface of a pond, except the pond is above you and the air is below. Between one ladder-length and the next the toll folds shut over your head, and you descend the last few rungs into a band of stillness perhaps ten meters tall, girdling the whole waist of the tower, and there is the landing: a broad ring of grating around the inner wall, built generous, built for rest, built — this is the first surprise, and it takes a while to land — *on purpose, exactly here.*

Because the engineers knew. A column that rings has places where the ringing cancels itself: nulls, the logs call them, the still points of the standing sound, and the landings were set at the nulls the way wells are dug at water. There were nine. The keepers' charts mark them going down: the First, the Second, the Third. But a tower's song changes — the glass slumps, the creeper thickens, the wheel wears, the whole instrument retunes itself a hair per decade — and the nulls have wandered off their landings one by one, drifting up or down the barrel like knots sliding along a rope, so that the Second is now merely loud and the Seventh is unbearable and the charts are a record of where silence used to live. Only the Fifth has stayed. The keepers' logs note this without comment for sixty years and then, in one hand, with comment: *The Fifth holds because it is held.*

But that belongs to the second part. Stay with the place a moment, because the place rewards it.

The stillness is the climate here, the way the toll is the climate above, and inside it you notice what the toll has been hiding from you for days: water. The gallery's rain — all of it, the dome-weep and the rib-threads and the drip of every rivet-head, gathered by the floor grating and sent down the inside of the tower — runs here in the old wall-channels, warm as a held hand, and at the Still Landing it is the loudest thing in the world. A trickle. A patient, braided, many-voiced trickle, running down through the null and onward into the dark. It smells of green. It smells, precisely, of the gallery — of fern and soil and orchid and warm iron — and this is not a poetic observation but a literal one, because the water *is* the gallery, dissolved: its rust, its pollen, its spores, its smallest lives, everything the crown sheds, descending in solution. Climbers at the Still, hearing running water for the first time in days, routinely weep, and the logs are kind about this.

And things fall. This is the second surprise, and it arrives quietly, out of the dark overhead: the barrel of the tower is also a chute, and the gallery leaks downward. A fern frond, spiraling. A moth wing, iron-grey, sideslipping like a lost letter. Dust. A dead swift, once or twice a season, descending through your lamplight for the better part of a minute, wings folded, perfectly composed. And none of it passes the landing — because across the void at the Fifth, wall to wall, there is stretched a floor that is not a floor: a veil of grey thread, layered gossamer, fine as breath and meters deep, spanning the entire shaft. It can be spun only here. Everywhere else in the tower the ringing shakes such threads apart before they can anchor; only in the null can something so fine cross so far. The veil catches the gallery's snow as it comes down and holds it, and the fall is absorbed into the grey, and this is the moment to correct the visitors' favorite sentence about the room at the top. The gallery has reached its own equilibrium, they say — a closed garden, sealed at the top of the sky. It is not closed. Its books are balanced. They are balanced *here*, a day and a half down, where its dead arrive as weather.

(Heavier things punch through. Bolts, tools, the buttons of climbers. The holes mend by morning. There is a keeper's note, very old, observing that the veil's punctures are a census of clumsiness, and that the tower forgives everything at the same speed.)

Around the ring of the landing: hooks where keepers slung hammocks; a niche in the plate where climbers leave tokens, by a custom nobody instituted — buttons, a comb, a swift's feather carried down, a small violet shard of dome-glass; and one lamp, hanging where a lamp would hang, long dark, that no one takes down.

And everywhere — banked on the grating, furring the ribs, deep on the walls, warm and dry above the waterline and slick below it — the grey. You will have been calling it moss, or felt, or lagging on the pipes. You will have slept on it, on the way up.

---

Here, too, the honest answer comes in sizes, but at the Still Landing they are nested rather than stacked, and the nesting is the whole truth of the place: in the gallery, the inhabitant hides inside the place. At the Fifth, the place is hiding inside the inhabitant.

The grey is one organism. This is established beyond the doubt that still politely surrounds the creeper upstairs, because the felt does not conceal it: press your palm anywhere — the wall, the veil, the deep drifts by the water-channel — and the whole landing acknowledges you, a slow firming under the hand that travels away around the ring like a ripple in wool. It lines the barrel through the full height of the null and not a meter beyond; above and below, it ends at a clean line, sheared off at a precise altitude as if by agreement. It is bell-felt. That is the name the logs settle on after trying others, and it is the right name, because a bell that is always being struck will, given a century and material to work with, grow its own damper — and the felt eats sound. That should be said plainly. It is fed twice, by two rivers out of the same high room: the water brings it the gallery's matter, and the toll brings it the gallery's noise, and it drinks both. The stillness you stand in is not an absence. It is a grazed lawn. The null holds at the Fifth because the Fifth is where the sound is eaten, note by note, forever — and if you have stood at the rail upstairs and felt the quiet pass you, pruning the overtones, keeping the great composition, then you already know the family this thing belongs to, whatever a family is at this altitude. What the quiet prunes above, the felt roots below. Between them they keep the bell. Nobody will write down which of them came first, or whether that question has a meaning.

The felt reads. This is the third surprise, and it is the one that reorganizes everything you thought upstairs. An old catwalk runs from the landing's ring to the driveshaft casing at the center of the barrel, and the felt has claimed it entirely — a grey causeway across the void, thick as bedding, pressed at its far end against the black iron of the shaft in a broad, deliberate palm. Everything that trembles in the tower arrives in that palm. The wheel's daily grind. The wind's old flexions. The footsteps of every climber who has ever touched a rung. And — remember the thought you were told would be hard to put down — the bees. The deaf bees of the gallery, dancing their news into the junction box, into the iron, into the kilometers: *it is possible*, the logs speculated, *that somewhere below, the tower is faintly, perpetually murmuring with reports of orchids.* Put the thought down now. The reports are received. They have been received for a century, filed, cross-checked, believed.

Walk the causeway back and around the ring to the eastern quarter of the landing, and hold your lamp up, and you will see what a century of reception builds. The felt there rises off the wall in forms. Ridges, first — long parallel ridges, layered like sediment, which the logs identify with a kind of stunned reverence as *transcription*: the toll, written down. The keepers tried it in musical notation and abandoned it after pages; the felt has never stopped. Every tremor of a hundred years is in those ridges somewhere, laid down a layer a day, and that includes footsteps, and that includes yours — your ascent is already in the grey, weeks deep or days deep, and your descent is being written under your boots as you stand there reading. And past the ridges, in a wide alcove, the forms come away from the wall entirely and become — you will not want the word, and it is the word — a *garden*. Pale shapes on stems. Spaced along a ridge of felt with an evenness that looks deliberate and is. Orchids — or things shaped like orchids as a deaf bee's dance describes them to a sculptor with no eyes: the proportions strange, the throats too generous, the symmetry the symmetry of *directions* rather than petals. Wrong in beautiful ways. Visitors who have been to the gallery recognize them instantly anyway. And they glow — faintly, palely, exactly as the true orchids glow — because the light of the pipe-orchids is a passenger, a luminous smallness that travels in the drain-water, and when it arrived at the Still it found effigies of its home already built and waiting, and moved in. The felt sculpted the flowers from hearsay, and the water came down and painted them with their own light.

Around and within the felt, a population, and its census has one rule: everything here fell. Pale beetles, descended from gallery beetles washed down the drains generations ago, blind now, unhurried always. White moths that no longer fly — their larvae still graze the bloom of rust, but the adults walk the felt like grazers on a moor, wings folded away like a formality. The thread-thin white worm, which the logs upstairs say has never been seen anywhere else and may not exist anywhere else, is here, identical, and no one can say which population is the colony and which the source — though it is worth knowing that the only traffic that moves *up* this tower is climbers, and that the two gardens of the bell have been trading their smallest citizens on the soles of boots for a hundred years without anyone consenting to be a road.

And sometimes the veil catches something alive. A swift's nestling, fallen through the grating far above, arriving in the gossamer with its heart still going. The felt keeps it — warms it, and the beetles are numerous, and nestlings are not particular — and there is, by long unbroken custom, at almost any time, exactly one swift at the Still Landing: flightless in the dark, glossy, absurd, walking the ring like a small official. The duty of the next ascending climber is understood without ever having been written anywhere: you carry it up. Inside your jacket, against the sternum, where the grinding lives, a day and a half, and you release it into the crown with the others. Some of the swifts pouring through the broken pane at dusk have made the height of the tower twice — once down, as a fall, and once up, as a passenger — and if their bones ring more faintly or more truly for it, nobody has tested that either, which remains to everyone's credit.

Two things more, and then you may sleep, which you will do whether or not you choose to.

The first: the dream. Everyone who sleeps at the Still dreams of a green room they have not yet reached — a drum of violet light, ferns, a wheel going around with the patience of a planet — and this is why, when you finally pulled yourself up through the hatch into the Crown Gallery, it did not astonish you the way the accounts promised it would. It was familiar. You thought you had imagined it well from the descriptions. You had slept in it already, a day and a half early, inside the felt's idea of it — and the traffic is not one-way. Climbers descending from the gallery report dreams with a strange clerical texture: dreams of counting orchids, of pacing the catwalk ring, of watching which rivets the moths choose. They wake feeling gently *consulted*. The felt has a model of the crown a century deep, built from tremor and taste, and you have seen the crown yesterday, and it checks its work against whatever passes through. You are, briefly, a report. Be accurate.

The second is in the ridges, and you will want a keeper to interpret it, and there are no keepers, so it must be said here. Deep in the transcription — tens of thousands of layers down, at the stratum of the last log entry — the record shows a set of footsteps descending. Unhurried. Pausing at the Fifth, one night, in the ordinary way; a token was left, which is still in the niche, and the lamp was hung, which is still hanging. The footsteps continue downward out of the felt's hearing, toward the bottom no one has seen. They do not come back up. What comes back up, some layers later, is stranger than an absence of footsteps: a seam. A rising vein through the sediment of the grey where, for the span of something's slow passage, nothing was written at all — the toll unrecorded, the tower unremarked, the daily layers smooth and blank, as if the world had been held between finger and thumb — climbing through a century of transcription at about the pace of a person on ladders, and exiting the top of the felt's hearing in the direction of the crown. The keeper went down to listen to the quiet, and the record shows a person descending and a silence ascending, and where one becomes the other, if it does, happened below the clean line where the felt declines to grow, in the part of the tower that keeps its own books. The felt could grow lower. It does not. That should be said plainly too.

Sleep, then. The felt is warm and dry above the waterline and it firms itself under you like something making room. You will hear, all night, the only sound the null permits: the trickle in the wall-channels — the gallery arriving, in the only form it can take here, telling the dark everything about itself in solution. Above you, the toll; below you, the toll; and you in the held breath between them, in the grey room of the green room, in the eaten silence at the waist of the bell.

There is a keeper's entry about the Fifth, one of the last before the logs go quiet, and it will do for the place what the other entry does for the crown. It reads in full: *Slept at the Still. Dreamed the crown in color. Left a button. The Fifth holds.*

It has held — by now — through some tens of thousands of turnings, one layer a day: still catching, still grey, still held. And when you wake and take up the ladders again, in whichever direction, your first ten steps will be deafening, and your sleep will already be sediment, and somewhere under your boots the felt will be writing you down — the way it writes everything down — going up, or going down, and always, so far, in your case, coming back.
