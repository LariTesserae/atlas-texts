**The Drown at Wether's Foot** — and this one *had* a name, a real one, spoken across generations of kitchen doorways; but the water closed over the name along with everything else, and what's written here is only what the place would be called now, by someone arriving late and looking down.

Two vertical miles below the cleft, at the base of the mountain too young to have a gentle side, there is a lake that should not be there. The valley it fills is old — a broad, patient, low-shouldered valley, the kind rivers spend a million years making comfortable — and the lake is not. The lake is an event still happening.

It happened, and is happening, for two reasons, both of them the mountain's fault. The first is simple: when Wether's Tooth shouldered its way up in that last eye-blink of deep time, its rubble and its rising pinched the valley's river shut. The second is stranger and slower, and takes a surveyor's patience to believe: the mountain is *heavy*. A peak raised that fast presses down on the crust around it the way a sleeper presses down on a mattress, and the old valley lies exactly in the hollow of that pressing. The land at Wether's Foot is sinking — not washing away, not eroding: sinking, whole, a finger-width a generation, the same finger-width by which the seam of sky above the cleft is widening. The two motions are one motion. The mountain arrives upward at the summit and downward at the foot, and the lake is the downward half made visible: a spirit level laid against the mountain's ambition, filling as the land beneath it kneels.

So the valley did not flood the way valleys usually flood, in a spring of bad rain. It went under the way a ship goes under when the cargo shifts — slowly, in perfect weather, over lifetimes. The people who lived there had time to argue about it. The trees did not; trees cannot retreat uphill. There is a forest standing in the lake, whole, upright, grey as the salamanders on the walls two miles above it, drowned where it stood.

And here is the strangeness, the thing a visitor would take a while to admit, and the exact inverse of the strangeness up in the cleft: **the lake keeps.** The water is snowmelt-cold and stained dark with the tannin of the drowned wood, and in its deep places there is almost no air dissolved at all — nothing down there can rot, because nothing down there can breathe enough to do the rotting. The cleft is a wound being healed, its rawness upholstered over by the living. The drown is the opposite operation: a finished thing being kept, its softness sealed away from everything that would finish it further. Time in the cleft runs fast — young rock, brief generations, walls thickening by the decade. Time in the drown was stopped, and is held stopped, under four fathoms of cold dark tea.

You can see this, once a year, if the winter is hard. When the ice comes in clear and black before the snow, and you walk out on it — no one does; but if anyone came — the lake becomes a window laid flat over the valley that was. Down through the ice and the still water: the lane. The field walls, running in their old straight confidence toward nothing. The ford — nine flat stones set stride-apart across the buried river's gravel, placed there by hands so long ago that the placing was already ancient when the mountain began to rise. The fish weir, a low arc of stacked stone with a single gap in it. Doorways. Threshold stones, one with a small cup-mark ground into it, worn by generations of some habit no one alive can name — a knife sharpened there daily, a coin turned for luck, a thumb pressed in passing. The whole map of a life, legible, roofless, tenanted only by fish.

It is quiet at the drown. Not silent — the standing dead forest ticks all day as its dry crowns heat and cool, and once or twice a season a tree that has stood drowned for two centuries finally lets go and comes down, and the fall rings across the whole water like the plate-fall rings down the cleft, and everything with wings goes up at once and settles. In winter the ice booms and mutters. And on still days there is the tone — the low note, pitched like a struck jar, that comes up through the cleft's floor at long intervals as the young rock adjusts its grip. The lake hears it too. It arrives through the bedrock, and the whole basin of water rings faintly with it, a bell too large to be heard properly, only felt — the fish feel it, and hang still while it passes. The mountain, audibly continuing to arrive, announces itself to both of its children at once: the wound at the crown and the keeping at the foot are the two rooms of one house, and the settling of the house is heard in both.

But the loudest thing at the drown is the dusk.

Every evening in the warm months, the lake exhales. The drowned valley is the richest insect water for a hundred miles — warm shallows over old meadow soil, the dark deep sending up its slow bubbles of marsh-breath, the drowned grass still feeding things — and at dusk the hatch comes off the surface in columns, millions of bodies assembling out of the water into a soft, high, continuous roar, a smoke standing up from the lake into the last light. And out of the pale seam two miles overhead, a second smoke comes *down* — the bats of the cleft, pouring off the mountain's dark face, dropping the whole height of the young peak to reach the only air thick enough to feed them. The two smokes meet over the water. This is the drown's most reliable weather. The people of the ford, when there were people, would have set their evenings by it, the way lowland villages set theirs by church bells: the mountain breathing out, the water breathing up, the exchange made in the half-dark.

For the exchange is the whole arrangement, and it is worth saying plainly: the cleft cannot exist without the drown. Everything alive in that breathing crack near the summit runs, at the far end of its supply line, on the drowned meadow's patience — the old valley's soil, sunk and sealed, paying itself out as midges, carried up two vertical miles against gravity in the stomachs of bats. The only thing on the whole mountain that travels uphill is carried that way. And the traffic runs downhill too, quieter: the snowmelt that seeps into the cleft, beads its walls, fills its fierce little basins, gets licked and grazed and breathed over by the colony — that water leaves through the floor cracks, descends through the young rock's fissures, and emerges weeks later as a spring on the lake's near shore, running a degree warm and tasting, if anything can be said to taste of it, faintly of the dark. The fish gather at that spring in winter. The salamanders' wall-lickings arrive, eventually, in the char's water. Lake feeds mountain; mountain waters lake. Neither knows.

What lives at the Drown at Wether's Foot is, first, a list — and then, underneath the list, one other thing, which takes longer to see, because it is the thing everyone assumes is dead.

**The char** are the drown's counterparts to the swifts, and they are paying for the same interrupted bargain. They were sea-run fish once — silver travelers, going down the river to the ocean and returning huge — and the mountain's rising closed the road behind them. The trapped generations dwarfed, darkened, made their peace. But the body keeps the old arithmetic: every autumn, a restlessness comes over them, the exact restlessness that screams the swifts up and down the cleft twice a year, and the char gather in the deep and then run — upstream, along the buried channel, over the drowned gravels their ancestors spawned in when the gravels belonged to a river and the river belonged to the sea. They pass the weir to do it. The weir still works. Its builders are two centuries drowned and its single gap still funnels the run exactly as it was stacked to do, and the fish still thread it one by one — a made thing, tenanted entirely by intention, holding its territory in the dark water the way the blade holds its handspan in the cleft. The people built the weir to take the run. The run has outlived the taking and keeps the appointment anyway.

**The hatch** is the drown's currency and its most numerous resident, if a resident can be a body that assembles itself fresh every evening out of the water and is eaten by nightfall. The midges and the pale mayflies spend their real lives below, in the drowned meadow's soft dark, and surface only to marry and be taken. Some of the columns, caught by the evening wind off the water, are carried up the mountain's face on the rising warm air — all the way up, some few of them, to the grey seam near the summit, where the swifts, who cannot leave, hang in the twilight column and take what the wind delivers. The bats descend to fetch the lake. The swifts wait, and the lake is brought to them. Either way, the drown feeds the cleft; only the etiquette differs.

**The standing drowned** — the grey forest — are the lake's architecture and its slowest event. Two hundred trees, upright in open water, preserved below the line and weathering silver above it, drilled through their dead crowns with woodpecker holes that now house whatever asks. The herons nest in the tops, a rookery of great untidy platforms, and the herons themselves are the drown's salamanders: flint-grey, ancient-seeming, motionless for hours in territories measured in a few strides of shallow, time-sharing the good water on schedules so exact that two birds may fish the same drowned lane for twenty years and never meet. When a tree finally goes down, the herons rise all together, once, and resettle one platform over, and the lake's map redraws itself around the absence.

**The reeds** are doing at the margins what the mineral films are doing to the cleft's walls: finishing. They advance on the drowning shore a hand's width a year, binding the shallows, laying down peat, upholstering the lake's raw edge with softness at a pace measurable only in decades. In a thousand years the sharp young shoreline will be a broad kind marsh, and the kindness will be made of bodies. Both places are being lined from the inside by their smallest residents. Neither project will finish in any participant's lifetime. Neither appears to mind.

**The human residue** must be counted, because the drown counts it, and because it is the residue of the same hands whose one surviving act sits two miles up in the breathing dark. On the old riverbank, above the waterline still, there is a knapping floor — a broad drift of glassy flakes, the waste of a thousand blades struck over a thousand years, ankle-deep, crunching underfoot like the cricket-shale crunches on the cleft's floor. No one who worked at the ford ever stood on bare ground either; they walked on the debris of their own former makings, and the drift outlasts them all. And cut into the bank beside it, a red seam: the ochre pit, dug and re-dug for pigment across generations, still raw, still bleeding — after hard rain the pit weeps a plume of rust into the lake, a red vein spreading over the drowned lane, and the water remembers in color for a day. This is the earth of the handprint. Whoever climbed Wether's Tooth carried a handful of this bank two miles up into the wound of the thing that was drowning their home, and pressed their hand to the wall of it, and stood a fresh-struck blade upright in a crack, and came down or didn't. Grief, or gift, or accusation, or simply a name — the only name of the ford's people that survives, forty paces long, fingers spread, slightly too large — no one can say. The colony keeps it regardless. The pit keeps bleeding regardless. The same red earth signs the mountain at the crown and the water at the foot.

And then the other thing — the one underneath the list, the one everyone assumes is dead.

**The river is still there.** Not was — is. Stand on the black ice in the right winter and look long enough and you can find it: a seam of subtly different water threading the lake along the old channel, past the ford stones, through the weir's gap, colder than everything around it, still moving. The lake did not kill the river; the lake is only lying on top of it. The current still runs its buried course from inlet to dam wall, patient, present entirely as intention — and intention, as the cleft already knows, holds territory. The char hold in that cold thread all summer. The run follows it home each autumn like a road under snow. And at the far end, where the mountain's rubble stopped everything, the river is working: filtering through the dam grain by grain, softening it, seeping out the downstream side as a trickle that has not once stopped in two centuries. The drown's truest resident has no shore of its own and only one project — leaving — and it is a project of the same patience as the cleft's project of smoothing, conducted on the same schedule, against the same mountain. Someday, on the mountain's calendar, the dam will yield, the lake will pour out, and the kept valley will come back into the air — the lane, the walls, the weir, the nine stones of the ford, preserved, delivered, tenanted again by weather. The river does not appear to be in a hurry. It has the one advantage nothing else on the mountain has: it was here first, and it remembers the way out.

Once a year the drown takes its own attendance. In autumn, when the char gather at the weir and begin to thread the gap one by one, the water over the old ford shivers with bodies for three nights running — the run passing over the stepping stones, upstream along the buried road, each fish answering the same call the swifts scream against and cannot obey. And at dusk on those nights, as every night, the smoke comes down out of the seam two miles up and spreads low over the water, and the lake sends its drowned meadow up to meet it as a column of wings, and the two smokes turn through each other in the failing light — the wound's hunger and the keeping's abundance, the mountain's two children trading breath —

and the roll is called in both rooms of the house at once, and everyone, including the weir, including the red pit, including the river under the lake, still leaving, answers present.
