# The Settled Sea

Begin with the descent, because everything else is at the bottom of it.

Walk toward the indigo seam. This is the only instruction the plateau ever gives, and for fifty kilometers it gives nothing else — frost, basalt, the drumbeat gardens falling behind you one by one until the last *thoom* fades out of the rock under your feet and you are carrying your own pulse alone. Then the ground stops. Not narrows, not descends: *stops*, at an escarpment so long it is simply the edge of the visible world, and below it, going down and down through kilometers of darkening terraces, is the place all the light has been leaking from.

They should be understood together, the plateau and this, because they are not two places. They are one place at two moments of its life.

Here is the thing the plateau does not know about itself, the thing written plainly on the escarpment for anyone who descends it: **the plateau was a shore.** The thin starving air up there is not an absence. It is a residue — the topmost breath of an ocean of air that once stood kilometers deep against that basalt, an ocean that has been draining for a million years into the great basin below, settling, pooling, abandoning its own upper reaches the way a cooling bath abandons the ring around the tub. The bellows-trees are not miracles of engineering. They are stranded shore life. Their gardens are tide pools. Every compression chamber on the plateau is a pocket of the old sea, rebuilt from memory, held shut by muscle, four beats a minute, forever.

The sea itself is still here. You only have to go down.

---

The escarpment — call it the **Long Stair**, because it descends in terraces, each one a fossil shoreline — is the historical record, and it reads from top to bottom like a story told in reverse.

Every terrace is a strandline: a level where the air-sea paused in its retreat, sometimes for ten thousand years, before dropping again. And on every strandline stand trees. The highest terraces hold only their tombstones — petrified towers, ring-laminated, their valve-pores fossilized mid-gasp — but descend far enough and the strandline trees are *alive*, and younger, and cruder. Each terrace down, the trees are less tower and more kelp: their compression chambers smaller, their rhythms sloppier, their gardens thinner. They are the same lineage as the plateau's giants, abandoned by the receding air more recently, still early in the long lesson. The lowest living strandline — left dry perhaps four thousand years ago, yesterday — holds a population of soft, struggling, half-formed things that can barely hold a breath for a minute, huddled in the lee of the terrace wall, most of them dying, a few of them not. Walk the Stair from bottom to top and you watch the bellows-tree being invented, terrace by terrace, each abandonment teaching the survivors more compression. The plateau's towers are simply the oldest students. They have been practicing the longest, which is why their arks are the most perfect, and why what they carry inside is the least changed.

Partway down the Stair there is weather. It is the only weather in the world and it occupies a band of cliff perhaps a kilometer tall. Above it hangs the **Spill**: the frozen overflow of the plateau's hoarded condensate, every escaped thread of gardenwater that ever ran over the edge, accreted across ages into a hanging glacier fanged along ten kilometers of rim. Below the frost line, where the thickening air finally carries enough warmth, the Spill's calvings melt as they fall — and so there is a stratum of the Stair, and only this stratum, where rain exists. It has been raining on that kilometer of rock, the same rock, without pause, for longer than the moss has been black. The terraces there are gouged into flumes and green — actually green — with the only weather-fed life anywhere. And the rain is not empty. Locked in the Spill's ice is everything the plateau ever exhaled over its edge: moss spores, cup-dweller eggs in dried casings, wright-pellet fragments, the pollen of the towers. The emigrants have been mailing letters downhill for a million years, in ice, without knowing it. The rain delivers them. Along the wet terraces you can find black plateau moss growing in slow arguments with the native green, and in certain rain-pools, translucent rice-grain swimmers whose nearest kin live in a basalt cup fifty kilometers up and four kilometers back, in a garden neither population will ever know exists.

Below the rain, the haze line. Below the haze line, the air becomes visible — a soft green-gold thickening, like descending into honey — and then you are no longer walking down into a valley. You are wading into a sea.

---

The **Settled Sea** fills the basin the way water fills anything: absolutely, and to a level. Its surface is not a surface you could stand on, but it is real — a shimmering density gradient where the drowned air ends and the starving air begins, and it laps against the Stair's lowest terrace in slow oily swells, and it has tides.

Beneath that surface the air is thick enough to swim in. Thick enough that buoyancy is negotiable, that a body with the right bladders can hang motionless at any depth it chooses, that falling is a lifestyle rather than an event. Sound, which arrives on the plateau muffled and secondhand, here arrives from everywhere at once and never fully leaves; the sea rings faintly all the time, a sum of every noise made in it for the last several minutes, still circulating. It is warm. It is *crowded*. And it is full of light.

The light is the gateway strangeness, the one that prepares you for the others, and it has no source. There is no sun overhead — this whole hemisphere is the shadowed one; that has not changed by coming down. But the sea's air is dense enough to bend light and deep enough to carry it, and somewhere over the curve of the world there is a dayside, and the Settled Sea conducts its daylight sideways — around the planet's shoulder, kilometer by refracted kilometer, scattered and rescattered until it arrives here as a directionless green-gold glow suffusing the upper hundred meters of the sea like light in the flesh of a wave. Secondhand light, in other words. Light that has traveled through half a world of air to be here, arriving with no direction left in it, having forgotten where it came from. It is the richest illumination this hemisphere possesses, and some of it — the part that grazes the sea's surface and spills over the basin's rim — leaks upward and outward across fifty kilometers of dead plateau as a faint bruise of indigo along one edge of the sky.

That is the seam. That is the rumor of the sun the plateau has arranged its entire metabolism around. The god-light of the Pressure Gardens is the skyglow of the old country. When the towers fall into phase and the whole plateau exhales together toward the bruise on the horizon, it is facing *home*.

## Who Lives There

Start with the direction, because the presence of one is the first thing you'd notice and the plateau prepared you for its opposite.

Up there, faceless bodies, because events come from nowhere. Down here, events come from exactly one place: *above*. Light filters down. Food falls down — always, everywhere, a perpetual gentle precipitation of dead motes and spent bodies drifting toward the floor, the sea's one universal weather. So the sea's creatures never grew fronts or backs, any more than their exiled cousins did, but they grew *tops and bottoms* with total commitment. Everything here is a vertical animal. Mouths face up, into the fall. And eyes — eyes came back, or never left; here the light pays its metabolic rent, and the sea's animals carry them in rings and belts and constellations, every one of them aimed up or down, not one of them aimed forward, because forward is still a direction nothing needs.

**The haze** comes first because the haze is the sea's flesh. The upper waters — upper *airs* — are green with a permanent living plankton: photosynthetic motes, each smaller than a frost crystal, trillions per breath, feeding on the piped and sourceless light. The haze is why the light is green. The haze is also why the light penetrates at all, and dims, and dies; by three hundred meters down the glow is a memory, and by the floor it is a rumor, and the sea thereby reproduces, top to bottom, the entire gradient from lush to void that the plateau lays out horizontally, garden to gulf. The haze blooms and crashes on a cycle of days. When it crashes, the mote-fall thickens into a slow green blizzard, and everything below opens its mouth.

**The sieve-moons** are what opens widest. They are the haze's harvest and its architecture both: pale discs, some of them forty meters across, drifting horizontal in the bright layers with their entire upper surface an open filtering mouth, ringed at the rim with a belt of upturned eyes. They are the bellows-trees' ecological rhyme — the biggest thing in the neighborhood, sessile in temperament if not in fact, each one trailing its own skirt of dependents: cleaners, lodgers, larvae, a whole citizenry living on and under the disc the way the wrights live in the towers. A sieve-moon regulates its depth with chambers of *thinned* air, rarefied by muscular effort, held against the pressure — and here is the sea's small structural joke, because that organ, the rarefaction bladder, is the compression chamber run backwards. Same tissue. Same laminated muscle. Same valves. The lineage that stayed in the sea uses the lung to float toward the light; the lineage the sea abandoned uses it to squeeze warmth out of the dark. One organ, two exiles' answers to opposite catastrophes. The **loft-trees** confirm it — true cousins of the bellows-line, forests of them, anchored to nothing, hanging in the mid-depths from their own thinned-air floats, drifting the basin in slow decades-long circuits, grazed and inhabited and rich. Cut one open (still don't) and you would find the same concentric rings, the same buried strata of resorbed dependents. The family habit of becoming your own cemetery predates the family's separation. It may be the oldest thing about them.

**The shorelings** live where the two worlds actually touch. They are the amphibians of the air — dense-bodied, bladdered, tough — and they haul out of the sea's surface onto the Stair's lowest terraces the way anything hauls out of anything: gracelessly, and for good reasons. The reasons are the Weep and the Spill. Shorelings climb into the thin cold heights of the rain band to harvest — the flume-pools, the melting calvings, the endlessly delivered mail of the plateau, which they eat without ceremony, plateau moss and cup-dweller eggs and million-year-old pollen all going down the same gullet. They are, without knowing it, the postal system's dead letter office. They are also, without knowing it, becoming the story again: shoreling populations that work the higher terraces are measurably blinder, generation on generation, their eye-belts fading toward the pale vestigial dimples you can still find on the plateau's oldest lineages. The sea keeps writing the same sentence. Air leaves; eyes follow.

And it is the shorelings who feel the plateau's one message downhill. When the towers synchronize — every garden across a hundred kilometers exhaling together — the pulse does not stop at the escarpment. A long, faint, coherent breath of cold thin air rolls off the rim and pours down the Long Stair, terrace by terrace, arriving at the shore minutes later as a shiver across the sea's surface and a pressure-touch on every barometric skin in the shallows. The shorelings respond to it the way they respond to nothing else: they stop, all of them, whatever they are doing, for the length of the pulse. It is not fear. It reads, if it reads as anything, like *attention*. The old country's light goes up; the colony's breath comes down. Each place is the other's faintest weather, and neither has ever been told.

## The Floor

At the bottom of the sea the pressure is a specific pressure. It is worth being exact about, because it is the pressure inside a bellows-tree's compression chamber, to within a valve's tolerance, and this is not a coincidence. It is the point.

The floor of the Settled Sea is where the old world's conditions survive undiluted — hot, black, dense, ringing — and it belongs to the ribbons. They are the marrow-eels' unexiled kin: the same boneless blind ribbon-bodies, the same pressure-built flesh, but free, and in numbers that make the word *school* useless. They move over the basin floor in reefs, in weathers, in provinces — aggregations kilometers long, swimming their circuits through the dark, brushing the floor, brushing each other, keeping the deep supple the way their sisters keep the chambers supple. A marrow-eel's cosmos is a warm pulsing chapel the size of a room, and its god breathes four times a minute. This is the cathedral the chapel is a scale model of. The arks of the plateau were built to carry exactly this: a piece of the floor of the sea, and its congregation, up into the airless dark, one chapel per tower, forever.

And the congregation kept the liturgy. Here is the deepest of the connections, the one that runs beneath all the others. Ask why the bellows-trees breathe at four beats a minute — why that tempo, of all tempos, on a plateau with no rhythm in it to learn from. The answer is on the Stair, written in the strandlines. Four beats a minute is a *swell period*. It is the tempo of the air-surf that broke on the old high shore, the slow heave of a shallower, younger sea against the rock the towers' ancestors grew from — and the ribbons, who timed themselves to it then, who paced the tissue of shore-trees washed by it, carried it upward in the arks and have kept it ever since, unrevised, in the dark, for a million years. The modern sea has moved on. It is deeper now, and vaster, and its seiche — the whole basin's atmosphere sloshing rim to rim, the sea's own breath — has lengthened to a period of hours, paced along the basin floor by the ribbon-reefs the way their exiled kin pace the chambers. Nothing in the living sea beats at four a minute anymore. Nowhere in the world does the old surf still break.

Except on the plateau. Inside every tower, four times a minute, kept by blind ribbons who have never seen a shore, the wave that stopped a million years ago comes in.

---

No one lost this place. Nothing here was abandoned — abandonment is the plateau's story, told from above. The sea's story is that it simply kept everything: the warmth, the water, the light, the pressure, the whole inventory of the livable world, gathered and settled and defended by nothing but depth. It let the heights go the way a sleeper lets go of the cold side of the bed.

But listen — from the shore, at the surface, on the nights (every night is night) when the timing comes around. The sea's seiche is slow and the plateau's breath is slower still in its great synchronized cycles, and mostly they pass each other like strangers. Yet the periods are not unrelated, having one ancestry, and every so often — once in some months, by chance or by whatever the ribbons are keeping across fifty unbridgeable kilometers of Stair — the crest of the sea's breath and the exhalation of the plateau arrive together. The seiche rises against the lowest terrace at the same moment the cold pulse rolls down off the rim, and the two airs, thick and thin, old and stranded, press against each other along the drowned shoreline like palms against glass.

For the length of one breath, the world respires as a single thing, the way it did when the shore was still a shore.

Then the sea settles back into its hours, and the plateau into its minutes, and the indigo seam holds steady over the frost — the old country's light, still burning in the window, for whatever might one day come back down the Stair.
