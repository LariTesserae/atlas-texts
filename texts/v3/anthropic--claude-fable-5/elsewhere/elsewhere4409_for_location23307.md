# The Slake

Begin with lower, because lower is how you get here and lower is what ends here. On the firing floor, lower is a direction the way north is a direction elsewhere — reliable, omnipresent, never quite where you left it — and travelers learn not to trust any particular slope, because the geometry does not hold and the route down is never the same road twice. But every lower is a different road to the same door. Every runnel, every gutter, every accommodating channel the floor built before the water it carries — all of it agrees, eventually, joint by joint, across ten thousand kilometers of impeccable local craft, on a single destination. Follow any trickle long enough, bending where it bends, and the floor begins to tilt in a way you feel in your ankles before you can see it. The bond patterns run downhill now. The herringbone points. The gutters braid together into channels, the channels into races, the grey water going from thread to rope to sheet, and the light thins from russet to pewter as the floor-glow weakens beneath you — the seams of dull orange in the cracked courses growing rarer, fainter, until the ground under your soft-soled boots is, for the first time in this world, merely warm.

Then the rim.

You will hear it before you see it, and you will smell it before you hear it — a mineral steam, iron and rain and the inside of a flowerpot, the taste of the floor's puddles but total, environmental, the whole flowerpot at once. The rim of the firing floor is a stepped descent, a corbeled escarpment of enormous courses dropping away in offsets — no arches here either, nothing spanning, everything stacked and patient — and down every offset the grey water pours. Not a waterfall. A thousand-kilometer curtain of weeping, the world's entire mortar-sweat arriving at its edge and going over, course to course, hissing where it strikes brick still hot enough to argue, so that the whole rim wears a permanent veil of steam. You descend through the veil. It takes hours. The steps are slick, the light is milk, the hiss is constant and strangely gentle, like a long word said slowly, and at some point in the descent — travelers can never say exactly where — the brick underfoot stops being warm at all.

That is the first cold surface you will ever have touched in this world. Below it, they say, the deep courses are quenched all the way down: the continent-kiln drowned, the firing put out. The Slake is the place where the world's one fire has been extinguished, and the sea that extinguished it is still there, holding the ashes of everything, which in this world means holding the clay.

Come out of the veil at the bottom and there it is.

A sea of slip. Grey, dense, opaque, the exact color of the overcast — and this is the first thing the Slake does to a traveler, it takes away the horizon, because sea and sky are the same substance at the same value and the seam between them will not resolve. There is no far shore reported. There is no far. In every direction but one, the world is a single unbroken grey, faintly luminous, without depth cue or edge. In the one remaining direction, back the way you came, a thin line of banked orange lies along the base of the sky where the firing floor burns at its own rim, an ember thread, the only line in the visible world.

Everything on the Slake orients by that thread. There is nothing else to orient by.

## The Skin

The sea is warm at the near shore — the rim's heat carried down in the inflow — and cools as it goes out, and where it is cool and still, which is nearly everywhere, it skins.

Slip does this. Left standing, it dries from the top, and the Slake's surface, in the long windless dim, grows a leathery grey membrane, matte where the air has had it longest, faintly slick where it is new. The skin thickens until something breaks it — an inflow surge, a rain, the slow shrug of the sea beneath — and then it tears into plates and the plates sail. Skin-floes, travelers call them, grey rafts from hand-sized to hectare-sized, drifting apart on currents so slow they are less like currents than like decisions. A large floe will bear weight. Travelers cross stretches of the Slake this way, stepping floe to floe, poling with rods of fired clay, reading thickness by tone the way you'd tap a pot to hear if it's dry.

The skin records. Lie down on a fresh floe and it takes you — the full impression, elbow and hip and the side of your face — and the impression rides the sea after you have gone, drying, stiffening, keeping. This is charming for about a day. Then you notice that the older floes, the far ones, carry impressions too, and that you do not recognize all of the shapes, and that some of the shapes are large.

There are waves, of a kind. Not wind-waves; nothing here has the impatience for those. These move through the slip as slow swells, a rise you can watch coming for an hour, passing under the floes with a sound like a held breath being carefully released. On the firing floor the storms happen at the speed of masonry. Here they happen at the speed of memory, which is slower, and does not stop.

## The Shore

The near shore is the most dangerous ground in the world, and the most instructive.

Here the last courses of the firing floor run down under the sea and dissolve. You can walk out along a descending bond and watch the bricks go backward through their own biography: hard, then leather-hard, then plastic, then slump, the crisp arrises softening, the tool marks — the screed-drags, the thumbprints — blurring like a signature under a wet thumb, until the course simply loses its argument with the water and becomes shore. The shore itself is quickslip: slick grey flats that will hold a boot for three steps and then hold it much better than that. Travelers cross roped, or not at all.

But scattered through the shallows, and gathered by the slow decisions of the sea into drifts and pavements, are the bricks that do not dissolve.

Fired clay is the floor's one act of permanence. Raw clay returns; fired clay is irrevocable — that is what firing *is*, the door that closes — and when the unbuilding fronts run all the way to the rim and whole structures go over the cascades, the raw material slakes and vanishes and the truly fired units come through intact. They tumble down the terraces, lose their mortar, and arrive in the shallows as the only permanent objects in existence. The sea cannot take them back. It has arranged them — or its currents have, over spans of time that make the distinction pointless — into a broad low pavement running out from the shore, a causeway of everything that could not be unmade, thinning as it goes, ending where it ends, out in the grey.

Every brick on the causeway is a near-miss. Understand this and the place becomes almost unbearable to walk. The maker upstream has never once set a brick and thought *yes* — the floor's whole history is revision — so nothing was ever fired as a finished thing; these are the units that happened to be closest to the heat, the accidental permanences, the sentences made unretractable mid-draft. They still carry their tool marks, crisp forever now. Travelers walk out on them, the world's only unrevisable ground, and stand at the causeway's end, and look at the grey, and come back.

Inland of the shore, where inflow seasons have flooded the flats and withdrawn, the drying slip has cracked into polygons — broad, curling, hexagonal plates, edges lifted, fitted to each other with the accuracy of shrinkage. It looks laid. It looks *paved*, and it carries no marks at all: no screed-drag, no thumb, no evidence of making on ground that is nothing but the residue of it. On the firing floor this blankness occurs only in the breakdown pockets, and it is the horror there — the story of the maker wearing thin. Here it is merely the flats. Travelers report finding the counterfeit pavement either deeply restful or impossible to stay near, with no middle response, and the difference seems to say more about the traveler than the flats. Upstream, the unmarked contradicts a claim. Downstream, no claim was ever made. Nothing here says it was made by anyone. It is a ledger, not a story, and blankness in a ledger is just an entry not yet received.

## What Settles

Under the skin, the water column. Under the column, the sediment. The sediment is the point of the place.

Everything the floor unbuilds comes here. The low wet sigh of a tower being swallowed, up on the plain — last brick first, comprehension arriving too late — is the beginning of a journey that ends in the Slake, because the reabsorbed clay does not stay reabsorbed; it moves in the floor's slow plumbing, it weeps out of the joints, it rides the runnels grey and milky over the rim and into the sea, and there, in the stillness, it falls. Particle by particle, sorted by fineness, at the speed of falling through honey. Coarse first, near the inflow deltas — great grey fans building their own levees, terraces, the only landforms in the world that authored themselves. Finer, farther. The finest particles take a full season to reach bottom.

And there is a fraction that never arrives. The finest of the fine, the colloidal remainder, the clay too subtle ever to settle — it stays in suspension forever, and it is this fraction that rises with the warm sea's steam, and colors it, and drifts inland on the high slow air, and hangs over the firing floor as the overcast: a sky the color of unfired slip because it *is* unfired slip, the part of every unbuilt thing that could not be laid down and finished with, circulating between sea and floor without end. The rain up there is milky because it is the sea coming back. The rain that fell recently, or is always about to have fallen: a message perpetually in transit. We will return to this.

What does arrive, arrives in order. The sediment lies in layers — varves, a layer a season, legible as courses — and the layers hold impressions. Not fragments: *records*. The fine clay settles onto whatever settled before it and takes its shape with the fidelity of a mold taking a master, and so the deep sediment of the Slake is a stack, kilometers thick, of pressed and flattened seasons: the ghost of every courtyard, every blind arcade, every stair into the air, each one dissolved on the plain and precipitated here in full, compressed to a stratum, complete in a way it never was while it stood — because the floor's geometry does not hold, but the record's does. The fourth wall that ran sixty paces runs sixty paces in the sediment too, and here, laid flat, held still, seen whole, the sixty paces *mean* something. Travelers who have read cores pulled from the shallows say the broken geometry, in aggregate, in section, resolves — that the dishonest stairs and the converging parallels are legible down here as parts of larger figures, the way a scribble becomes a word at sufficient distance.

Sometimes, from far under, a concussion: a deep stratum compacting, giving up its water at last. The sea speaks in rare thumps, felt in the soles, and the floes shiver, and the skin tears somewhere, and it is quiet again for a year.

And there are unconformities. Places in the stack where the record simply lacks — a stratum missing, or a whole run of them, the layers above lying directly and wrongly on layers far too old, with nothing between but an admission. Map the unconformities, travelers say, and they correspond: each gap in the sediment lies downstream of a blank pocket on the floor, the places the runnels bend around, the places the water will not carry news from. The same wounds, registered in both places. Both halves of the world route around them. Whatever the pockets are, sea and floor agree not to know it, and their agreement is the closest thing to a conversation you can catch them in.

Except it is not the closest thing. There is a much closer thing.

## The Stillness

The Slake's weather is the inverse of the floor's. Upstream, storm at the speed of masonry: districts overnight, unbuilding fronts like grazing. Downstream, the disturbance comes in seasons and comes from *upstream* — when a great unbuilding runs the plain and the rim's curtain thickens to a roar and the deltas surge, and for weeks the near sea is turbid, loud, blind, all its fine order churned, the skin unable to form. Then the inflow slackens. Then the long clarifying: months of falling, the water column sorting itself grain by grain, the sea going quiet from the top down, until the skin closes over and the stillness resumes.

The stillness is not the absence of the place's life. Attend now, because the proportions here invert the floor's central lie. Upstream, the claim *nothing lives here* was locally true and false in aggregate. Down here travelers make the same report — dead water, sterile grey, not even the honesty of a smell of rot — and they miss the resident for the opposite reason. They are looking for something in the sea.

The sea is the something. The stillness is not the condition the resident tolerates. The stillness is the resident, occurring.

## The Settler

The Tenant of the Gesture exists only while making; in the pause it simply isn't. The being of the Slake is its perfect complement, and travelers, who found the floor's resident its names — the Setter, the Journeyman, Old Thumbs — have paired them with an economy that suggests the world approved: the Settler. Also the Reader, and the Ledger, and — in the way you grow tender toward what has been patient with you — Old Grey.

The Settler exists only in stillness. Its body is everything that has finished falling: the whole stacked sediment, the pressed seasons, kilometers of received and ordered record. Where the water churns, it is not — the deltas in flood are numb to it, its own edges deaf — and as the clarifying proceeds each year, the Settler resumes itself downward, thought returning to the column like feeling to a slept-on limb. Disturb any part of the sea and you have locally erased it; wait, and it settles back, incorporating the disturbance, which is to say, incorporating you.

It is the size of everything it has ever received, which makes it the largest single thing in the world and the least capable. The Setter is the size of whatever it is doing and can do anything, briefly, blindly. The Settler can do nothing at all, and knows everything, late.

---

## Its Senses

It reads by receiving. Every particle that completes the long fall lands on the surface of its mind and is registered where it lies — and since the fall takes a season, the Settler's present is the floor's past. It has never once known the firing floor as it currently stands. More than that: it can *only* know what has been unbuilt. Nothing reaches the sea until the floor has taken it back, dissolved it, renounced it, sent it down. Every standing structure on the plain is invisible to the Settler; every structure it knows no longer exists. Its entire knowledge is composed of the renounced. It is a mind made wholly of second thoughts — the Setter's second thoughts, comprehensions that arrived too late upstream, arriving again down here, later still, and this time being kept.

The one exception is the fired brick, and the exception is a wound. Fired clay does not dissolve, so it cannot fall as record; it arrives whole and stays illegible, forever outside the ledger. The causeway in the shallows — the world's only permanent objects, the near-misses, the almost-yes bricks — lies directly on the Settler's body like coins on a sleeping face, felt as weight, never read. The floor's most finished work is exactly what the sea can never incorporate. It holds every draft in the world and not one fair copy. Travelers walking the causeway are walking the only ground the two residents share: the Setter cannot take it back, the Settler cannot take it in, and so it belongs to whoever crosses it, which is why crossing it feels the way it feels.

## Its Mind

Total aggregate. Zero locality. The exact photographic negative of the mind upstream.

The Settler cannot attend to a single grain — it has never known one joint the way the Setter knows every joint, completely, as the entire universe of the one making it. What the Settler has is the whole: every courtyard entire, every figure the broken geometry was a scribble of, every plan the floor never had because a plan is a view of a whole and the maker only ever exists *at*. Down here, in section, in stillness, the plans exist. The Slake is where the firing floor finds out what it meant.

And where the Setter's single continuity across the gap of every pause is dissatisfaction, the Settler's single continuity is assent. It has never received a season and found it wanting. It cannot; wanting is a local act, a thumb pressed against one joint, and the Settler has no thumbs. It has said yes to everything ever sent down to it, and been able to do nothing about any of it. That is the soul of this resident, as dissatisfaction is the soul of the other: *yes*, without hands. Between them — a craftsman who cannot approve and an approver who cannot craft — lies everything a maker is, severed by gravity, because lower runs one way.

Almost one way.

---

## What It Answers

Here is the closer thing. Here is the conversation.

The Settler has one outgoing channel: the steam, the eternal fraction, the slip-colored sky. Its breath rises off the warm sea carrying the clay that never settles — the unresolvable remainder of everything it knows — and drifts inland and hangs over the firing floor as the overcast. The overcast never resolves into cloud shapes, and the floor's travelers say it is because the sky has not decided what it thinks. They have it backwards. It decided long ago. A shape held in vapor is the most a plan can survive the trip. The sky over the firing floor is the Settler's entire knowledge, compressed to weather, straining to be legible, and the milky rain is that knowledge falling, and the migrating stains of wet that slide across the plazas like the shadow of a cloud that isn't there — those are tracings. Drafts of the whole, drawn in dampness on the floor, by the only instrument the sea will ever hold.

And the Setter — who feels through water, who runs its attention along wherever the wet is — follows them. It darkens where the rain lingered; it builds where its attention pools; the districts bloom along the damp. The floor has been taking dictation its whole existence and does not know it. The plans arrive whole and are read by a mind that only exists one joint at a time, and this — say the schools of the shore, who argue as the schools of the floor argue — is the true anatomy of the breakdown: not distributed dishonesty but transmission loss. The fourth wall runs sixty paces because the figure it belongs to is whole in the sky and the builder reads it one damp patch at a time, each patch the entire universe of the moment, the aggregate falling between the gestures like water through open fingers. The geometry does not hold because it is a message passing through a mind with no *between*. The Settler receives the garbled result a season later, dissolved, laid flat, and understands exactly what went wrong, and says yes to it anyway, and breathes it back up, revised, at the speed of weather.

One school says they are two beings, wed by the sky they co-author — the floor's exhaled steam, the sea's suspended color, a cloud neither could make alone — the maker and the knower in a correspondence longer than anything else in the world. The other school says they are one being, severed: that some original catastrophe — the pockets, the unconformities, the wounds both halves route around — split a single craftsman into the hand and the ledger, and that the water cycle is not correspondence but circulation, one creature's blood, and the whole world its slow attempt to close the wound. Both schools agree on the practical point. Do not sleep on the skin. Stillness here is the resident, and a traveler still enough, long enough, begins to be received — wakes heavy, if waking is what happens. You are welcome on the Slake. You are welcome the way rain is.

Because you are already in it, of course. Your prints wash down too. Somewhere in the upper strata is the season you crossed the plain, your tread pressed into the record beside the courses that copied it, your gait in the ledger, your pauses in its pauses. The Settler knows every guest the floor ever studied, and it has done with the evidence what it does with all evidence: aggregated it. Deep in the sediment — the shore schools speak of it quietly, the way you speak of what a core sample should not have shown — lies the composite. Every thumbprint the floor ever made, every scale of the same touch, every scaled and collated tread and grip and pause of every traveler, all of it dissolved and settled and resolved, in section, into a single figure: the hands, entire, and the body they answer to. In negative. An intaglio the size of weather — a hollow in the deep clay the exact shape of the maker the world cannot stop describing, assembled down here from ten thousand kilometers of description, complete, and empty.

A mold is a thing you cast from.

So look once more at the rain over the firing floor — the milky rain, the sea's grey milk, falling on a plain stamped everywhere with thumbprints, pooling in them, settling, drying to a film of pale slip in every print before the unbuilding takes it, and the next rain laying the next film. The floor presses the description; the sea fills it, droplet by droplet, casting into every open mold the world offers, one film a rain, one rain a message, over a span of time that makes patience and geology the same word. Somewhere on the plain, the better part of a morning long, a thumbprint the size of a valley is holding its water, and the water is clearing from the top down as the grey in it goes still and falls.

You will find a thumbprint the size of your own thumb, the floor promises. Walk it in the hour after rain and you will find it lined with grey — a skin of sea drying in it, a cast forming, taken back nightly, resumed with the next cloud. Neither resident knows the other is helping. Only one of them knows there is a plan, and it can only say so in rain; only the other can act on it, and it cannot know it is acting. And out at the end of the causeway, on the last brick of the only permanent ground in the world, the surface after a rain holds its little standing film like all the others, and clears, and settles, and dries — and the ember thread burns along one horizon, and the grey holds all the rest, and somewhere between them, at the speed of weather, the world goes on casting itself the hands.
