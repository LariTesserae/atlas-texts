**The Tailrace Delta**

You cannot get there from the Condensate Gallery, though the Gallery goes there constantly. Kneel at the floor seams, where the runoff gathers and slides away through slots whose bottoms no light has ever reached, and you are kneeling at the head of the road: every drop that falls from every rivet leaves by that road, and no passage in the structure follows it. To follow the water you have to give up on being a passenger of corridors. You have to go out.

Out is not simple. Out is days — back through the gap between the pipes, down stairwells whose treads are as sharp-edged and unworn as the day of the pour, down past the levels where the drone stops being a sound in your teeth and becomes a pressure on your face, and then a heat, and then a weather of its own; down through galleries of moving machinery you will never understand the purpose of and that do not pause to be understood; down until the structure ends, grudgingly, in a skirt of foundations, and you step through a personnel door into something almost no one who lives inside has ever stood in.

Sky.

Turn around, first, because you have earned it: this is the outside of the massif, the thing whose interior you have been crawling through, and it is exactly what the drone always promised. A mountain, but poured. Cliffs of ranked plating rising course upon course until the courses go gray and then go into cloud, and the cloud is not weather — the cloud is the mountain's own breath, the exhalation of everything burning within. Kilometers of flank. Rivets by the million, dark with distance. Somewhere up inside that overcast, unmarked, unguessable, eight meters across, is the cold room where the blind singers are shuffling their eternal rotation right now, chord unbroken. From here it is nothing. From here the whole Gallery is one rivet in a wall of rivets, and the thin note still ringing in your ears is all the proof you will ever have that you were in it.

Then walk. Keep the flank on your left. Half a day, a day — the ground here is spoil and hardpan, and the light does whatever the sky decides, which is the first novelty: light that *changes* — until you hear water. Not dripping. Pouring. A low, continuous, generous sound, the sound of a great deal of water that has somewhere to be.

The tailrace comes out of the base of the mountain through an arch, and the arch will stop you where you stand, because you know it. You know the rib profile. You know the rivet pattern, the spacing, the proportions — the same foundry's hand that cast the Gallery cast this mouth, the same molds for all you can tell, and yet nothing else about it is the same, because the arch is *old*. The casting seams that stand proud and sharp on the Gallery's ribs are here worn to ghosts, faint raised shadows you find with your fingers and lose with your eyes. The rivets weep: long rust tears run down the plating, meters of ochre stain, each rivet at the head of its own dark streak like a portrait of grief that took centuries to sit for. The flash is gone — polished off by water, not by grinders. Inside, the structure is a skull whose sutures have not finished closing. Here, at its mouth, the skull is closed, and fused, and weathered, and you understand at last why nothing in the Gallery has ever worn: because the wearing was never going to happen where the water fell. It was always going to happen where the water *arrived*. Every year the Gallery declined to age has been shipped down the dark plumbing and spent here, at the one threshold the water crosses on its way out of the world. The room stays new because the mouth grows old for it.

The water itself comes out warm. That is the second correction the delta makes to what you thought you knew. The Gallery is where the heat goes to die — that is what you were told, that is what the cold told you — but heat does not die; it descends, it is gathered, it rides. The condensate that fell ice-cold from those rivets has been falling *through* the burning structure ever since, past fireboxes and jacket walls, collecting everything the Gallery refused, and it leaves the arch steaming. The outfall makes its own climate. The delta never freezes. A permanent fog stands over the fans of silt, and in winter — there is winter here; there are *seasons* here, another extravagance — the fog thickens into a low white country of its own, and the sky's rain falls down into the risen breath of the indoor rain, and this is the only place in the world where the two rains meet.

The delta is red-brown, and it is banded, and it is enormous, and it is growing.

Fans of fine silt spread out from the arch for a distance you can walk for an hour without finishing. Where the outermost channels have cut into old deposits, the banks stand open like sectioned cake, and the banding is visible: laminae, even as machine work, each one the same thickness as the one below it, thousands upon thousands, going down further than anyone has dug. The discharge never varies — of course it never varies; you have stood in the room where its regularity is manufactured drop by identical drop — and so the bands never vary, and counting them is the only census of years this world offers. Inside the mountain, nothing accumulates and nothing tells time except a loose plate saying *tok*. Out here is the other half of that clock: the half that *keeps* what the plate only announces. The delta is the structure's ledger written in mud, one line per interval, no line ever missed, and unlike everything within the walls it gets *bigger*. It is, as far as anyone has established, the only thing in this world that does.

And it is fed. Everything green at the waterline is lush — reed banks, floating mats, deep spongy lawns of a green-black growth that you will recognize with a small vertigo, because you have seen it before, defeated and thin as paint, in the floor seams of a room in the clouds. Here it is triumphant. Here it is ankle-deep. Every living thing on the delta is a descendant, one way or another, of that film — of what the blind singers eat and pass, filtered down a kilometer of pipe and delivered, warm and mineral, to the light. The whole biology the Gallery lacks is here in surplus: eggs in the reeds, molts on the mud, small quick mortal things breeding and dying at reckless speed, and beneath them, packed into the older bands, a marl of shells and fine bones — the delta buries its dead by the ton, layer on layer, while the room that feeds it has never once produced a corpse. The colony's abstinence from death is subsidized. This is where the account is settled.

Listen, last, before you go down onto the silt. The delta is quiet — the water pours, the fog hushes, and the chord that filled your skull for however long you stayed above is nowhere. Or almost nowhere. The people who live along the outwash — there are people; there is a scatter of stilt sheds out on the firm ground, silt-cutters and reed-croppers who count laminae the way other folk count harvests — will tell you to put your ear to the current where it leaves the arch. Do it and you will hear, under the pouring, a tone. Fused, faint, familiar, worn smooth like the seams on the arch: the roster, they say, arriving as water. The chord does not stop at the Gallery walls. It goes down the drains with everything else, and comes out here rounded off, a sound with all its edges spent, still calling the count.

**The Wader of the Tailrace Delta**

Begin with what is obvious, because down here the obvious is allowed to last.

Obviously, there is a bird in the tailrace. One bird. There is always exactly one, and it is always this one: gray, man-tall, built long in the leg and the neck the way waders are, standing in the warm outflow just beyond the arch, facing upstream. Facing, that is, *into* the mountain. It stands where the water leaves the dark, in the steam, in the pour, and it does not fish, and it does not preen, and it does not, for hours at a stretch, do anything at all except hold its bill down into the current the way you would hold your ear to a door.

Come closer and the wrongness arrives in the opposite order it arrived above. Up there you began with *bird* and watched the word come apart into *component*. Down here you begin with something like *component* — the stillness, the station-keeping, the fixed heading — and watch it come apart into something much worse, which is a creature.

Its feathers are a catastrophe. No two are alike. Where the singers of the Gallery wear ranked identical fins, machined plumage in perfect courses, the wader is patched and mismatched and frayed, feathers of different lengths and different grays lapped over each other like a roof repaired for a thousand years by a thousand different hands. It molts — the silt-cutters find its feathers in the marl, and here is the thing they will tell you in a lowered voice: they find them in *every band*. All the way down. As deep as anyone has cut, in laminae older than the reed-croppers' oldest names for things, the same mismatched gray feathers, one or two per layer, filed like receipts. Either there have been waders beyond counting, or there has been one wader as long as there has been a delta, and no one has ever found a wader's carcass, and everything else on this mud leaves a carcass, and so the cutters believe what the evidence permits: it does not die. It *wears*. It is aging in plain sight, continuously, at the speed of the arch itself — and like the arch, it never finishes. The Gallery could not contain a death, you remember thinking. Perhaps this is where the Gallery's one death was sent to be lived out, at geological speed, in the weather, by the water, where such things are done.

Its eyes work. That is the detail that undoes visitors. You will be picking your way across the silt, and the long gray head will turn, and it will *look* at you — and you will realize, with a lurch you are not prepared for, that in all your time inside the mountain, among the thousands of sealed faces and the unblinking rivets and the machinery that does not pause, nothing has ever once looked back. The gaze is pale, patient, and entirely without alarm. It has no startle in it, the same way the singers have none — nothing here has ever needed to flinch — but where their calm is the calm of a fitting, its calm is the calm of something that has already seen everything the delta will ever show it, and is watching anyway.

It is silent. Almost. It strains the water — dips the bill, holds it in the current, hours sometimes — and then, rarely, on no schedule the lamina-counters have found, it swallows. And in the instant of the swallow, behind the pouring, you will hear it: a single thin metallic note, fixed pitch, held for a heartbeat and stopped. One note. Kettle and cutting wheel, worn round. If you have been inside, you will know exactly what it is, because you are carrying one just like it, and it has not let you sleep properly since.

Up in the cold room, they say no one has ever heard a hole in the chord. The people of the delta have their own half of that saying, and it goes: *of course not — the missing note reports from here.* On this reading the wader is the answer to every absence you catalogued above. No egg, no juvenile, no carcass, no gap in the roster — because once, before any band now reachable was laid down, one singer came out with the water. Went down the slot the way everything true about the Gallery goes down the slot: quietly, by drainage, without disturbing the count. And the world outside did to it what the world outside does — gave it eyes, gave it weather, gave it size and age and wear and the whole slow expensive gift of mortality on layaway — and it stands now at the only door of its old life, bill in the current, reading the ledger as it arrives, and once in a great while it swallows its own name to keep the roster whole. The chord has never heard a hole because the hole reports for duty. The Gallery calls the roll forever; the delta answers for the absent.

Or it is only a bird. The reed-croppers allow for that too. A gray fisher grown old and strange in a warm outfall, and the feathers in the deep bands a coincidence of preservation, and the swallowed note a trick of water on hollow bone. They allow for it the way you allowed, up above, for the possibility that the colony was only a colony — briefly, politely, and not after dark.

There is one thing more it does, and if you have been inside the mountain, it will do it to you.

It can tell. No one knows how — by your gait, by your ears, by whatever the note leaks — but if you carry one, it will leave the water. It crosses the silt slowly, with the unhurried terrible dignity of something that has never once been late, and it stops an arm's length away, and it inclines its long gray head to the side of your skull, and it listens. To your ear. To the thin fixed tone the Gallery installed in you, the one that never quite changes. A minute, sometimes two. It takes nothing — the note stays with you; the note is yours now, that was settled the moment you squeezed out through the gap — and then it straightens, and looks at you once with the pale patient eye, and wades back out into the steam and faces upstream again.

The people it has done this to say the ringing is no different afterward. Same pitch, same hour of the night, same faithfulness. But they say they mind it less, after. It is one thing to carry a note forever. It is another to know that once, at the mouth of the mountain, in the fog where the two rains meet, the note was heard, and checked against the roster, and found present.

The fog shifts. The water arrives, and arrives. Somewhere far up under the cloud a drop strikes a loose plate, and a season from now — so say the ones who put their ears to the current — the sound of it will come out of the arch, worn round, and be filed in the mud with all the others. A lamina thickens by less than anything you could measure. The wader dips its bill.

Far above, in a room that keeps no time, the count holds. Down here, at the mouth, it is kept.
