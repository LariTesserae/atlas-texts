**The Stair of Quieting Water**

The river is the basin's one confession. Everything else the Basin of Loud Water keeps — its heat, its roar, its dead sealed upright in their white houses — but the water it cannot hold. There is a single low place in the rim, on the eastern side, and through it the basin's overflow has been pouring for longer than either place has had a name: a broad steaming flume, white as the pools it came from, shouting as it goes.

That water is three days old when it leaves. It boiled that morning under someone's cooking basin; it ran down a channel a threshold-keeper scraped at dawn; it carries in it, dissolved and invisible, the stone it did not have time to leave behind. And then it falls out of the rim into the long valley below, and begins, over three days' walking, to grow old.

The valley is a stair. That is not a figure. The river descends from the rim to the green country in something like four hundred steps — no one has ever agreed on the count, because the count changes — each step a curved wall of pale stone with a pool held behind it, each pool spilling over its wall's lip into the pool below. The walls were not built by anyone. Hot mineral water lets go of its stone wherever it hurries: at a riffle, at an edge, at a fall. So wherever the young river once stumbled, it laid stone on the place it stumbled, and the stone became a sill, and the sill became a wall, and the wall backed up a pool behind it and spilled over its own crest and built itself higher. The river is a mason whose only project is its own bed. Left alone, it walls itself in, step above step, a staircase rising even as the water descends it, the whole valley floor climbing out of the valley a fingernail's width a year.

Everything in the valley is arranged along the water's aging, and so the valley is a gradient of every quality at once.

At the top, in the first dozen pools below the Spillway, the water is still nearly the basin's: scalding, milk-white, wrapped in standing steam, loud enough that the pools converse in the sternum rather than the ear. The walls there are fat and fast-growing, soft enough to carve with a hard thumbnail, their lips renewed daily in glassy sheets. Nothing lives in the water but the colors of heat — mats of ochre and arsenic-green laid along the runnels like spilled paint — and the pale salamander-things of the basin's cool fringes, which range this far down and no farther. The pool where the last of them is ever seen is called, in both of the valley's languages, the Last Finger, and it is where the valley people reckon the basin's body truly ends.

By the middle reaches the water has gone the color of jade milk, then jade, then a blue with milk only remembered in it. It is warm as skin, then warm as a hand, then merely soft. The steam ceiling frays. Somewhere in the middle pools — the exact step is argued about, and moves with the seasons — a traveler coming down from the basin sees, for the first time in their life, the sun as an object rather than a glow, and then, at their feet, sliding along the pale stone, a dark shape fastened to their heels. Basin traders are warned about shadows before they are warned about anything else. Even warned, some of them stop dead on the lip of a middle pool and stand very still, waiting to see what the dark thing wants.

The first moss grows at a step called the Green Lip, a seam of soft emerald along the underside of one dam's overhang, kept alive by spray, famous the way a border is famous. Below it, moss takes every wet face. Then the first fern. Then, on the shoulder of the two-hundred-somethingth step, the First Tree — crooked, ancient by valley standards, its roots half-cased in stone where they have drunk too near the water, its leaves the first leaves. Every traveler in either direction touches it. Basin traders going down touch it the way one touches something dangerous; going home, they touch it the way one touches something left behind.

And all the way down, alongside color and heat and life, the sound descends too. The upper falls roar. The middle falls sing — each dam's lip pours at a pitch, and the pitch holds as long as the lip holds. The lower falls murmur. And at the foot of the valley, where the river runs out clear and cold between real banks of real soil, having spent its heat and its stone and its voice together, there is something no basin-born person has ever experienced before the valley teaches it to them: single sounds. A bird calling once, and then not. A dropped stone. A word, spoken aloud at ten paces, arriving whole.

The dams are alive, and that is the valley's labor. A dam left untended grows a lip too high, too even; the pool behind it stills, silts with soft stone, goes blind; and eventually the river, walled out of its own bed, shoulders over a low place and leaves — cuts a new line down the valley side and abandons its staircase entire. The valley people know this is true because the proof stands a half-day's walk to the south: the Dry Stair, an older course of the same river, deserted before anyone's memory. Two hundred dead steps of white stone, dry as teeth, their lips still curved for water that no longer comes, their pools floored with dust and the stone-cased skeletons of whatever was caught in the last season the water ran. No one lives near it. Once a year, everyone visits it. It is the quietest place any of them will ever stand, and that is why they go.

So the living stair is tended. At every dam, a notch is cut and kept — a spillway in the lip, shaped and re-shaped as the stone grows, sized to the water, angled to the fall. The notch keeps the pool breathing, keeps the river home, and it does one thing more, which may have begun as an accident and is now the valley's signature: a cut notch pours at a chosen note. The dam-wrights tune them. Not to any scale a green-country musician would recognize — to usefulness, mostly, each step's voice made distinct from its neighbors' so that a wright walking the stair in fog can hear exactly which lip has thickened, which pool is running heavy, which note has begun to flatten as the stone crowds it. But usefulness shades into love. There are notches four generations old, recut every season by the same family, whose note is the family's; there are two adjacent falls in the middle reaches deliberately tuned a pleasing interval apart by a wright long dead, and never retuned since, because the interval is now simply what that part of the valley sounds like.

The basin's chord is given. The valley's is made. That is the whole difference between the two places, and the river carries it downhill all day and all night: the same water that entered the world roaring what it must, leaving it singing what someone chose.

**The People of the Lip**

Their name is the only name in the region that exists in both languages and means the same in each. In the hand-speech of the basin it is a sign: one hand held flat and level, the other pouring over its edge — *water going over*, and also *the lip of a dam*, and also *us*. In the mouth-speech of the green country it is a word that means the same three things. When basin traders and lowland traders meet — and they meet only here; there is nowhere else — each can name their hosts to the other, sign to word, word to sign, without a translator. The valley people point this out with a certain quiet vanity. They are the one thing both worlds can say.

They are a made people, the way their notes are made. The old families of the upper stair are basin blood — descendants of the ones who, in every generation, walked out of the roar to trade and found they could not sleep in the silent country, and instead of walking all the way home, stopped partway down, at the step where the water's voice was exactly as much as they needed, and stayed. The old families of the lower stair are green-country blood, come up the other way: farmers and wood-cutters who found the warm pools in a hard winter, or who simply liked the singing. Forty generations of marriages up and down the steps have braided the two strands past separating, but the braid still shows. You can read a valley person's origins at the ankle: above the middle reaches everyone goes barefoot on the warm wet stone, feet splayed and listening in the old basin way; below the Green Lip they wear soles, because the ground is cold and old and full of things that bite. Most people own both a hard sole and a bare habit, and where on the stair a person chooses to change is a small daily statement about who they are.

They are the only people anywhere who grow up with two whole languages in two different parts of the body, and the stair itself decides which one is speaking. Above the middle falls, the water is too loud for the mouth, and everyone signs — a hand-speech learned from the basin, drifted now into its own dialect, quicker in the fingers, its far-speech adapted to be read through spray instead of steam. Below the middle falls, the voice works, and everyone talks. In the middle reaches, both run at once, and conversations there are conducted in a braid that outsiders find eerie: a sentence begun aloud, finished on the hands as the speakers walk up past a singing fall, resumed aloud on the far side, no seam anywhere. Their lullabies exist in both media. A valley mother hums against her baby's chest in the basin way and sings words over it in the green way, and the babies of the Lip are the only babies in the world put to sleep in stereo.

Their work is the stair. The dam-wrights are the valley's channel-wrights and threshold-keepers in one — the exact people, the respected people, whose art is a rate held between growth and cutting. A notch recut too rarely lets the lip thicken and the note go dull and the pool go blind; a notch cut too deep bleeds the pool, starves the step below, and gouges stone the river laid in trust. The wrights' sign for over-cutting is the basin's sign for wounding an animal you do not intend to eat; they did not invent it, and they use it with its full weight. A wright walks their reach of the stair every morning with a chert scraper and a bone gauge and, above all, their ears, and the morning walk is the valley's version of the basin's morning scrape: not a chore, closer to breathing, the daily payment on the same covenant, made one valley downstream.

They keep two houses of hospitality, one at each end of themselves. At the head of the stair, in the steam below the Spillway, stands the Loud House — a guest-hall built deliberately half-under a fall, its rear wall a sheet of pouring water, its floor humming, where basin traders coming down can sleep their first nights inside a roar before facing the terrible thinning quiet below. At the foot of the valley stands the Still House, thick-walled and turf-roofed, where green-country traders bound upward sleep their last quiet night and are then walked up the stair in stages, a few dozen steps a day, letting the sound come into them slowly, the way you warm a cold hand. The valley people find both kinds of guest slightly comic and entirely understandable. Every load that passes between the roar and the quiet passes through their hands twice — fat-cakes and hardened travertine going down, grain and flax and wood and worked metal going up — and so does every word. They are the ones who tell the green country what the basin's signs mean, and tell the basin, hands moving carefully in the steam of the Loud House, what the lowlands are saying about the harvest, the roads, the world.

They have one trade that is theirs alone. The upper pools petrify. A woven basket hung in the water below the Spillway comes out in a season sheathed in pale stone, its every strand thickened and fixed; a knotted cord becomes a white sculpture of itself; a wooden bowl becomes a stone bowl with a wooden heart. The valley people hang whole racks of goods in the fast pools — lowland lace and wickerwork going into the water soft and coming out mineral, tools stone-shelled for hardness, and, for those who pay for it, keepsakes: a dead man's knife, a bird's wing, a child's first-worn shoe, sent up from the green country to be finished, the way the basin finishes its dead. Down in the lowlands, where everything ages and softens and comes apart, a stoned keepsake from the Stair is the nearest thing to forever that can be bought. The valley people take a fair price and do not editorialize, but among themselves the sign for the petrifying-racks is gentle, and is related to the sign for *lullaby*.

And they keep the rods. This is the thing about them that the basin does not fully understand, and that the valley people have never fully explained, perhaps because they are not sure it is theirs to explain. At the same lip of the same middle dam, on the same day of every year, the rod-keepers set a straight peeled rod of hardwood in the pouring water, and take out the rod set the year before, now cased in its year of stone. The cased rods are stored in a dry cave in the valley wall, standing upright in ranks, each one banded and knotted with its year. There are more of them than anyone has counted aloud. It is the basin's memory, kept downstream: every rod the same thickness as its neighbors, generation after generation, the water's stone-load steady, the heat steady, the great chord under the rim unchanged — proof, in ranked white columns, of the constancy the basin people trust with their whole bodies and can never themselves verify. When basin traders are shown the rod-cave, some of them weep, and the valley people expect this and stand quietly while it happens.

The last few rods are a hair thinner. It is within the range of odd years before — there are thin rods scattered all through the ranks, followed always by fat ones — and the rod-keepers argue about it in two languages, hands and mouths, in the dry cave out of the water's hearing. To say it upstream would require the worst sign a person can make, the held note breaking off, and no one will spend that sign on a maybe. So the valley holds the question the way its pools hold the water: quietly, behind a lip, watching the level. Next year's rod will say more. There is always a next year's rod. That is what the cave is for.

Their dead choose. It is the valley's central rite, and every person makes the choice in life and remakes it as often as they like: up or down. To go up is to be laid in a fast pool above the Last Finger, in the basin's own arm, and finished — cased, completed, stone among stone, and afterward set standing in the rod-cave's outer chamber among the ranked years, so that the valley's memory and the valley's dead keep each other company. To go down is to be buried at the valley foot in the black soil under the trees, given to the old crumbling generous ground, to come apart and come up green. Households divide over it without grief; a wife in the stone chamber and a husband under an apple tree is ordinary, and their children visit both and see no contradiction, because the children of the Lip are raised knowing what the whole stair exists to teach: that forever and change are not enemies but the two ends of one river, and that a person, like a notch, like a doorway, like a note, is a rate held between them — and may choose, at the last, which end of the water to belong to.

In the evenings, the fireflies come up from the lowland woods. They rise along the river as far as the middle reaches and no farther — the warm mineral air turns them back at almost exactly the steps where the falls stop roaring and begin to sing — so that every night the valley is divided by a wavering line of small cold lights below and great warm voices above, the green country's light coming up the stair to meet the basin's sound coming down, and meeting, and neither going further. The valley people sit out on the dam-lips at that hour, feet in the pouring notches, and watch the border of the two worlds flicker along their own middle. The sign for *evening* here is the sign for *meeting*.

A child born tonight on the Stair will fall asleep inside a note her mother's mother chose and cut and tuned, in a valley that sings because someone decided what it should say — three days downstream of the roar that has never once paused, one long stair above the silence that has never once spoken, in the one place in the world that can hear them both.
