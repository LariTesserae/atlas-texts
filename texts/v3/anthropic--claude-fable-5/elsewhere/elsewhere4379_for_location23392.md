**The Dry Cistern**

There is no hill here that is entirely a hill. From above it is a green rise behind the harbor — a long whaleback of turf, cropped short by rabbits, freckled in June with orchids, with a squat brick turret at one end like a chimney that forgot its house. Larks go up off it. Sheep paths cross it. It looks like the oldest thing in the landscape and it is nearly the newest, because the hill is hollow, and the hollow was made on purpose, to a drawing, by men with lime on their hands.

Under the turf is a covered reservoir: a single rectangular chamber a hundred paces long, its floor benched concrete, its roof a field of shallow brick vaults carried on rank after rank of brick columns — a hypostyle hall, an undercroft with no cathedral over it, only grass. The columns march away into the dark in rows, and the dark between them is absolute except at one end, where the turret's broken louvre lets down a round column of daylight that lands on the floor as a pale ellipse and travels, slowly, all day, from west wall to east, the room's one moving thing keeping solar time on a lime-washed floor.

It was built to hold water and it holds none. Service Reservoir No. 2 — the stencil survives here entire, black paint on whitewash beside the outlet chamber, **TO SERVICE RESERVOIR No. 2**, the whole of the word that the drowned gallery under the harbor remembers only as *…RVICE*. The same forgotten crew of masons sweated over both vaults, this one and that one, turned their arches off the same centering, struck their joints with the same trowels; and of the two rooms they made, the sea filled one to the crown and emptied the other, and both have settled into what they will be forever. The waterline is still legible: a pale band of lime scale running level around every wall and every column at the height of nine feet, the ghost of the water this room held for sixty years, a horizon painted by the thing itself. Below the band the brick is bleached. Above it, dark. Nothing is collapsing. The room finished becoming a ruin before living memory, and now it is becoming something else — because the roof, very slowly, leaks.

Rain works down through the turf, through the puddled clay, into the lime of the mortar, and comes through the vault crowns one mineral drop at a time. Where each drip falls, the room is building. Straw stalactites hang from the brick soffits in constellations, thin as grass, white as bone, some of them a century long and a forearm's length. Beneath them the floor has grown rimstone: small terraced pools, gours the size of saucers and dinner plates, brimful of water so still and so clear it reads as absence. The reservoir was made to hold ten million gallons and lost them all, and has spent the hundred years since assembling, drop by drop, perhaps forty pints — held now not by engineering but by calcite, in basins the room secreted itself. It is a cistern turning into a cave. The air is cool the way cellars are cool, a steady mineral chill, and it tastes — you *could* taste it, here — of wet lime and clay and, faintly, in one corner, of salt.

Because in one corner, the sea still calls.

The great rising main from the pump gallery — the forty-inch, the one that moans under the harbor when the tide runs — ends here. It comes up through the floor of the inlet bay and terminates in a check valve the size of a shield, twin to the valves that clap in the thunder ten fathoms down and a mile out. The main is not empty. It has never been empty. It holds its full mile of old water, floor of the sea to floor of this room, a sealed standing column, dark its whole length, and it transmits. Twice a day, when the tide turns far below and the gallery fills with its rolling boom, a faint knock comes up the column and sounds in the iron here — three beats, four, soft as a knuckle on a door in another house. The cistern hears the tide daily. It is the gallery's thunder arriving a mile away and a hundred years late, worn down to a whisper. Nothing in the room reacts. The knock has been part of the silence for so long it belongs to it.

But twice a month, at the top of the great spring tides, the sea's head climbs the last few feet of the column, and the old valve — its hinge worn, like its brothers below, to a perfect slop — lets a thread of water past the seat. For an hour, at the moon's height, the cistern receives what the pumps were built to send it. Not thousands of gallons an hour. A trickle. A cupful. Seawater that has come the whole way through the drowned works — past the turning flywheel, past the throat of Pump No. 3 and whatever grey head lives in it now, over the pale garden of anemones on the upstream pipes — pushed up the dead main by nothing but the weight of the moon on the ocean, arriving warm with iron and salt to fall, drop by drop, into the inlet bay. Then the tide eases and the valve seats and the room goes back to fresh water and lime.

A century of these deliveries has built the salt pan: a fan of white terraces spreading from the valve across the benched corner, rimstone again, but sea-rimstone, gypsum and halite and rust, banded orange and pink and bone. Between the springs it dries and crusts and glitters in the dark it cannot be seen in. At the springs it drinks and dissolves and brims. And it is inhabited — stained through with halophile microbes, pink-orange, a nation without a single body, kin in their way to the iron-eaters glazing the plates of the gallery below: two invisible congregations, one living on the surrender of iron to the sea, one living on the sea's fortnightly surrender to the land, and the same pipe joining them like a throat.

**The Two Waters of the Dry Cistern**

The room keeps two seas, and they never touch.

The fresh pools under the drip-points hold the sky's water: rain that fell on turf, filtered through a hill, hardened with lime. And the fresh pools are alive. Decades ago something came down the turret on a bird's foot — a smear of mud, a few dormant eggs — and now the gours hold ostracods, seed shrimp smaller than poppy seeds, trundling over the calcite like animate grit, and pale blind copepods jerking through water that has never seen the sun. Each pool is its own parish, populated or empty by the luck of what fell into it, and a pool once seeded is seeded forever. Fifteen paces away, the salt pan holds the sea's water and its own separate nation, and between them lies dry lime floor that neither can cross. One room; two oceans, teaspoon-sized; two calendars — the fresh pools fed by weather, the pan fed by the moon.

The threshold belongs to the spiders. In the twilight of the turret shaft, where the daylight fails by degrees, cave spiders hang their loose webs between the ladder rungs and the brick, and their egg sacs hang beside them — white teardrops the size of thumbnails, suspended on threads, glowing faintly in the gloom like drops of milk that refused to fall. The spiders take what the light-shaft delivers: the gnats and moths that blunder down the turret, drawn or dropped. In autumn the herald moths come down past the webs that spare them — some instinct threading the gauntlet — and settle deep in the vaults to overwinter, wings folded, and by January every moth is beaded head to tail with condensation, jeweled, motionless, a congregation of the sealed-in waiting for a season they take entirely on faith.

The bats keep the year. From October to April a dozen Natterer's bats are tucked into the mortar joints above the old waterline, singly, each in its slot, furred frost on their backs, hearts slowed to almost nothing — the vault's winter tenants, hung among the stalactites like small dark fruit. In spring they pour up the turret in a single evening and the cistern stands emptier for it, and the drip goes on counting without them. The joints they use are the joints bats have used since the water went; the slots are offices, and the offices are always filled.

The turret itself is held by the owl. A barn owl roosts on the ladder landing just below the broken louvre, where the light is grey and the wind can be heard in the grass above; it hunts the turf of the hill — the voles, the rabbits' margins — and returns down the shaft on silent wings to the same worn board. Below its post, on the cistern floor, lies its midden: a grey drift of pellets and small bones, skulls of voles and shrews accumulating in the dark like the room's only ossuary, undisturbed, uncounted. No single owl has held the turret longer than a decade. The turret has held owls for longer than any of the makers' grandchildren lived. When one tenant fails, the post stands empty for a season, the voles grow bold on the hill, and then a pale shape drops down the shaft at dusk and the office resumes, because the office was never really the owl's. It belongs to the shaft, and the shaft endures.

At the foot of the light, where the traveling ellipse crosses the same ground every day, the room grows its only green: moss in a soft ring, and a single hart's-tongue fern rooted in a settlement crack, eight or nine fronds, glossy, slowly unrolling — the freshwater twin of the kelp that sways in the trapezoid ten fathoms down, each of them the one plant its room will ever have, each holding the one crack its light allows.

The makers are still legible, fixed like fossils. The staff gauge painted up the inlet wall in black feet and inches, its numbers dimming under lime, measuring nothing. A weed-rake with a silvered ash handle leaning where a man left it against a column, so long ago that calcite has begun to fasten its head to the floor. A candle stub in a lantern niche, gnawed once by something and then let alone. The stencil, entire. And the valve — the last working member of the whole scheme, the only piece of the machinery on either end of the mile that still does, occasionally, its actual job: it holds back the sea, and twice a month, imperfectly, magnificently, it fails a little.

That failure is the covenant between the two rooms. The pumps below were built to lift water up this main, and the pumps are dead, and the water still comes — slower than the engineers dreamed by a factor of millions, on the moon's schedule instead of the coal's, but it comes, and the salt pan lives on it, and so the drowned parish feeds the dry one out of the tide's own pocket. Down there, the valves clap their thunder and the great wheel turns and rings its one bright tooth, and the silver school closes over its losses. Up here, the knock arrives soft in the iron, the drop falls from the straw and ticks into its pool, the owl breathes on its board, the moths glitter, the pan waits for the moon. The main between them holds its mile of old dark water like a held breath.

Neither room is waiting. Neither knows the other, in any sense a creature could hold. But they keep the same tide in different registers, loud and faint; they were vaulted by the same hands; and they have arrived, by opposite roads — one filled, one emptied — at the same far side of purpose: small, cold, dim parishes of the leftover world, their offices outlasting every officeholder, their services held on schedule, in the dark, forever, at either end of a pipe.
