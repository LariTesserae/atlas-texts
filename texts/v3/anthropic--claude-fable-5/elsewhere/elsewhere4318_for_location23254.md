# The Selvage

You find it by bone long before you find it by eye. You learned the trick at the Choir Pit without knowing you were learning it — that pressure behind the teeth, that hum in the sternum below the floor of hearing — and once you have walked away from the pit and out onto the silence of the great flat, you discover the trick will not leave you. The plain is not silent. The plain has never been silent. Kneel anywhere on those fawn miles of wind-polished stone, press your palm flat, set your jaw against the ground, and it is there: a deep mingled tremor running through the rock like a pulse through a wrist. The airborne roar of the pit dies within a mile of its rim. But the low notes, the fundamentals, the ones you feel rather than hear — those go into the stone, and the stone keeps them, and carries them, and the whole vast flat is a drum-skin conducting the voices of its cities in every direction at once, forever.

Walk east across that hum for six days and the flat begins to change under your feet. Not in color, not in texture — in *answer*. Your footsteps start to ring thinner, brighter, the way ice rings when it thins toward open water, and the pressure behind your teeth sharpens by degrees until it stands at the very edge of becoming sound.

Then the world simply stops.

---

## The Edge

The flat ends in air. A long low escarpment runs north to south farther than you can see in either direction — days of it, an unbroken rim — and below the drop, forty feet down, lies a country that has nothing to do with the country above. Soft gray dunes, fine as ash, rolling away eastward under a haze to a horizon they never quite reach. At dawn a ground-mist stands over them, thigh-deep, and the air that rises out of it carries a smell the plain has never once offered you: cold clay. Damp mineral. The first rumor, anywhere in this world, of water — not water itself, but the memory of it, the direction of it, somewhere beyond the dunes and below the haze.

The stone of the flat does not slope down to meet the dunes. It *overhangs* them. The wind coming off the soft country has spent ages scouring beneath the plain's rim, hollowing it, so that the last several paces of the flat are a cantilevered lip of stone only a few feet thick, jutting over its own blue shadow. Stand on that lip and you can feel it flex minutely under you, alive, a diving board of rock the length of a small nation. The underside — the eave, call it — is a continuous shallow cave running the whole length of the escarpment, smooth-ceilinged, cool, humming.

And here is what the lip does. A drum sounds loudest at its free rim. The whole plain has been carrying the ground-borne voices of its cities in silence, mile after mile, note after note, and at the Selvage the carrying finally becomes speech: the thin overhanging stone trembles with everything the flat has conducted into it, and the trembling, at last, moves air.

Kneel at the very edge and you can hear it. Faint. Sourceless. Rising out of the rock itself rather than from any direction — a braided drone, soft as breath, made of many sustained notes woven together. You count them and lose count. Nine. Eleven. The braid resists arithmetic. But one of them you do not need to count, because you know it the way you know a voice through a wall: the fundamental of the Choir Pit, six days behind you, arriving through a hundred miles of stone still warm with the same sun. You carried that note out of the central court in your marrow. Here it is again, waiting for you, one strand among its sisters.

The Wing knows the cities as a melody, flown one pit at a time. The Selvage knows them as a chord. It is the only place in the world where the whole choir can be heard at once — and it hears nothing else, and it never stops.

---

## The Hem

Where there is steady vibration, the felt comes. You know this now.

Along the extreme rim of the lip, following the line of loudest flex, runs a ribbon of the same crystal-whiskered velvet that furs the alleys of the pit — but here it grows as a single narrow band, a hand-span to a forearm wide, stitched along the edge of the world for as far as you can walk in either direction. A hem, in the exact sense: the finished border of the great fabric of the flat, the seam that keeps the whole cloth from fraying into silence.

And the hem is not one color. It is many, in long ordered stretches.

The felt, remember, sorts itself by pitch — each filament feeds in the band of frequency that washes it. Along the Selvage, the strength of each city's note in the stone varies with distance: walk north and one strand of the chord swells while another fades, because you are drawing level with one pit's latitude and leaving another's behind. The felt answers. Opposite each city, the hem flushes into the color of that city's register — a mile of saffron giving way through mottled borders to a mile of oxblood, then that deep green that is nearly violet, then a pale gold you never saw in the pit at all, belonging to a city you will never visit. The Selvage is a map. It is the choir rendered as a spectrogram in living moss, and to walk the hem is to walk a readout of the health of every city on the plain, note by note, mile by mile.

Which is how you find the gap.

Half a day's walk south, the hem goes gray. Not dim — dead. A long stretch of pale, papery, layered growth with nothing living on top of it, laminae of the dead compressed a finger deep, the color of old paper. Some strand of the chord is missing here. Somewhere out on the flat, at this latitude, there is a thumbprint pressed into the stone that no longer sings — a pit gone silent, its flues choked or its terraces fallen, its felt starved gray in days the way felt starves, generations ago now, to judge by the depth of the dead layers. The choir has lost voices before. The hem does not mourn. It only records, the way the felt records everything, in velvet it cannot read.

At the very center of the gray stretch, low on the lip, there is a single thread of new color. A few years old, no more. Bright as a wet thing.

Hold the thought.

# Who Keeps the Selvage

At the pit you took the census by pitch, descending through the chord. Here the chord arrives pre-woven, and pitch will not divide the residents. What divides them is *ground*. Everything that lives at the Selvage is defined by its distance from the last yard of ringing stone — by how much silence it can bear to stand on. Take the census, then, from the rim outward, and downward.

---

## The Rim-Runners

On the lip itself, patrolling the hem, live cousins of the pit's masons — the same knuckle-sized armored bodies, the same broad listening bellies, but longer-legged, built for distance, brick-gray shading to dune-gray along the flanks. They are deaf to the air, like all their kind. They hear the world through their undersides, and the world they hear is the chord.

Each one, in the first hours after hatching, imprints not on the wall of its birth but on a single strand of the braid — one city's fundamental, arriving through a hundred miles of stone. That note is its name and its parish. It will spend its life walking the stretch of hem where its note runs strongest, scraping wind-blown dust from the ringing rim so the lip stays thin and loud, glazing the worn places with that same buttery vitreous paste, keeping the edge in tune exactly as its inland cousins keep the flues. It is, in the only sense that matters, a citizen of a city it will never see — a maintainer-in-exile, keeping faith with a note, clearing the channel so that its distant home can go on being heard at the end of the world. It does not know this. It knows that when the belly-song thins, hunger follows, and that scraping makes the hunger stop. Same religion. New parish.

They never, under any circumstance, step off the stone.

---

## The Slack

Because below the eave, the dunes are not empty. They are only silent, and here those are different things.

The soft gray country — the Slack, the shape of the land insists you call it — swallows sound completely. A dropped stone lands without a report. Your own footsteps, should you descend, vanish as you make them. Nothing in the Slack can be heard coming, and something in the Slack *comes*. You will not see it. What you will see, from the safety of the lip, are the funnels: smooth conical collapses in the dune-face, a few feet across, appearing overnight where yesterday there was unmarked sand, each one the record of something rising and taking and settling again. The small armored things of the pit never learned to flee because nothing there hunts. The rim-runners of the Selvage never learned to flee either — they learned something older and simpler, which is *never to leave the ringing ground*. On the stone, every footfall for miles arrives through the plate as warning. On the sand, nothing arrives at all. In the pit, silence meant starvation. At the Selvage, silence is where death lives. Same god, other face.

---

## The Eave

Under the lip, in the long cool cave of the overhang, the loudest surface at the Selvage is the ceiling — the belly of the flexing stone. Banners of felt hang from it, streaming in the updraft off the dunes. And in one place, and one place only, bell-trees grow from it.

They grow downward. Rooted in cracks in the ringing underside, trunks of layered amber curving out over the sand, their parabolic leaves cupped upward against the stone belly like hands held to a chest to feel it breathe. There are perhaps a dozen, and beneath them, wedged in every crevice, is the explanation: seed. Hundreds upon hundreds of the little tuned vanes, inert, waiting, accumulated over centuries — because this is where the Wing sleeps.

It is here every night, and here you can finally see it at rest: vast, gray as the haze, folded against the ceiling of the eave with its long trailing fringes hanging like a second growth of felt, breathing — you would swear it — in time with the deepest strand of the chord. Its daily circuit of the cities begins and ends at this roost. The melody it flies has a tonic, and the tonic is the place where all the notes arrive together; the Wing goes out each afternoon into the sequence and comes home each night to the sum. The seeds it combs from the flue-tops shake loose here as it settles, and most wait in the cracks forever, and a dozen, over the centuries, have found ringing stone and become the only bell-trees in the world that listen downward.

On the storm-nights when the chord runs high and strange, these trees do what their inland cousins do — they ring back. But their voices do not go into the air. They go into the plate. Through the stone, westward, inward, a hundred miles: the one answer the edge ever sends, arriving in every city at once as a glassy shiver under the wind, which the masons of the pits feel through their bellies and press themselves flat to hear. The choir sings outward its whole life. Once or twice a year, from the rim of the world, something faint sings back.

## The Ones Who Come Up from the Mist

And now the cairns.

They stand along the lip at long intervals — you passed the first without understanding it — small dry-stacked steles of pale glazed brick, waist-high, weathered but standing. The brick is the pit's brick. The same butter-colored glaze, the same finger-dimples pressed into every unit, and now, with the smell of the Slack in your nose, you understand something the pit could never tell you: that brick is fired clay, and there is no clay and no fuel on the whole of the dry flat, and the cold damp mineral smell rising off the dunes each dawn is the smell of the pit's own body before it was born. The cities were built of the low country. Someone carried the rim inward, brick by brick, generations of walking, and raised instruments out of it, and tuned them, and left. The builders did not appear from nowhere and vanish into nowhere. They came up from below the edge, and they went back down.

Each cairn stands opposite one city — you can confirm it by the hem, whose color runs strongest at each cairn's feet — and each has, at shoulder height, a niche. You know the niches. Every house in the pit has one. These are not empty. In each rests a single object: a fired clay bar the length of a forearm, dimpled with ancient fingerprints, and when you lift one and strike it against the heel of your hand it rings a pure sustained note and goes on ringing far longer than clay should. A tuning reference. The true keynote of its city, fixed in fired earth centuries ago. The ritual reconstructs itself in your hands before you can think: strike the bar, kneel, press your ear to the stone, and compare — the note as it was meant to be, against the note as it arrives today through a hundred miles of rock. Is the city still in tune. Is home still singing.

The bars are worn. Some are worn to nubs. The felt around some cairns is crushed into two smooth knee-shaped hollows.

You never see them. But be on the lip at first light, when the mist stands thigh-deep over the Slack, and you will find what the dawn leaves: footprints in the skin of dust along the rim — bare, long-toed, human enough — coming up out of the silence by hand-cut stairs worn glassy in the escarpment face, pausing at a cairn, kneeling, and going back down before the light hardens. They live below. In the one country the choir cannot reach — whether because generations bred inside a scream needed, at last, the unimaginable luxury of quiet, or because they keep some appointment down there with kilns and clay, or because of what they left singing in the low note behind them, the Selvage does not say. But every dawn, some of them climb up out of the silence they chose, to the one place where everything they abandoned arrives at once, and strike a bar, and put an ear to the world, and take the pulse of cities their great-great-grandmothers tuned. They walked out of the instrument. They never stopped listening to it.

And at the gray gap — the dead stretch, the missing note — the cairn is not abandoned. It is the most worn of all. Its bar is polished to a shine by handling, its niche-walls burnished by centuries of taking-out and putting-back, its knee-hollows pressed to bare stone and damp, this morning, with mist. Someone has knelt here every dawn for generations, striking the true note of a city that stopped answering long before they were born, and listening anyway.

At the foot of the stairs below that cairn, half-buried in the sand, sits a stack of new brick. Pale, glazed, finger-dimpled, its shine unscoured by any wind. And out on the flat, westward from the gray gap, the dawn dust shows other prints — not up-and-down prints. Prints going *inward*, laden-deep, toward the latitude where a silent thumbprint waits in the stone. And in the center of the gray hem, that single bright thread of new-grown color, a few years old, fattening on a note so faint you cannot find it — yet.

The Selvage does not say what it means. The Selvage never says. But the hem records, the way felt records everything, and the newest laminae at the gray gap are not gray.

## The Census, Closed

So the count, from the rim outward: the hem in its millions, wearing the health of the choir as color. The rim-runners in their faithful thousands, citizens of cities they will never see. The dozen inverted trees of the eave, and the one Wing asleep in the sum of its melody. The unseen tenants of the funnels, population unknown, patient as sand. And the ones who come up from the mist — the builders, or their children's children — who live in silence and visit sound, kneeling at the edge of everything they made.

And, briefly, you. Because you should understand what your six days of walking did. Every footstep you set on the flat went into the plate, and the plate carries everything, in every direction, without judgment and without loss. Your steps have been arriving at the Selvage since the morning you left the pit — and arriving at the pit, and at every city on the plain, all along, a tremor too faint to fatten a single filament. Or not. The felt does not distinguish, and the plain does not forget. You strike the bar of the Choir Pit's cairn once, softly, and hold it to the stone, and the note in the clay and the note in the rock lie against each other and agree: still true. Still singing. Then you set the bar back in its niche the way the worn glaze teaches you to, and turn west.

The chord fades behind you — a drone to a pressure, a pressure to that hum behind the teeth, the one that will never entirely leave you now. Ahead, six days out, a city is singing your arrival before you make it. Behind, at the rim of the drum, someone is kneeling in the mist over a note that stopped generations ago, and somewhere between the two, brick by carried brick, a gray strand of the hem is being tuned back toward color.

Put your hand flat on the stone anywhere. It is all still arriving.
