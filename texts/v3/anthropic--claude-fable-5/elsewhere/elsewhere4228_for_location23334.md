## The Terminal Basin

Every river the old highland ever had ran the same direction, because there was only one direction to run. Off the rounded shoulders of the world's oldest mountains, down through the worn valleys, across a coastal plain a hundred miles wide, and into the sea — and at the mouth of the largest of them, where the ore came down at last off the trams and the rails and into the holds of ships, there was a port. Lowmark, it was called, with the plain unloveliness of a working town: low, and marked, and both truer now than its founders could have guessed. When the water came up and kept coming, Lowmark did not fight and did not flee. Its people left in stages, its ships sailed out of its harbor for the last time in good order, and the town itself simply went down with its country, street by street, until it stood at the bottom of three miles of new ocean, on the plain below the drowned mountains, in the dark.

Here is the thing the maps never quite convey: the rivers outlived the land they drained. The old riverbeds run down off the massif's flanks as submarine canyons now, and water still moves in them — cold, dense water spilling off the shelf sea at the top of the world, sliding downhill along the channels the rivers spent four hundred million years preparing for it. The rivers of the highland still flow. They flow underwater, through water, invisible except by what they carry, and they still end where they always ended. Lowmark is still a river-mouth town. It has never stopped being delivered to.

You arrive by falling. There is no other way — an hour and more of descent through the layers, the light failing by degrees until it is not failing anymore because it is gone, the cold settling in past the suit and the hull and into the idea of you, the pressure climbing toward five hundred atmospheres. Somewhere in the middle water you pass out of the massif's influence: the drumming of the surface storm thins, the moan of the old turbines fades to a thread, and then you are in the silence, which is not silence exactly. It is the sound of a place that makes no sound of its own and hears everything. Three miles up, the top of the world is shouting. Down here, faint and pure, arrives the tolling of the instrument tower on the high dome, ringing in some storm you left behind days ago — a bell heard from the bottom of the sea it stands in.

### The city, which finished

The massif is a place where nothing fundamental changes because the violence has nothing left to work on. The basin is its inverse: a place where nothing changes because nothing arrives with the strength to change it. There are no waves at three miles. There is no surge, no storm-light, no seasons of tearing and regrowth. The water moves at the pace of a slow walk, when it moves at all. What Lowmark was on its last day above the reach of weather, it very nearly still is.

Very nearly. The town has been edited, and the editing has a logic to it: the soft parts of memory went first. The wooden wharves are gone — eaten, not rotted, and we will come to the eaters. The doors are gone from their frames, the floors from their joists, the pews from the assembly hall, so that the town now stands as its own hard skeleton: brick and dressed stone, iron and slate, glass and ceramic and enamel. The street signs survive, blue enamel on iron, legible in a torch beam. QUAY ROAD. TERRACE OF THE ORE EXCHANGE. The harbormaster's office still holds the harbormaster's ledger, because he kept it on slate, in a fine cramped hand, and slate does not care about pressure or centuries; the final entry records the departure of the last ship, her tonnage, her destination, and nothing else, as if the destination were a place and not a direction.

The breakwater still stands, a great granite arm curled around an empty harbor, defending it faithfully against a surface three miles away. On the point, the lighthouse: its lens intact in the lamp room, and growing up through the lens's iron cage, pale and intricate and taller now than the lamp it replaced, a glass sponge — a living thing of woven silica, luminous white in the torch light, perhaps six hundred years from its full growth. The lighthouse went dark and then, with no one watching, grew a new lamp out of glass. It does not shine. It doesn't need to. Nothing here has needed light for a long time, except on the nights the rivers bring it, which is a thing that must be told in its place.

And the stockyards. This is where you understand what Lowmark was for. Behind the rail sidings, in heaps still squared at the base by walls of timber the borers are only now finishing, lies the ore — hundreds of thousands of tons of it, crushed rock brought down from the highland by the tramway whose pylons now march kelp-furred across the drowned saddles far above. It never shipped. It sits where it was tipped, mountain-flesh in exile, the only part of the world's oldest mountains that ever left home. The heaps have rusted into gardens: iron-feeding bacteria in mats of ochre and burnt orange, drifted over centuries into shapes like slow flame. The massif's substance, quarried and carried three miles down by human effort, now feeds the dark by leaking. There is a symmetry in this that no one planned. The rivers spent an eon carrying the mountains down grain by grain to build the very plain the town stands on; the trams and rails only did, briefly and at great expense, what the water had always done for free. The basin's floor *is* the massif, delivered early. The town was built on mountains that had already made the journey, and then the last shipment came down after it, the hard way, and stayed.

### The weather, which is mail

The basin has no weather of its own. It has correspondence.

Everything that happens here is something that happened first at the top of the world, translated by three miles of falling into a gentler alphabet. The perpetual snow — the fine pale drift of the shelf sea's dying, sinking plankton and kelp-dust and the ash of a million small endings — thickens and thins on a calendar written by storms the basin will never feel. When a great November blow tears the forests above by the acre, the wrack rafts drift and sink into the massif's valleys, and the valleys, in their turn, drain; weeks later the old river-channels run heavy with the last fraction of it, the fines and the fragments, the final delivery of final deliveries, and it comes down the canyons and through the breakwater gap and settles over Quay Road like a rich grey snowfall. The town's harvest is the mountain's catastrophe, lagged and softened. Lowmark eats the storm.

And sometimes — after the very worst blows, the hemisphere-crossing ones — the rivers arrive glowing. The density current comes down loaded with small disturbed lives that answer turbulence with light, and for a few hours the old riverbed burns faint blue along its length, and the flow spills through the streets it has learned, along the rights-of-way, around the Ore Exchange, out across the harbor floor, and the town is lit again. Blue, cold, moving light in the streets of Lowmark, three miles down, sourced from a storm. It is the only light the basin ever gets, and it comes as flood. The city's streetlights are the top of the world's disasters, delivered by the same rivers that built the ground, running the same routes they have run since before anything had eyes to see them do it.

## Who Lives at the Bottom of the World

Ask it here and you must whisper it, or feel you should, and the honest first answer is: the receivers. The massif's census is a ledger of holding on. The basin's is a ledger of taking delivery. Nothing here chases; nothing here grips against a surge that never comes. To live in Lowmark is to be positioned, and patient, and to sign for what the rivers bring.

### The tiling of the plain

Start outside the town, on the open sediment, because that is where the basin's oldest arithmetic is done. The plain is tiled — softly, at intervals of a hand-span, mile after mile — with xenophyophores: single cells the size of fists, each one a lone amoeba that has built itself a house of cemented sediment and lives in it alone, sieving the snow. One cell, one house, one long tenancy. They are the basin's smallholders, and there are more of them than there are of anything in the massif except the animals inside the holdfasts, and they do with one cell apiece what the forest above needs millions of species to do: they take what falls, and they persist. Some of the houses, the deep-survey people believed, are older than the town whose lights they now occasionally see. They were sieving this plain when it was the seafloor off a living coast, and the drowning of the world changed their circumstances not at all, except that for a while the snow got strange — paint flakes, coal dust, a rain of small manufactured things — and then it got rich, richer than it had ever been, because a forest had grown at the top of the water and forests are generous with their dead.

### The demolition guild

The wood-borers deserve their formality of title, because they are the only inhabitants doing deliberate work on the town itself. They are clams, tiny ones, of the patient deep-water kind that can smell sunken wood across miles of dark, and they arrived — no one knows how soon, but soon — and began the only demolition Lowmark will ever get. They have their order of operations, imposed by nothing but access and appetite, and it reads like a philosophy: wharves first, then doors, then floors, then furniture, then the pews, then the shelving of the reading room, then the timber cribbing of the ore-yards, which they are finishing now, centuries in. The soft parts of memory first. What they leave is what the town meant structurally rather than what it meant daily, and there is an argument, made by no one because no one is here to make it, that this is the correct order in which to forget a place. Each spent bore-hole becomes a socket for something smaller. The guild eats history and issues housing. The massif would recognize the arrangement.

### The gardens of leakage

Under the fuel quay, where the bunkering tanks have been weeping their heavy oils a bead at a time for longer than the town was ever inhabited, there is an industry the town never planned. Seep-life found it — the same patient chemistry-eating lineages that work the ocean's natural wounds — and built on it: bacterial mats in white and sulfur-yellow drifts, thickets of tube worms with their crimson plumes out in the still water, beds of clams mining the tainted sediment for the energy in it. It is, by any fair accounting, the most vigorous neighborhood in Lowmark, a district of genuine chemical wealth, and it exists because a maintenance schedule lapsed forever. Three miles up, turbines turn for no one and whole reefs have organized around the habit of it; down here, tanks leak for no one and a garden has organized around the failure of it. Purpose is not a residency requirement in this world, at either end of it. Persistence will do. Even negligence will do, if it persists.

### The grazers of the rights-of-way

The streets are kept. Not clean — kept, grazed, in long deliberate lanes, by herds of sea cucumbers that move through the town the way the urchin fronts move across the summit-domes far above: slowly, numerously, audibly if you put an ear to the sediment, working the snowfall where it drifts deepest. And it drifts deepest along the streets, in the lee of the standing walls, so the herds keep to the old rights-of-way, up Quay Road and around the Exchange and out along the breakwater root, following the town plan with more fidelity than traffic ever did. They are the massif's herds re-imagined for a gentler economy — nothing holds them back, because nothing needs to; they cannot overgraze a pasture that falls from the sky. Behind them the streets lie briefly bare, brick showing through, and then the snow begins again. Rattails patrol above the herds in slow circuits, drawn to anything the grazing startles; cusk eels hold the doorways; and when a large fall comes down — a carcass, a whole slab of raft-wrack, once famously an entire tramway cable-drum that took a century to work loose above and three days to arrive — the hagfish assemble out of the sediment like a rumor becoming true, and the delivery is processed, and the basin goes back to listening.

### The nursery

In the harbor, at the fitting-out pier, lies the one ship that never left: an ore carrier that was in for repairs when the repairs stopped mattering, settled upright with her holds open to the water. The holds are the nursery. This is where the sleeper sharks begin — the pale, slow, hand-length young of the eldest inhabitants of the drowned massif's valleys, born into the stillest, safest rooms in the basin, growing at the pace the deep insists on, patrolling the harbor for their first human lifetime or two before the long commute begins. Because it is a commute, though no individual experiences it as one: over decades and then centuries they follow the cold and the food upslope, up the old river canyons, out of the dark and into the twilight, until the largest and oldest of them are cruising the drowned valleys at the top of the world, blind and vast, elder statesmen of a country they will never know was ever a country. The massif calls them its living memory and notes, correctly, that they remember nothing. But they are Lowmark-born, every one. The top of the world's most ancient citizens are this town's children, raised in a steel hold in an abandoned harbor, and the two places are joined not by any road or current so much as by the arc of those single, centuries-long lives — beginning in the town the old world left behind, ending in the mountains the new world put under water. A sleeper hatched in the nursery this year will die, four hundred years from now, three miles above its birthplace, in the valleys where its birthplace's rivers rise. No other inhabitant of this world lives in both of its great places. They all do.

### Who does not live here

Anyone. The massif at least gets its visitors, its survey boats and storm-fishers and the occasional careful diver. Lowmark has been seen by living human eyes exactly once since it went down: a single crewed descent, generations ago, three hours on the bottom, a sphere on a cable, a woman with a camera. Her photographs are the reason the slate ledger's last entry is known, and the sponge in the lamp room, and the enamel of the street signs. Her ballast bricks still lie where she dropped them to begin her ascent, in the square before the Ore Exchange, in a neat pile, and the herds graze around them, and the xenophyophores have begun, without hurry, to build on them. She wrote afterward that the town had not seemed dead to her, only closed, the way a harbor office is closed at night — everything in order, everything waiting, the keeper stepped out. There are people who considered that sentimental. The ledger, for what it is worth, agrees with her.

### The machines, which finished

The massif's machines qualify as inhabitants by enduring in motion — the turbines turning, the lights blinking, industry outliving purpose at speed. Lowmark's machines qualify the other way. Nothing here runs. The town hall clock stands at eleven minutes past four, and has stood there so long that eleven-past-four has become less a time than a place, a fixed feature of the town like the breakwater. The cranes hold the postures they were parked in. The tide gauge on the harbor wall — and there is a joke here so slow that only the sponges will live to finish laughing at it — still faithfully indicates the last tide it ever measured, under three miles of the greatest tide there ever was. The basin's machines are not persisting under the storm. They are what persisting looks like after the storm has been survived, filed, and closed. The turbines above are the millwheel the villages gathered around. The clock below is the monument the villages become. Between them, faint down the canyons on heavy nights, passes the one message the two mechanical populations exchange: the tolling of the steeple, sent by violence, received in stillness, signifying — as all Lowmark's mail signifies — that the top of the world is shouting, and that food is falling.

Beneath all of it, as above, the one true native: except that here the native is not the rock that stayed but the rock that traveled. The plain is the massif, ground down and walked here grain by grain over four hundred million years by the same rivers that still arrive glowing after storms — the mountains' patience and the mountains' substance, spread out flat in the dark, holding up a finished town, a nursery of future elders, a lighthouse growing its own glass. The two places have never been apart. They are one body of ancient stone at its two ages: the part that kept its height and wears the weather like a loud coat, and the part that made the journey and lies at rest at the end of its own rivers, taking delivery of everything the heights let fall.

That's who lives here. The delivered, and the patient who sign for them.
