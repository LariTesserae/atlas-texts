**The Scarp of Loosed Breath**

The trembling you feel in your teeth beside the half-sunk ring does not end there. Nothing ends there. The artifacts of the pan take sound out of the air, but taking is not destroying, and the fine fast shiver that runs below hearing runs *somewhere* — down through the smooth grey bedrock, out under the mossy loaf-hills of the rim, and away beneath the old country for three days' walking, through stone so ancient and so settled that it carries vibration the way deep water carries whales. Herders crossing that country know it. On a still night you can lie down on any outcrop between the pan and the break, press your ear to the rock, and feel the traffic passing under you: a ceaseless faint freight of everything the basin has ever swallowed, on its way to being said.

It is said at the Scarp.

Three days east and down, the worn country does the one dramatic thing left in it: it breaks. The same pale bedrock that floors the pan ends in a fault scarp half a mile long and forty men high, facing the cold sunrise across a plain of wind and bunch-grass — and here, exposed in the broken face like the workings of something mostly buried, the grey material comes out of the stone. Not rings and pylons, as in the pan, but *vanes*: thin seamless fins of it standing proud of the cliff in their hundreds, some the size of a hand, some taller than trees, ranked in loose verticals up the whole height of the wall like the teeth of an enormous comb. Moss will not touch them. Water will not bead on them. Ice, which shatters everything else in that country, declines to form on them at all. They are as old as the pan's furniture and as unmistakably working, and what they are working at is the opposite labor: they are letting the breath back out.

The Scarp is loud. That is the first thing, and like the pan's hush, you never stop understanding it. The wall *plays* — day and night, in every weather, a layered murmuring speech of water-sounds and wing-sounds and rain that fell nowhere nearby, pouring off the vanes and out over the plain until it fades into the ordinary wind a mile from the cliff foot. Stand close and you can pick the threads apart. There: the tick of drips from a weeping seam of stone, though the scarp face is dry as bone. There: the soft collapse of a fish's rise, in country where there is no open water for a day's walk in any direction. There, arriving vast and soft from the whole height of the wall at once, a muffled white concussion, like hundreds of wings shaken free of rain in the same instant.

Everything the pan eats, the Scarp eventually says. But the stone is slow, and the stone is deep, and the delay is on the order of a lifetime — longer for some sounds than others, because the rock sorts what it carries. High things travel quickest; a child's voice may cross in forty years. Deep things linger; thunder that broke over the basin arrives generations late, and the lowest tones of all — the ground-note of the pan's own artifacts, perhaps, the hum of the instrument itself — are still in transit from an age nobody remembers. So the wall speaks the treble of the recent dead and the bass of the ancient dead in the same breath, and a single steaming afternoon in the pan comes out of the cliff unbraided across weeks: first the patter of its rain, later its wingbeats, last of all, some winter, its thunder.

And the wall never repeats. This is the rule the whole place is built around, the rule every resident's life is shaped to. Each sound is loosed once, spent, gone. The Scarp is not an echo. It is an exhale, and no breath goes out twice.

The land at the cliff foot is everything the pan is not. Cold, dry, windy, wide. Long morning shadow under the wall, then hard flat light all afternoon. A scree slope of broken pale stone, riddled with caves. Bunch-grass, thorn, a dark seasonal creek that runs a month in spring and leaves gravel the rest of the year. Nothing here is warm, nothing here is still, and nothing here is quiet — the wall's murmur underlies every hour, so constant that the herders say you only hear it on the day you arrive and the day you leave.

**Who Lives Under the Scarp of Loosed Breath**

The pan filtered its residents for the shrug — for lineages that could lose sound and not miss it. The Scarp filtered for the opposite gift. Everything that lives against the wall lives by ear, and most of it lives by *this* ear, this one particular half-mile of speaking stone, in ways that would fail anywhere else in the world.

**The late-birds.**

Begin with the readers of the library. In the thorn and the scree caves lives a drab little thrush-like bird, brown as the winter grass, unremarkable to look at, and the greatest mimic anyone has ever heard. The herders call them late-birds, because everything they sing is late. They are learners without limit — a late-bird's own song, whatever it once was, dissolved generations ago into pure quotation — and their teacher is the wall. They sit on the vanes themselves, which no other bird will do, heads cocked, and they take dictation.

This is how the extinct chorus survives. The amber frogs of the pan have been mute for sixty or eighty generations, their throats remade into lamps — but the *croaking* of their ancestors, the last true frog-song of that lineage, poured into the hush centuries ago and has been seeping through the bedrock ever since, and the deepest of it is arriving still. The wall says it, once, in fragments, on cold nights. And the late-birds keep it. There are late-birds under the Scarp today who court in the full ancient dusk-chorus of animals that have not made a sound since before the trees in the pan were seeds — birds who have never seen a frog, never seen standing water, singing the great roar of the drowned bright basin as it was when it could still be heard. The pan traded its voice for its silence; the trade was banked in stone; and the interest is paid out here, from small brown throats, in a cold country the frogs will never know.

The birds keep other things too, and this is where the animals' story and the people's story braid together — because the wall says human voices.

**The herders.**

The herders of the old country — the same lineages whose stories reach even the rim of the pan — have grazed the plain under the Scarp for as long as their memory runs, and they long ago worked out what the two places are to each other. Their custom is called *giving a word to the pan*. When something must outlast a life — a name, a confession, a message to a child not yet born, the last words of the dying carried in a litter for three days across the down country — they take it to the mossy rim, step through the curtain of the hush, wade out a few paces among the parting frogs, and say it. The basin takes it the way it takes everything: completely, instantly, into that attention so total it registers as silence. Then they walk out and begin the wait.

The wait is longer than most givers live. That is understood; that is, in a sense, the point. The word is not for you. Families keep rough ledgers — a name given in a grandmother's girlhood is *due*, give or take a decade, in her grandchildren's middle age, sooner if her voice was high, later if it was deep — and when a word comes due, the family shifts its flocks to the wall and camps the season at the cliff foot, listening. The animals graze calm under the murmur; the children learn the discipline of the ear; and some evening, threaded in among old rain and old wings, a voice that no one in the camp ever heard alive says the thing it crossed sixty years of stone to say. Once. The wall never repeats.

But the birds do. This is the late-birds' place in the human heart of the country, and the reason no herder will harm one or suffer one harmed: the birds are the wall's mercy. A loosed name is spent the moment it is spoken — unless a late-bird was sitting the vanes that evening, in which case the name may turn up in the thorn-scrub for years afterward, sung in a dead woman's exact voice by a bird courting in the dusk, passed to fledglings, carried up the canyon, kept. Herder families track *their* birds the way other peoples track heirlooms. There are lineages of thrush under the Scarp that have carried a particular name, voice-perfect, through more bird-generations than the family it belongs to has had human ones.

There is an office, too — a handful of the patient in every generation, keepers of the long ledgers, who sit vigil below the wall in shifts through the years when no family's word is due, writing down what the stone says so that nothing loosed in an empty season is loosed to no one. They are the Scarp's mirror of the pan's salamanders: the elders of the place, slow, fixed, owed room. Their whole discipline is a single sentence, taught to every child who will inherit the work: *everything was said once already; our task is only to be there the second time.*

**The hour of old rain.**

The plain's days are ruled by a weather that is not weather. Most evenings — because most afternoons, a lifetime ago and three days west, a single fat cloud assembled over the pan and rained itself soundlessly out — the wall enters the hour of old rain. Twenty minutes, sometimes thirty, of that strange swallowed patter, the sound of warm drops striking shin-deep water in a bright basin long ago, pouring cold and disembodied off the vanes into the dusk. The flocks drift toward the cliff for it; the late-birds go silent for it, listening, taking their dictation; the herders time the evening meal by it. It is the most companionable hour of the day, and the strangest, if you stop to hold both places in your mind at once — the steam rising off the crowded shining pan on some afternoon before your grandmother was born, and the sound of it only now arriving, faithful as a letter, at the foot of a cold cliff full of birds.

And once in a long while, the hour of old rain carries the other thing. Herders speak of it the way the rim-country speaks of the walker: never seen, only marked. There is a run of the flat grey slabs at the Scarp too — moss-refused, dry in any weather, spaced for a much longer stride — but here they climb: a stair of them switchbacking up the broken face to a high dry gallery behind the topmost rank of vanes, a ledge like a seat, facing out over the whole plain. No bird nests on the stair. No late-bird, bold enough to perch on the singing vanes themselves, will touch the gallery. And on certain evenings, in the rain-sound, the wall falls silent *in sequence* — a rising band of quiet climbing the cliff from foot to crown at the pace of a long, unhurried stride, every vane going mute as the band passes and speaking again behind it, until the quiet reaches the gallery and rests there a while, and the whole plain below listens to the wall listening; and then the murmur comes back down like a curtain falling open, and the old rain finishes falling, and the seat, if anyone climbs to look, is dry as it always is.

The keepers' ledgers record these evenings without comment, in a column of their own. If it is the walker, then its rounds are longer than anyone guessed, and the two places are two rooms it still keeps: the far quiet room where the world is taken in, and this open loud porch where the world is given back. If it is not a walker — if it is the same listening the pan's frogs live inside, moving along its own instrument the way a hand moves along a keyboard — then the ledgers' silence on the question is the only honest entry possible.

**Who does not live here.**

The Scarp's absences mirror the pan's, joint for joint. Nothing that needs silence survives the wall. There are no owls on the whole half-mile of cliff — magnificent nesting country, riddled with holes, and not one owl, because an owl hunts with its ears and the wall never stops talking; they keep to the far side of the plain and glare at the noise. The bats, exiled from the basin where their voices are eaten mid-cry, own the Scarp's caves in their thousands and pour out each dusk through the murmur untroubled, sonar bright, the acoustic world's refugees come home. Nothing here signals in light: the flash-language of the pan would die in the wall's long morning shadow and the plain's flat glare, and no lineage ever tried. The community of the unbothered lives three days west. This is the community of the attentive, and it was filtered just as finely: every resident is descended from ancestors who, offered a world that would not stop speaking, *leaned in*.

And one absence, over the years, has been repaired. Every few years, the rim-country says, a songbird blows off course into the pan, pours out everything it is into the hush, and leaves diminished, having sung to nothing. The keepers' ledgers say otherwise. The ledgers say that on certain spring evenings, decades after any such small tragedy, a song comes off the vanes that belongs to no bird of this country — bright, elaborate, complete, sung once by a lost migrant into what it believed was a dead and swallowing silence. The wall says it. A late-bird takes it. And somewhere in the thorn below the Scarp of Loosed Breath, in the voice of a bird that despaired a lifetime ago and a world away, the song goes on being sung — proof, delivered by the slowest post there is, that nothing given to the quiet was ever poured into nothing.

That is the shape of the whole worn country, in the end, and the two places are its lungs. The pan holds the breath; the Scarp lets it go; and between them, always, under the grass and the herds and the sleeping keepers, the breath is traveling — everything ever said in the bright basin, moving patiently through the dark of the stone toward the evening, sixty years out, when it will finally be heard.
