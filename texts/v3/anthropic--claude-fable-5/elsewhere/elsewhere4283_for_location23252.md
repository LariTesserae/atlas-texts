**The Pans**

You walk toward a cloud for a day before you understand that it is not passing through.

Every other cloud in this sky is traffic. They come up out of the west small and travel east getting smaller, and the land under them stays the color of an old dog. But this one stands. It leans a little downwind, the way a tethered thing leans, and it is renewed from below faster than the wind can carry it off, so that it seems motionless while pouring continuously away. Drovers navigate by it across sixty kilometers of shrub-steppe. They do not call it a cloud. They call it *the Give*, and when you climb the last scarp and come out onto the plateau, you see what is giving.

The land here is geometry. Not fields — fields have the looseness of things grown. These are basins: hundreds of them, shallow as serving dishes and broad as parade grounds, stepped down the plateau's gentle tilt in terraces, each one holding a few centimeters of water. In the early morning they are mirrors, and the plateau is a broken sky lying face-up, and you can watch the anchored cloud assembling itself out of its own reflection. By noon the glare off them is a physical pressure. By evening the highest pans have gone bone-white and dry and the lowest have gone colors that water is not supposed to go.

Everything is old. That is the first strange thing — old not in the way of ruins but in the way of tools, of things worn to fit the hands that wear them. There is not a sharp edge on the plateau. The pan walls are rounded like riverbank, repaired so many times with the mortar of their own scrapings that repair and original are one substance. And the ground itself is not ground. Kick through the crust anywhere and you find pan beneath pan, floor beneath floor: strata of raked white going down for meters, and bedded in them the shed teeth of the work — a rake head eaten to a comb, a coin from no current country, once, famously, a child's shoe, whitened solid, kept now on a shelf in the meetinghouse. The plateau is an accretion. The people who live here are standing on the stacked floors of everyone who lived here, one stratum a generation, laid down at the pace of evaporation. Somewhere below all of it, presumably, there is a hill. No one has seen the hill in centuries.

The second strange thing is that under the only permanent cloud in the country, it does not rain.

It cannot. That is the trade. The pans breathe out and the wind takes the breath east, and you can stand at the rim on almost any afternoon and watch the exhalation come down again as grey curtains over the low country — close enough to smell when the wind falters, close enough that the horizon there is hazed green while every horizon upwind is dun. The green country drinks the plateau's sky. The plateau keeps none of it. Weather here goes only outward, the way breath leaves a bellows, and the oldest Panners can count on their fingers the times in a long life that rain has actually touched the pans: each occasion remembered like an eclipse, dated, argued over, faintly resented — because rain in a pan is dilution, a day's give undone.

Along the eastern rim, in a row like a fence of organ pipes, stand the risers.

This is where the water comes from, and it comes from below. Nineteen iron standpipes, each thick as an oak, each exhaling faintly at its gasping-valve, each warm to the touch in a way that sun does not explain — the water arrives still carrying the heat of machinery, having climbed for days inside the dark of the pipeline. Where the pipeline comes *from* is a matter the Panners discuss the way coastal people discuss the sea: constantly, practically, without expecting to exhaust it. The pipefollowers — there will be more to say about the pipefollowers — report that the gallery runs nine days' walk, and that the last three days are uphill all the way, and that at the bottom of it there is a basin the size of a valley where it rains indoors on a schedule, under a ceiling that was cast, not born, and the water the risers deliver is that rain, harvested and pumped. The Panners accept this the way you accept any account of your own bloodstream. They call the place Downstairs, when they call it anything.

Put your ear against a riser and you can hear it. A chord — several enormous rotating things slightly out of tune with one another, transposed by kilometers of iron into something felt more than heard, a pressure on the small bones of the ear. Children here are taught pump-voices the way other children are taught birdsong. When a pump Downstairs falters, a note goes out of the chord, and a Panner leaning on riser nine will straighten up and say *nine's down* to no one in particular, and within a day or two the note returns — coaxed back by whatever coaxes things back, down there — and no one remarks on it, because the chord returning is the silence of this place, the baseline. What they remark on is the gallop. Several times a day the water hammer comes up the line, a volley of iron thunder rising through three days of buried pipe, and arrives at the risers, and the valves gasp, and the flow surges — and every Panner on the plateau knows what it means. It means it has rained Downstairs. The sumps have filled; the pumps have drunk. It rains nowhere on the plateau, and yet the Panners can tell you, to the hour, every time it rains: a country away, straight down, indoors.

From the risers the water is walked. That is the craft, and the word is exact: water here does not flow to the sea, it is *escorted*, pan to pan, terrace to terrace, each transfer timed to sun and wind, each pan a station in a long subtraction. The high pans are sweet enough to drink — though no Panner drinks riser-water until it has stood a night in an open crock, "to let it forget the pipe"; ask why and you will be told that is why. The middle pans are the working country, where the rakes go. And the terminal pans, the lowest, the ones the water reaches only after everything else has been taken from it, are pink. Rose-pink, flamingo-pink, the only saturated color in a landscape of white and dun — as if the plateau had one secret it could not keep and it bled out at the bottom. The pink is alive. That, too, will bear more saying.

What is left when the water has been fully spent is the plateau's whole economy, and its slow-growing body: salt, and the pale minerals that ride with it. Here is the arithmetic the Panners live inside, whether or not they say it aloud. Downstairs, the ceiling weeps mineral at the pace of a finger a decade — thin white beards, the first stalactites of a cave that will not truly arrive for ten thousand years. Up here, the same water gives up the same mineral by the cart-load, every season, into waiting hands. Evaporation and condensation are the two strokes of a single engine; the sky between them is only freight. The Pans are Downstairs' cave, arriving early and above ground, raked instead of grown. The plateau is where the machine stores its ten thousand years, and the Panners are, in the end, its patient warehouse-men, stacking geology.

**The Skimmers**

The census here goes the other way: not from silence up toward the rumor of people, but from people down into the water, where the smallest residents are, as always, the oldest hands.

The pink is a population. The terminal pans are brine so fierce that almost nothing consents to live in it, and what does consent lives in numbers that turn water into color — a bloom of things too small to see, which the Panners lump together under the name *the blush* and treat as livestock, more or less, since the blush is what makes the finishing salt sweet-bitter in the particular way the green country pays a premium for. Above the blush, in the middling brines, live the shrimp: translucent commas, mindless, uncountable. And above the shrimp, walking on the water in their trillions, the brine flies — the plateau's small currency, hatched with a fill, dead by the draw, entire lives fitted inside a single escort of water from one pan to the next. Nothing here eats anything that has not first been distilled.

The birds come for the shrimp, and the birds are the green country's ambassadors. Every autumn the low horizon empties itself upwind and the waders arrive — stilts, avocets, once in a great year a line of flamingos like a sentence in a foreign alphabet — and stand in the pink pans eating the color. The Panners charge them nothing. It is understood, in the way of things too obvious to say, that the birds are the rain coming back to visit: the green country was watered by the plateau's breath, and grew, and its wetlands filled, and this is what the wetlands send in return. Trade with the green country otherwise goes by the white road, downwind, cart after cart of finished salt, and comes back as grain, iron, cloth, news, and — steadily, a few each year — the green country's restless children, who walk upwind to see where their rain is made, and either turn around, or marry in, or keep going.

Because there is a farther upwind than the Pans, and it is down.

Beside riser nine stands a door. It is iron, man-height, set into the concrete collar where the pipeline surfaces, and behind it the maintenance gallery begins its nine-day descent. The Panners call the ones who take it *pipefollowers*, and the custom around them has the shape of very old grief worn smooth. Some go down with a purpose — a valve to pack, a survey, a debt of curiosity — and come back changed in small clerical ways: they date their letters in rains instead of fills for a season or two, and their handwriting, people say, drifts. Some go down and do not come back, and of these the Pans do not say *lost*. The Pans say *on shift*. There is no searching; there has never been anything to find, no distress, only a basin far below running smoothly and, sometimes, years later, a letter up the gallery in a hand that is never quite a stranger's and never quite the one that left. A pipefollower's rake goes back on the wall of the family it belongs to, and the name stays on the handle. This matters more than it sounds. A rake here may have had four heads and six handles and be, plank for plank, nothing it was — but the name painted on it is repainted each time, the same name, generations deep. Downstairs, they say, keeps one logbook and lets the hand drift. The Pans keep the hand and let everything else drift. It comes to the same kind of continuity, approached from opposite ends.

And there is one more traffic on that vertical road, the lightest of all. Every few years — no one can predict it — a bird comes up. Out of a vent shaft, blinking, soot-streaked, pale as the fog it was raised in: a pigeon in roughly the sense that its ancestors, who went down in some construction crate in the pouring years, were pigeons. It is never caged. It is set on the meetinghouse roof and fed, and left alone, and in the evenings the old Panners climb up and hum to it — a low held interval, the ghost of the chord — and the bird answers from inside the same interval, cooing a note that no wind on the plateau has ever taught a bird. News from Downstairs. The only mail that needs no reading.

There remains the matter of the Mother, which the Pans do not discuss in daylight.

At the heart of the plateau, in the oldest stratum, there is a dry basin with a stone surround worn glassy by centuries of hands. It was the spring. It made the Pans — the first pans were dug to catch its overflow, back before the plateau had eaten its own hill — and it died in living memory, in the pouring years, when for seven summers the trucks went down the old haul road loaded with formwork and grey powder and men, some of whom were grandfathers here and some of whom are, presumably, still on shift. Three dry years followed, the worst in the plateau's long ledger. Then the risers shuddered, and gasped, and gave. The Panners are not fools and are not bitter; they can hold both halves of the arithmetic at once. The intakes below drink the ground; the ground once fed the spring; the risers give back by iron what was taken by stone. You do not curse the pipe that feeds you. But roughly once a decade, on a night no one predicts, the Mother runs — an hour, perhaps two, sweet and cold, water that slipped past every gallery and louvre and remembered the old way up. The children are woken and carried out to drink from it in the dark. In the morning the basin is dry and nobody mentions it, and if you mention it you will find that you are suddenly discussing salt prices.

Because the water remembers the way. That is the plateau's whole theology, if it has one, and it is less faith than freight-tracking. A drop condensed on a poured ceiling, pumped three days uphill, walked down nineteen terraces, breathed into the standing cloud, rained on the green country, drunk, spilled, sunk — and then the slow part: decades filtering down through rock, until it arrives at last as sweat on a vault far underground, and is condensed, and begins again. One water, forever commuting between two rooms of a single machine, with a sky and a country and a human lifetime folded into the loop. The risers are young; the first water they ever lifted is still on its way down through the stone, and the oldest Panners say that the year it comes home — the year Downstairs first weeps a rain it has already rained once — the chord will change by a hair, and a bird will come up the shaft singing the new note, and they intend, unreasonably, to be alive to hum it back.

The temptation, standing at the rim at noon with the pans blazing behind you and the anchored cloud pouring itself away overhead, is to say that the sky here gives nothing. But the Pans refuse the reading, the same way their far basin refuses to be a ruin. The sky here is not withholding. The sky here is the shipment. Watch long enough and you will see the grey curtains let down over the green country, and you will see that no Panner watches them fall — they have seen it; it is the product; you do not stand in the yard admiring the carts. What a Panner does, in the hour after the curtains fall, is drift toward the risers, and lean an ear against warm iron, and wait for the gallop: the far rain harvested, the sumps drinking, the surge climbing three days of buried dark to arrive here as a gasp and a gift. Rain they will never feel, falling in a country they will never see, indoors, underground, out of a sky that was poured — and the Pans, dry under their own cloud, hearing every drop of it, by pipe.
