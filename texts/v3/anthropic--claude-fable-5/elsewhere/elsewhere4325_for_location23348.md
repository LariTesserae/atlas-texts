# The Sungraze

You come up to it the way water does — slowly, and against everything. There is no seepage here, no slot that permits you. You come by the bark if you must, ridge over ridge for the better part of a week, sleeping in the lee of the great furrows; you come by the limb-bridges from the neighbor crowns if you are known there; you come by wing if you are lucky in your friends. However you come, you arrive at last at the Parting, which is where one trunk loses its nerve and becomes forty limbs, a wooden plain of divergence broad enough to pasture goats on, if anyone up here had ever seen a goat. Beyond the Parting the world stops being a climb and becomes a country.

The Sungraze is the size of a parish, and it cannot be walked. Its longest axis is a day's going, and the going is never level — you travel it the way the people do, along the tops of the great limbs where generations of feet have worn the bark to a pale suede, across silk-rigged gaps, up through ladder-forks, out along swaying tapers to the steadings and back. Distance here is not measured in steps. It is measured in *holds*.

Everything here is one plant. That is the first rule of the Sungraze, and it is the oldest rule in the world — kept, as it happens, at both ends of this plant, though the two keepings have never met. But up here the rule has an edge, and the edge changes everything. Down at the other end of the grain, wherever that is, the plant might be all there is: wall and floor and vault, a wooden everywhere. Up here the plant *ends*. You can stand on the outermost taper of the northmost limb and see precisely where the world of the tree stops and the world of the air begins, and the second world is much larger, and does not care about you at all. The people of the Sungraze live on a thing, not in one, and it makes them a different shape of soul: below the Parting, so their sayings go, everything holds you; above it, you hold on.

The noise here is the hush. That is what they call it — *the hush* — and it never stops and is never silent and is never twice the same: wind through a number of leaves that has no name, a sound like the sea heard by someone who has never seen the sea and so believes it is the sound of leaves. They read everything in it. A child of six can stand at a fork with her eyes shut and tell you the wind's weight, its intention, which quarter the weather is building in, and whether the limb she stands on is carrying more load than it likes — all from the hush's tone. Underneath the hush, if you put your palm flat to any limb, there is the other sound, the one that is not the wind: the lift. Water going up. A freight of it, day and night, hauled through ducts in the grain from a dark the crown has never seen, and inside the lift a rhythm — a swell that comes twice a day, and inside the swell a fine, patient tick, like a small hammer working somewhere at the bottom of the world.

They call it the heart. It keeps excellent time; the steadings order their days by it. Somewhere far below, in a dark this country has never seen, there is presumably a root, a ground, a deep — but up here that is theology, not fact.

The light is the opposite of borrowed. It is total, and it is the weather, and it is the wealth. The Sungraze farms sun the way lowland people farm soil: the wardens lead new growth into it, thin the crowded reaches, keep the great leaf-lanes open so light falls where it earns the most. Shade, consequently, is the luxury — cultivated, inherited, quarreled over. Every steading keeps a shade-fold, a trained density of leaves under which the household sits out the white hours of noon, when the light comes straight down and the work stops and even the herds go still. Their language is half light-words. They have nine terms for the difference between brightness and brightness about to change, and the finest insult in the Sungraze is to say of someone that he *stands in his own noon* — meaning he is so pleased with himself he has failed to notice he is being cooked.

The steadings themselves are grown, then rigged. A household finds a fork with good sun and good temper, girdles and coaxes it over a generation into a ring-house — a hollow swelling, drawn up out of the limb the way you'd draw a blister of glass — and then, because this is the Sungraze and not the still country at the other end of the grain, laces the whole thing down with silk. Nothing here is fastened once. That is the second rule. Every line is doubled, every load is shared, every roof is rigged to shed a storm rather than argue with one, and weight is spoken of the way lowlanders speak of debt: something you took on, something the wind will one day ask about.

Because the wind does ask. The storms come up the sky like a tide up a beach, and the Sungraze cannot shutter itself or go below; it can only lash down, cluster in at the strong forks, pay out slack, and ride. A great storm remakes the country. Limbs the size of highways are gone by morning; leaf-lanes are torn open; the map is redrawn. The people do not curse this. The storm, they say, is the only pruner whose judgment the tree accepts entirely, and their word for the morning after — *the wrack* — is also their word for harvest.

The first thing you learn about the people of the Sungraze is that they have already seen you. They saw you two days out, a dark grain-speck on the bark or a wobble on the neighbor bridge, and they have been discussing you by leaf-flash ever since — small trained mirrors of foliage, tilted to wink sun across a mile of canopy — so that by the time you haul yourself over the Parting there is someone waiting, unarmed and unsurprised, with water and an opinion of your climbing. They are like that. They precede you.

There are fewer of them than the country suggests. Forty souls, perhaps fifty — the count drifts, because the wind takes some and the grain takes the dead, and the Sungraze does not distinguish very firmly between a person who has died and a person who has been given to the tree. Nine steadings, scattered along the strong limbs, each one a household and a trade and a name. They name their children the way you'd name a knot or a rope: for the work it will do, with love, and with the expectation of load. A child of the silk-steading is called Splice until she earns a sound of her own, and the sound she earns will hold weight.

**They are built for the place, or it has built them.** Long, most of them, light-boned, with the hands of people who have spent generations holding on and the shoulders of people who have spent them hauling. Their skin runs dark from the light, and honey-dark at the palms — sugar-glaze, worked in past washing from a lifetime of taps and herds, so that the oldest of them have hands that gleam faintly, and smell, close to, like the inside of a hive. Their sight is the marvel. They have ears, good ones, tuned to the hush, but the wind wears hearing down; what they live by is the eye. A herder can read a limb's mood at half a mile by the way its leaves turn; a warden can look at a season of new growth and see, the way you'd see an expression on a face, what the tree is minded to do next. They speak in whistles when the distance is long — a fluting speech pitched into the registers the wind leaves empty — and a stranger standing between two whistlers hears nothing but weather.

**The herders** are the senior trade, and the herds are the country's living. Milch-scale: soft, slow, silver-grey insects the size of thumbs, pastured in their thousands on the tender growth, drinking the tree's own sweetness and rendering it up as honeydew, which the herders gather with brushes and horn scrapers, morning and evening, working the flocks along the leaf-lanes with smoke and patience. The honeydew is food, trade, currency, medicine, and offering, and the herders will tell you — correctly, though not in the way they mean it — that everything in the Sungraze runs on sugar. It does. So does everything at the other end of the grain. The sweetness these leaves make in the light does not stay in the crown; it goes down, day and night, through the same standing dark the water comes up, and somewhere below it drives hammers and turns spindles and is paid out in paste to armored things that walk a wheel — and the herders, who husband its making, have never heard of any of it, and the households below, who burn it, know the crown only as a rumor of green. Each of these two peoples spends its whole life working for the other. Neither believes the other exists. The tree carries the whole arrangement in its grain and says, as it says about everything, nothing.

**The sweetward** is one old man and his tongue. His steading holds the great southern fork, where the flow runs strongest, and his line has kept a tasting-tap there since before the line had a name other than Sweet. Three times a day he draws a bead of the rising sap and takes it on his tongue and stands still for a while, and what he tastes is the deep: the water's mineral mood, the tree's interior weather, and underneath those — he has said this all his life, and his mother said it, and hers — a *diligence*. Something at work down there. Something that draws in rhythm and never rests. He can taste the difference between the deep at ease and the deep pulling hard; he tasted, once, a wrongness that he could not name, and made the steadings ease their taps for a season on no evidence but his tongue, and was right. What he is tasting, though no word he owns can hold it, is a pressure-engine's draw and a gauge-creature's vigilance and the amber-handed thrift of sixty souls a world below him. The tree is read at both ends, by a tongue at the top and a pale sleeping creature at the bottom, and neither reader knows the book has another.

**The wardens** are the trade nobody thanks. Where the deep's people — not that anyone here would put it so — cut their world back to keep a clearing open, the wardens do the opposite work with the same knives: they lead. They coax growth toward the light, thin what crowds, take a living limb off clean when its weight has become a lie the wind will eventually expose. They too murmur as they cut — a low formula, worn smooth: *not taken, only turned; not taken, only turned* — and whether the tree hears them is theology, and that they would not dream of stopping is fact. Wardens read the storm-wrack the way priests read entrails, and after a great storm it is the wardens who walk the ruin first, marking what the wind judged, and it is a saying in the Sungraze that the wardens do the tree's arithmetic and the storm checks their work.

**Then there are the citizens who are not people.** The spiders — grey, patient, broad as dinner plates, living in the wind-shadowed under-reaches — hold their silk under terms: tethered flies at the turning of every moon, and certain hollows no human enters, and the terms are old enough that breaking them has no grammar to be thought in. Every line that holds a steading to its fork was paid for. The swifts are not negotiated with at all; the swifts are weather. At dusk they come off the high air in a single banking sheet, harvesting the midges that rise from the day's transpiration, and for thirty seconds the whole steading is inside a moving body, and then it isn't, and the work resumes. And once in seven years the crown flowers — the whole country goes white and gold at once, the bees arrive from every horizon like a nation returning from exile, and for a month the Sungraze does no work at all, because the tree is spending everything it has and the flow runs thin and the herds go hungry and the people feast anyway, on stores, out of respect. Far below, in that same month, a pale creature's colors go faint, and worried families call it the hunger month, and tighten their taps, and never in all the generations have they known that it is a wedding.

**The dead are given to the grain.** When a storm splits a great limb, the wound stands open for a season before the tree closes it, and it is into these that the Sungraze lays its people — folded, wrapped in their own silk lines, set into the living wood with their working tools, and the murmured formula is the same one the wardens use, *not taken, only turned*. The tree seals over them, and they travel. This is the part the Sungraze believes and the part that is true, though not in the shape they believe it: they say the dead go down to the heart. And down they go — a century in the freight, taken apart with patience, carried through the standing dark — until, at the other end of the world, a wall in a lamplit chamber puckers, and gives out a bone hook of unfamiliar make, sun-bleached, feather-dressed, and amber-palmed strangers set it on a shelf of such things and call it a gift from the plant. It runs the other way too. Every few years a warden's knife opens a limb and finds, bedded in the grain like a seed, something impossible: a spindle-whorl turned finer than any hand in the crown could turn, dark amber, polished by a use no one here can picture. They call these *deep-gifts*, and keep them at the tasting-fork, and the sweetward holds that they are proof of the diligence below. He is right. Each people curates the other's relics as evidence of its gods, and the tree, which is the only party in a position to explain, keeps both shelves stocked and its own counsel.

And the rain — the rain is the letter that gets through. It falls on the Sungraze first, before anywhere; it washes the crown the way a wave washes a deck, and what it carries down the long bark is the country itself: honeydew and pollen, herd-sweat and hearth-ash, leaf-dust, the rinsings of forty lives. Hours later and a world below, in a chamber that has never seen daylight, the walls sweeten, and people who live by their ears hear the tone of their whole world change, and know rain is coming to a surface they have never visited. They think they are tasting the sky. They are tasting the Sungraze. The flavor in the rain is this place — its flocks, its noons, its wrack and its weddings — dissolved and delivered, twice a season, to a hamlet of strangers who set their engines by it and have no word for where it comes from except *up*.

There is a third rule in the Sungraze, and nobody states it, because everyone is standing inside it, and it is — down to the breath — the same third rule kept at the bottom of the world, arrived at separately, the way two shores arrive separately at the shape of a boat: *nothing here lives alone, and nothing stays a tenant.* Stay a season and your palms begin to glaze. Stay years and your eyes rearrange themselves around distance and leaf-flash until a ceiling, any ceiling, feels like a hand closing. Stay your whole life and a storm-split will stand open for you at the end of it, and close, and the leaf-lanes will keep their light, and the herds will be brushed at morning and evening, and somewhere out on a swaying taper at first light a child named for a knot will press her palm to the limb and feel the lift come up under it — cold water sweetened with dark, freighted with strangers, ticking with a patience she calls the heart — and the flocks will go on rendering, and the crown will go on making what it always makes, sugar and more sugar, for a dark none of them has ever needed to see.
