**The Decanted Town**

The lake does not keep everything. It looks like it does — the silted outlet, the sealed notch, the ice lid in winter, the whole apparatus of a place that has stopped answering letters — but water is never truly landlocked, only delayed. Under the silt that closed the old outlet lies the moraine that made this basin in the first place: a great buried ramp of glacial gravel, half a mile deep down the mountain's shoulder, and through it, grain by grain, gap by gap, the lake leaks. Not a stream. Not even a trickle you could point to. A patience. Water enters the gravel at the bottom of the lake, cold and green-lit, having spent its surface days sweeping the drowned lanes and swinging the bell, and then it goes into the dark and spends, by the town's best reckoning, three years getting down the mountain.

It does not travel alone. The gravel is inhabited the way all old darkness is inhabited — by things that gave up eyes as a bad investment. Blind amphipods, colorless, no longer than an eyelash, live their whole generations in the spaces between stones, in a country with no weather, no light, no bell, drifting downhill at the speed of the water itself. Most are born and die in transit. A few make the entire journey and arrive, blinking-white and astonished, in the light at the bottom, where they last about a minute.

The light at the bottom is the Decanted Town.

The water comes out at the foot of a cliff below the tree line, in the back of a covered wellhouse, in a pool the size of a bed — and it comes out *pouring*, all of the lake's slow leakage gathered by the mountain's interior plumbing into one continuous, muscular, glass-clear gout of cold. The pour has not stopped in living memory or in dead memory either. It is the town's foundation, its clock, its undertone, and its founder: everything else — the wellhouse, the trough, the channels, the lanes, the terraces, the belfry, the two hundred roofs — stands downhill of that pool the way an argument stands downhill of its premise.

The people who built the town came from above. This is not a legend; it is a property line. They came down over three or four generations as the lake rose on their old hamlet — no rout, no exodus, just a house moved per decade, a family per silting-season, the whole village descending the mountain the way honey descends a jar wall. They knew where they were going before they went. They had always known the spring was their lake come round again; they had watched their grandmothers descend to it for the good water even when the hamlet still stood in air. So when the water took the streets, they followed the water. They took their dead, their tools, their names, and the two smaller bells. They left the great bell, which was too heavy to want, and one other thing, which was the past.

The town they built remembers the hamlet the way a body remembers a posture. The same dry-stone seriousness, the same terraced lanes stepped into the slope, the same square with the same pattern of trough and channel — a person who could swim the drowned lanes and then walk these would feel their feet already knowing the turns. But there is one great difference, and it governs everything: *this town believes in drainage.* Every street has its cut channel, and every channel is scoured, inspected, argued over, and cleared, obsessively, ritually, by law and by temperament. The town's founding trauma was a blocked outlet. Its civic religion, accordingly, is the open one. Twice a year the whole population turns out with hooks and shovels for the clearing of the channels, and the year's honors go to whoever finds the worst obstruction. No one now alive could tell you with any precision what happened to the old hamlet — "the lake took it," they say, with a shrug that closes the subject — but the knowledge survives below the level of story, in the marrow, as a horror of silt. Somewhere above them, in four fathoms of green light, slate-colored crayfish are keeping the gutters of the town that taught them this. Neither maintenance crew has ever heard of the other. The inheritance simply split at the waterline and continued on both sides.

The spring is the town's newspaper, and it arrives three years late. This is the strangest and most beloved fact of the place. The water keeps the seasons — a delay of whole years leaves the calendar intact — so the spring runs the lake's weather, only aged. Its readers, and the whole town reads it, know the signs:

— A long cold surge, arriving in early summer, means a heavy snowfall on the heights three winters gone. The famous proof of the delay is famous precisely: a snow that buried the pass to the eaves arrived at the wellhouse three years afterward as a month of water so cold it made the trough groan, and the elders timed it, and the number has been civic property ever since.

— A faintly green week each autumn — a cast in the water, a taste like the inside of a leaf — is the lake's turnover come down, the drowned town's loudest season delivered as flavor. The green week is when the bells are rung longest, no one remembers why.

— A whitening. The water is hard — it comes down carrying dissolved stone, the chalk of a century of grazed snail shells, the mineral sweat of the old granite — and it *builds*. Kettles grow pale linings. Spoons left in the trough acquire a skin. The wellhouse spout has grown, over the generations, a smooth lip of flowstone, a millimeter a decade, which the springkeepers polish and would sooner lose a hand than chip. "The water is a mason," the town says, approvingly, and does not follow the thought to its conclusion — which is that the old hamlet is emigrating. Dissolved above, deposited below, grain by patient grain, the substance of the drowned village is being carried down the mountain and reassembled as stone in the town its people built. Up there, the plant has kept the shapes and let the stone go. Down here, the stone is arriving without its shapes. The two places are, very slowly, exchanging bodies.

— And a shiver. This one the town speaks of quietly. On still nights, alone in the wellhouse, a person standing with their feet on the flowstone will sometimes feel — not hear, feel — a long low pressure pass through the pool and through their shins, a note too deep and too slow to be a sound, lasting most of a minute. It comes at no fixed hour, roughly hourly. The springkeepers call it the bell's breath and will discuss it no further. Whether a bronze tone can really travel down half a mile of drowned gravel is a question the town has never submitted to anyone qualified to ruin it.

**Who Lives in the Decanted Town**

The town holds perhaps four hundred people and would tell you, if asked, that it is nothing special — a spring town, a stone town, a place with strong opinions about gutters. It does not know it is a second draft. Its residents live there the way the char live in the drowned lanes above: seriously, territorially, each keeping a station their family has kept for years — this house beneath the second terrace, that workshop in the lee of the trough — passing through doorways with the unhurried entitlement of people whose entitlement is four generations deep. They observe a kind of curfew too. When the bells ring the evening peal, the whole town settles a degree lower into itself, the way a congregation sits.

The bells deserve the first entry, because of the hole in them. The belfry — squat, corbelled, four stories, built to the old proportions by masons working from their hands' memory rather than any drawing — carries three bells on a yoke made for four. The two smaller bells came down from the hamlet; the third was cast here, in celebration, when the hundredth roof went on. The fourth mount is empty and is kept empty, oiled, maintained, its rope hung and coiled, and every peal is rung *around* it: the ringers leave a rest, a full silent beat, where the great bell's note would fall. Ask them why and they will say that is how the peal goes. It is the truest thing in the town, that silence — a note held open for a bell that rings anyway, hourly, four fathoms under a lake three years upstream, into water that carries the sound to every fish in the basin and, if the springkeepers are right, down through the mountain to a pool where an old woman feels it in her shins. When the peal sounds, the swifts that nest in the belfry pour from its four windows simultaneously, scatter to the rooftops, and sift back in over the following hour. No one ordained this either.

The springkeeper is the town's cistern-char, though the town would not put it that way. The wellhouse is the cleanest and most defended room in the town, and she has it — the office always falls to someone old, and the current keeper is old enough that her opinions have gone vague at the edges and hardened at the core, one eye pearled, her authority total. She does not patrol. She does not need to. Young people come to the wellhouse door and hang there a while and go away. Her actual duties are three: keep the spout's stone lip polished, keep the pool's mouth clear, and read the water — temperature, taste, cast, hardness — announcing its news to the square each morning like a postmistress sorting a mail that left its sender three years ago. When a blind white amphipod comes through, she lifts it out on a wooden spoon and sets it in the trough, where the char get it. It is not kindness exactly. It is protocol for a traveler who made the whole journey.

The char in the trough and the millpond are the town's founding act made flesh. Fish cannot pass through half a mile of gravel; these were *carried* — a bucket of fry brought down the mountain by the last family to leave the hamlet, on the recorded grounds that the water should know its own fish. The strain has held ever since, deep-bodied, pewter-flanked, rose-bellied in autumn, stationed at the spring mouth and the channel junctions with proprietary calm. They are the only residents who live in the old water and the new town at once, and they comport themselves accordingly, like consuls.

The dredgers are a guild in everything but paperwork. They keep the channels, and keeping the channels has long since stopped being a chore and become a temperament: they patrol by night as often as by day, they meet at the junctions and have brief unarmored disagreements about precedence, they know every channel's grade to the finger-width, and the gutters of the Decanted Town are, in consequence, the cleanest gutters in any town anywhere, maintained by people who have confused inheritance with employment so thoroughly that the result is indistinguishable from civic pride. Their patron creature — carved, crudely, on the guild's lintel — is a crayfish. They believe they chose it because crayfish live in clean water. The lintel does not correct them.

The masons still work dry stone, and they train their apprentices with an exercise older than the town: each beginner must build, before touching a wall, a small closed vessel of fitted fragments — granite grains, shell chalk, slivers of slate — no piece larger than a thumbnail, no mortar, watertight. They call it *the little house* and cannot say where the exercise came from. At the bottom of a square four fathoms deep and three years upstream, by the hundreds, caddis larvae are doing the same assignment in the same local stone, and have never missed a term.

The cemetery is the oldest quarter, older than any building, because the dead arrived first. They were carried down grave by grave as the lake rose, and their headstones — cut in the hamlet, in the hamlet's granite, by the hamlet's chisels — stand in rows below the town like an advance party that secured the ground. Once a year, in the green week, each grave gets a cup of spring water poured at its head. The stated reason is respect. The unstated shape of the act is plainer: the dead lived their whole lives beside that lake, and once a year the town brings them a drink of it — three years old, filtered through a mountain, tasting faintly of leaves — so that even underground they can keep up with the news from home.

Beside the trough in the square there is a small carved niche — a shrine, or a lamp-rest, copied stone for stone from one nobody living has seen — and the town keeps moss in it. Deliberately. It is watered by hand, shaded by custom, and it is the only moss in the whole scrubbed, drained, dredged, silt-fearing town that is not scoured away on sight. Ask why the niche keeps its coat and you will be told that the niche keeps its coat. Up the mountain, in the drowned square, herds of great snails graze every wall bare and leave one niche untouched, its moss soft and undisturbed, and whether the two customs are one custom split at the waterline, or two accidents grown into each other's shape, is not a question either congregation can hear, let alone answer.

Visitors connect the worlds. The dipper that works the lake's inlet stream works the spring's outflow too, the same bird or its indistinguishable heir, commuting over the ridge with nothing to declare. And once in a few years an otter finds the lake above, hunts it hollow for a fortnight, crosses the ridge, and comes down the outflow into the town's millpond — and for a few days the Decanted Town endures what the Steeped Hamlet just endured, doorways used by a predator, char scattering, the springkeeper standing furious in the wellhouse door — until the otter exhausts the easy hunting and moves on downvalley, leaving both settlements, in sequence, to reform their ranks with the same relieved fussiness. It is the only living thing that visits both, and it takes away nothing but fish and the knowledge, held briefly and without interest, that the two places are one place folded over a mountain.

There are no ghosts here either — or rather, the town is built of them, if a ghost is a shape that outlives its reason. The rest in the peal, the little house, the mossed niche, the horror of silt, the char in the trough, the cup poured on the graves: every one is an archway of pure habit holding the form of a doorway whose stone dissolved long ago. Up the mountain, on the upper lane of the drowned hamlet, there is an arch of pure vegetation doing exactly the same work, and fry shelter inside it by the thousand every spring. Down here, the customs shelter the town.

And between the two, always, the water. Tonight's pour left the lake three autumns ago; it swept the lanes, it rang against the bronze, it steeped in the green town, and it has spent the years since in the dark, in the company of blind travelers, carrying a dissolved gram of the old village down toward the wellhouse spout, where it will set as stone under an old woman's polishing hand. The hamlet does not know it is remembered. The town does not know it is remembering. The mountain holds them one above the other like two rooms of a single house — one steeped, one decanted, one loud with water and empty of people, one full of people and quiet but for the pour — and the peal goes out each evening with its held silence in the middle, a note-shaped hole, into which, four fathoms up and three years back, a bell nobody rings is always, patiently, ringing.
