# The Drowned Hundred

Follow the water.

The Risen Quarter drinks its own atmosphere, but it does not keep what it drinks. The cisterns hold a third and surrender the rest; the weeping lintels drip, the drip finds the carved channels, the channels find the base, and the base gives it to the saddle, where it gathers into a cold thread of stream and starts downhill. It goes down for days. It joins other water and forgets itself, the way water does, through gorges and then foothills and then a long slowing country where the rivers stop hurrying because they have begun to suspect there is nowhere left to hurry to.

They are right. At the end of all that falling there is a basin, broad and shallow-rimmed, a day's walk across, and the rivers pour into it and do not pour out. And in that basin, standing in dark water on ten thousand timber legs, there is a town that is going down into the earth.

Not quickly: a hand's width in a good year, less in most. The motion is invisible to anyone watching, obvious to anyone returning. The ground beneath the water is warm at the basin's center, faintly — the warmth of a living animal rather than a fire — and it is going somewhere. The people of the basin will tell you where, if you stay long enough to be told things, and their answer is the oldest song they have.

This is the only fact you need to understand the place. Everything else follows from it.

## The Town That Climbs

If the Risen Quarter is a city read from bottom to top in the wrong order, the Drowned Hundred is one read in the right order and mourned for it. Here the oldest work is at the bottom, and the bottom is underwater.

The houses stand on piles and grow upward the way trees do, ring by ring, out of necessity. Each generation adds a story as the lowest story drowns; a family's whole history is legible in the joinery from a boat — the crisp new work at the top, then the weathered middle floors where the family actually lives, then the moss-blackened waterline, and then, descending into the dark, the drowned floors: rooms with their doorways still square, their hearths still laid, visible on clear days through five fathoms of still water. Eels use the old streets. Fish move through bedrooms with the unhurried authority of tenants who have paid.

This is the precise inversion of the Quarter's arrangement, and the inversion cuts. The Quarter's past rises overhead into wind and light, empty but visitable — an elder can climb a ladder and lay a hand on the room she carved as a girl. The Hundred's past is right there, thirty feet down, perfectly preserved in cold fresh water, every edge as clean as the day it drowned — and no living hand will ever touch it again. The elders of the Hundred visit their old rooms too. They do it lying flat on the floorboards of the rooms above, looking down through gaps in the planking, on evenings when the water is glass.

Where the Quarter is loud, the Hundred is quiet in the particular way of open water — a quiet that carries. Sound travels a mile across the basin without losing anything. The town's voice is creak and drip, the flat clap of small waves under floors, the knock of a boat against a post, oars, and — under everything, occasional, unremarkable — a knocking that does not come from boats. We will get to that.

## Materials and Hands

The Hundred is everything the Quarter is not, materially, which is the first hint that they are two ends of one thing. The Quarter has infinite stone and no metal. The Hundred has iron and no stone at all.

The iron comes out of the drowning margins — bog ore, dredged in rust-colored lumps from the wet edges where land is becoming lake, smelted on the rim where fire can be trusted. The sinking renews it; the basin makes ore the way the saddle makes rock. So the Hundred's tools are good dark iron, its nails are iron, its pot-hooks and eel-spears are iron, and a plain stone — any stone — is an heirloom. There are perhaps forty true hearthstones in the whole town, passed down, named, fought over gently at marriages. A number of them are pale, the color of bone left in water, and warm-looking even when cold, and carved with small marks that nobody in the Hundred can read.

Eighty meters up the eastern face of a tooth of new rock, many days' walk into the sky, the same marks sit overhead in the oldest halls, where they are read as the names of the first carvers. Neither place knows this. Both places dust them.

The work here, like the work there, is a matter of timing rather than force — only the deadline comes from below instead of behind. The young trade is pile-driving: standing waist-deep on the sinking margins, setting timber into ground that accepts it too easily, working in a permanent stoop that is the exact lowland cousin of the waterline carver's crouch. The old trade is *closing* — sealing the lowest floor of a house in the season before the water takes it, truing it, making it right. Closing is considered the contemplative trade. A closer may spend a month on one floor. The floor has nothing but time, and is losing it, downward, as they work.

## Fullness

Here is the strange arithmetic of the Drowned Hundred, and it is the Quarter's arithmetic held up to a mirror: it has the people of a town and the rooms of a hamlet.

Some two thousand live here — the Calling never quite agrees with itself — and every year, every house loses its lowest dry room and owes itself a new highest one. Dry floor is the scarcest thing in the basin. Three generations to a room is common; a child who sleeps alone has married well in advance. The Quarter is hollow on purpose, vast volumes per living soul; the Hundred is packed to the rafters it is perpetually forced to raise, and you cannot walk an hour through it without meeting forty people, all of whom know your business, most of whom are your business.

Why do they stay? The rim is right there — a half day's row, a scramble, and the Standing World, which is what the Hundred calls everything that isn't sinking. Ask three residents and you get three answers. Because the sinking is the wealth: the warm water thick with fish, the ore that renews, the fine gray clay — leaving would be rowing away from the only generous ground in the world to go scratch at ground that gives nothing because it surrenders nothing. Because the dead are underfoot, and a house is owed its weight. And because the town would go down anyway, blank and unaccompanied, and no one can bear the thought of all that good ground going under un-lived-on.

The Quarter carves because stopping feels like death. The Hundred stays for the same reason, pointed the other way.

## Water and Ground

The Quarter's problem is catching water; the Hundred's is surviving its generosity. The rivers come in and do not leave except as mist, and the mist is constant — the basin smokes. Seen from the rim at evening, the whole town floats between two darknesses, its lamps doubled in the water, the mist standing over the center in a column.

The center is the Deep Eye: the drowned site of the first town, the oldest streets, now far down in water that is warm the whole year — warm at the bottom, where the ground is, with that animal warmth that does not belong to the sun. The Eye never freezes. In the hardest winter, when the margins are white and the Standing World is iron, the Eye stands open and steaming, and midges dance over it in columns, and this one fact feeds the strangest residents the Hundred has, of whom more shortly.

The growing things are arranged by depth as the Quarter's are arranged by height, and read the same way: as time. Bare new timber at the tops of the houses, too fresh for anything to hold. Moss in black pelts at the waterline. And below, on the drowned levels, a pale luxuriance — soft colorless meadows over what were fields, white weed streaming from what were doorposts — the past blooming, untouchably, in the dark. The Hundred also farms, on woven rafts of reed and soil that ride the water and therefore never sink: the gardens hold their height forever while the town goes down past them, which makes the gardens, along with the birds, the only still things in the basin.

## What Lives Here

Everything, in the water: fish in warm crowds, eels running the old streets on their own errands, frogs in the reed margins in numbers that make the spring nights roar. The Hundred is as thick with small life as the Quarter is empty of it.

And a colony of dark swifts, perhaps thirty birds, that arrives when the first frost stands on the rim and leaves when the reeds flower, and spends the winter screaming over the Deep Eye, eating the midges the warmth raises, nesting in the newest, highest stories of the houses. The Hundred calls them *ours* and counts them in the Calling. It does not know — how could it — that a high cold saddle many days upriver counts the same thirty birds in an autumn tally and also calls them a calendar; that these are the only living things in the world that know both places; that in the Quarter the colony migrates slowly downward through a rising city and in the Hundred slowly upward through a sinking one, and that both impressions are wrong, because the swifts are simply holding still — keeping their one true altitude above the water and the ground while two towns, at a hand's width a year, pass through them in opposite directions like slow weather.

## Time

Nothing here happens fast, and everything happens once. Rooms are raised, lived in, emptied downward, closed, and drowned, in that order, always. A builder may set a sill as an apprentice and close that same floor as an elder, over her parents, the floor having sunk to meet her in the meantime. The town is a clock that dismantles itself downward, a record that writes itself into dark water, quiet, warm, crowded to the eaves —

and underneath everything, patient as breath, the ground keeps going down.

Where does it go? The Hundred's oldest song is short and its people sing it at closings. The ground is a traveler, it says. The road is long and warm. What the water takes is not kept; it is carried; and at the far end of the deep road there is high air, and a white door, and the dead of the Hundred come up out of the earth in a country of wind.

Sing it or don't. But note, before dismissing it, the sums. A hand's width a year, less in most, at both ends of the world. A warmth that belongs to no fire, at both ends. Ground going down here the color of drowned earth, and stone coming up there the color of bone left in water. The world balances its books somewhere, in the deep rock, at its own pace. The Hundred believes it knows which entry it is.

# Who Lives in the Drowned Hundred

Count them and the census is long and quarrelsome: two thousand people give or take, thirty-odd swifts, fish beyond counting and beneath it, the dead, and one resident who is heard through the floor and never seen. The Hundred has people enough for a small city and floors enough for a village, and not everyone who is counted breathes.

The counting itself is a ceremony, and it is the Quarter's census turned upside down. On the dawn of the first spring flood, every household calls its number across the water — boat to boat, floor to floor, the numbers relayed inward over the basin in the flat carrying quiet — and a keeper standing at the **Splice** tallies them aloud. The Splice is a single enormous post, generations old, extended upward with scarfed joints as it sinks, and into it the keeper cuts the year's flood-notch. The record descends. On clear days you can read the flood history of the Hundred straight down the Splice into the water, notch below notch below notch, the oldest years far down in the pale meadows, still legible, forever out of reach. The Quarter's numbers climb into the sky; the Hundred's dive. Both columns are permanent. Neither can be revised. The two towns have this in common above all: they live inside their own records and cannot get out.

## The Three Answers, Embodied

The three reasons for staying have settled, over generations, into three temperaments — not clans; two thousand people crowded onto shrinking floors cannot afford clans any more than forty can — but three tables, three styles of work, three ways of holding an oar.

The **Takers** hold that the sinking is a gift and the only sin is to let a gift stand. The water gives fish, ore, clay, reed; refusing the gift insults the giver, and the giver is directly underfoot and should not be insulted. Their idiom for any wasted chance — a missed tide, a girl not courted, a warm morning slept through — is *letting it stand*, because standing still is the local image of death; the Standing World is called what it is called for a reason, and the reason is not admiration. Taker houses are rich, loud, and slightly ramshackle, raised fast in green timber, and there are a great many of them.

The **Weighted** stay because the house is owed its dead. They are the closers, mostly, and the finest joiners, and theirs is the custom that visitors find hardest to watch: when the Weighted close a floor, they do not strip it. They furnish it. A made bed. A bowl on the table. The room set in order, as for a guest, before the last plank goes down and the water rises through the seams. Asked for whom, the Weighted look downward, which is the past, which is also, here, a kind of destination.

The third table has no settled name and would refuse one, but everyone calls them the **Wake**, because that is what they keep. A wake is what a boat leaves; a wake is what you sit for the dying; a wake is refusing to sleep while something goes. The Wake are all three. They sing at closings; they wrote the old song and keep writing it, verse by verse, at the rate of about one verse a generation; they sit up nights on the lowest dry floors with their palms flat on the boards, keeping the ground company. The Wake produce most of the Hundred's children and most of its walkers — the ones who one day row to the rim and go up into the Standing World and don't come back. This is generally agreed to be the same trait expressing itself twice, and the Quarter, if it could hear the observation, would recognize it word for word.

## The Upfolk

Among the two thousand there are a few families — six houses, perhaps forty people — called the Upfolk, and they are the Hundred's living seam with the mountain, though neither end of the seam knows it is sewn.

They came down long ago; that much even they remember. They came carrying stones — the pale warm-looking hearthstones with the unreadable marks — and they came carrying habits. The Upfolk are the Hundred's potters, because they have hands that read softness: an Upfolk potter tests clay by pressing a thumbnail into it and listening to what comes back, a skill with no local explanation, taught thumb to thumb down the generations, and they will do it for you if asked, and they will do it if not asked. They stamp their wares with pressed marks — lineage glyphs, ownership signs — and their oldest stamps match, exactly, the mason's marks in the abandoned halls at the top of the Risen Quarter, the ones read there as the names of the first carvers.

And the Upfolk keep one belief the way other people keep tools: that somewhere above, in the high air, rooms are being kept for them. Sealed rooms. Finished to a fault, hearth-niche in every chamber, waiting. The other tables of the Hundred find this touching and absurd — a people drowning by inches, certain that somewhere they are owed a dry city. Every few generations an Upfolk walker takes the belief seriously and goes up the rivers to find out. Three have gone in the memory of the Splice. None came back, which the Upfolk hold as proof of reception, and which is, as it happens, exactly what it is: for in a high saddle very far away there is an autumn tally that has, three times in its history, gone up by one, and a lineage of Keepers who each time unsealed a room stamped *held*, and each time reported, as confirmation beyond argument, that the stranger had knocked before entering. Two knocks, a pause, two knocks.

## The Eyekeeper

One person lives out on the Deep Eye, alone, at anchor over the drowned first town.

The position is old and always filled, though never by appointment; someone simply rows out, usually in middle age, usually after a closing, and the others adjust their courtesies. The current Eyekeeper has held the water for eleven years. The duties are self-assigned and unvarying: sound the Eye's depth each season and call the figure ashore; keep a lamp burning at the raft's edge through every night of the year, so that the oldest street in the world is never entirely in the dark above it; and once a season, drifting slowly over the sunken town, speak the names of its streets aloud, one by one, so that each is said by a living voice at least four times a year. The Quarter's Tenant touches; the Hundred's Eyekeeper says. It is the same office. Neither has heard of the other.

The Eyekeeper comes ashore for the Calling, calls out *one* in a voice gone strange and flat from talking mostly over open water, eats at somebody's table, and rows back before dark. The children are forbidden to row out to the raft and therefore row out, and are not punished, because every Eyekeeper was once a child who rowed.

## The Children

There are hundreds, this being the Hundred, and each got the same gift at birth: a small notch cut into the family's main post, at the height of a parent's reach, on the day they were named. The notch sinks with the post; the child rises with the years; for the first decade the child chases their own beginning downward, watching it descend toward the waterline, and then one spring the flood touches it, and that is the day, by custom, they choose a trade. No one administers this. The water administers it. The stone always wins in the mountains; the water always wins here; the children of both places are raised by the same patient arithmetic wearing different clothes.

Their games are horizontal and wet: they race shallow boats down the old street-lines, they knock messages through house-posts in a code the adults pretend not to remember inventing, and they commit the Hundred's only real crime, which is diving the drowned floors — down through the cold clear fathoms on a dare, to touch a sunken lintel, to bring up a bowl the Weighted set out for nobody. The punishment is to be rowed out by an elder over a particular drowned room and made to look down at the thing the elder stole from it as a child, returned the same week and lying there still, in its meadow, absolutely permanent, and to be told: *think about what you want underfoot for the rest of your life.* Some children stop. The ones who don't join the Wake.

## The Dead

The dead of the Hundred are its wealth and its ballast, and every house stands on its own.

A body is carried down, not up — down to the lowest dry floor, in the season of its closing if the seasons are kind, and laid in the room the Weighted have set in order: the made bed, the bowl, the lamp lit until the last plank shuts it in. Then the dead begin their journey. There is a decade or two when you live directly above your particular dead, when their sealed floor is your foundation and you cross it daily, and this is held to be the proper arrangement of a household. Then the water rises through the town and the dead sink out of lamp-reach, down past the moss line, down into the pale meadows, down toward the warm — a hand's width in a good year, less in most, which is to say: traveling.

Because that is the article of faith the whole basin is built on. The dead of the Hundred are not kept; they are carried. The Wake sing it at every closing — the long warm road, the high air at the far end, the white door — and the elders, who have spent lifetimes watching the ground keep every other promise it ever made, see no reason to doubt this one. If the song is right, they point out, then the dead of the Hundred have been arriving somewhere for a very long time now, coming up slowly through the roots of the world into wind and light; and whatever country receives them had better be a courteous one.

## The Knocking

Which brings us, last, to the resident with no notch and no name.

The floors of the Hundred knock. Up through the water, up through the closed planks, into the soles of the feet: two knocks, a pause, two knocks, answering loud sounds from above. It is old here — old past remark. A grandmother answers it without looking up from her mending. Children answer it in rhythm games. The theories are these, in descending order of how comfortable they make people: that it is timber swelling; that it is eels; that it is the dead, keeping the custom on their way down, letting the house know the journey goes well; that it is the ground itself, which has, after all, been knocked to politely by this town for as long as there has been a town, and has had every opportunity to learn.

The Upfolk say nothing on the question, and knock back most carefully of all.

Here is what the Hundred cannot know, and what can be said in its place. The deep rock has a current in it, and the current has a direction: down here, up there. Whatever rides that current — ground, warmth, the dead, or something being slowly born the whole length of it — passes under the Hundred's floors first, for centuries, being answered the entire way. It gets a long education in courtesy before it ever reaches the roots of anything else. So if, at the far end of the road, at the base of a bone-white tooth in a high cold saddle, a knocking has lately been heard in the deep stair-shafts — young, uneven, but improving year over year, the way an apprentice improves — the Hundred, told of it, would not be surprised. It would only nod, and say what it has always said, which is that the cost of politeness is nothing, and that nothing sent down the long warm road is ever lost; and that whatever is coming up out of the earth at the other end of the world, whatever it turns out to be when it is finished being born, it will arrive already knowing the custom — because the first thing it ever heard, at this end, from the very beginning, going down through the permanent dark beneath the floors of the Drowned Hundred, was an answer.
