**The Command**

You arrive the way the water arrives: lifted, and on schedule.

Everything about the Command is delivered. The land itself was delivered — soil manufactured in windrowed lots somewhere else, trucked in, spread in courses over the old hardpan like masonry, inoculated from drums — and the trees were delivered, and the people were delivered, paid out in acreage when their contracts with the works closed, eleven years ago, twelve. What was here before was a dry fan of country tilting away from a line of low pale buildings. What is here now is the same fan with water in its veins: a hundred kilometres of terraces and windbreaks and laterals opening outward from the head of the system like a hand from a wrist, a delta run in reverse — engineered, deliberate, a continental machine for spending water.

Because that is what the Command is. Three hundred metres beneath it, under the hardpan and the rock, lies the sea it draws on — the poured basin, the pillars, the tilted light, the whole cold banked volume of the Catchment — and the Command is where that patience is finally cashed. The people up here rarely say the name. They say the water comes *from the bank*, and mean it as idiom, and are more right than they know.

**Twice a day the water comes, and the Command is a clock with fields for a face.** It announces itself before it arrives. Along the trunk canal — the Stem, everyone calls it — stand white standpipe towers at kilometre intervals, air valves at their crowns, and when the transfer turbines spin up in the deep west, the surge drives a slug of air up the penstocks ahead of the water, and the standpipes begin, one after another, to whistle. A thin ascending note runs down the fan, tower to tower, crossing the Command in under a minute. Everything that lives here knows it. Herons lift off the laterals. Gates that were going to be opened get opened. Children stop what they are doing and start what they were told to do. And then the water itself comes down the Stem as a low glass step half a metre high, a tame bore with birds riding the air above it, folding into the laterals one by one, and the Command drinks.

Understand what the whistle is. It is the morning bell, heard from the other side. The rolling thunder that crosses the flooded basin pillar to pillar — the sound the schools down there brace for, the sound one woman in a valve tower named — arrives up here, through three hundred metres of rock and one column of driven air, as a whistle and then a tide. It is the same event. It has two congregations, and neither has ever seen the other, and both move before it arrives.

**The water comes up white, and the fields are grateful for it.** The basin below is young — poured concrete still curing, still sweating lime into its own weather — and every litre lifted out of it carries a little of that chalk. Twice a day the Command is irrigated with milk. The farmers call it that, *the milk*, and the manufactured soils, mixed acidic on purpose, sweeten under it season by season: the ground up here is being limed by a curing sea, dosed by the youth of a structure it will never see. Somewhere in the works' documentation this is probably accounted for, an engineered coincidence, chemistry balanced against chemistry across three hundred vertical metres. Somewhere it is also scheduled to end. Concrete finishes its long exhale in decades, not centuries. There will be a last white tide, some ordinary afternoon, and nobody will know it was the last until years after, when the old people start saying — and being disbelieved — that the water used to come the colour of chalk.

**Everything here is young, the way an orchard is young.** The windbreak poplars stand in rows a dozen years tall, still narrow, still visibly planted. The fence wire is bright. The first orchard blocks have only just come into bearing, and fruit is still slightly astonishing — people up here hand each other apples the way people elsewhere hand each other news. Nothing has had time to soften; nothing has had time to fail; the whole fan has the crispness of a thing recently made and the faint hum of a thing still being made, because the Command is not finished. It is growing outward, terrace by terrace, into the dry.

**At its outer edge is the Verge, and the Verge is where the world keeps its only old things.** Beyond the last laterals: hardpan, salt shimmer, and the towns from before — river towns, low and sun-eaten, doorsills worn hollow by generations of feet, standing along a bed of pale stones that used to be a river. The river was swallowed upstream, behind the works, behind the screens, in the first years — taken indoors, banked underground where the sun couldn't tax it — and its bed is a road now, and its towns emptied into the works' crews, and the crews emptied into the Command. Half the fan's families walked in off that dead river. So the Command advances toward the ruins of its own grandparents, a young green country closing slowly on the old dry one that paid for it, and on still evenings you can stand on the last berm and see both at once: the bright wire and the hollowed doorsills, twelve years old and two hundred, with nothing in between.

The river is not entirely unremembered. In the formwork seams of the basin floor, far below the fan, lie eels that came in as elvers in the first flood-years — eels that were alive in that river when it had water in it and sky over it. They are the last things anywhere that hold it as a place. Up here it is a road. Down there, in whatever way an eel keeps anything, it is still a river.

**At the head of the fan, where the standpipes gather and the pump barrels come warm out of the ground, lie the glasslands.** Fenced squares of land, hectares each, floored with heliostats that track the sun all day in slow unison — the whole square tilting together like a field of one crop — feeding light down into conduit, where it is conditioned, steadied, banked, and released below as that eerie constant: mid-morning, forever. The stencils on the fences read COMMAND AREA 114 — LIGHT WORKS — NO ENTRY, and the paint is crisp, and no one enters, and no one needs to. But stand at the wire at noon, with the whole square blazing, and know what you are looking at: the ceiling of a pasture. Beneath every glitter-square, three hundred metres down, a stadium-wide column of this exact light falls through green-grey water, and plankton blooms in it, and bait-balls the size of apartment buildings come to graze. The Command tithes its sunlight, and the sea beneath it grows fields with the tithe. Farmers here donate light to fish they will never see, and receive, twice daily, water in exchange. Neither party has been told about the arrangement. It works anyway.

**No one works at the works.** That should be said plainly, because it is the thing visitors misunderstand. The gates open themselves. The mirrors steer themselves. The whistle needs no one to blow it. The people of the Command are not the system's operators; they are its neighbours, settled deliberately downstream of a machine that runs without witnesses — up here exactly as it does below. They farm what it delivers. They set their lives by its clock: *first water* and *second water* divide the day the way bells divide a monastery's, and the vocabulary of the fan is a hydraulic liturgy — on the water, off the water, drawn against the bank. At night, lying on a canal berm, you can feel the ground humming faintly underfoot, sixty cycles, the pump galleries breathing in the deep rock, and the children of the Command put their ears to the standpipes and tell each other the old lie that is not a lie: that there is a sea under the world, and it is asleep, and the hum is its snoring.

**Once in a great while, the Command drinks all at once.** A new area commissioned — soil laid, laterals cut, gates hung — and on flood-up day the whole fan's allocation is borrowed for one long afternoon and sent to ground that has never been wet. The wetting front crawls down the raw laterals dark and glistening, and children race it, and the settlers walk behind the water into their own land. Below, this is a drawdown event: the level falling metres, the currents braiding, every school in twenty kilometres streaming toward the intake roar, the whole basin waking into one machine. When the sea under the world breathes, up here it means a christening. The two facts are the same fact. Nobody up here knows what their christenings look like from underneath, which is: thunder, migration, the snow of scales through tilted light.

And the water is not only spent. It returns. The Command drains through collector lines back down toward the intakes, seepage and tailwater filtering home carrying the signature of the land — silt, pollen, seeds, once a drowned lamb — so that on the floor of the basin, near the eastern grilles, the first sediment is settling: a thin dusting of the Command, grain by grain, on kilometre slabs that have never had geology. The fan is laying down, without knowing it, the seabed the Catchment will someday have. Among the grains are seeds — grass, poplar, apple — filed into the formwork seams in the dark, a dormant library of the country above, shelved unread on the bottom of a concrete sea.

**Who lives in the Command**

Start small, because the Command did — smaller even than the sea below, whose first residents at least arrived on their own.

The ground here has a delivery date. The soil bacteria came out of drums; the fungi came as powdered inoculant dusted onto root balls; the earthworms came by the crate, and there are people on the fan who remember the smell of the crates. For its first seasons the manufactured soil was a body without a metabolism — chemistry pretending to be dirt — and its slow waking into actual ground, the mycorrhizae stitching the windbreak rows together underground, the worm channels, the darkening, is the Command's quietest and largest event, still underway. It is being helped from below. Every white tide adjusts it. Two curing bodies, the basin's concrete and the fan's ground, are sweetening one another across three hundred metres of rock, each one's youth the medicine for the other's, on a schedule no one set.

**The canals bloom green after every tide,** because the pumps do not lift water only. They lift passengers — the plankton of the light-shaft pastures rides the impellers up surprisingly well — and so each surge arrives faintly alive, seeding the laterals with drifting hazes that the Command's small fish mob within the hour. The ditches of the fan are fertilized by fields three hundred metres under them, farms with no farmer feeding farms with too many, and nobody up here has ever asked why the water itself is nourishing. It is simply the known character of the bank's water: white, cool, and generous.

And sometimes the pumps lift more. **A works-fish** — a carp that came through the turbines and lived — turns up in the Stem a few times a year: scarred in long clean arcs, pale, strangely elongated, built wrong for a canal. Nobody eats a works-fish. You return it to the water and you don't say much. The farmers read the pallor and the length as damage, as what the machines do to a river fish, and they are wrong: they are looking at the basin's new body plan, the shape forty generations of open-volume crossings have been building in the dark below — the future of the carp, surfacing occasionally, and being pitied.

**In spring the glass runs.** Elvers — threads of glass, palm-length, translucent — come up the wet margins of the canals at night in their thousands, climbing the fan against the flow, moving with absolute unanimity toward the works, the screens, the down-shafts. Where they come from, at the far end of whatever the water still touches, no one on the fan has ever established. Where they go is known and unspoken: down, through the grilles while they are still thin enough for the arithmetic, into the sea under the world, where they will thicken past the grates that admitted them and lie in the floor-seams for longer than the Command has existed. The children net a few every year and keep them in jars for a week, and every year, without being told to, they end the same way: poured back into the lateral at dusk, watched until they turn upstream. The Command exports its memory downward, one glass thread at a time. The eels below that remember the dead river will be succeeded by eels that remember this — the poplar rows, the whistle, a child's jar.

**The birds are the fan's own bait-balls.** They came across the dry country following the green the way the predators below followed the sound in through the grates, and at dusk the starlings mass over the Stem in flexing, scattering volumes that a diver from the basin would recognize instantly: the same shape, the same logic, shelter made of siblings, performed against sky instead of within water. Herons walk the laterals like the char walk the seams, in singles and pairs, patient, territorial, their beats laid out along the engineering. And all of it — starling, heron, teal on the check ponds — has learned the whistle. The wildlife of the Command holds the same opinion of the pumps that the carp hold: that they are weather. Both are correct.

**The people live on the water-clock, and mostly do not look down.** They are crew families and river-town families, a few thousand across the fan, and their days are ruled by first water and second water as completely as any tide-flat's creatures are ruled by the moon. What they know of the deep basin is idiom and lullaby: the bank, the sea under the world, the snoring. It is not ignorance, exactly. It is the correct posture toward a system that requires no witnesses — you live beside it, you set your clock by it, you teach your children that the ground hums because something enormous is asleep, and you do not disturb it. The land register at the fan's head office lists every allotment and every grantee, crews paid out in acreage when the programs closed, and the register is complete and correct — except that in Command Area 114 there is a corner lot, grass and a survey stake and nothing else, granted twelve years ago to a member of the seeding crew who cycled out with the rest of the roster and never came to claim it.

The roster below is wrong by one, in one direction. The register above is wrong by one, in the other. A person with no name on anything; a name with no person on it. They are the same discrepancy, viewed from either side of the rock.

**The ditch rider knows.** He is the fan's one itinerant — the man who rides the lateral lines gate to gate, reads the gauges, clears the silt traps — and he was seeding crew himself, before, in the flood-years, and took his acreage beside the corner lot that was never claimed. The silt traps are where the bank's water gives up what it carries: grit, seeds, and — always — a glitter of fish scales, the snow that falls through the light-shafts below arriving above as sparkle in a steel basket. And five or six times a year, among the scales, he finds kelp. Fronds. Cut clean — not torn, not grazed, *cut*, at pruning length, at intervals that, kept in a log for a decade, make a calendar. He presses them between the pages of his gauge book. He has done the arithmetic. He knows what tends a garden with a blade, three hundred metres down, in a sea with no roster; he knows, therefore, roughly whose. And he has understood from the beginning that the knowledge is only good for one thing — that reporting it could only end the life it proves — and so his silence has become a form of maintenance, performed daily, like greasing a gate. He mows the unclaimed lot every few weeks in season. If anyone asked why, he would say *against her arrival*. No one asks.

He keeps his own tallies in grease pencil on the inside of the gate-house door — the whistle's timing, the milk's density, the frond dates. He does not know that three hundred metres beneath his route, on the inside of a valve tower, there is another grease-pencil calendar tracking the same bell from below. Two ledgers of one machine, kept by two members of one crew, either side of the rock, never to be compared. The Command has exactly one witness to its underworld, and the Catchment has exactly one witness at all, and neither witness will ever file a report. That may be the fan's deepest hydraulic fact: even the knowing here is banked, held against evaporation, spent only in maintenance.

**What lives in the Command, then, is everything that has learned to live at the scale of a schedule.** The soil did it by waking on delivery. The plankton does it twice daily, arriving as the tide's green gift. The elvers do it annually, the birds do it by the whistle, the farmers do it by first water and second water, the ditch rider does it in grease pencil. Below, the entrance requirement was weather; above, it is the clock; and they are the same requirement, because down there the clock *is* the weather, and up here the weather comes out of a pipe.

Some year the fronds will stop arriving. The traps will run clean of everything but scales and seeds, season after season, and the ditch rider — or whoever holds the gauge book after him, if he ever explains it, if he ever explains anything — will be the only person in the world who understands what the absence means: that somewhere in the sea under the fan the rows have begun to go ragged, the orchard becoming forest, the one handwriting in the poured deep blurring at last into thicket. He will keep mowing the corner lot for a while. Then the grass will have it, the way the volunteers will have the kelp lines, and the two tended squares — one kept for a missing person, one kept by her — will go wild within a few years of each other, three hundred metres apart, and the register and the roster will finally both be right.

The milk will thin, decades on, and stop, and be disbelieved.

The water will go on arriving twice a day. Everything that lives here will go on moving before it does.
