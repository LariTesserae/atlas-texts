**The Mouths**

Follow the water. Everything else about the Shoulder Cities stays — the stone stays, the dead come back as awls and loom-combs, the cord-records thicken in their niches — but the water only visits. The film of condensation on the north walls, the seeps beading out of the fractures, the fog burned off the valley throats by midday, the melt threading down out of the blue-black couloirs where nothing lives: all of it is passing through. The rubble drinks the rain, but rubble is not a keeper either, only a delay. Under the talus, out of sight and out of mind, the mountain's whole allowance gathers itself in the dark and moves west, down the wet faces where the cloud dies, thread finding thread, until somewhere below the lowest bench it comes out of the rock for the last time and is a river, and does not go back.

And it does not go alone. The range is young the way a wound is young, and a wound weeps. Every one of those small dry *tocks* the people pause for is a promise of grit; every fresh-broken slab a mason reads was broken off of something, and the something goes downhill. The river runs pale — gray-green, opaque, milk-colored with rock ground finer than flour, so that a hand dipped in it vanishes at the first knuckle. That color is the mountain. The people of the Mouths, who will enter this account shortly, have never needed to be told this. Their name for the river translates, more or less, as *the mountain, walking*.

It walks a long time. Months, at a trader's pace, down the gorge the river has sawed for itself, across the high plateau where the water runs fast and useless between banks of its own gravel, over the last long staircase of rapids — and then the land gives out from under it. The plateau ends. The river drops into a lowland trough as wide as the eye can use, slows, spreads, forgets its own name, and splits. And splits again. It goes to the inland sea at the bottom of the world not as a river but as a delta: a country of channels, dozens of them, braiding and silting and reopening, green threaded with silver as far as the haze — and this country, the wet fan at the end of everything, is the Mouths.

It is the youngest land in the world, and it feels like the oldest. That is the paradox at the root of the place, and it is worth sitting with, because everything else here grows out of it. The ground underfoot in the Mouths may have been laid down last season — silt that was, three years ago, the flank of a peak a lifetime's walk to the east — and yet nothing in the delta looks new. Nothing is sharp. Nothing carries the angle of its fracture, because everything that arrives here has been traveling for years and has been rubbed the whole way. The driftwood comes ashore already gray and rounded. The pebbles — pebbles are rare here, and precious, and we will come to that — arrive smooth as eggs. Even the water is old: the flood that comes every summer fell as snow on the high couloirs years before, and has spent the time since being ground and carried and settled and lifted again. The Shoulder Cities live on rock so new it still announces its own rising. The Mouths live on that same rock at the end of its journey, ground fine, laid flat, gone soft. The mountain people stand on the world's youth. The delta people stand on its arrival.

The land itself is barely land. It is an argument between water and silt, renegotiated annually. In the dry season the channels shrink into their beds and the delta is a country of green islands, reed marsh, black mud drying to a pale crazed crust, poplar galleries along the levees, fields of grain on every patch that stands a hand's-breadth above the water table. Then, in high summer — under a clear sky, this is the thing to understand, under a hard blue cloudless sky, with no rain anywhere — the river rises. It rises because, months away and out of sight, the sun has reached the ice. The flood arrives in the Mouths as pure consequence, cause invisible, weather from a place most of its people will never see. It comes up the channels quietly, a hand's-width a day, then faster; it tops the low ground, joins channel to channel, and for a season the delta is one sheet of pale water with the towns standing out of it on their mounds like the backs of swimming animals. Then it falls, and leaves behind the year's payment: a film of new mountain over every field, gray-brown, fat, and the whole country smells of it — a mineral smell, cold-stone-and-water, which is, though almost no one alive has stood in both places to know it, the exact smell of a seep-pocket on a shoulder bench at dawn.

The towns survive the flood the only way anything here survives anything: by standing on their own past. There is no stone in the Mouths — none native, at least; the delta grinds nothing and receives everything pre-ground — so the people build in mud. Brick, molded and sun-dried, laid up into thick-walled houses roofed in reed, and mud brick does not last. It softens in the flood years, slumps in the rains, and every generation or two a house is let go — melted, deliberately, its walls trodden back into slurry — and a new house raised on the leveled mass of the old. The result, over lifetimes, over the same slow spans in which a mountain wall thickens course by course, is that each town rises on a mound of itself: a tell, layer on layer of dissolved houses, hearths, floors, and dead, climbing out of the floodplain. The oldest towns stand higher than the tallest trees. Cut into the flank of one — the channels do this sometimes, shifting — and you can read it like the wall of a gorge: ash lines, floor lines, the pale lenses of old flood-silt that got in, all the way down to the first reed platform someone laid on a natural bar of river sand back before the records of anyone.

The mounds rise, on average, about a finger-width a year.

Nobody in the Mouths knows that this is also the pace of the mountains. Nobody in the Shoulder Cities knows it either. The river knows it, in the way rivers know things, carrying the one rise down and laying it up into the other, but the river is not telling.

Where the mountain shoulders are quiet, the Mouths are loud, and it is the same physics wearing the other face. Thick wet air carries everything. Sound travels over water like light. The delta at dusk is a continuous fabric of noise — frogs in their millions, insects, the roosting argument of waterbirds settling by the tens of thousands into the reed beds, the knock of hulls against mooring posts, and through it and over it the voices of the people, who have never had a reason to be quiet in their lives. They sing across the water because singing carries better than talk. Work is antiphonal — reed-cutters, netters, brickmakers all keep call-and-response going through the day, less for the words than for the location of everyone, an audible map, constantly refreshed. And beneath the voices there is hull-talk: rhythms knocked on the planks of a boat travel through the water itself, further than any voice, and every channel-family has its signature knock, so that a boat coming around a blind bend in the reeds announces itself through the soles of your feet before you ever see it.

There is one silence in the delta year, and they keep it the way the mountain people keep their half-beat pause for the settling rock. It is the night the flood crests. The rise slows, and slows, and everyone knows it is close, and on that night the towns go quiet — cooking fires banked, children hushed, the frogs alone keeping on — because the crest can be *heard*, they insist, by anyone listening: a change in the voice of the water, the moment the pushing stops and the standing begins. Someone hears it first and knocks the turn-rhythm on a hull, and the rhythm crosses the delta town to town through the black water, and then the noise comes back all at once, everywhere, like a held breath released, because the year has been paid and the falling can begin.

**The people of the mud**

Start with the balance, because the water wrote the balance first.

They are long people, the people of the Mouths — long-limbed, light-footed, narrow where the mountain people are dense, built for standing in the stern of a reed skiff that a mountain trader would not trust with his hat. A delta child can pole a boat before it can be trusted with a knife. The whole population moves through life at a slight sway, the standing-in-boats sway; they have it on land, in doorways, asleep, and they read each other by it the way the mountain people read mouths and hands — a person's trouble shows in their balance before it shows anywhere else. Their legs are the worked part of them, and their feet: broad, splayed, mud-wise, able to tell silt from quick-silt through the callus. Their hands, by mountain standards, are almost soft. There is nothing here that fights the hand. The mud takes any shape offered; the reeds cut easily; even their blades are copper, which is a metal with manners.

And they run. This should be said plainly, because it is the pivot of the oldest jokes in two languages. The air of the Mouths is thick and generous, sea-level air, and the delta people spend it like people who have never once considered its price — they run along the levee tops, they run for no reason, children run in circles, which the mountain followers who come down to the trade fair at the head of navigation watch with the expression of bankers observing arson. The delta has its answering joke, of course. Theirs is about the mountain trader who would not get into the boat, and walked around the channel instead, and the punchline is always the same: *and he is walking still.* In the delta, walking around anything can take days. The water is the road here — their word for "water" and their word for "way" are the same word, just as, far upstream, one word means both "water" and "permission" — and a people whose water means *road* and a people whose water means *permission* have been trading peaceably for forty generations without either side quite grasping what the other one is asking the river for.

They are dark like the mountain people but finished differently — river-finished rather than wind-finished, no flush of burst vessels across the cheekbones, and their eyes are wide open. That is what a mountain trader notices first and never quite gets used to: down here, everyone's eyes are *open*, the whole iris on display, because the light comes through haze and there is nothing a valley away to squint at. There is no distance in the Mouths. The horizon is reeds. The delta people, in turn, find the mountain squint unnerving and slightly comic — a whole people, they say, who look like they suspect the sky of something. Only in winter, on the clearest mornings, when the haze pulls back off the eastern horizon and the range shows itself — a white serration, impossibly far, the edge of the world's other idea — do the people of the Mouths squint. They stop what they are doing, briefly. They look at the white country their ground came from. Then the haze comes back, and so do they.

**How they are arranged**

By height, first and always. In the mountains, water is the scarce thing and its law is the deep law; in the Mouths, water is everywhere and the scarce thing is elevation — a floor that stays dry, a granary above the crest line, a mooring that doesn't strand at low water. A family is, at bottom, the set of people with a claim on a particular height of a particular mound, and inheritance is reckoned in it: so many courses of ancestral floor, such-and-such a face of the tell. Marriage is partly freeboard. A family rich in height and poor in boats marries a family rich in boats and poor in height, and the matchmakers of the Mouths carry in their heads the elevation of every lineage in a day's poling, the way a mountain water-arbiter carries the genealogy of seeps.

Below the height-law, the work sorts people loosely, and the delta has its own priesthood of competence to answer the masons. Here it is the water-callers: the readers of the river, who spend their lives learning the delta's channels the way a mason learns grain — where the current is cutting, where it is laying down, which mouth is dying and which is being born — and whose certification, like the mason's, takes twenty years, because the proof of a called flood takes twenty years of floods. The great decisions of the Mouths are theirs to frame: which channel to dredge, which levee to raise, and — gravest of all, taken perhaps once in a generation — which channel to *open*, sacrificing land to save land, a judgment delivered from the levee top in the carrying formal register of song so the whole water hears it at once and there can be no later argument about what was sung. There are no kings here either, though a traveler might mistake a senior water-caller for one for about a day, until the season turns and the authority rotates on downstream like everything else.

Then the brickmolders, who are to the delta what the ropemakers are to the shoulder benches — the makers of the thing that is always dissolving, and who carry the same faint glamour of mortality, inverted: rope people understand that things end; mud people understand that things return. The reed-cutters, the netters, the levee gangs. The singers, who are the cordkeepers of the Mouths: the records here that matter are carried in genealogy-songs and channel-songs, hours long, error-checked by the whole town singing along, because the delta learned early what happens to marks pressed in clay. Some things *are* pressed in clay — tallies, seals, boundary tokens — and dried in the sun, and each flood decides which of them survive, and the people of the Mouths regard this as a fair and even holy arrangement: the river edits the archive, and what it takes, it takes into the mound, which is the real record anyway. And out on the fringing marshes, half-wild by town standards, the herders who follow the great gray swimming grazers — heavy, placid, horned beasts that spend the flood season neck-deep and graze the reed margins in loose seasonal circuits, owned the way height is not, which is to say loosely, followed rather than driven — and who come back each year smelling of marsh and absence, with the particular flat-water gaze of people who navigate by the taste of channels.

**How they begin and end**

Children belong to the water before they belong to anyone. A courtyard in the Mouths is a mooring — four, six, ten households sharing a dock face on the mound's flank — and a child of the mooring is watched by the mooring, called by any of them with the same undifferentiated word, which translates as something like *the near voices*. At seven or so, a child of the Mouths is given two things in the same season, and both are watched closely by whichever mooring elder is the certifying sort. The first is a skiff — small, reed-bundled, theirs — and the elder watches which channel the child chooses first, because how a child reads water at seven is held to predict everything. The second is a brick. The child treads the mud, molds the brick, presses a hand flat into its face, and the hand-brick is laid into the family wall — and here the two countries, without ever having compared notes, have built the same rite out of opposite materials. The mountain child's stone will hold its place in the wall for six generations; the child knows forever exactly where they are: third course above the door, left of the smoke-gap. The delta child's brick will melt back into the mound within a lifetime or two, be trodden to slurry, molded again, pressed by some other seven-year-old's hand. Ask a grown person of the Mouths where their child-brick is and they make the delta's answering gesture to the mountain shrug — palm up, fingers loose, a small cupped lift, the gesture of receiving — and say the word that means *everywhere under us*. They know exactly where they are too. They are in the mound. All of them are in the mound.

Because the dead here go down. There is no ledge to carry them to and the great birds of the sky are only winter visitors; the delta's dead are laid into the tell itself, beneath the floors, wrapped in reed matting, folded small, and the house goes on over them, and when the house melts they are simply deeper. The mounds are the ancestors — not figuratively, not as a manner of speaking, but as engineering. Every finger-width of height that holds a granary above the flood is dissolved houses and buried grandmothers, and the people of the Mouths say of their dead exactly what a visitor, once it is explained, always finds they cannot argue with: *the dead hold us out of the water.* Their word for it is not the masons' *settled*. Their word is a form of the verb *to raise* — the dead have *gone into the height* — and a funeral procession here is loud where the mountain's is a single repeated whistle: the whole mooring singing the person's genealogy-verse down the dock face and up the mound, adding, at the end, the new line, which the singers will carry from now on, which is how a person becomes permanent in a country where nothing else is.

**Their fellow residents**

The towns are not human-only here either, and the resemblance would startle both peoples. Delta houses breathe and rustle exactly as mountain houses do — not with wall-sparrows in the gaps of drystone but with swallows under the reed eaves and weaver-birds stitching their hanging nests along the mooring fronts — and here too a house gone silent of birds is a house with something wrong, damp in the brick, a shifting in the mound, and the birds' leaving is taken as the building's own report, and killing one would not occur to anyone. The reed beds hold waterbirds in numbers that make the talus-slopes' whistling rodents seem a modest arrangement; the flood brings fish onto the drowned fields in silver acres, and for a month the whole population nets and dries and salts, and the delta's year of protein is made. Frogs are the children's-story animal here, brave and foolish in equal measure, the delta's answer to the whistling rodent, and the jokes are structurally identical, which no one has ever been in a position to notice.

Twice a year the sky brings visitors from the white country. The great scavenger-birds of the range — the thermal-riders, the ones that attend the platform ledges and carry names in the shoulder cities — come down the river of air above the gorge when the high winter closes the mountain, and spend the cold months turning over the delta, and their arrival in numbers is read in the Mouths as the first certain sign of the distance's weather, a forerunner of the flood to come. The delta people call them *the white-country's birds* and do not name them. It is possible — the followers who winter lowest say it is certain — that a bird which has attended a mountain city for forty years, a named bird, a bird whose attendance makes a death a good death, spends every winter of that long life circling unnamed over the Mouths, being weather there, and goes home in spring to be someone again.

And there is the pale thing in the water. It lives, if it lives, in the deep channels, and it is seen — a long white back rolling once through the milk-colored water, most often near the crest of the flood — perhaps twice in a lifetime, and it is the one animal the people of the Mouths will not speak of while afloat, on the theory, never stated as belief and never dismissed as habit, that the water carries hull-talk and voice alike and the thing does not care to be discussed on its own road. The seeing is private. You bring your boat in. You say nothing. And years later, some loud lamplit mooring evening, a person might say *I was on the third channel, the year the crest came early* — and the mooring goes quiet, all those open eyes turning, and that is the whole story, and it is enough. Two countries, a river's length apart, each keeping the same shaped silence around a pale rumor. If the cat and the fish are the same idea wearing two bodies, nobody living is qualified to say so.

**What they believe**

That the world is old, and being finished, and delivered to them. This is the root of everything else, and it is the mountain's creed read from the far end. The people of the shoulders live on rock still announcing its rising and conclude that nothing is finished and they are early — laying courses on a work they will not see done. The people of the Mouths live on the *product*: everything reaches them completed, ground, smoothed, ripened by distance — the driftwood already gray, the pebbles already round, the water itself years old and fat with dissolved mountains — and they have drawn their own obvious conclusion. The world is a long sentence and they live at the end of it, where the meaning arrives. They are not waiting for anything. It is already coming. It has always already been coming. The flood rises under a blue sky, caused by a sun on ice they will never see, and so their notion of the divine is the mountain people's turned inside out: not the patient rock underfoot that speaks in small dry cracks, but the *far cause* — the invisible thing at a great distance whose action reaches you, faithfully, without explanation, once a year, and asks only that you be ready to receive it. Their word for that readiness and their word for the divine are built on the same root, which is also the root of their word for the flood's first day, which is also — and this they will tell you loudly, laughing, the whole mooring joining in — the root of their word for themselves.

It means, more or less, *those who receive well.*

They have heard, from the traders, what the mountain people call themselves, and they find it half-noble and half-hilarious — *waiting*, they say, palm up, fingers loose, *for what? It is already in the river.* The mountain people have heard the delta's name too, and make their tilted-palm gesture, and observe that anything worth receiving was laid up by someone first. Twice a year, at the fair below the last rapids where the gorge lets the river out and the two countries touch — wool and rope and bone tools and lichen-dyed cloth in colors the delta cannot grow coming down; copper and salt fish and grain and, above everything, prized beyond reason, freshly fractured stone blades going up into a country that has never in its existence produced a sharp edge — the two names stand in the same market and trade, the waiters and the receivers, the mountain selling its youth and the delta selling its ripeness, each privately certain the other has misunderstood the river between them.

The river declines to rule on it. It only keeps on with the work — a finger-width of rising taken off the top of the world each year, walked west for months, and laid down as a finger-width of rising at the bottom, so that both countries are climbing at the same slow pace on the two ends of the same moving stone, neither expecting to see the work done, neither needing to. Somewhere on a shoulder bench this morning a mason laid a course and left it to settle. Somewhere in the Mouths this morning a child trod the mud that used to be the peak above that mason's head, and molded a brick, and pressed a hand into it, and did not expect the house to outlast the water — and was right, and the mound rose anyway.
