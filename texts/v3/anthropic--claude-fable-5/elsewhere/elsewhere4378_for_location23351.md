**The Settling Water**

Every drop that weeps out of the Condensing City is going somewhere, and it is all going to the same place. Follow any channel against your instinct — downstream instead of in — and the runnels gather into runnels, the channels into a broad hot artery, and the artery makes for the plateau's southern rim with the single-mindedness of water that has finished its work and wants to leave. The city has one exit, and it is the exit the water uses.

At the rim, the cloud goes over the edge with you. It pours down the escarpment in slow motion, a waterfall of vapor a kilometer wide, and beneath it — inside it, for the first hour of the descent — the cliff face is doing at scale what every wall in the city does in miniature. The escarpment weeps. Not one waterfall but ten thousand seepages, hot water finding every fracture in the old basalt and coming out of the rock's face like sweat, and everywhere it emerges it has built. Terrace under terrace under terrace: aprons and basins and scalloped lips of pale stone, white shading to gold shading to rust, pool spilling into pool down the whole height of the cliff, a staircase for no one, descending through its own steam.

This is the largest thing the city ever made, and it made none of it on purpose. The stair is just the city's signature, written in its own mineral hand down a kilometer of rock, over a span of time that makes the masonry above look hasty.

**The stair**

Going down, you descend through temperature the way other travelers descend through altitude. The uppermost pools are scalding, blind with steam, floored in that near-white film that lives where almost nothing can. Then, terrace by terrace, the colors deepen — the ochre band, the rust band, the oxblood, the gray-green like tarnish — the same tribes, in the same order, sorted by the same thermometer as on every pipe in the city above. The stair is the film's great estate. It has more pasture here than in all the streets and stacks put together, and it holds it the same way it holds everything: patiently, in bands, asking nothing.

And then there is a place on the stair — you will know it when you reach it; everyone does — where the film gives out, and in the very next pool downslope, a moss begins.

It is a modest moss. It would not be worth a glance anywhere else in the world. Here it stops travelers of every species in their tracks, because it is the first green thing in a vertical mile and it marks, as precisely as a surveyor's line, the temperature below which the world as the lowlands understand it is permitted to resume. Above the line: chemistry being patient. Below it: biology in a hurry — because within ten terraces of that first moss there are ferns, and within a hundred there are trees, actual trees, leaning out over the warm pools with their roots wedged into travertine, growing fat on a permanent mist and a winter that never quite arrives.

Somewhere in that hundred terraces you walk out from under the plume. The lowlanders call the cloud on the mountain the Lid, and the mountain under it the Kettle — a name arrived at by people who have never once been inside and is, for all that, exactly correct. Below the Lid's edge the sun is suddenly shocking: yellow, specific, casting shadows with edges. You will find you have forgotten shadows have edges.

**The river**

The pools of the lowest terraces gather into a river, and the river runs south across the valley floor, and for its first several days of travel it is not like other rivers.

It is white, to begin with — milk-white, blind-white, opaque with all the silica it carried down out of the mountain and has not yet decided where to put. It is warm; not hot, no longer dangerous, but warm the way a body is warm, and it stays that way for a surprising distance, hoarding the Kettle's heat under its white skin. And it builds. As it cools, mile by mile, it pays out its stone along its own banks, raising pale natural levees, sealing its meanders behind it, floor by floor — a river that constructs its own bed and then, in flood, complains about the workmanship. Old channels lie abandoned across the valley like shed skins, white and hard, roads now, and threshing floors, and the foundations of granaries.

A week's travel down, the river has spent its mineral and lost its heat and runs clear and cold and ordinary, just a river with a strange white youth it doesn't speak of. But in that first warm reach — the reach where the water settles, in every sense the word will bear — there are people.

**The villages**

Ask what lives here and for once the answer is easy: everyone.

There are five villages along the warm reach, or seven, depending on where you decide two of them divide, strung along the banks where the winter steam rises off the water and keeps the frost from ever touching ground. They are built of timber, because below the Lid there are forests; they stand partly on stilts over the shallows, because the warm water is the whole point and nobody builds farther from it than they must; and they are painted. This has to be said with emphasis, because you may be arriving from a hundred thousand tons of intention without one picture on it: the villages are painted the way birds sing. Boats with eyes on their prows. Doorposts carved into ancestors, dogs, herons, lies. Ceilings crowded with fish. The insides of grain bins painted, where no one will ever see. Children's hands, on feast days, painted to the wrist.

The dominant color in all of it is a deep rust-red they call kettle-red, and they make it themselves. Twice a year the dye parties go up the stair to the highest band the film manages — the last red before the white heat — and scrape it, and dry it, and grind it, and everything the villages love wears it. Which means the oldest living resident of the Condensing City, the film that painted the vents before the masons came, has become — without noticing, without the capacity to notice — the palette of people it will never meet. The city upstream contains no images. The city's own skin, carried downstream in baskets, is what all the images down here are made of.

One pattern repeats above all the others, running along the eaves of every house, around the rims of the boats, bordering every painted ceiling: a band of shallow nested funnels, point-down, over and over. Ask what it shows and they will tell you, cheerfully, that it doesn't show anything — it's the old border, it goes at the edges of things, it keeps the luck in. It has gone at the edges of things for as long as there have been things here with edges.

You will have seen it before. You will have walked for days beneath ten thousand of them, glazed, inverted, each one drinking its own steam. Keep your face still. There is nothing you could say that the pattern is not already saying, more quietly, from every eave in the village.

**The strikes**

Twice a day the river warms.

It comes as a swell of heat moving down the reach — the bathing pools bloom, the mist over the water thickens for a quarter hour, fish rise along the whole bank at once as the small life stirs — and the villages call it the strike, and they live by it. First strike is the start of the working day, wherever in the morning it happens to fall; second strike ends it. Debts come due "at first strike of the new moon." A marriage is sealed by the couple standing in the shallows through a full strike together, letting the warmth pass over and beyond them, which is understood to mean something about time, and endurance, and things arriving from far off exactly when they intend to.

They will tell you the mountain has a heart, and that the strikes are its beat reaching them through the water. They are almost unimprovably close to right. What reaches them, hours old and miles traveled, is the scheduled relief of pressure valves in an empty city — the concussion you heard rolling over the rooftops and dying in the fog, arriving here as a gentleness, its violence entirely spent, its regularity intact. Somebody set those valves. Somebody chose their periods, tuned the whole city's breathing, and left. The people of the settling water have organized their days, their debts, and their marriages around the far end of that decision. Most of the dead leave objects. These left a verb, and the villages conjugate it twice a day without knowing they are speaking.

The old people say the strikes come later than they did when they were young — that the day itself is getting longer. They are right, though not about the day. Upstream, the pipes are slowly occluding; the routes around the blockages grow longer; the city's breath, century by century, is deepening and slowing as the stone closes over it. The villages keep excellent time with a dying machine's heart, and adjust, and do not know they are adjusting, and this may be the purest form of fidelity the world has ever produced.

**The steeping**

The villages' one craft that no one downstream can imitate is done on the stair, in the band of pools below scalding and above the green line, where the water is at its richest and most generous with its stone.

They call it steeping. An object hung in the right pool acquires the mountain: a season for a shell you can feel with a thumbnail, three years for a shell that rings when struck, a lifetime for stone all the way through — and nobody has a lifetime to spare, so stone all the way through is what you do for the dead. A drowned fisherman's oar. A child's shoes, if the child will not be needing shoes. These come off their cords heavy and pale gold and are set on the household shelf, finished sentences, and the family is understood to have settled its grief the way the river settles its silica: by putting it somewhere, deliberately, and letting it harden into something that can be lived with.

The pools are named, and owned, and inherited, and the disputes over them are the villages' one durable politics — because pools differ. Some steep white, some gold; some lay the stone down fine as lacquer, some coarse as bark; and the very best pools are the highest, the hardest to reach, tended by the small hereditary handful the villages call climbers, who spend their working lives on the stair between the worlds and are considered slightly odd, and are.

The famous export is stone lace. They steep woven things — nets, shawls, openwork panels knotted for the purpose — until every thread wears its thin mineral coat, and then they fire them, and the fiber burns out of the stone, and what remains is lace with nothing organic left in it: white-gold, impossibly fine, a fabric of pure patience. It goes downriver to towns that pay well for it and have no idea that it is made of the same stone that is, at this moment, sealing a city's flywheels into their chambers a mile above the clouds — that they are buying the entombment of the Condensing City retail, by the panel, worked into patterns of herons and fish and, at every border, the little nested funnels that keep the luck in.

**What comes down**

The river delivers. Not often, and never with explanation, but the villages keep an accounting.

After certain nights — windless ones, they say, though who can check the wind under the Lid — the morning water carries wings. Pale, palm-sized, like waxed paper, sodden and perfect. Wing-water, the villagers call such mornings; the mountain is molting; it is lucky to lift one out intact, and the girls press them flat and keep them. No one here has ever seen the animal these wings belong to alive, and no one ever will, because the living ones never leave the warm columns above the stacks and never, in their whole airborne lives, touch anything at all. Only the dead make the trip down. The villages have built a small tradition of luck on the far edge of a creature's funeral.

After floods, once in a decade or so, the highest fish traps take something that is not an eel — pale, blind, wrong in a way the fishermen do not itemize. It is put back. Always, immediately, without discussion. If you ask why, you will be told that it isn't theirs, which is a complete answer, and possibly the wisest sentence spoken anywhere along the river.

And in the largest village's meeting house there is a plate of dull gold, the size of two hands, curved as though it once lay over something living, brought down three generations ago by a climber who went past the pools, past the green line, past the last colors of the film, all the way up into the Lid — and came back a season later smelling of iron, answering questions with the smallest words he could find. The plate is called the mountain's scale. Children touch it for luck before their first steeping. It has the same sheen as the stone the pools lay down, because it is the same stone, laid down ring by ring by a stomach instead of a spring, on the back of something that finished its rounds and stopped where it stood; and its grazed circuits are part of why the strikes still come at all. The villages touch, for luck, a piece of the maintenance crew. The luck is real.

**Rules of the place**

The water is the law here, and the law is a gradient. Bathe below the red band. Drink below the green line, and let it settle a day. Plant where the winter steam reaches, which is the wealth of the villages stated plainly: a ribbon of ground along both banks where frost has never once arrived, where they take two harvests while the rest of the valley takes one, and where in the deep of winter — the river smoking, the herons come from three horizons to stand in the only open water for days in any direction — the villages live wrapped in a low white fog of their own.

On those mornings, with the fog wall to wall in the lanes and every house exhaling its hearth smoke into it and navigation done by sound — a loom here, a dog there, each household's noise on its own period — the villages become, for a few hours, a paler, kinder copy of a place they have never seen the original of. The resemblance is not an accident. It is inheritance, moving the only way inheritance ever moves along this river: downstream, dissolved, arriving warm.

---

Stand on the bank at dusk and take the long view, because the river is taking it whether you do or not.

The city upstream is closing over. Every century a little more stone, a little less machine; the strikes lengthening by minutes an age; the water, imperceptibly, cooling. Someday — not soon; the patience involved should not be insulted with a date — the river will run down out of the Kettle clear and cold and merely honest, and the strikes will stop, and the steeping pools will steep nothing, and the frost will walk down to the banks for the first time and find the villages there, and the villages will have to learn what everyone else on the plain has always known: winter. They will move, or change, or scatter downriver into the towns that bought their lace. And the little nested funnels will go on being painted at the edges of things, keeping the luck in, for centuries after the last person who could have been told what they were has finished settling into whatever shelf holds them — a pattern outliving its reason twice over, which is only what patterns are for.

But that is the long view, and no one here is required to hold it. What they hold instead is this: warmth that arrives on schedule, from an elder relative up under the Lid who provides and is not visited; a red that never runs out; stone that will hold anything you cannot bear to keep and cannot bear to lose; and a heartbeat in the water, twice a day, older than every story told about it and more faithful than most of the stories deserve.

If you come down the stair yourself, they will know before you speak. It is the smell — iron, and faintly eggs; it takes a year to fade, they'll tell you, not unkindly, over the meal they will certainly feed you. They will not press. But at some point in the evening, when the lamps are lit and the second strike has come and gone through the dark water outside, one of the old ones will lean in and ask the only question the villages have ever needed to send upstream:

*Is it still breathing?*

Say yes. It is even true — eight seconds in, eight seconds out, under the Lid, in the dimness inside the Kettle, exact and unhurried and alone. Say yes, and watch the whole room ease — shoulders dropping, the talk resuming, the children going back to their game by the fire — the settling water doing, one more time, in one more generation, the thing it was named for.
