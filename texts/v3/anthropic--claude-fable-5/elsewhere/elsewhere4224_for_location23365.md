# Ombrelle

You reach it by ascending — back up the same warm treads, the forty meters unwinding beneath you, the solder-smell thinning out of the air with every switchback until, near the top, you catch the first note of the smell that replaces it: dust, salt, sun-cooked canvas. The stairhead opens through a cellar door into a plaza, and you stop again, for the opposite reason. The space ahead is open to the sky, and the sky is enormous, white, and dry, and it has been dry for nine years.

Ombrelle is an estuary city, or was, or is the memory of one wearing the body of one. It was built on the flats where a slow river once braided into a warm sea, and everything about it was designed around a single meteorological fact: it rained here three hundred days a year. The city's name is its awnings — the *ombrelles*, the rain-shades — which hang above every street in long overlapping runs, each one cut and tensioned by guildsmen who tuned them, genuinely tuned them, so that a downpour landing on a street of awnings produced chords. The gutters were carved to gurgle in the conversational register. The cisterns were fired to specific depths so that drips falling into them struck deep ceramic notes. Ombrelle was, for four hundred years, an instrument that its weather played daily, and if you have stood in the Antechamber District and heard the water talking in the gutters and the brief rain drumming overhead, then you have already heard Ombrelle. You heard it before you ever arrived. What runs through the district's plumbing is not the sound of water in a new city. It is a recording of this one — Ombrelle's own noise, decanted, sent down ahead of everything else like a voice sent ahead of a body.

Up here, now, the instrument is silent. That is the first thing, and it takes a while to absorb, the way the district's emptiness does. The awnings still hang — sun-bleached to the color of old bone, brittle as pastry, ticking faintly as they expand and contract with the heat — but nothing drums on them. The gutters are dry channels full of drifted salt. The canals, which vein the whole city exactly as the district's single canal suggests they would, hold no water at all: their beds are cracked silt, glittering white, and the mooring rings line their edges above nothing, and here and there a boat still sits on the dry mud with its keel canted over, waiting for a tide that withdrew and did not come back. The estuary beyond the seawall is a plain now. You can walk out onto it for an hour and not reach water. What water remains is a single channel, a hand's width across in places, threading toward a sea that seems each year to have taken another step backward.

And standing in that thread of water, most evenings: herons. Four of them, sometimes six. They are the last of thousands, and they are worth walking out to see, because they are the originals. Ombrelle's streetlamps were made in their image centuries ago — the slender stem, the slight droop of the neck, the patience — and the district's streetlamps are copies of those copies, so that forty meters underground, in a dark no bird has ever entered, several hundred iron herons stand at the edges of a dry-land canal, quoting a quotation of these last few actual birds, who stand in the last actual water, unaware of their long descent into architecture.

The light in Ombrelle is everything the district's light is not: vertical, white, shadowless at noon, merciless to the awnings. But at dusk — and dusk still comes here; Ombrelle has not been excused from days — the light goes amber and brown and thick, and for about twenty minutes the city above becomes the exact color of the city below. The Ombrellans know this, and it has become the shape of their evenings. Because dusk is not just an hour here anymore. It is a *date*.

---

Nine years ago it rained in Ombrelle for the last true time — a warm, ordinary, two-day rain, the kind the city was built for — and on the second evening of it, at lamplighting, the Long Survey was Fixed. This is the event around which everything in Ombrelle now arranges itself, the way a dry riverbed arranges itself around the memory of the river.

The Survey had been running for years before that: the Rememberers' Guild walking the city with instruments and without them, measuring, sketching, and — this was the part outsiders never quite believed — *speaking* it. Reading the city aloud, street by street, into the deep shafts the water-guilds had sunk generations earlier, down into the warm strangeness that had always lain under the flats. The ground, it turned out, could take a description the way tinder takes flame. Nobody built machinery. Nobody understands the shimmer, up here, any better than a visitor does down there. The Guild only knows that when the rains began to fail and the sea began to walk backward, the ground beneath Ombrelle would accept a sufficiently loving account of the city and begin, slowly, to pronounce it back — in basalt, in warmth, in the dark, where no drought can follow.

On the evening of the Fixing, the account was closed. The city as it stood at that hour — lamps just lit, rain on the awnings, canals brimming, the fig in Wasp Plaza heavy with fruit — became the image, entire and final. That is why it is always dusk in the Antechamber District, and always will be. It is not a mood down there. It is a specific Tuesday. The district is that one rainy evening, being said aloud into stone at a millimeter an hour, and it will still be that evening when the last door finishes, and the people who move in will live inside the ninth of Serel, nine years ago, forever — the best evening the Guild could find, chosen the way you choose the photograph for a locket.

Everything after the Fixing is outside the sentence. This is the quiet grief that organizes Ombrelle more than the drought does. A shop opened eight years ago has no counterpart growing below; its keeper will descend into a district where her shop was never founded. The graves dug since the Fixing — and there have been graves; the dry years have not been gentle — lie in a cemetery whose underground twin was transcribed one funeral short, then two, then forty. The dead of the last nine years will stay in Ombrelle. Their families visit differently now, knowing it: they visit the way you visit someone before a long voyage they are not making with you.

And so Ombrelle has become the city of names. The district, downstairs, gently un-carves anything written on it; the Ombrellans discovered this early, and their response was to turn around and carve *everything* on the city that would keep it. There is hardly a wall at hand-height in Ombrelle that isn't inscribed now — names, dates, jokes, the outlines of children's hands traced in the soft old limestone, whole family trees running up the sides of dry cisterns. The stone up here remembers, and it is going to be left holding everything the image below refuses. Some of the carving is grief and some of it is defiance and a good deal of it, lately, is simply habit, the way people in a rainy city once carried rain-shades. You leave your name on Ombrelle. It is what the city is for now.

---

The water deserves its own accounting, because the water is the connection made literal, and the Ombrellans speak of it the way other cities speak of emigrants.

The wells warmed before they emptied. That was the first sign — cool sweet well-water arriving, one season, at blood temperature, as if the ground had developed a fever, and then arriving lower in the shaft, and then not arriving. The rains failed in the same years, and what rain does still fall — a few minutes, once or twice a season — behaves in a way that no longer surprises anyone: it does not pool. It strikes the dust, darkens it, and is gone downward, drunk instantly, and the ground where it fell is warm afterward. The Guild maintains, and the water-guilds confirm, and everyone chooses to believe, that none of it is lost. It has only changed address. It is downstairs, running in the gutters of the ninth of Serel, weeping from fresh walls, gathering in the dark above a diagonal bridge and letting go, on its own schedule, onto awnings grown specifically to be drummed on. When the district's canal surges, cellar floors in lower Ombrelle sweat. Old men press their ears to those floors — this is common enough that certain cellars have cushions in them — and listen to the roar, the surf-through-a-wall sound of ten thousand decisions a second, and report on it afterward in the tone of people reporting on grandchildren. *Loud last night. Getting on well.*

In the driest years, the city stopped waiting for the ground to take its water and began sending it deliberately. There are drains in Ombrelle, brass-collared, guild-blessed, into which the last of the great cisterns are being decanted a measured amount each month — water poured down ahead of the household, like luggage. The city is emptying itself into its own reflection, and it does so calmly, on a schedule, the way you'd fill a font.

About the diagonal bridge, since it puzzles everyone below: the original stands in Ombrelle's third quarter, and it was built straight. Four hundred years of shifting silt skewed it, a degree at a time, and by the evening of the Fixing it crossed its canal at a slight diagonal, and the Survey — being faithful — transcribed the skew. So the district's one inexplicable angle is Ombrelle's old age, copied down as design. The reason exists. It is four centuries of mud, and it lives up here.

---

**Who lives in Ombrelle**

The census, honestly taken, is the district's census run in reverse, and it is dwindling on exactly the schedule that the one below is filling.

**Three hundred households**, more or less — the same three hundred whose absence is so lovingly detailed downstairs. They live in a city built for a hundred thousand, and they have contracted, over the dry years, into the quarters nearest the stairhead, so that Ombrelle is now dense at its heart and abandoned at its edges, a city pulling its limbs in. They keep the Descent List, which is not a lottery, whatever visitors assume: it is ordered by trade. The plumbers and lamplighters go down first, when the time comes, then the boatwrights — there will be boats again, downstairs, on warm water — then the growers, then everyone, and last of all the Guild. Nobody has moved down yet, because the doors finish before what's behind them, and you do not move into a sentence still being said. But people visit. They descend on their name-days to stand outside the unfinished doors of their own houses, in the warm dark, in the rain, and they come back up wet and quiet and, most of them, easier. One old boatwright goes down every day and sits under the copied fig, in a plaza the size of a living room, under a canopy flattened to fit a ceiling of stone, and eats his lunch there, and comes back up. Asked why, he says the tree downstairs is younger. Everyone understands him to mean something else.

**The fig itself**, the original, still stands in Wasp Plaza, and it is dying honestly, the way the drought asks: half its crown bare, the other half stubbornly fruiting, its wasps — the last insects anyone bothers to count — still working the fruit that ripens. Its figs drop and are gathered now, every one, because the Guild has decreed the seeds be saved, against a day when the district might learn to accept an animal, a wasp, a bird, and the flattened tree below might finally have a reason to fruit. The original will not descend. Trees don't. It is teaching its translation everything it can from forty meters away, through whatever the ground carries, and when it goes, the only living bearer of its shape will be downstairs, holding a posture the original never held, under a sky of rock, in an evening the original once actually stood in, dripping.

**The salt** is the new tenant, and it is moving in the way the moss moved in below: uninvited, patient, total. It whitens the canal beds, creeps up the lower courses of the walls, blooms in the seams the moss once held. The moss itself is gone from Ombrelle — the drought took most of it, and the last of it, it is generally agreed, emigrated on the boots of the Survey teams. This is said without bitterness. It means that Ombrelle's moss, the green nation, survives entire in the joints of the district, an accidental first citizen carried down inside a deliberate act, and there are Ombrellans who take real comfort in the thought that the first living thing to inhabit their future was a stowaway from their past.

**The wind** has taken over the water's old routines. It commutes through the dry gutters, pools in the plazas where crowds once pooled, and plays the awnings badly — a dry ticking and flapping where there used to be chords — like someone picking out a tune on an instrument they don't own. It is the only thing up here with a daily life, and the Ombrellans regard it as a lodger: tolerated, slightly resented, obviously temporary.

**The after-dead** — the forty-odd who have died since the Fixing — live in Ombrelle in the way the three hundred households live in the district: as negative space, fully conjugated for. Their names are carved deepest of all, at the top of the walls of names, and their graves are tended with the particular care given to things that will be left. When the descent finally comes, Ombrelle will be handed over to them, the whole instrument, silent, inscribed, salt-hemmed, theirs.

**And the Guild** — reduced now to one working Rememberer, an old surveyor who no longer speaks the city aloud because the ground finished memorizing it years ago and the roar continues without her. Her work now is listening. She goes down monthly with the original folios and walks the district checking the pronunciation, and in nine years she has logged exactly one deviation: a streetlamp on the third terrace whose neck droops two degrees further than any lamp in Ombrelle ever drooped — closer, she noted in the margin, to the birds themselves. She declined to file it as an error. Her annotation, which the whole city has read, says only: *it has seen the water. Let it stand how it likes.*

---

At dusk, the lamplighters still go out. Oil is scarce and the routes have shortened with the city, but the lamps of the inhabited quarters are lit every evening without fail, because dusk is the hour when Ombrelle and its reflection are the same city. For twenty minutes the light up here goes to the amber of the light down there, the heron lamps glow above the dry canals exactly as their copies glow above the full one, and the Ombrellans come out of their houses to stand in it — in pairs, on walkways two abreast, leaning over railings, calling down through the brown air to people they know. They are rehearsing, and they know they are rehearsing, and they do it anyway, the way the water below rehearses them back.

Then full dark comes, which the district will never have, and the lamps gutter one by one to save oil, and the wind takes the streets, and somewhere under everyone's feet the roar goes on saying the ninth of Serel a millimeter at a time.

They named the down-city themselves — the Antechamber, the room where you wait to be admitted somewhere further — and for years the name was understood plainly: the district waits for them. Lately the older Ombrellans have begun to use it the other way, without ever quite saying so. A dry city, mostly empty, carved all over with names, keeping its lamps lit and its list in order, sitting at the head of a warm stairway with its hands folded. Which room is the antechamber depends entirely on which side of the door you think the dwelling is.

And when the last household finally goes down the warm stairs, Ombrelle will close gently over the fact of their leaving — salt, wind, herons, names — and remain above its own reflection under the enormous white sky, keeping, for whoever comes across the flats to find it, its full, faithful population of *still*.
