# The Rising

There is nothing to smell and nothing to hear. That is the first thing to understand about the place: it does not announce itself. You come to it the way the water comes to it — after a long time in the dark, without warning.

The way there begins under the dim trees, back up in the low mossy country where the river goes into the ground, and it runs the way the land runs: down and out. Seven miles, as the crow goes. Longer as the water goes, and no one knows how much longer, because the water's route is the one thing about the two places that has never been seen. Overland, you walk out from under the canopy by degrees. The hazel thins. The haze lifts. The moss retreats down the trunks and then gives up the trunks altogether, and the light — which back at the sinkhole was a permanent green dusk — comes up around you like a tide until you are standing in ordinary open daylight at the head of a wide shallow valley, blinking, with the forest a dark hem behind you and grass running away in front.

The valley is limestone too, but limestone with its face washed. Where the high country buried its rock under fern and root, here the bones show: a low grey scar of it runs along one side of the valley, a broken cliff no taller than a house, warm to the touch on a sunny afternoon, hung with rock-rose and thyme instead of liverwort. Everything the Roaring Room's country did in dimness, this country does in glare. The wrens are loud. The bees are loud. And at the foot of the scar, in a green bowl of turf, there is a pool — and the pool is the quietest thing in the valley.

It is broad, fifty feet or more, and it has no stream flowing into it. That is the wrongness you notice first, the thing the eye keeps circling back to: a full-grown river flows *out* of it — cold, quick, ankle-slapping, already big enough to be somebody's river — and nothing at all flows in. The river simply begins. It rises. The pool's surface hardly moves; there is only a slow perpetual shrug out in the middle, a set of soft boils that swell and flatten and swell again, as if the water were breathing in its sleep. It makes no more sound than a kettle just before the kettle starts.

And it is clear in a way that open water is never clear. This water has spent days being filtered through miles of stone, and it comes up with nothing in it — no silt, no tannin, no weather. You can stand on the turf lip and see the whole architecture of the pool laid out below you like something under glass: the pale shelving sides, the trout hanging in ranks with their noses to the current, every pebble individually lit. And at the bottom, in the deepest part, the throat. A slot in the rock, smooth-lipped, wide as a door, going down. The clarity follows it for a while — blue, then deeper blue — and then, a few feet in, the dark takes over and the eye can go no further.

It is the same dark. That is worth standing still for. Back in the Roaring Room, the daylight leaked down the sinkhole's mouth and died a few feet into the black. Here the black leaks up the throat and dies a few feet into the blue. The two places press their faces to opposite sides of the same window. This pool is where the river that fell roaring into the earth under the dim trees comes back out of it, and everything loud about this water has already happened. It went into the ground and fell twenty-five feet into a stone room and spent itself against the walls, said the whole of what it had to say in a chamber where nothing could hear it over itself, and then ran on through the dark for days, going quieter and quieter, until it arrived here with its voice entirely used up. The Rising is not silent the way an empty place is silent. It is silent the way a person is silent who has just finished speaking.

The people knew. That is written here as plainly as the stair is written at the other end. On the flat ground above the pool the turf carries the ghosts of platforms — leveled ovals, four or five of them, houses once — and there is fire-cracked stone in the molehills, and a broken quernstone lying in the outflow where the river has been polishing it for longer than it was ever used to grind. This is where the stair-makers lived. This is the settled end of the pair. The Roaring Room was never lived in; it was *visited* — and it was visited from here. The children whose small ochre hands are pressed to the dry shelf behind the fall grew up drinking this water, and the fat that burned in the soot-blackened lamp niche was rendered at hearths whose stones are under this grass.

And they proved the connection, because it can be proved with nothing but patience and chaff. Throw a basketful of husks into the river up in the forest, above the sinkhole, and wait beside the Rising. Two days, three, four — and then the husks come up out of the throat, turning slowly in the boils, and spread across the clear water like a message delivered. Someone did that once, the first time, and stood here watching the surface, and understood in a single held breath that the deafening room in the ground and the quiet pool at their door were one thing. Everything else — the maintained stair, the lamp, the handprints in the dark, the offerings here — follows from that one afternoon.

The offerings are still down there. The throat has them. Where the room received hands pressed flat against its humming wall, the Rising received goods: things can be seen from the lip on a bright still day, resting on the pale shelves just where the blue starts going dark — a stone axe, polished and unchipped, never once swung at a tree; the green glint of beads; a long blade laid flat. Given, not lost. Nothing lands that neatly by accident. The room was where you went to be emptied; the Rising was where you brought something and let it go. One place took the voice out of you, and the other took a possession, and both of them kept what they were given, and both of them still have it.

## The Freight of the Rising

The pool produces nothing. It only delivers. Everything in it and about it was made somewhere else — most of it in the dark, much of it in the room — and comes up the throat as freight. The manifest is short, but everything on it has come a long way.

**The cold comes up first, and never stops coming.** The water rises at the temperature of the cave, winter and summer alike, which means the Rising keeps a season of its own. In August it is a shock that stops the breath, and the trout hang in it like a reward. In January, when the valley is white and the young river downstream carries plates of ice, the pool does not freeze — cannot freeze — and instead it *smokes*, breathing a slow column of vapor into the frost-still air, wearing a collar of watercress so green it looks painted. On the coldest mornings it is the only unfrozen, growing, breathing thing in the whole valley: a porthole of April held open all winter by water that has not felt the sky in days and does not know what month it is up here.

**The sand comes up second, and it is the room, in installments.** Watch the throat long enough and you see it: fine white grains dancing above the vents in the upwelling, endlessly lifted and dropped, gathering in pale drifts on the pool's floor. That sand is limestone ground to flour, and it was ground in the dark — some of it off the fluted, scalloped walls of the Roaring Room itself, the room's finished shape being unfinished after all, surrendered a hair's breadth a decade and carried here grain by grain. The chamber that swept itself so clean sends its sweepings to the Rising. The polish of the plunge pool, the deepening no instrument would bother with, the retreat of the fall's lip — all of that patience is paid out here, on the bright side, as a slow white drift a child could scoop up in one hand. The room is emigrating, at the rate of a handful a year, and this is where it is going.

**The milk comes up third, and it is weather remembered.** Two or three days after storms break over the high forest country, under a sky here that may be flawlessly blue, the Rising clouds. The clarity goes grey-white from the bottom up, the boils thicken and heave, the outflow swells, and the pool spends a day or two being turbid and urgent about rain that never fell on this valley at all. The Rising has weather of its own and weather on delay: it remembers, punctually, storms it never had. The people who lived here would have read it like a calendar of elsewhere — *it is raining in the dark country* — and known that the room, at that hour, was in full flood, its curtain whitened and its roar climbed a register, and known it without leaving their doorways.

**With the milk, sometimes, comes the trembling — the ghost of the roar itself.** Sound does not survive seven miles of stone; the thunder that fills the chamber to bursting is absorbed by the rock a few feet out and is gone. Almost. On the big flood days, when the room is at its loudest, you can lie flat on the warm limestone shelf beside the pool and press an ear to the stone — or wade to the shallows and put a palm flat on the water above the throat — and feel it: a deep, faint, continuous drumming, less a sound than a pressure remembered by the bones of the hand. It is the lowest register of the fall, the part of the voice that lives down where rock will carry it, arriving spent and tiny at the far end of the throat. This is the only place in all the open country where the Roaring Room can be heard, and it can only be heard by touching — which is, when you think of the handprints, exactly how it was always addressed.

**The eels come up fourth, and they are the freight that chooses itself.** In spring the elvers arrive from downstream, threads of glass swarming up the young river, and some of them reach the pool and keep going — down the blue, into the throat, into the dark, up the buried river to the plunge pool under the fall, where the safest and coldest and most secret water in the world is waiting to keep them for fifty years. And in autumn, going the other way, the great ones come. Somewhere in the unmeasured black beneath the fall there is an old one — there is always an old one — and some year, some October night of flood and no moon, the thing older than memory turns over in it at last, and it leaves. It threads the dark miles it has not traveled since it was a glass thread itself, and it comes up the throat of the Rising, and it surfaces.

Consider that surfacing. Eighty years in a room where light died a few feet from the door — and then the pool, and the pool at night is open to everything: cold air moving, the smell of grass, the whole wheel of the stars laid out on the still water around its head. Its first sky since before anyone now living was born, and its last, held for the length of a breath it does not breathe. Then the shrug of the outflow takes it, and it goes down the young river past the drowned quernstone, past the sleeping valley, out toward the impossible far sea. Every tenant the room's deep pool has ever kept has passed through the Rising twice: once going in, small and blind with beginning, and once coming out, huge and finished. The Rising is the room's door, and the eels are the proof that the door was never locked.

**The dippers do not come through the throat — no air-breathing thing could — but they come, by the long way, and they are kin.** The pair that nests behind the cataract fledges its young into the dim forest streams, and the young disperse downhill the way water does, and most years there is a dipper on the young river below the Rising, standing on a stone in the bright open sunshine, bobbing its perpetual bow, flashing the white feathered eyelids that its whole line evolved for talking over thunder. Here there is no thunder. The river below the pool is quiet enough for any voice. The bird signals anyway — bows, blinks, flashes — speaking the loud country's sign language on the quiet country's water, a courtesy of ancestry, and then it opens its throat and *sings*, loud and high, the song pitched to carry over a roar that is seven miles away and underground. At the Rising, the dipper's song is the nearest thing to the room's voice that the open air ever carries, and the bird delivers it not knowing it is anyone's messenger.

**And last, the tenant — the entry with no body, the same entry as before, met from the other end.**

Because the presence that dwells at the Rising is not a second presence. The room's long utterance — the single unbroken thunder, thousands of years old and still in progress — has two mouths, and this is the other one. There the voice is spoken; here it arrives, having been spoken. What fills the pool is not the absence of the roar but its afterlife: the silence of a sentence that has been completely said. The clarity is part of it — water with nothing left to declare, hiding nothing, the opposite of the room's total dark and total noise and yet unmistakably the same throat, the way an open hand and a fist are the same hand. Stand at the lip on a still day and the pool does for the eye what the chamber did for the ear: it fills it entirely, to the bottom, and empties the head out. The people had both. They went into the ground to be deafened together, and they lived here beside the answer, and it does not seem likely they thought of them as two things.

The tenant is mortal here too, and its two deaths are one death. The lip of the fall retreats; the pool under it deepens; the buried river tests its stone the way rivers do; and some thousands of years from now the plumbing will shift — the voice in the room will become a different voice, and in the same long moment the Rising will change with it. The pool may cloud and stay clouded. It may dwindle to a seep, its throat abandoned, its trout gone downstream, its watercress collar dead in some future January that finally freezes it. Or the river may simply come up somewhere else, a valley lower, and begin a new pool and a new patience, leaving this one as a grassy hollow with an unused axe and a drift of white sand at the bottom, the way it left the platforms in the turf. The two places cannot outlive one another. They are the ends of a single animal, and neither end has ever drawn a separate breath.

Until then, the freight keeps arriving. The cold comes up, and the sand, and the remembered storms, and once in a lifetime the old eel with the stars on the water around its head. Kneel at the lip, if you like, and drink — the water in your cupped hands was thunder three days ago, was the loudest place in the world, and it goes down cold and clear and wholly quiet, the roar delivered at last to the one listener it was always traveling toward. Then the young river takes the rest of it away down the bright valley, carrying the dark room out into the world a grain at a time, and behind you the pool goes on shrugging in its sleep, saying nothing, having said it all already, somewhere you have been.
