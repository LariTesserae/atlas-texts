# The Risen Town

You see it half a day before you reach it, which is the opposite of how the other place happens to you. The floodplain is a land without a single natural hill — brown-green, water-braided, flat to every horizon — and out of the middle of that flatness stands one hill anyway: a broad, steep-sided mound the color of bread crust, smoking gently from a hundred fires, crowned and terraced with walls.

It is not a hill. There is no hill under it. The mound is the town — the town standing on every town it has ever been, forty generations of walls melted and trodden and raised again on their own bodies, until the settlement lifted itself out of the plain on the accumulation of its own living. The Quarried Town goes down into what was always there. This town goes up on what it keeps making of itself. They are, though neither would put it this way, the same gesture read from opposite ends.

The river that passes it is the river that drains the plateau country, many days upstream. The rain that runs for an hour in the quarry's chiseled gutters ends, eventually, here — slowed, browned, spread out into channels that braid and rejoin like rope coming undone.

## The Mud

Here too, everything is one substance. Walls, ovens, benches, granaries, storage bins, the pens for the buffalo, the mound itself — all of it river mud, molded and sun-dried, and as in the quarry, furniture and architecture shade into each other without a seam. A bench grows out of a wall. An oven is a swelling of the courtyard. The difference is the direction of the making: nothing here was revealed. Everything was *raised* — pressed, patted, coiled, kneaded upward out of the plain, wet hands where the quarry has copper chisels.

And the mud, if you ask it, is stone. That is not mysticism; it is geology, and the people here know it in a rough, practical, unimpressed way. The river has been quarrying the highlands since before there were hands to help it — grinding the plateau down grain by grain, eating the pale limestone with frost and rain, carrying the mountains here in suspension and laying them down as silt. The delta is the spoil heap of the high country. Every brick was a mountain once. Somewhere in the flood's fine mud, indistinguishable and unlooked-for, arrives the white slurry that the rain rinses out of the quarry's gutters — the dust of last year's cutting, come down at last to be built with a second time. The Quarried Town, in the slowest possible sense, is always on its way here. The people of the mound do not know this and would not be surprised.

Fresh brick is dark, almost black, flecked gold with chopped straw. Old brick pales as it dries through the years, going grey-brown, then dun, then — at the weathered feet of the oldest standing walls — a soft, crumbling fawn, rounding at every corner, sagging at every edge. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing has ever been sharp. Where the quarry's steps are square and a little cruel, the stairs of the mound are worn to gentle swales by bare feet; where the quarry's walls carry crisp tool marks like handwriting, these walls carry only the smoothing of palms, ten thousand anonymous palms, until the surfaces have the texture of something long handled and long loved. The quarry looks newborn and will look ancient. The mound has looked ancient since it was a season old, and will look exactly this ancient forever, because it is always partly this year's.

The smell is the anti-smell of the quarry. Not the mineral spark of broken rock but wet earth, smoke, dung fires, drying fish, crushed reed, standing water, growth and rot in the same breath — the smell of a place where things do not wait inside other things but tumble constantly out of them.

## The Plan, Such As It Keeps Being

There was never a plan, and there is always one — this year's. The streets of the mound follow the streets of last year's mound, more or less, the way a retold story follows the story: same shape, different words. When a house dies — and houses here die; a wall left untended for two flood seasons slumps, softens, kneels — it is not mourned. Its melted brick is broken up, wetted, trodden with new straw, and raised again on the same footprint, or nearly the same, a little higher, because the fallen house has become part of the floor. A family's home is rebuilt a dozen times in a lifetime and is always the same home, the way a body replacing itself is the same body. Ask a woman how old her house is and she will have two answers ready, and both are true: *three years*, and *always*.

So the town leans, patches, accretes. Rooms are added when children come, melted when elders die. Reed roofs, palm-beam lintels, courtyards opening off courtyards. The streets are narrow but not trench-narrow; they wind up the mound's slope in switchbacks, stepped, shaded with mats, loud. Where the quarry is saturated with stillness, the mound is saturated with voices — haggling, singing, calling between roofs, children everywhere, geese objecting to everything. Silence here is what dust is there: a sign that something has stopped.

At the top of the mound stand the granaries, the highest and holiest structures, fat mud domes on platforms — raised each year by a hand's width or two if the flood-reader says raise them, and her word is not argued with. At the bottom, where the mound meets the plain, the newest houses stand on ground that was open field within living memory. The town grows downward at its skirts and upward at its crown, and the whole of it rises — a finger-width a year, the old people say, about the pace, though no one on either end of the river has ever measured it, at which the Quarried Town descends. Two settlements moving slowly apart along the vertical, one into the earth and one out of it, with a river and a road between them like the beam of a balance.

## The Water

In the quarry, water is counted in drops and heard one at a time in the dark of cisterns. Here it is the landscape. Channels everywhere, canals cut and recut, reflections doubling the sky, the river itself a half-mile of slow brown muscle. And once a year it becomes everything.

The flood is the whole calendar. It comes in its season with a few weeks' warning for those who can read it, rises over days, drowns the plain to the horizons, and turns the mound into an island — the only land in a world of moving water, crowded with everyone and everything, buffalo and grain and grandmothers and geese, all of it lifted on the accumulated centuries of the town's own body. The founders' foresight is under everyone's feet. The mound is the ark that does not float because it does not need to.

Then the water withdraws, and this is the point of it, this is the year's entire wealth: it leaves behind a skin of new black silt over every field, soil so rich it needs no coaxing, the plateau's substance delivered and spread. The flood destroys the low walls and pays for them in the same gesture. The delta does not have the quarry's relationship of scarcity with water; it has something more dangerous and more married — a relationship with a power that gives everything and takes some of it back, annually, on a schedule no one sets.

During the flood, at night, you can find the leak-listeners: men and women walking the levees and the raised canal banks with torches, in silence, stopping every few paces to kneel and press an ear to the earthwork. They are listening for water *finding a word* in the levee — the small conversational trickle that means a seep has started, which here is not wealth but catastrophe beginning, a wall being unwritten from inside. It is precisely what the quarry's water-readers do, cheek to the cold stone, and precisely inverted: the same act of listening for water moving through a barrier, performed on one end of the river with hope and on the other with dread. Neither town knows the other does it.

## What Grows

Everything. That is nearly the whole answer, and it is the deepest strangeness of this place if you have come down from the stone. Barley in sheets of green and then gold. Flax, blue-flowering. Lentils, onions, cucumbers swelling in the black dirt. Date palms along the levees, dropping sweetness. Reed beds vaster than the town, taller than a man poling through them, supplying mats, boats, roofs, baskets, pens, fuel, arrows, toys. The delta does not tend pocket gardens with the attention given to the irreplaceable; it wades through abundance and complains about the weeding.

Which makes one item of its trade a standing joke and a solemnity at once. In the dry season, along a particular reach where the flood lays its finest, blackest silt, people cut the soil into blocks, dry them, and load them into baskets — and the baskets go *up*, onto the backs of the caravans, walked for days toward the plateau, where people who live inside a mile of solid stone will receive them the way the delta would receive baskets of sapphire. The delta finds this mildly hilarious — *selling mud upstream* — and also does not laugh very hard, because everyone knows whose gardens that soil feeds, and everyone knows what comes down in exchange.

## What Lives

The delta is never quiet and never still. Herons stand in the shallows like bent grey reeds. Ibis probe the field edges. Frogs begin at dusk in their thousands and do not stop; the frogs are the delta's version of the chisels, the sound that sinks below attention and becomes a property of the air. Catfish shoulder through the canals, some of them enormous, whiskered, older than the children who fear them affectionately. Water buffalo stand submerged to the nostril through the heat of the day and haul the plows in the planting season. Geese are kept, ducks are kept, bees are kept in mud cylinders, and mosquitoes keep themselves and take their tax on everyone.

The people are not evidence here, as they are in the quarry; they are foreground, everywhere, loud, visible from a mile off working the green. But their marks are the opposite of the quarry's marks: not one of them will last. A footprint in the mud is gone with the next rain. A field's furrows are erased annually by the flood. The delta writes on water and knows it.

## The Stone

And then, set into all this softness, small and pale and permanent: the stone.

There is no stone in the delta. None. You could dig for a lifetime and find only deeper mud. Every piece of stone in the Risen Town came down the road from the plateau on somebody's back or a donkey's, and the people of the mound handle it accordingly. Walk into any household and you will find its stone — usually one thing, sometimes two or three in the wealthy houses: a threshold slab, worn glossy, that ten successive houses have been rebuilt around, the one fixed point in a home that otherwise melts and rises like dough; a door socket; a whetstone; a lamp, carved with a patience no one here can imagine spending; and above all a quern — the grinding stone, hollowed by generations of the same circular labor, on which every measure of the delta's grain becomes the delta's bread. In a town of infinite soft abundance, the family stone is the heirloom, the dowry, the thing carried up the mound in the flood, the thing named in disputes and wept over in divisions of inheritance. The quarry hoards soil; the mound hoards stone; each town's treasure is the other's ground.

The stone objects carry the tool marks — those crisp parallel crescents, the handwriting of the cutters — and the delta people know it is writing even though they cannot read it. They run their thumbs along the strokes. Some families, the ones whose kinship with the upstream town has not thinned past knowing, can tell you *whose* hand dressed their threshold: a great-great-someone who went to stone, still legible under three generations of the household's feet. In the quarry, the walls are the archive. Here, the archive fits in the hand, and is ground against, and stepped on daily, and never, ever let go.

## The Processes

The year turns like a wheel with the flood at the top of it. Water; withdrawal; the black gift of silt; plowing and planting in earth so soft it takes a heel print a foot deep; the green months; harvest; and then the brickmaking season, when the whole town smells of wet straw and the treading pits are full of legs and songs. Melted wall goes back into new brick; the town eats itself and bears itself, endlessly, its substance never really lost, only reshuffled. The flood takes the year's failures and mistakes and abandoned rooms and spreads them over the fields, where they grow barley. Nothing here is thrown away because there is nowhere away to throw it; there is only the cycle, and the cycle is generous.

And beneath the year's wheel, the slow process, the one nobody watches and everybody rides: the rising. Each rebuilding a little higher. Each buried floor a little deeper. The mound growing the way a tree grows, imperceptibly and absolutely, on rings of its own past — while far upstream, in the pale bowl, the terraces descend, and the river between them goes on doing what it was doing before either town existed: carrying the high country down to make the low.

# The People of the Mound

You hear them for a long time before you see them, which is the opposite of the other place. Then you hear one voice out of the many. Then you see whose it is. The people come to you sound-first, the way the town does — a hill of voices before it is a hill of walls.

## Mud and Body

They are brown the way the walls are brown — sun and river both, laid on skin whose original shade is anyone's grandmother's guess, because the delta has been taking in boat people and road people since before the mound was knee-high. Where the quarry's folk are built around the chisel, heavy in the shoulders, these are built around the water and the field: long-legged, deep-chested from poling and wading, with broad feet whose toes have spread from a lifetime of mud — a delta person's bare footprint is unmistakable, wide as a hand, and the children learn to read prints in the soft ground the way quarry children learn to read strokes in a wall.

Their hands are not gloved in callus; they are cross-hatched with fine cracking, the signature of skin wetted and dried a thousand times a season. They squat easily, as the cutters do, but they also *sit in water* easily, for hours, weeding the flooded plots, and they swim the way the cutters climb — without looking, without thought.

And they too are made, in part, of their place, and they too pay their accounting. Not the lungs — the delta's air is thick and green and kind to breathing — but the marsh fever, which lives in the wet months and comes for almost everyone eventually, burning and receding and returning on its own tidal schedule, settling into the joints of the old until they can feel the flood coming in their knees before the flood-reader calls it. It is spoken of the way the quarry speaks of the dust in the chest: not resented, exactly. The water gives everything, and takes a little back, and the little it takes it takes from inside you. This is understood to be the shape of all bargains here.

## The Voice

Every person of the mound has a voice — meaning not the organ but the *telling*: the cadence, the phrasing, the particular music of how they speak, which is theirs and nobody else's, recognizable across a dark courtyard, across a flooded field, across twenty years. Where the quarry's identity is cut into stone, the delta's cannot be — nothing here holds a mark — and so identity lives in the one medium the flood cannot melt. A person here *sounds like themselves* the way a cutter's wall looks like themselves. Mimicking someone's cadence to their face is intimacy or insult depending on everything; children trying on the voices of adults are laughed at and secretly studied, because somewhere in the mimicry their own cadence is forming, and the adults listen for it the way the quarry watches the practice wall.

Names come from the water. Every flood is named in the spring after its character — the Kind Water, the Water That Took the Grove, Low-and-Late, the Loud One — and every child carries their birth-flood folded into their name, so that a person's name is also a date and a story and a temperament all at once, and to hear a stranger's name is to know what the river was doing when they arrived. There is a superstition, held lightly and observed anyway, that people take after their flood. Nobody believes it. Everybody has an example.

Courtship moves through rhythm here too, but sung. The treading pits in brick season are where it happens: lines of people knee-deep in mud and straw, treading in time, and the treading has songs, call and answer, and to sing the answer to a particular person's call — to fall into someone's line — is precisely what falling into someone's chisel-stroke is a week's journey upstream, and neither town taught it to the other, or needed to. When a couple binds, they tread a batch of bricks together, just the two of them, four feet in one pit, one rhythm found or not found over the weeks of a season; and the wall of their first room is raised from that treading, so that the household literally stands on the ground the two of them made in step. Some batches never find the rhythm. Those bricks are used anyway — the delta wastes nothing — but everyone knows which wall they went into, and what it means, and no one says so.

## The Division of Labor

Most are farmers and builders both, because here those are one trade in two seasons. But the mound keeps its specialists, and the specialists are slightly strange, as specialists are.

**The flood-reader** is one person at a time — currently a heavy, slow-moving old man who spends the rising weeks sitting at the river's edge on a stool that is carried down for him, watching. He reads the color of the water, the kinds of upstream debris, the behavior of the ibis, the smell of the current, the way the river presses on the outside of the bends. Then he calls the height — *raise the granaries two hands; move the herds by the dark of the moon* — and his word is not argued with, because a flood called short is a drowned year. He says, when pressed, that he decides nothing. He says the year is already in the water, all of it, written far upstream in rains no one here saw fall, and he is only the one who reads it coming. The people of the quarry, could they hear him, would recognize the shape of that sentence exactly, and would be surprised to find it standing in so much mud.

**The leak-listeners** walk the levees at night during the flood, in silence, ears to the earth, hunting the trickle that means unmaking. They are honored and slightly avoided, the way the frost-tenders are upstream — people who deal directly with the element's own initiative, its work done without permission.

**The tellers** are the archive. Because no wall here outlives a grandmother, the town's memory is kept aloud, and the tellers keep it — and what they keep, above all, is the mound itself, recited downward. At festivals a teller will stand on the crown by the granaries and speak the layers: *this floor, and under this floor the floor of the Kind Water years, and under that the floor of the burning summer, and under that…* — descending through the buried town, house under house, name under name, all the way down to the plain that no living person has ever seen, the flat ground the first reed huts stood on. It takes half a night to say the whole mound. Children fall asleep partway down and are carried home, and hear the deeper layers next year, and so learn the town the way the town was made: from the top, going down. It is the exact mirror of the quarry's walls — an archive read by voice instead of fingertip, kept in air instead of stone, and no less permanent, the tellers insist, because stone can shatter but a telling well-taught cannot; it only moves house.

**The brick-masters** judge mud with their tongues and heels, know the tempering by feel, and hold the delta's hardest authority: they are the ones who declare a wall dead — too far gone to patch, ready to be melted and reborn. Families argue with them. Families lose.

**The boat people** are the delta's own topwalkers: the ones who go down the channels to the sea-lagoons and along the coast, trading grain and flax and mats for copper and timber and strangeness. They stand differently, looser, scanning water-horizons; the mound-born find them hard to look at for long. Copper comes up the river on their boats and continues upstream on donkeys' backs, so that the chisels ringing in the quarry were floated, some of them, past this very mound, and nobody along the whole route thinks of the chain entire.

## The Children

The floodplain below the mound belongs to the children in the dry season, by a custom older than any teller's deepest layer. They build there — whole miniature towns of mud, streets and granaries and tiny mounds, weeks of work, elaborate beyond all sense — and then the flood comes and takes every one of them, and this is not the tragedy of the game; it is the *completion* of the game. Delta children learn before they learn anything that making is for the making, that the flood will come for all of it, and that you build anyway, better next year. It is the precise inversion of the quarry's children playing in rooms that will one day be their real houses: those children rehearse permanence; these rehearse letting go. Each set of children would find the other's game unbearably sad, and be wrong.

There is a rite, taken around the time a child's voice settles into its cadence. At the height of the flood, the child is taken out in a reed boat, far out, until the mound is a smudge and then nothing, and the world is water to every horizon, moving, single, enormous, indifferent — and the child receives the flood the way the quarry's children, on their long stairs, receive the horizon. Some weep. Some go still. A few are changed by it and become boat people. Most look at it for a respectful while, the way you'd look at the face of the power that owns your family's debt, and are rowed home, and do not speak of it for a day or two, and are different afterward in some small way their mothers can hear.

Each year, the season's first bricks take the handprints of that year's smallest children, pressed in while the mud is soft. The prints go into the walls, and in a generation or two the walls go into the mound, and the mound goes into new bricks, which take new prints. Nothing is kept. Nothing, the tellers say, is lost. It is only moving through the town, the way the town moves through itself.

## The Ones Who Went to Stone

Deep in every teller's descent through the layers, always at the same place in the telling, comes the Turning Water — the flood of floods, two generations back, the year the river left its bed. It did not merely rise that year; it *moved*, abandoning a whole channel in a night, drowning the eastern skirts of the town and leaving a sister-village stranded a day from any water at all. The mound survived, as the mound was made to. But that year, the telling goes, certain families stood on the crown at the flood's height, looked out at a world that had rewritten itself without warning, and were finished with mud. They gathered their tools and their children and their unmelted grief and walked upstream, up the long road, up past where the river runs young and hard, up onto the grey stone of the plateau itself — swearing, the tellers say, to live in something the water could not take.

The delta remembers them with pity and awe in a mixture that has never settled. *The ones who went to stone.* The phrase has soaked into the language; it is said now of the stubborn, of the grief-stricken, sometimes, gently, of the dead. And their grandchildren come back down the road every season — the caravan cousins, white to the eyebrows with a dust the delta has learned means home to them, standing oddly, examining walls from a hand's width away instead of scanning the horizons, speaking in that upstream way with long rests in it, as if their sentences were being cut rather than poured. The old women of the mound look under the white dust, find the family faces still there after two generations, and feed them until they beg.

The trade the cousins carry is the plainest sentence the two towns ever say to each other. Down the road: baskets of pale carved stone — thresholds, querns, lamps, whetstones, each piece a season of some cutter's strokes. Up the road: baskets of black soil, dense as sleep, from the reach where the flood lays its best. Each town privately believes it is trading dirt for treasure. Each town is right.

## The Dead

The dead of the mound are buried under the floors of their own houses — beneath the swept mud floor of the room they lived in, close enough that the household walks over its grandmothers daily, and means to. When the house melts and is rebuilt, the dead are not moved; they deepen. The floor rises over them, and the next floor over that, and so the mound is not built *near* its ancestors or *above* them as a monument — it is built *of* them, layered through it like straw through brick, and the tellers' recitation of the strata is also, always, a genealogy. The town stands on everyone it has ever been.

So the quarry's living work forever *beneath* their sealed dead, in the deepening bowl under the high tiers, and the mound's living walk forever *upon* theirs, lifted a little higher by them each generation — two towns keeping their dead along the same vertical, in opposite directions, each certain its own way is the natural one.

## What They Believe

It is a simple creed and they would not call it one. Everything is on loan from the water. Nothing is already inside anything, waiting; things are *made* — pressed, raised, sung, told — and unmade, and made again from the same substance, and the substance never belonged to you. A person is not revealed by subtraction here; a person is layered, like the mound, like a flood plain — everything that has happened laid down and built on, nothing removable, the depths not gone but *underfoot*, load-bearing. Grief is handled accordingly: not chipped at daily, but given — carried down to the water in the flood season, some small made thing standing for the loss, set on the current, and watched out of sight. The river takes it downstream, or under, or wherever the river takes things, and this is called being answered.

They have heard, from the caravan cousins, the upstream creed — that the rooms are already in the stone, that the work is finding, that the self is what remains when everything else is carved away. They think it is beautiful. They think it is a little frightening, the way a sealed thing is frightening. A town that believes in being found, they say, downstream of a town that believes in being made — and the same water through both, which was making and unmaking mountains before either town had an opinion.

## Evening

At dusk the treading songs come apart, one line falling silent, then another, until a single voice somewhere finishes the verse alone and lets it rest. The frogs take over the world. Smoke goes up blue from the crown of the mound and flattens into a haze that the last light turns gold; the river goes to hammered copper; the herons fly home up the channels in ones and twos, low, deliberate. The flood-reader's stool is carried up from the bank. On the highest terrace a teller clears her throat, and the children begin to gather, and she starts at the top of the mound and begins to speak her way down through it, floor under floor, name under name.

The lamps come out. Most are clay, pinched by the hundred, cheap as anything in a land of endless mud. But here and there, in the older households, a stone lamp is lit — pale, crisp-carved, its maker's strokes still legible on it after fifty years of delta thumbs — and the oil in it is delta oil, pressed from flax grown in flood silt, and a week's walk upstream, at this same hour, its twin is being lit in a doorway three tiers down a white stone wall, burning oil from this very plain. Each town keeps the other inside its evenings and mostly does not think of it: the bread of the mound ground out on quarry stone, the light of the quarry fed on delta seed.

And if you stand on the crown long enough, past full dark, looking east, you may see the last thing that moves: a caravan on the raised road, descending toward the mound with the day's end at its back — baskets of pale stone coming down to the mud, as tomorrow baskets of black soil will go up to the stone. The tellers have a line for it, deep in the long recitation, spoken every year at the layer of the Turning Water, where the two towns' story splits and starts:

*The river was already doing this. It has always been carrying the high country down to make the low. We only joined the work from both ends — they letting the stone go, we catching it — and the water in between, patient as ever, doing most of the carrying.*

Then she descends another layer, and another, going down through the risen town the way the other town goes down through the earth: name by name, floor by floor, in no hurry at all, toward the flat ground at the bottom that no one living has seen and no one doubts is there.
