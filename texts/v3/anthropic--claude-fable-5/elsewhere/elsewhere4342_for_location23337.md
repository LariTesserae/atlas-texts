## The Snowing Lake

Follow the wind out of the bowl.

It leaves the way it always leaves — pouring over the rounded rim with the last of the organ-drone still in it, dropping down the mountain's dead shoulder, through the tappers' two green valleys, out across a steppe so wide the mountains behind it go blue and then go rumor. The wind is loud when it starts. It spends its voice on the grass. By the time it has crossed the steppe it is only a pressure, and by the time it reaches the great closed basin at the bottom of the windward country — a shallow immensity of land with no further down to offer — it has nothing left at all. The air over the basin is famously dead. Kites will not fly there. Smoke rises straight.

And where wind dies, dust falls.

It has been falling for longer than the lake has had a name: the fine bronze flour the wind strips off the whole range, the same powder the high garden combs and eats and shouts through — all of it that was not eaten, everything the fronds missed, carried a hundred miles and then, over this one place, gently let go. It comes down in a slow perpetual sift, invisible on clear days, a warm brown gauze on heavy ones, finer than pollen, patient as anything in the world.

It falls on water.

---

**The pale water**

The lake fills the basin's deepest fold — three days across by paddle, longer if you keep, as everyone keeps, to the whisper of the shores. It has no river in and no river out. It is fed from below, by cold springs rising through the basin floor, and some of the old corers will tell you that this water fell first as snow on the far mountains, sank into the tappers' valleys, and walked here underground; that the lake and the garden drink from opposite ends of the same stone. Whether that is hydrology or courtesy, the water arrives cold and it arrives secret, and nothing that enters the lake ever leaves it except as air.

The surface wears a skin. The day's dustfall floats before it drinks, and so the top of the lake is milk — a pale, matte, unreflecting blond that takes the sunset the way unfired clay takes a glaze. Push a paddle through it and the wake shows dark, the skin closing slowly behind you like a healing.

Below the skin, the water clears to the color of deep glass, and then, a few fathoms down, to a black that is not the absence of light so much as the presence of stillness. The lake is stratified and has been forever: a warm turning layer above, and beneath it, denser, colder, faintly bitter with ten thousand years of dissolved mountain, a body of deep water that has never once been stirred. The bowl in the high country has never been quiet. The deep of the lake has never been moved. These are the two permanent conditions of the windward country, and they are the same condition wearing different elements.

It is silent here. Not hushed — *silent*, in the way the garden is loud: totally, structurally, as a property of the place. Sound stops at the milk skin as if it had signed something.

---

**The snow**

Under the surface it is snowing. It is always snowing.

The dust that settles through the skin keeps falling, slower in water than in air, a soft continuous descent visible in any shaft of light you carry down: bronze motes, mineral flakes, the crumbs of the whole upwind world. This is the lake's weather, its only weather. Heavy years and lean years, storms of it after the big spring gales, thin fasting winters — but never nothing. The lake has not seen a day without snow since before there were eyes to check.

And the snow is not all mineral. Sift it — the corers do, with cloth, the exact fine cloth the tappers weave for dust-veils, arrived here down the trade chain with its purpose inverted — and some of what you catch will have legs.

The living fraction of the mountain dust falls here too. The aerial mote-swarms of the high country — the poppy-seed grazers who live and breed and die aloft, the garden's plankton, the glitter in its bronze haze — are carried out over the steppe in their uncountable dying billions, and the dead air over the basin is where their flying ends. Most arrive as husks. Some arrive alive, land on the milk skin, and live out a last bewildered day on it, a doomed colony renewed every morning by the wind's delivery. They cannot swim. They were never once thirsty, and the first water of their lives is the last thing that happens to them.

Then they sink, and become weather too. Everything below is waiting for them.

The garden eats the dust out of the air with a roar. The lake receives it out of the water in silence. Between the two, grain by grain and mote by mote, the mountains are being moved.

---

**What lives here**

The garden swarms; the lake abstains. Where the bowl holds a hundred loud kinds, the lake holds a few, and they are long-lived, slow, and profoundly quiet, and the shortness of the list is not poverty. It is discipline. It will become clear whose.

**The skimmers.** On the milk skin live the lake's only hurrying animals — flat, felt-footed things the size of a spread hand, who run on the surface tension harvesting the day's fallen motes. Their feet are furred so densely they make no ripple; they lift each foot from the skin the way you'd lift a secret. They are fast, numerous, and utterly noiseless, and if you watch one long enough you will understand that its silence is not ease. It is *care*. A skimmer that splashes does not get old.

**The veils.** A fathom or two down, hanging at the boundary where the warm water rests on the cold, the lake keeps its gardens — vast slow sheets of living tissue, translucent, pale as smoke, some of them broad as sails, suspended horizontally in the water column with their whole bodies spread to the snow. They filter. That is all they do and all they have ever done: comb the falling dust from a falling medium, exactly the work the great fronds do in the wind above the bowl, solved a second time in a second element, as if the windward country had one good idea and two climates to say it in. On heavy-fall days the veils ripple — feeding weather, the corers call it — and the ripple is the largest motion the lake permits itself. They are dimly luminous along their gathering edges, a low grey-silver glow, just enough to read the shape of them by. In the black water they hang like the ghosts of the garden's fronds, doing the fronds' work forever, without one sound.

**The listeners.** And below the veils, in the top of the dark, live the reasons for everything else's manners.

A listener is long, eyeless, the grey of deep water, and grows all its life — the young are the size of an arm, and of the old ones it is better to speak from shore. They do not hunt by sight; there is nothing to see. They do not hunt by sound; there is none. They hunt the *absence* — hanging motionless in water so still that stillness itself is legible, reading the lake's whole body for the one thing that does not belong in it, which is disturbance. A skimmer's misstep on the skin far above registers on a listener's flank like a shout. A dropped paddle is a beacon. The strike, when it comes, is the loudest event the lake ever hosts, a single vertical eruption through the milk, and then the skin heals over and the silence resumes, deeper for having been demonstrated.

This is why the lake is quiet. Not calm — *enforced*. Every living thing in it has spent its whole descent learning not to be heard, the way every living thing in the bowl has spent its whole descent learning to shout. The garden's roar and the lake's hush are both the work of everything at once. Both places are unanimous. They have simply voted opposite ways.

The corers say the oldest listener lies at the bottom of the warm water's reach, too big now to rise, and has been listening since the lake was a marsh. They say it has heard four sounds in its life, and is still considering them.

---

**The layered floor**

Below the listeners, the deep begins — the cold, dense, never-stirred water — and in it nothing lives. Nothing has to. The deep is not a habitat. It is a library.

The snow that escapes the skimmers, the veils, and everything else falls at last through the dead water and lands on the lake floor and *stays*. Nothing burrows here; nothing rasps or drums or rearranges. Each year's fall lies down on the last year's fall in a fine even sheet: pale in the dry gale seasons when the mountain sheds heavily, dark in the still seasons, pale and dark, pale and dark, an annual banding laid down in perfect order for ten thousand years and more. The floor of the Snowing Lake is the past of the entire windward country, filed by year, in the dark, untouched.

Everything is in it. The mote-bodies, by the trillion per stripe — the garden's dead, delivered by wind and filed by water. Frond-hairs. Ash from steppe fires, each fire its own grey line. Pollen from valleys that no longer grow that crop. Once, in a core raised two generations back and kept still in the head village, a single perfect wing of oiled paper, six-ribbed, pressed flat in a layer a century down — a shouter's wing, blown a hundred miles from a bowl its finders had never heard of, its voice long since echoed home, its body arrived late and quietly entered into the record.

The garden keeps its dead in amber, by accident, on the backs of the patient ones. The lake keeps everyone's dead on purpose, in order, forever. They are the windward country's two archives, and only one of them can be read.

---

**The marks of hands**

The lake people read it.

They live on the water — pontoon villages and stilt-platforms moored over the shallows, because the shores are a treacherous flour-mud that will take a leg to the hip — and they are ordinary in every respect but one inheritance, which is the reeds. Long jointed tubes, worked and waxed, coupled section to section, lowered through the black to the floor and pressed home and raised again with a core of the layers standing inside: a striped rod of years. A *read*.

The reads are dried and racked under the village eaves, hundreds of them, the settlement's true wealth. An old reader can lay a thumbnail on a stripe and tell you the year — the year of the black ash, the year the falls nearly stopped, the seven fat pale years in a row that the songs still mention. Valley disputes travel weeks to be settled here. Whose grandfather's field burned first; whether the drought came before or after the treaty. *Ask the lake* is the region's phrase for any question too old for the living, and people do ask it, and the lake, sectioned and dried and held to the light, answers.

On open water they whisper. Ashore they are as loud as anyone, but past the mooring posts the voice drops, by rule so old it is no longer explained. Some tellers frame it as plain sense — the listeners strike at noise, and a shout can bring something up under a hull. Others say the deep is *taking things down*, and one does not talk over a scribe. The rule is the same either way.

Their lamps burn amber. Warm fist-lumps of cured resin, arrived up the long trade chain — the tappers' valleys, the river markets, six changes of hands — that give a clean bright flame in the damp where oil sputters, and a smell the whole lake knows and cannot place: pepper and hot copper, the smell of a kitchen where something has been baking for a thousand years. At the lake, the resin's source is only a story. *A mountain that sings*, the traders say. *A garden with no water in it, louder than a river.* The children do not believe it. What would a loud place even be for?

And once a year, at the turn of the still season, the villages pole out over the deepest water for the Paying. A single amber bead, drilled and threaded and cut loose. The oldest reader hums — very softly, hardly a breath — a low sustained chord, an eerie perfect fifth that the lake people believe is theirs, that came down the valleys generations ago in the throats of resin traders who caught it from tappers who caught it, humming over their work, from a hollow stalk on an eastern rim they never went near. The garden's oldest voice, walked a hundred miles in people, because people are the only current that can carry sound that far. It is hummed once, over the silence, and the bead goes over the side.

It falls through the milk, through the glass, past the veils' grey glow, through the black, through the snow, turning, and lands on the year's fresh page, and stays. Somewhere down there, threaded through the centuries of stripes, is a line of amber beads — the garden's blood, entered into the lake's memory, one drop per year, a correspondence between two beings who have never met, kept up by hand.

They sink their dead, too, weighted, in one deep fold set aside for it. Their phrase for the burial — for the moment the water takes the body from sight — translates, exactly, as *the echo going home*. It is the same phrase the tappers use for a dead glider's voice being passed around the colony and released. Two peoples, joined by nothing but a trade chain and a hummed fifth, keeping the same idiom at opposite ends of the wind. Neither knows the other says it.

---

**And the oldest resident of all**

Everything here turns over, as everything does. The skimmers live a season. The veils tear in heavy weather and regrow. Even the listeners die, in the end, and sink through the water they policed, and are filed like anyone — there are stripes in the old reads with a listener's whole length pressed through them, a grey seam across forty years of pages. The lake people hand their reeds and their whisper down because no reader lasts. Nobody who keeps the record is kept out of it.

But the record has never lost a year. Not once. Through every death, every dynasty of readers, every fire and famine written into its own stripes, the floor has taken its annual page without fail — the same unbroken keeping, changing hands, changing contents, never changing into forgetting. It is older than any body in the basin. Every resident is born onto it, feeds it, and is filed by it; the silence is its house rule, the snow is its diet, the listeners are its wardens, the veils its tithe-collectors skimming a living from what falls, the people its clerks, paid in verdicts.

That, finally, is what lives in the Snowing Lake. The record does. It has a body of layers and a blood of cold spring-water and a skin of falling dust, and it replaces every servant it has, constantly, while remaining itself — which is the other way, the mirror way, of being the thing that lives in the high bowl. For the roar and the record are the two residents of one country, and the wind between them is the present tense turning into the past. The garden is the world *happening* — all voice, no memory, its dead released as echoes, its whole substance rebuilt nightly, forgetting itself with perfect health. The lake is the world *having happened* — all memory, no voice, refusing to lose so much as a grain. One place cannot keep anything. The other place cannot make a sound. Between them the wind runs its endless errand, carrying the day's dust and the day's dead out of the loud country and laying them down in the quiet one, so that grain by grain, mote by mote, wing by paper wing, the Roaring Garden is being written into the floor of the Snowing Lake — the bowl slowly grinding itself away upwind, its whole unrepeatable racket surviving nowhere, in the end, except here: in order, in the dark, in silence, on the one shelf in the world long enough to hold it.

The corers do not put it that way. They only pole out, once a year, and hum the fifth they think is theirs, and lower the bead, and watch the milk close over it.

They say the deep is listening. They say silence is how you address it.

And a hundred miles upwind, at that same hour, the light goes bronze on the eastern rim, and the drumming starts.
