# The Cistern at the Top of the Spindle

You reach it by climbing for days through the inside of something no one built recently — up the throat of a structure so tall that the weather lives in its middle floors and the clouds are a memory below you. And at the very top, where you expect wind and emptiness, there is a room full of warm water.

It is not a large room. Call it a chapel's worth of space: an octagonal chamber perhaps nine strides across, walled in metal so old it has stopped being any particular metal. The rivets have worn down to smooth coins. The seams have healed over with mineral skin, pale green and ochre, deposited by centuries of the same water moving in the same slow circuits. Nothing here has an edge anymore. The great valve-wheels along the walls have softened into discs, like sea-worn glass, like the faces of statues touched by too many hands. Whatever this room was for, it finished being for it long ago. Now it simply *is*, in the settled, final way of riverbeds and mountains — except there is no rock here, no soil, no natural substrate at all. Every surface is made. The floor is grating. The ceiling is plate. Even the sediment drifting in the corners is rust, paint-flake, and the shed scales of things that live here.

The water fills the chamber nearly to the top. A gap of perhaps a forearm's width remains between the surface and the ceiling, a thin silver lung of air that fogs and clears with the temperature of your breath. The water itself is blood-warm. Heat rises through the floor grating from somewhere far below — an engine, or the descendant of an engine, still turning in the dark of the spire's core. You can feel it before you hear it, and you hear it constantly: a deep, four-beat throb that the water carries into your chest, *whum-whum, whum-whum*, loud as a giant's pulse. Every so often a pressure-cycle completes somewhere in the walls and a long shuddering groan travels around the octagon, pipe to pipe, and the whole room rings like the inside of a bell that is being patient about it.

Light enters through a single industrial porthole, high on the western wall, its glass thick as a fist and gone slightly amber with age. Up here, above everything, the daylight is plain and clean — no golden drama, just honest sun — and it comes through the water in one broad, tilting shaft that swings slowly across the chamber over the course of a day like the beam of an unhurried lighthouse. Where the shaft lands, the room is illuminated; everywhere else is a soft green-brown gloaming, never quite dark.

In that shaft of light, the garden shows itself.

The vegetation here did not arrive politely. It grew up through the warm water from spores or seeds carried in some forgotten resupply, generations ago, and it has made the machinery its trellis. Long ribbon-weeds, the color of old bottle glass, anchor in the mouths of disused pipes and stream upward toward the air gap, where their tips break the surface and unfurl small waxy leaves into the silver lung. A kind of freshwater coral-moss — nobody named it; nobody is here to name things — upholsters the lower third of every wall in dense rust-red velvet. Pale flowering stalks rise from the floor grating in scattered clumps, two here, three there, with long bare water between them. The room never feels crowded. It feels *furnished*, sparsely and deliberately, the way an old keeper might arrange a few good plants in a large quiet office.

And it is inhabited — generously, though never thickly. Small copper-sided fish, each no longer than a finger, move through the chamber in loose strings of six or seven, riding the slow thermal circulation the engine-heat drives: up the warm eastern wall, across beneath the air gap where they sip at the surface with little audible kisses, down the cooler west, around again. An eel the color of pewter, old enough to have a moss garden growing on its back, lives in the horizontal intake pipe at floor level and emerges twice a day, regular as the valve-cycles, to make one full deliberate circuit of the room. Translucent shrimp graze the velvet walls. Something small and many-legged keeps house inside the worn valve-wheel on the north wall and rearranges its collection of paint-flakes when the light shaft passes over. Now and then a fish is taken — a flick, a puff of glitter, the strings re-forming a little shorter — and the room absorbs this the way it absorbs everything, without pausing its pulse.

The strangeness reveals itself slowly, the longer you float in the warm and the throb. The water is *too high to exist*. There is no rain at this altitude, no spring, no plausible source — and yet the level never drops. Watch the pipes long enough and you understand: the water is being lifted. Somewhere in the kilometers of structure below, the old engine is still performing its single remaining duty, raising water up the entire height of the spire, beat by beat, to keep this one small room full. Bubbles here behave oddly, too — they rise, but reluctantly, wobbling, sometimes pausing mid-water as if remembering how far up they already are. And once or twice a day, with no warning, the entire body of water in the chamber rotates a quarter-turn — gently, all at once, plants leaning, fish carried sideways without alarm — as some vast and ancient mechanism beneath the floor advances one notch in a sequence whose purpose ended before the moss was born.

The fish do not mind. They have never known a room that doesn't turn.

This is what the place is, in the end: the last warm cell of an enormous dead machine, a pocket of summer pinned to the top of the sky, loud with its own heartbeat, gardened by accident, populated by tenants who arrived as stowaways and stayed as heirs. Everything sharp has worn smooth. Everything purposeful has relaxed into habit. The engine lifts the water because lifting the water is what it does; the water holds the garden; the garden feeds the fish; the eel makes its rounds.

High above the world, behind a fist of amber glass, the little flooded chapel goes on beating.
