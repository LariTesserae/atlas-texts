**The Stilling Room**

You reach it by accident or not at all: a slot in the hillside no wider than a doorway, half-curtained by roots, descending eleven or twelve meters through cold sandstone before it opens. The opening is the whole of it. A single chamber, roughly the size of a modest chapel — you could cross it in twenty steps if the floor allowed twenty steps, which it does not.

It is dark in the way only underground places are dark: a darkness that has texture, that you feel against your face like fabric. There is no light source. There has never needed to be one. The garden here grew up without it and does not miss it.

Because it is a garden — that is the first thing your hands tell you, before your eyes give up trying. Every surface is upholstered. The walls carry mosses an inch deep, pale in the beam of any lamp you bring, ivory and bone-green, dense as wet felt. From the ceiling hang root-curtains and long blanched ferns, ferns that have never performed photosynthesis in their lives, their fronds soft and colorless as paper soaked and dried a hundred times. The floor is a mat of liverworts and creeping things layered over older layers, generations deep, springy underfoot, exhaling cold humidity with each step. Foliage crowds foliage. Leaves touch leaves touch stone. In places you must push through it, parting fronds like beaded curtains, and they close again behind you without complaint.

The cold is constant and exact. A degree or two above freezing, never less, never more. Your breath fogs; the fog drifts and settles as droplets on the moss, and this is most of the room's weather. Water arrives in no hurry — condensation creeping down the root-curtains, a slow seep along one wall that has, over centuries, lacquered the stone with translucent mineral, a single drip somewhere off in the dark that falls perhaps once a minute into a basin you never quite locate. That drip is most of the room's sound. The rest is your own breathing, the small creak of the vegetation taking your weight, and occasionally — rarely — a soft fibrous sigh as some old frond, finished, lets go and settles into the floor to become floor.

How the garden lives without sun is not a mystery the room bothers to dramatize. Half-buried in the moss, scattered through the chamber like dropped tools, are the artifacts: a dozen or so objects, none larger than a footstool, of a smooth gray material that is neither warm nor cold to the touch — which is to say it is precisely the temperature your hand expects, always, which is its own quiet impossibility. Some are spheres. One is a low ring, like a well-mouth, grown over almost entirely. The moss prefers them. It mounds thickest around them, and the ferns lean toward them the way ordinary plants lean toward windows.

They are feeding the room. Not with light, not with heat — with something more direct. Whatever process turns energy into sugar in a leaf is happening here without the intermediate steps, the chemistry handed its conclusion. The plants are pale because pigment would be pointless. The artifacts make no sound, draw on no visible source, and have evidently been doing this long enough that the question of *how* has eroded away like everything else, leaving only the settled fact of it. One sphere near the back wall is cracked — a hairline seam, mineral-stained — and the vegetation around it grows thinner, slower, a fairy ring of decline a meter across. The room is very old. Even its machines are weathering now, at the pace of stone.

There are other marks of hands, faint ones. A run of shallow steps cut into the descent, worn bowl-smooth. Three niches carved into one wall at shoulder height, empty, their edges softened, their floors carpeted in moss like everything else. Someone tended this place once, or built it, or merely visited often enough to want stairs. They are long gone and the garden has absorbed their absence without remark.

Nothing animal lives here. No insects tick through the leaf litter; no fish stir the seep-pools; nothing has eyes to need light or legs to need room. Now and then a thread of fungus surfaces through the moss, pale on pale, and that is the closest the chamber comes to a visitor. The ecology is closed, slow, and complete: the gray objects give, the plants take, the dead become the floor, the water goes around. A frond can take a decade to unroll. The mounds over the artifacts thicken by a fingerwidth in a generation.

Stand still long enough and the room makes its case for itself: that growth does not require sun, that abundance does not require warmth, that a thing can be lush and lightless and patient all at once. The drip falls. Somewhere in the dark a leaf, finished after forty years, lets go.

It will still be doing this — all of it, at this same unhurried rate — long after the hillside above has changed its mind several times about what it wants to be.
