**The Pumphouse Quarter**

The place is small enough to walk across in ten minutes, if walking were possible, which mostly it is not. You move through it instead — climbing, stooping, following the catwalks where the catwalks survive, and where they don't, following the roots that have replaced them, which are broader and more trustworthy anyway.

It was a works district once: a pumping station, a boiler hall, two rows of workers' terraces, a chandlery, a small foundry. Hamlet-sized. Built low in a warm fen at the bottom of everything, where the water table was never more than a forearm's depth below the brick, and where the whole reason for the settlement was to keep that water moving — lifting it, channeling it, sending it elsewhere. The pumps still run. That is the first thing you understand about the place, before you understand anything else, because you hear it from a quarter mile out: a deep, patient, two-stroke concussion, *whump... whump...*, the sound of an old compound beam engine that has been turning over for so long that the rhythm has stopped being mechanical and become geological. The frogs — what few there are — time their silences to it.

The vegetation did not invade the district. That's the wrong word for what happened. The district *composted upward into* the vegetation, over a long enough span that there was never a moment of ruin. Strangler figs took the chimneys first, and the chimneys, still warm, still venting, suited them; the figs grew into hollow living flues, and now smoke rises through wood. The terrace houses are recognizable as houses only by their rhythm — door, window, door, window — pressed like fossils into walls of root-mass and laurel and a dense, rubbery epiphytic growth that drinks the permanent steam. Brick shows in patches, the deep oxblood red of brick that has been wet for a century, soft enough at the corners to mark with a thumbnail. Iron shows too: pipe runs, valve wheels, stair rails, all of it sleeved in moss so uniformly that the moss reads as a manufactured coating, a green lagging applied by some thorough crew. In places it is hard to say whether a pipe carries water or sap. In a few places it genuinely carries both — condensate weeping from a flange joint, taken up by aerial roots that have plumbed themselves into the leak and now depend on it.

Everything drips. The air is warm and fully loaded, just short of fog, and the boiler hall keeps it that way; the place manufactures its own climate inside its boundary, and the boundary is sharp — you can stand at the edge of the quarter with one shoulder in ordinary fen air and one shoulder in the works' breath, blood-warm and smelling of hot iron, wet leaf litter, lubricating oil, and the faint sweetness of cellulose breaking down. Beyond the boundary the marsh is just marsh. Inside it, every surface sweats. Water beads on leaves the size of doors and falls in fat irregular drops onto corrugated roofing that has thinned to lace, so that beneath the engine's *whump* there is a continuous, randomized percussion, tin and leaf and standing water, loud in aggregate. People who live here — there are a few, you see their lamps, their laundry lines strung between fig buttresses, their narrow punts tied to valve stems — speak close to each other's ears out of habit, even outdoors, even at night.

The terrain itself is barely terrain. The ground floor of the district is flooded to mid-shin in clear, tea-dark water over brick paving you can still see, herringbone-laid, perfectly intact, greened. Streets persist as channels. The old camber of the roadways now governs the slow drainage, so the water moves — but at the pace of a thing with nowhere urgent to be, a current you confirm only by watching a leaf for a full minute. Duckweed gathers in the lees of doorways. Where the foundry stood there is a pool deeper than the rest, blood-warm from a buried steam line that no one has ever found the valve for, and in it grows a single colony of giant water-lily, pads thick as table tops, that has occupied that pool for as long as anyone's grandmother and has expanded, in living memory, by perhaps one pad's width.

That is the tempo of everything here. The engine runs; the figs thicken; the brick softens; the water moves its leaf. The works produce something still — the engaged hum of dynamos says so, the wires sagging off ceramic insulators grown halfway into bark, the few electric lamps burning steady yellow inside their cauls of moss, day and night, because no one remembers which switch is which and the cost of being wrong is darkness. But production here is not an event. It is a condition, like the humidity. Coal comes in by punt along the widest channel, infrequently, in small amounts; the figs are fed the ash. There is an understanding — never written, possibly never even said — that the engine and the green have arrived at terms. The plants do not foul the moving parts. In exchange they get the warmth, the steam, the leaks, the carbon. Maintenance consists mostly of pruning, done with the reverence of surgery.

Animals are scarce, and the scarcity is felt. The constant percussion drives off most birds; the warm water suits few fish. What remains has specialized: a kind of pale gecko that hunts moths on the lamp housings, hands splayed on the warm glass; an eel or two in the foundry pool, glimpsed once a season; the metronome frogs. Their rarity makes each appearance an occasion. Children — there are two or three — keep tallies.

Light comes down ordinary and honest through the canopy, broken into coins and bars, neither dim nor bright, and at midday the steam plumes from the fig-chimneys catch it and stand briefly gold above the green mass of the quarter, above the *whump... whump...*, above the dripping, before sliding back into the general warm gray breath of the place — which will be the same tomorrow, and was the same forty years ago, and is changing all the time, too slowly for any single life to catch it in the act.
