# The Nursery

You reach it by going down — not far down, just below the frost line, where the soil gives way to something that is no longer soil. A throat of green descends maybe ten meters, narrow enough that the moss on either wall brushes your shoulders, and then opens into a chamber the size of a small chapel. That is the whole place. It has walls you could walk to in twenty steps in any direction, a ceiling you could touch from another's shoulders. It is bounded, complete, and not finished.

Nothing here was built. Everything here was grown, and most of it is still growing, very slowly, toward a configuration that does not exist yet.

## Substrate

There is no stone visible anywhere. Whatever rock the chamber was hollowed from — if it was ever hollowed, if it didn't simply *accrete* this way — has been overgrown so thoroughly that the plant mass has become the geology. The walls are a deep, layered felt: liverworts over older liverworts over a compressed peat of their ancestors, banded like sedimentary strata, except the strata are alive all the way down. Push a hand into the wall and it gives, cold and dense, with the resistance of a body. Withdraw it and the depression closes over hours, like very slow water.

The floor is a single cushion plant, or perhaps many cushion plants that long ago stopped maintaining the distinction. It is firm in some places and treacherously soft in others, and the soft places are warm-less — there is no warmth anywhere here. The temperature sits a degree or two above freezing and never moves. Breath fogs. The fog does not disperse so much as get *absorbed*; the wall-felts drink it visibly, the moss darkening in the shape of your exhalation.

Everything weeps. The ceiling carries a permanent condensate, droplets hanging in such density that the dim light catches them like a low, inverted field of dew. They fall on no schedule anyone has found. Sometimes a hundred fall in a minute; sometimes none fall for an hour, and the silence of their not-falling is its own kind of pressure. The water that does fall is taken up immediately. Nothing pools. The chamber is saturated and yet has no standing water at all, the way a lung is wet without containing a puddle.

## What the Plants Are Doing

Here is the thing that takes time to understand, and that some visitors never let themselves understand: the vegetation is not a covering. It is an *instrument*, and it is mid-construction, and it is building itself.

In the center of the chamber — the single feature around which everything else seems oriented — there is a structure perhaps two meters tall that resembles a fern crozier, an unfurled fiddlehead, except it is woven from thousands of individual stems that have braided themselves with a precision no tropism in any textbook accounts for. Stems cross each other at exact angles. They fuse at junctions and the junctions are doing something: if you watch in the dim light long enough, you'll see a faint glassy thread inside each stem, a vascular channel that carries not water but something denser, slower, that refracts strangely — light bends *around* the threads slightly, the way it bends around mass.

The braid is not finished. New stems rise from the cushion floor at a rate of perhaps a millimeter a day, blind and pale, and travel — over weeks — toward the structure, where they are incorporated. Where they should go seems to be already decided. There are gaps in the braid shaped exactly like stems that have not arrived yet.

No one made this. There are no tools here, no marks, no cut surfaces, no seam anywhere of intention applied from outside. Whatever engineered this place engineered it as a *seed*, somewhere far upstream in time, and what stands in the chamber now is the seed's slow argument with physics, conducted in cellulose, at a pace of centuries. The structure is embryonic. The chamber is a womb. What it is gestating, nobody can say, because the thing being grown is the kind of thing that can only be described by itself, once complete.

## The Loudness

For a small, cold, dim room, it is shockingly loud.

The dominant sound is the moss itself. The wall-felts produce a continuous low chorus — not metaphorically; the layered mats trap and release water under tension, and every release is a tick, a pop, a wet syllable, and there are millions of release-points firing at all times. The aggregate is a dense crackling roar, like rain on leaves heard from inside the leaf. It rises and falls on cycles of about eleven minutes, for reasons that may be circulatory.

Under that: the braid hums. A real, felt-in-the-sternum hum, low and unwavering, the sound of the glassy threads doing whatever it is they do. The hum has no overtones. Acousticians who have recorded it report that it is, in a strict sense, *too pure* — a frequency with no harmonic structure at all, which should not be producible by any vibrating physical body. The moss-roar seems arranged around it, the way an orchestra is arranged around an A.

And over everything, the fauna.

## The Fauna

The chamber is crowded with animals, layered, near-touching, and almost all of them are working.

Pale amphipods — translucent, cold-bodied, the size of grains of rice — move through the wall-felts in slow processions, and they are carrying things: spores, fragments of stem, beads of that dense refractive fluid held in their mouthparts like lanterns. They deliver these to the braid. Whether they were recruited, bred, or simply found the arrangement agreeable is unknown; they have been doing it long enough that their gut flora and the braid's vascular chemistry are now the same chemistry.

Blind salamanders the color of candle wax lie embedded in the cushion floor with only their heads exposed, dozens of them, evenly spaced, motionless for weeks at a time. They appear to be *listening*. When the eleven-minute moss cycle peaks, they all swallow at once — an audible, chamber-wide gulp — and the hum of the braid shifts by a fraction of a hertz.

There are snails whose shells are grown from the same glassy thread-material as the braid's interior, so that each snail is, in some small way, a component that has wandered off. There are colonies of springtails that assemble themselves, on no observable signal, into brief geometric sheets on the ceiling — a hexagonal raft of ten thousand bodies, held for a few seconds, then dispersed. Each assembly precipitates a fall of condensate. Each fall feeds a specific region of the floor. It is irrigation, or it is liturgy, and at this depth in this cold there may not be a difference.

## Light

What light there is comes from the droplets. Each hanging bead of condensate on the ceiling holds a faint internal glow, blue-grey, weaker than moonlight — and the glow is not in the water. It arrives in the water. The droplets seem to condense light out of the air the way they condense moisture, gathering some thin ambient illumination that has no identifiable source, concentrating it to barely-visible. The chamber is dim the way the inside of a closed eye is dim: not dark, but lit by something that has no business being there.

Drop a droplet — knock one loose — and its light goes out the instant it leaves the ceiling. It falls dark. The floor drinks it dark.

## The Pace of the Place

Nothing here will surprise you in an afternoon. The chamber operates on durations that make a human visit a single frame of its film. The braid will not complete in your lifetime, or your children's. The wall-strata thicken by a finger-width per century. The salamanders may be the same individual salamanders that lay here when the seed first opened, however long ago that was; nothing about their metabolism, at this temperature, rules it out.

But stand still long enough — an hour, two — and you begin to feel the gradient, weak but real, the way you feel a slow river by the way it leans on your legs. Everything in the chamber is moving toward the center. The stems, the amphipods and their lanterns, the falling water, the absorbed breath, the pure hum, the light in the droplets. All of it converging, with the patience of a thing that has never been in a hurry because it has never once been uncertain, on the unfinished braid — on the gap at its crown, which is shaped exactly like something, and is empty, and is waiting.

You will want to know what it's becoming. The chamber does not withhold the answer. It simply gives it at its own speed, one millimeter a day, in a language of cold stems, to whoever is still there in a thousand years.
