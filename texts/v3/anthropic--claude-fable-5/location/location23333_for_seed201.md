**The Cirque at the Top of the Weather**

There is a bowl near the summit of a mountain so new that the mountain itself has not finished deciding its shape. The ridgelines around it are knife-fresh — no glacier has yet had time to gnaw them round, no million winters of frost have shattered them into scree. The rock breaks in clean planes, pale grey with a faint blue cast, and the edges of the bowl stand up against the sky like something cut that morning. You could lay your palm flat against a fracture face and feel that it has never been touched by anything but light.

The bowl is small. You could walk its rim in twenty minutes, if the rim were walkable. From lip to lip it is perhaps three hundred paces — a single room of a place, a stone amphitheater hung at an altitude where the air comes thin and tastes faintly of metal and snow. Above it there is only more sky than seems reasonable. Below it, on most days, lies an unbroken floor of cloud, white to every horizon, so that the bowl exists as an island in a sea it never touches.

And the bowl is green.

This is the first strangeness of the place, and it announces itself slowly. The cold here is constant — your breath plumes, meltwater refreezes in the shadows by mid-afternoon — but the light is enormous. Sunlight at this elevation has had almost nothing taken from it. It arrives whole, doubled by the cloud-sea beneath, which throws it back upward so that the bowl is lit from above and below at once. Shadows here are weak, tentative things. The plants have learned to live on light instead of warmth.

They grow low and dense and ancient-seeming, though nothing here is ancient. Cushion plants in hard domes, some the size of a fist, some the size of a sleeping animal, packed so tight you could stand on them without leaving a print — though you would feel them give, very slightly, like cold flesh. Their surfaces are stitched with flowers almost too small to see individually: thousands of them, white and yolk-yellow and a blue so pale it reads as silver. Between the cushions run carpets of moss in colors moss should not have — rust, wine, a green so dark it is nearly black — fed by the bowl's one reliable gift of water: condensation. Every night the thin air lets go of what little moisture it holds, and every dawn the entire bowl is jeweled with it, every dome and blade beaded silver, dripping in slow single drops into the gravel. There is no stream. There is no pond. There are only pockets — a dozen hollows in the fractured rock where meltwater and dew collect into basins no bigger than cupped hands, each one ringed by the densest, greenest growth, each one drunk down to stone by evening and refilled by morning.

The second strangeness is the silence — not the silence of absence but of acoustics. The wind, which screams across the outer ridges hard enough to polish the rock, does not enter the bowl. It passes over the lip and you can see it go: cloud-streamers tearing past overhead, fast as river water. But inside, the air barely moves. Sound behaves accordingly. A drop of meltwater falling into a basin can be heard from thirty paces. The bowl holds small sounds the way a cathedral holds a whisper, and so everything that lives here has learned to be quiet, and the place teaches you, within minutes, to be quiet too.

Everything that lives here — and this is the third strangeness — is many things, arriving one at a time.

The bowl is never crowded and never empty. It functions as a station, a waypoint, a green stone in the white river of the sky. Birds come up out of the cloud-sea below: choughs with feathers like wet ink, rising on currents you cannot feel, wheeling once around the bowl in total silence before dropping onto a cushion-dome to probe for insects. Finches small as thumb-joints, dusted rose and grey, appearing in twos and threes at the dew basins, drinking with quick tilted heads, gone again. A hare lives somewhere among the eastern fractures — white year-round, since the bowl never decides on a season — and is seen mostly as a stillness that was not there before, a shape resolving out of the rock. Once or twice a day a raptor crosses overhead without stopping, a slow crucifix shadow that makes the whole bowl pause, every small thing pressed flat, the silence going one degree deeper. Then it passes, and a finch resumes, and the bowl breathes again.

Insects work the flowers through every bright hour — dark-bodied flies furred like bees against the cold, moving slowly, almost ceremonially, in the thin air. At night, things move that are heard only as a click of stone, a soft displacement. By morning there are tracks in the dew before the dew is gone: the splayed stars of bird feet, the neat punctuation of the hare, and occasionally something larger that came up over the lip in the dark, circled the basins, and left.

People have been here. Not many, not often, and not recently — or perhaps recently; it is hard to say, because the cold and the stillness preserve everything. At the bowl's southern lip, where a natural notch gives the only sane passage in, someone long ago cut steps into the live rock — eleven of them, shallow, made with a hammerstone and patience, their tool-marks still crisp as the day they were struck. Just inside the notch stands a cairn taller than a man, built without mortar, each stone chosen and turned and seated so well that the whole thing rings faintly if you tap it. Wedged among its stones are offerings: a spiral of horn, a flat pebble drilled through and threaded on a cord of twisted grass long since bleached white, a small blade of knapped stone the color of honey, translucent when held to the light. Near the largest dew basin, half-grown-over by cushion plants, lies a single slab worn smooth in its center — a grinding stone, or a seat, or both — with a faint stain of red ochre still ghosted into its surface.

That is all. No walls, no shelter, no hearth. Whoever climbed here did not come to stay. They came, it seems, to do something brief and considered, and to leave the bowl as they found it.

The mild and persistent weirdness of the place is this: the plants grow in rings. Not everywhere, and not obviously — you might spend an hour in the bowl before you notice — but the oldest cushion-domes are arranged in slow concentric arcs centered on the deepest dew basin, as though the vegetation were a ripple frozen mid-spread, or as though something at the center had once instructed the bowl in how to grow and the bowl was still, patiently, obeying. The rings do not align with sun, slope, or water flow. The cairn-builders noticed; their steps and their stone are placed precisely between two arcs, touching neither.

And through all this quiet, the place is furiously alive with process. The cloud-sea below rises and falls like a tide on a six-hour rhythm, sometimes lapping at the lip of the bowl, sending feelers of mist over the edge that frost the nearest cushions white before retreating. Frost forms; light destroys it. The basins fill and empty. Rock, still settling from its violent recent birth, occasionally ticks and splits — a sound like a knuckle cracking in an empty room — and a new fracture face is added to the bowl's collection of clean surfaces, which the moss will reach within the decade. Birds rise out of the white and sink back into it. The hare turns its head. The flowers, thousands per arm-span, track the doubled light across the doubled sky.

It is a small green eye at the top of the weather, wide open, cold, brilliantly lit, watching almost nothing, missing nothing at all.
