**The Cleft at Wether's Tooth** — though no one named it; the name is only what it would be called, if anyone came.

Near the summit of a mountain too young to have a gentle side, where the rock is still sharp enough to open a hand laid flat against it, there is a split. Not a cave — caves are old things, dissolved patiently out of limestone over ages. This is a wound. The peak rose fast, geologically speaking, shouldering its way up in the last eye-blink of deep time, and somewhere in that rising the stone cracked along its grain and pulled apart, leaving a cleft maybe forty paces long, twelve wide at its broadest, and deep enough that the sky becomes a pale seam far overhead, then a thread, then nothing. The walls have never been touched by weather long enough to soften. Every surface is faceted, angular, edged. Frost-shatter has barely begun its work; the spall that does fall comes down in clean plates that ring like crockery.

It is dark in the cleft. Not absolute dark — the overhead seam admits a grey dilution of light for a few hours when the sun crosses the gap, a brief vertical twilight — but functional dark, the kind the body navigates by sound and the prickle of nearness.

And it is *full*.

This is the strange thing, the thing that should not work at this altitude: the cleft is one of the densest concentrations of living bodies for a hundred miles in any direction. Above the snowline, where the open slopes are scoured and lifeless, the cleft holds warmth — barely, a degree or two above the murderous outside, the rock exhaling the last heat of its own formation, the deep stone still faintly tepid like something recently alive. Cool, not cold. And every flying, crawling, clinging thing that the mountain's rise stranded up here has found it.

The walls are shingled with bodies. Bats, yes — small, dense-furred, packed in overlapping rows like roof slates — but also a kind of swift that has stopped migrating, and pale crickets the length of a thumb, and a slow tide of soft-shelled creatures something between a snail and a moth that graze the condensation films, and salamanders the colour of wet flint that should not exist at this elevation and seem to know it, moving with the deliberateness of trespassers. Layered. Near-touching. In places the rock cannot be seen at all under the living shingle, and the shingle breathes — not metaphorically: the whole wall rises and falls in a slow communal rhythm, thousands of small respirations having drifted, over years of contact, into something close to synchrony. Stand in the dark and you can hear the cleft inhale.

The sound never stops. It is loud in the way a river is loud — a roar built from small sources: wing-leather, chitin, the click-language of the crickets, the drip-counting of water, the occasional shear and plate-ring of falling stone followed by a wave of disturbance that travels through the colony like wind through grain and then settles. There are also sounds without obvious sources. A low tone, around the pitch of a struck jar, that comes up through the floor at long intervals — the young rock still settling, still adjusting its grip on itself, the mountain audibly continuing to arrive.

Water is intermittent and precious. There is no stream. Snowmelt finds the crack from above and descends as a slow seep, beading the walls, gathering in three or four basins fractured into the floor — pockets, each one fiercely attended. The condensation film on the colder facets is grazed continuously; the walls are licked clean in slow rotations by the salamanders, who seem to hold territories measured in handspans and hours.

Vegetation has barely begun. A few mats of black moss where the light-seam's twilight reaches. A single twisted juniper, root-jammed into the cleft mouth, older than the crack itself — it was growing on the slope when the slope split, and the split missed it by a stride. It is the only elder thing here. Everything else is younger than the wound it lives in.

And the strangeness, which a visitor would take a while to admit to themselves: the animals molt stone. Or appear to. The pale crickets shed casings that mineralize within days, hardening to a brittle grey shale, so the floor of the cleft is drifted ankle-deep in what looks like rock but crunches like sugar and is, on inspection, ten thousand perfect hollow crickets. The bats' droppings crystallize. The whole colony is slowly, patiently, secreting a new interior surface onto the young raw rock — life finishing the geology, upholstering the wound from the inside, at a pace measurable only in decades. The cleft is being healed by what lives in it. In a thousand years the sharp walls will be smooth, and the smoothness will be made of bodies.

Nothing was ever built here. But once — the staining of the residue suggests centuries ago — someone climbed this high, entered the dark, and stayed long enough to do two things. On a flat facet near the floor there is a single handprint in ochre, fingers spread, slightly too large. And below it, set upright in a crack with what can only be called care, a flaked stone blade, never used, edge still fresh as the mountain. Not lost. Placed. The colony has shingled around these two things and left them bare, a small clearing in the breathing wall, as if the print and the blade were also residents, also counted, also keeping their handspan of territory in the dark.

The cleft changes, but slowly, on the mountain's schedule. The seam overhead widens a finger-width a generation. The basins deepen. The breathing wall thickens its layers. Once or twice a century a great plate shears loose and the roar goes silent for a full minute — the only silence the place ever knows — before the small clicking voices start again, one by one, taking attendance.
