**The Fissure City of the Rising Crest**

You would never find it from the air. At this altitude the mountain is all blade — a summit ridge so new it has not learned to be round, stone the color of fresh bone where it split last winter, and split again in spring. The peak is still rising. You can feel it if you press a bare palm to the rock: not movement exactly, but intention, a deep mineral patience pushing up through eight kilometers of crust. The strata up here are tilted nearly vertical, like a stack of plates shoved sideways, and between two of those plates — a gap you could miss entirely, a dark seam no wider than a doorway — the city is.

Step in sideways. The seam runs back and down into the ridge for perhaps two hundred meters, opening into a chamber shaped like the inside of a closed book. That's all of it. That's the whole world. Above, the crack admits a ribbon of sky so high and thin and overexposed it reads as white-grey rather than blue, and the light that falls from it is dim and slatey, the light of a winter afternoon that never advances toward evening. The rest of the illumination is industrial: dull orange furnace-glow seeping from a hundred small apertures in the chamber walls, strings of carbide-yellow lamps, the occasional violet stutter of an arc welder somewhere up in the scaffolding, gone before your eye finds it.

There is no water here. Not frozen, not hidden — absent. The air at this height is so thin and so dry that exposed frost sublimates within minutes, skipping the liquid state entirely as if embarrassed by it. Whatever moisture the city needs comes up in sealed copper canisters on the funicular line, rationed by weight, worth more than the silver the smelters pull from the rock. Nothing green has ever grown in the fissure. The only thing resembling vegetation is a dark felt of mineral bloom on the cooler walls — manganese dendrites, branching black fern-shapes that are pure chemistry, geology's memory of plants it has never met.

And yet the chamber is *crowded*. That is the first shock of it: this hidden pocket, this single contained room in the body of a mountain, is built up like a vertical town. Structures cling to both faces of the seam in tier upon tier — smelting galleries, winch-houses, cable stations, dormitory cells stacked like swallow nests, all of it joined by ladders, chain bridges, counterweighted lifts. The architecture is riveted plate and quarried block, hasty and dense, structures shouldering into each other, new construction cantilevered off the corpses of old construction. Nothing is more than a few decades old and nothing looks finished, because nothing here is permitted to be finished.

The mountain will not allow it. This is the central fact of the fissure, the law every other law bends around: the rock is still moving. The seam widens by centimeters a year, lurches in irregular shudders, sheds slabs without warning. A foundry that stood true in autumn leans like a drunk by spring. So the city exists in a state of perpetual triage — half of all labor here is demolition and reconstruction, gangs prying apart a tilting gallery to rebuild it three meters higher, salvaged girders flowing through the chamber in slow rivers, hoisted, lowered, reset. Watch the whole space for ten minutes and it seethes. Cable cars sliding along their wires. Lifts rising past descending lifts. Ore carts, lamplighters, riggers walking chains with their arms out. The visual impression is of an anthill cut open and pressed behind glass — frantic, dense, ceaseless.

But it is nearly silent.

This is the second shock. The air is too thin to carry sound well; the great drop-hammer in the lower forge, which would deafen you at sea level, lands here with a sound like a knuckle on a door. The furnaces breathe rather than roar. Voices don't carry across the chamber at all — the inhabitants signal instead with lamp-flashes and mirror-code and a system of plucked guy-wires whose low notes travel through the rock better than through the air. So the city's turbulence unfolds in a kind of hush: a hundred crews working, building, tearing down, hauling, all of it wrapped in the muffled, cottony quiet of extreme altitude, under which you can hear, if you stand still, the true voice of the place — the rock itself, ticking and groaning as it rises, an irregular deep settling like a great ship's hull.

The animals love it here. That is the third shock, in a place with no water and no plants: the fissure is *thick* with life that has rerouted itself through the city's metabolism. Crag-swifts — small, sooty, hook-winged — nest by the thousand in the warm exhaust flues and pour out through the sky-crack at irregular hours in silent boiling clouds. Slate beetles graze the manganese bloom and the sulfur crusts around the smelter vents, and are in turn hunted by pale, long-toed lizards that live in the lamp housings for the heat. There is a species of fat grey snow-vole that has never seen snow; it drinks condensation off the cold legs of the water canisters and steals insulation felt, and the inhabitants tolerate it the way port cities tolerate gulls. Higher up, where the seam narrows toward the sky, something larger roosts — leathery, patient, glimpsed mostly as a folded shape among the topmost cables. The riggers leave it alone. It eats the swifts, and the swifts are infinite.

The ecology, such as it is, runs on heat and metal and the city's leavings, a food web with a furnace at the bottom instead of a sun. When a gallery is demolished, the animals move through the wreckage in the work crews' wake like a second, smaller crew, harvesting larvae from rotted timber lagging, licking flux residue, taking the warm spaces the moment they open. The inhabitants have a saying, signed in lamp-flash, that translates roughly as: *the mountain rises, the city climbs, the beasts arrive first.*

The temperature holds at a steady raw cool — never bitter, because the chamber traps furnace heat, never warm, because the rock drinks heat insatiably and the sky-crack bleeds it upward into the thin white air. Everyone and everything in the fissure lives in that narrow thermal band, pressed close around the engines, in the dim orange-and-slate light, in the near-silence, in the slow violent upthrust of the youngest rock in the world — a hidden room full of motion, sealed inside a blade of stone that has not finished becoming a mountain.
