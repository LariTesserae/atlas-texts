**The Steading on the Worn Height**

Begin with the plateau itself, because everything here is the plateau before it is anything else. This is a mountain that finished being a mountain long ago. Whatever peak it once carried has been carried away — grain by grain, frost-split flake by flake — until what remains is a high table of grey-gold stone, rounded at every edge, smooth as a knuckle. There are no cliffs, no drama. The land simply rises for a long time and then stops rising, and you are here, two miles above the old forgotten sea, on a surface so settled into its final form that even the cracks have healed over with moss.

The air is thin and cold and wet. Not raining, mostly. Cloud comes up the long flanks of the height in slow pale herds and crosses the table at knee level, at shoulder level, leaving everything beaded. Water gathers in the shallow dished places of the stone — pools no deeper than a hand, perfectly clear, rimmed with green. The cold keeps a skin of ice on them through the morning; the ice creaks and tings as it lets go around noon. That sound carries. Everything carries up here. The plateau is a sounding board with nothing on it to absorb a noise.

And yet the green is everywhere, low and fierce. This is lushness lying down. Cushion plants in mounds a thousand years old, dense as upholstery, flowering in pinpricks of white and rust. Moss in continuous pelts, fist-deep, swallowing your footfalls. A dwarf forest grows here too — willows and junipers the height of your ankle, with trunks thick as wrists, gnarled into spirals by twenty centuries of wind. You walk over the canopy of a forest. It took longer to grow than most nations last.

In the middle of the table, occupying perhaps the space of a small town and no more, is the steading.

Call it a town because the shape of it is a town: lanes, walls, doorways, courtyards, a kind of square at the center, structures rising two and three storeys with windows set in rows. But no hand built it, and no tool ever touched it. The steading *grew*. It is made of the same stone as the plateau — is, in fact, continuous with the plateau, rising out of it the way a reef rises out of its own dead generations. Whether some slow mineral life accreted it, or whether the mountain, in its extreme age, simply began dreaming in the shapes of habitation, is not a question the place answers. The walls have no joints. The lintels were never lifted into place; they were always there, or arrived the way a tree arrives at a branch. Every corner is rounded, every threshold worn into a smooth saddle, though nothing with feet has crossed them in living memory — worn, perhaps, by the wind alone, which has had time.

The streets are floored with moss. The interiors are empty — not abandoned-empty, with the litter and sadness of departure, but empty the way a shell is empty: a form that implies an inhabitant without ever quite confirming one existed. Stand in one of the rooms and the space is hollow, resonant, scoured. A little willow forest grows on the windowsills. Condensation runs down the inner walls in slow braids and collects in basins that the floor provides, as if the architecture had always intended to drink.

Now the sound, because the sound is the soul of the place.

The steading is loud. The whole structure is hollow and flued and chambered, and the wind that crosses the plateau — which is constant, which is the plateau's oldest companion — enters it and is *played*. Low organ-notes from the big chambers, a held bass drone you feel in your sternum from streets away. Overtones shrieking off the window-rows when a gust comes through cross-wise. Whistles, moans, a deep periodic *whoom* from somewhere under the central square, where some buried gallery breathes in and out with the pressure changes. It never stops and it never repeats. When weather moves across the height — and weather is always moving, cloud, sleet-squall, sudden blue clearing, cloud again, the whole cycle inside an hour — the steading changes its voice with it, swelling before the squalls arrive, so that the town forecasts its own storms by singing louder. From a distance, on the open table, you hear it long before you see it: a chord standing in the mist.

Life is scarce but not absent. A single kind of bird haunts the steading — dark, crow-sized, with a white throat — perhaps a dozen of them in all that volume of streets and rooms. They nest in the upper windows and ride the wind that the architecture funnels, and they have learned, or always knew, to call on the same pitches the buildings sound, so that you cannot always tell bird from town. In the moss-lanes there are small things: a vole-like creature that tunnels under the cushion plants and is seen once a day if at all; pale moths that emerge in the cold dusk and gather at the mouths of the loudest flues, drawn to the vibration the way other moths are drawn to light. Once in a great while, something larger crosses the plateau at its edge — a long-legged shape in the mist, never close, possibly a deer, possibly not — and the birds go silent for it, and the steading, for a few minutes, sings alone.

The light is honest and ordinary: high, cold daylight, much of it diffused through cloud into a bright pewter glare, broken several times a day by clearings when the sun comes through hard and every wet surface — the pools, the beaded moss, the streaming inner walls — flares at once, and the steading stands lit like a thing made of pale gold for ten minutes before the next cloud-herd dims it down again.

The rules of the place, as far as they can be known: the steading does not grow anymore, or grows too slowly to catch — it is finished, the way the mountain is finished. Nothing here was ever made; everything was arrived at. Water moves, wind moves, cloud moves, the small lives move, and through all of that motion the place itself holds utterly still, in the way only very old things hold still — not frozen, but *settled*, like a note at the end of a long resolution, sounding on.

You could cross the whole of it in twenty minutes. People who find it tend to stay longer than that, standing in the hollow rooms, listening to the building think out loud.
