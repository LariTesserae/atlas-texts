**The Crown Gallery**

You arrive from below, which is the only way to arrive, climbing the last of the rivet-ladders through cloud so thick it beads on your eyelashes, and then the cloud simply stops — sheared off at a precise altitude as if by agreement — and you pull yourself up through a hatch in the floor of the only room there is.

It is one room. That should be said plainly, because everything else about the place resists plainness. A single cylindrical hall, perhaps forty paces across, seated at the very top of a tower whose base no one has seen in living memory. The tower goes down into the cloud deck and keeps going; drop a bolt through the hatch and you will not hear it land. Up here there is only the gallery: a drum of riveted iron plate, ribbed like the inside of a boiler, capped with a dome of glass panes so old they have gone faintly violet and begun to slump in their frames, each pane sagging a few millimeters per century, pooling like very patient honey.

There is no ground. There never was. The floor is grating and plate, the walls are plate, the soil — and there is soil, generous black drifts of it banked in the corners and mounded along the catwalk rails — is not earth at all but the accumulated product of the place itself: a century of flaked rust, condensed soot, the bodies of insects, the droppings of swifts, shed fern fronds, all composted by warmth and damp into something that smells exactly like a forest floor and contains not one grain of natural stone.

And it is lush. That is the first surprise after the emptiness. The gallery stands above the weather, but the weather visits: most afternoons a tongue of cloud licks up the tower's flank and presses against the glass, and the dome weeps. Condensation runs the ribs of the ceiling in steady threads, drips from the rivet-heads in a slow interior rain, and everything that can hold a root has been claimed. Ferns the color of bottle glass crowd the catwalks. Mosses fur the lower walls in continuous green velvet, so deep in places you can sink your hand to the wrist. A kind of creeper with leaves like waxed paper has taken the eastern stair entirely; the stair still exists, presumably, somewhere inside it. Orchids — or things shaped like orchids, pale and faintly luminous in the violet light through the old glass — grow only on the warm pipes, nowhere else, spaced along them with an evenness that looks deliberate and probably isn't.

Because the pipes are warm. The whole gallery is warm, greenhouse-warm, blood-warm at the railings, and this is the second thing: the machine.

The center of the room is occupied — *occupied* is the right word, the way a throne is occupied — by a vertical shaft of black iron, a meter and a half thick, rising from the floor grating and disappearing through a collar in the dome. Around it at chest height turns a flywheel the width of the entire room save for the narrow catwalk ring, a single casting of impossible age, its rim worn glossy. It rotates once per day. You can stand beside it for an hour and not be certain it moves at all; come back at dusk and the scratch you noted at arm's reach in the morning is gone around the far side, traveling, patient, somewhere over the empty air.

No one knows what it drives. The shaft goes down into the tower with everything else.

And here is the cruelty, or the joke: a machine turning once per day should be silent, and the gallery is the loudest place most visitors will ever stand. The tower is a resonator — a hollow iron column kilometers tall, and every torsion in the shaft, every microscopic settling of bearing against race, travels up its full length, gathering harmonics the whole way, and arrives in the gallery as sound. Not a roar exactly. A *toll*. A continuous, layered, full-body tolling, like standing inside a bell that is always in the act of being struck, with under it a grinding so low it registers in the sternum rather than the ear, and over it a chorus of metallic shrieks and sighs as the tower flexes in winds that happened, far below, some seconds ago. Conversation is impossible. Visitors who stay learn a hand-language, or learn silence. The sound never stops and never repeats; old keepers' logs (there were keepers once; their bunks are still bolted to the western wall, ferns growing through the springs) record attempts to transcribe it musically, abandoned after pages.

The animals do not mind. This is the third surprise. The gallery is full of life — swifts that have nested in the rib-hollows for so many generations they have begun to differ from swifts elsewhere, shorter-winged, deeper-keeled, suited to a sky that is mostly *up* and *down*. They sleep in the noise. There are moths the size of spread hands, dusted iron-grey, which rest flattened against the plate walls in such numbers that some panels appear to have a soft texture until disturbed. Beetles work the soil drifts. A single colony of pale bees, deaf or indifferent, keeps comb in a junction box and harvests the pipe-orchids in slow circuits. Everything here is slightly wrong in the same direction: muted in color, heavy-bodied, unhurried, calibrated to a world with no predators, no seasons, no quiet, and no ground.

The light comes and goes normally — dawn through the violet panes, the cloud-glare of afternoon, true dark — and it is almost the only ordinary thing.

What is strange, finally, is not any single feature but the *balance*. Nothing here is new and nothing is failing. The rust feeds the soil feeds the ferns feeds the beetles; the condensation waters everything and drains through the grating, down the inside of the tower, perhaps all the way down. The flywheel turns. The glass slumps. The swifts pour out through a broken pane at dawn — the one pane that has been broken for as long as records exist, never repaired, never widening — and pour back in at dusk like smoke running in reverse. The gallery has reached its own equilibrium, a closed garden the size of a small chapel, sealed at the top of the sky, deafening and serene, doing whatever it is doing at the rate of one revolution per day.

Stand at the rail long enough and you stop hearing the sound as sound. It becomes a pressure, a climate, the medium the place is made of. The keepers' last log entry, undated, reads in full: *Still turning. Still green. Going down to listen to the quiet, back before it stops.*

There is no entry after that, and the wheel has come around — by now — some tens of thousands of times.
