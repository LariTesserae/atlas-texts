**The Vent Hollow**

You find it only because the mountain breathes.

Near the summit — eleven thousand feet of new black rock, so freshly cooled that the ridgelines still hold the shapes of their own pouring — there is a seam where the stone exhales. A thread of steam, no wider than a wrist, rising from a crack in a slope of glassy basalt. If you kneel and put your face to the crack, you feel heat like an open oven and smell sulfur and something else, something faintly animal, like wet shell. If you are small enough, or patient enough, or wrong enough in the right way, the crack admits you.

Inside is a chamber the size of a single room. That is the whole of it. There is no second chamber. People who have circled the hollow report that it has one wall more than it should, and that walking its perimeter takes either four steps or an afternoon.

---

**The stone.** The walls are young lava, quenched so fast it never had time to decide what it was. Obsidian in sheets and ropes, black with a deep red sheen where the light catches it, every edge still sharp — nothing here has been worn. Frozen drips hang from the ceiling like teeth. There are stretch-marks in the rock, places where the cooling skin tore and re-fused, and these tears glow faintly at their centers: not with heat, exactly, but with the *memory* of heat, a dull orange that brightens when watched and dims when ignored, as if embarrassed.

The floor slopes to a pool perhaps two arm-spans across. The pool is the heart of the place.

**The water.** It is hot — hot enough that a hand withdrawn from it comes out pink — and it never stops moving, fed from below by the mountain's own circulation. The walls weep constantly. Condensation gathers on every overhang and falls in fat, slow drops, and the whole chamber is saturated, the air thick enough to drink, beading on eyelashes, on stone, on the backs of the things that live here. Mineral water has begun to lay down the hollow's first soft features: thin white terraces at the pool's rim, delicate as frost, the only gentleness in a room of blades.

The drops fall and make almost no sound. This is the first strangeness most visitors notice. A drop strikes the pool and you see the rings spread, but the *plip* arrives late, or quietly, or not at all, as though sound in here must pass through something thick before reaching you. The chamber is nearly silent. It is silent the way a full theater is silent.

Because it is full.

---

**The inhabitants.** There are no plants in the vent hollow. Nothing green has ever grown here; the rock is too new, the water too hot, the chemistry too strange. Instead there is fauna — fauna in numbers that should be impossible in a space this size.

They crowd every surface. The pool is paved with them: flat, plated creatures like coins of polished horn, overlapping in shingled rafts, each one slowly fanning thread-fine limbs to comb minerals from the water. The walls carry climbers — pale, many-jointed, the size of a thumb, with bodies that seem assembled from droplets of milk glass. The ceiling holds hangers, soft-bodied bells that open and close with the rhythm of the chamber's humidity, drinking the air itself. Between and beneath and among all these move the runners, the burrowers, the wall-grazers, the drop-catchers that station themselves under the weeping points of the ceiling and snap shut around each falling drop with a click too soft to count as sound.

They are nearly touching, all of them. In places they *are* touching — layered two and three deep, the lower ranks apparently untroubled, the whole mass shifting in slow peristaltic waves as individuals work their way toward the pool or away from it. Heat pours off them collectively. Their bodies eat the vent's chemistry directly; this is an ecosystem with no sun in its bloodline, built on sulfur and stone, and it is *thriving*, packed to saturation, an entire biosphere in a room.

And it hunts. Watch long enough and you see the predators: longer creatures, glassy and quick, that move through the crowd like a thought through a mind, and where they pass, a coin-creature is gone — not eaten, exactly. Gone. Sometimes it returns a moment later, two inches to the left, slightly smaller. The prey do not flee. Whatever is being taken from them, they have made their peace with its taking.

---

**The tools.** Here is the thing that unsettles even those who accept the rest.

The wall-climbers use the obsidian. They chip it — bracing against the rock and striking with a hardened limb until a flake comes free, a flake with an edge finer than anything hands could make. They carry these flakes. They use them to pry mineral crust from the terraces, to open the plated coin-creatures (carefully, taking only a little, leaving the animal alive — it is not clear whether this is predation or *harvest*), to scrape channels in the soft white stone that redirect the weeping water toward their crevices. Near the pool's edge there are middens of spent flakes, sorted — actually sorted — by size. No structures. They build nothing, shelter in nothing made. But the floor around their crevices glitters with worked stone, the way the floor of an old cave glitters, and a visitor who sits still long enough may feel a small weight on their boot and look down to find a climber pressing a fresh flake against the leather, testing the edge against this new material, head-cluster tilted, evaluating.

---

**The breakdown.** The hollow is young — younger, perhaps, than it has any right to be, and this may be the root of the trouble. The rock cooled fast, and some things did not cool with it.

Distances inside the chamber are negotiable. The pool is two arm-spans across and also, from certain angles, a sea; lean over it and the rafts of coin-creatures recede into a haze of distance that cannot fit inside the mountain. Drops fall from the ceiling that you watched fall already. There is a patch of wall, slick and red-sheened, where your reflection lags — it catches up if you wait, joining you with a small apologetic haste. The faint glow in the rock's torn seams pulses in time with the bell-creatures' opening and closing, or the bells pulse in time with the rock; the causality runs visibly in both directions and the chamber does not seem to find this a problem.

The fauna navigate all of it without confusion. They were born to a place where geometry is weather. When the distances stretch, the climbers simply wait, flakes held close, until the far wall comes back. When time eddies, the drop-catchers catch the same drop twice and grow fat on the repetition. The predators may not be predators at all but the chamber's way of editing itself — removing an animal from a place it no longer fits and reissuing it, slightly revised.

---

**What it is like to stay.** Hot. Wet. Crowded and nearly soundless. The air presses on you like a hand. The light is moderate and sourceless — partly the seam-glow, partly a gray daylight that filters down the entrance crack and then, somehow, fills the whole chamber evenly, as if the light, too, has been redistributed by whatever redistributes everything else.

You sit at the pool's rim because there is nowhere else to sit, and within minutes the population has adjusted around you, incorporated you, a new warm rock to be climbed and assessed and chipped at. The terraces grow under your hands — actually grow, white mineral lace advancing a fingernail's width while you watch, the only erosion-in-reverse, the hollow building its first soft history over its sharp birth.

The mountain breathes through the crack. The steam rises into the cold outside air, eleven thousand feet up, and dissolves. From the slope below, the seam is nothing — a wisp, a smell of sulfur, a glitter of black glass.

Inside, the room continues. Dense, hot, silent, and entirely, almost rudely, alive — a single ember of world that the universe has not yet gotten around to making consistent, and that the things who live there hope it never does.
