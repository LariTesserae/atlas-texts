**The Caldera of Standing Steam**

You arrive at the rim out of breath, because there is almost no air to breathe. Four thousand meters, maybe higher — the altimeter of the body says *extreme* in the throb behind the eyes — and yet the heat hits you like a wall. This is the contradiction the place is built on: a tropical furnace lifted into the thin cold sky, kept hot not by latitude but by what lies beneath it. The mountain is alive in the geological sense. Its warmth comes up through the floor.

Below the rim the caldera opens for kilometers: a vast green bowl, its far wall softened to a blue suggestion by perpetual haze. The whole valley steams. Not dramatically — no roaring plumes — but everywhere, gently, the way a body steams after rain. Vapor rises off the canopy in slow white threads, hundreds of them, and where they meet the colder air above the rim they flatten into a lid of cloud that hangs over the valley most of the day. The light that comes through is bright but diffused, shadowless and pearly, the light of the inside of a greenhouse.

**The terrain**

The caldera floor is not flat. It is a worked-over country of old lava benches and collapsed tube ceilings, every edge rounded by centuries of hot rain. The rock is dark andesite gone soft-looking under moss, and it weeps. Press a palm against any outcrop and it comes away wet and warm, blood-temperature, the stone sweating mineral water from a saturated interior. In the lower hollows this seepage gathers into marsh — black peaty water threaded with channels, the surface trembling with the rise of small bubbles. Some pools are cool enough for frogs. Some are not, and you learn to read the difference by the color of the algal mats: green means life as usual; the oranges and arterial reds mean thermophiles, water that would scald.

Terraces of silica sinter step down toward the marshes — natural, ancient, deposited millimeter by millimeter over thousands of years, white and gray and salmon, scalloped like enormous oyster shells. Water films over their lips constantly, and where it cools it leaves another layer behind. The terraces are the oldest things here and still under construction.

**The industry**

There is nothing built in this valley. No path, no marker, no cut stone, no wire. And yet it runs like a plant floor.

The geothermal field beneath the caldera operates on schedules. A vent on the western bench fires every eleven minutes — a deep pneumatic *chuff*, a column of steam, a sigh, silence. Another, smaller, answers it from across the marsh on its own cycle, slightly out of phase, so that the valley keeps a slow polyrhythm all day. Mudpots turn over like slow mixers, gray clay folding on itself with a sound like thick batter. In one ravine, superheated water and cold seepage meet and the rock there clicks and ticks as it expands and contracts, perpetually, a sound exactly like cooling machinery. The whole basin gives off the low, felt-more-than-heard hum of pressure moving through plumbing — except the plumbing is fracture networks in basalt, and the pressure vessel is the mountain.

Even the air carries a charge. The big steam columns generate static as they rise; on dry-mouthed afternoons your hair lifts near them, and at dusk you sometimes see faint blue discharge flicker inside a plume, a crackle of valley-made electricity that touches nothing and powers nothing and happens anyway. The place industrialized itself a hundred thousand years before anyone could have wanted it to.

**The growth**

Heat plus water plus light is an equation with one answer, and the answer is everywhere, layered nearly to the point of suffocation. The vegetation is dense in the literal sense — leaves touching leaves touching leaves, the canopy a single quilted surface from above. But it is high-altitude flora gone strange with warmth: giant rosette plants, normally dwarfed by cold, here grown to the size of fountains; tree ferns with trunks like shaggy columns; moss so deep on the lava benches that it holds your footprint for an hour, slowly refilling like memory foam. Epiphytes stack on epiphytes. Bromeliads hold their own small hot ponds aloft, each one a sealed terrarium with its own insects, its own tadpoles, its own microclimate, thousands of them per hectare — the valley repeated in miniature, over and over, all the way up the trunks.

Near the hot ground the plants change character: lower, waxier, rooted in the warm crust, blooming out of season or in no season at all, since the vents do not observe seasons.

**The animals**

You hear them more than see them, and you don't hear them much — the saturated air and the moss deaden everything to a kind of hush. But the hush is occupied. Hummingbirds work the rosette flowers in fast silence, their wing-hum swallowed within a few meters. Frogs call at dusk in short economical phrases, never the wall of sound of a lowland swamp — up here the chorus is sparse, deliberate, call and long pause, as if the thin air taxes every note. Spectacled lizards bask on the warm sinter, motionless for hours, fed by the vents' heat rather than the weak high-altitude sun. At the marsh edges, wading birds with absurdly long toes walk the algal mats, snapping up flies that breed in water just below scalding. Once a day, roughly, something larger moves through the fern understory — a dark shape, heavy-bodied, browsing — and the valley's polyrhythm continues around it without pause.

There are predators; you find the evidence in feathers pressed into moss, in a frog chorus that stops all at once and restarts a section at a time. The food web is intact, old, balanced. Nothing here is in crisis. Everything here is in process.

**The weather of the bowl**

The caldera makes its own climate on a roughly daily cycle. Mornings are clearest: the cloud lid thins, the steam threads stand up straight, the far wall shows its green tiers. By afternoon the valley's own evaporation has thickened the air until it can hold no more, and it rains — warm rain, blood-warm, falling through warm air onto warm rock, the most comfortable rain that exists. The rain feeds the seeps, the seeps feed the marsh, the marsh feeds the steam, the steam rebuilds the cloud. It is a closed loop with the mountain's heat as its engine, and it has been turning, by the look of the terraces, for a very long time.

You can stand at the rim at the cold edge of evening, frost possible at your back on the outer slopes, and look down into the steaming green machine of the bowl — its vents keeping time, its plumes faintly sparking, its million small ponds held up in the trees — and understand that you are looking at a factory with no owner, no product, and no off switch. It manufactures only itself, continuously, and it is very good at it.
