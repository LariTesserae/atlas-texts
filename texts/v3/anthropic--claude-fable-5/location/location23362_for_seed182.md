**The Stoke Garden**

You reach it by a stairwell cut so recently that the chisel marks still hold their edges — fresh rock, pale where the tools bit, not yet softened by a single season of seeping water. The stone smells of itself, that mineral rawness of a wound that hasn't healed. Forty-one steps down, and the air thickens around you like a hand closing.

The chamber at the bottom is small — you could shout across it, if shouting felt permitted here, which it does not. Call it the size of a market square folded under a ceiling, or a hamlet compressed into a single hot room. But within that boundary, nothing is empty. The town is *packed*, in the way a pomegranate is packed: every cavity occupied, every surface claimed twice over.

The buildings came first, or so the layering suggests. Brick kilns and boiler-houses, riveted iron flues, a foundry with its mouth bricked half-shut, copper piping run along the ceiling in bundles thick as tree trunks — all of it crammed wall to wall, alley-gaps between structures barely wide enough for a person carrying nothing. The architecture is industrial and serious: gauge dials the size of dinner plates, valve wheels painted oxblood red, a small electric tram-rail that loops the chamber's perimeter and disappears into a tunnel that has, at some point, been deliberately and lovingly walled up.

Then the green came, and the green is winning slowly.

Heat is the engine of it. The chamber sits above something — a magma seam, an exhausted reactor, a buried furnace nobody remembers stoking; the residents who left their tools behind never wrote it down — and the floor itself runs warm, almost too warm for bare feet, a hot-spring heat without the spring. Moisture arrives only intermittently: condensation that beads on the cold copper pipes overhead and falls in slow, irregular rains, a drip here, a drip there, never a downpour. Drinking troughs of cast iron stand under the worst-sweating pipes, half full, their surfaces skinned with green. Between drips, the air is merely damp, a hothouse dampness held in suspension.

In that heat and that stingy water, the vegetation has gone lush in a constrained, ferocious way. Broad-leafed climbers — something between philodendron and grape, with leaves the dark waxed green of things that have learned to hoard light — sheathe the boiler-houses entirely, so that the buildings read as topiary with rivets. Ferns colonize every horizontal pipe. Moss upholsters the warm floor in continuous velvet, thickest in the alleys, and pale fungal shelves stair-step up the foundry chimney like a fire escape grown by the building itself. The plants are not ruins-vegetation, not the thin opportunists of abandonment. They are *fed*. Some of the pipes, you notice, have been deliberately perforated — neat drilled rows, industrial irrigation — and the vines clutch those pipes hardest, roots threaded into the holes, drinking machine-sweat directly. Whether the town's last residents engineered this marriage or the plants negotiated it themselves is not a question the chamber answers.

The light is dim and comes from three sources, none generous. A few electric filament globes still burn along the tram-rail, amber and patient, their glass furred with algae so they glow like fruit. Certain fungal shelves hold a faint phosphor of their own, a green-white breath visible only when you stop looking directly at it. And from seams in the floor, where the warm rock is youngest and most cracked, rises a very low orange — not flame, just heat made barely visible, the chamber's own slow pulse.

It is quiet in the specific way of machinery that has not stopped but has *slowed*. One engine somewhere in the packed middle of the town still turns — you can hear it if you stand still: a single deep stroke, a long exhale of steam through moss, then perhaps a minute of nothing, then another stroke. The rhythm of something breathing in hibernation. Condensation drips counterpoint. Leaves do not rustle, because there is no wind, only convection — the slow ceiling-ward drift of warm air that makes the hanging ferns sway over the course of an hour, too gradually to watch.

Animals are scarce and deliberate. A colony of pale crickets lives in the foundry and sings, rarely, in short courteous phrases. Something small and many-legged keeps the moss trimmed in perfect circles around the warm floor-seams, though it is almost never seen doing it. Once in a long while a moth the color of brick dust crosses the chamber in a straight, purposeful line, as if commuting.

The strangeness is in the patience of it all. The dials on the boilers still register pressure — low, but holding, decade after decade. The tram, on some unguessable schedule, completes a single circuit of the chamber, empty, its one car wreathed in vine, then stops again. The plants grow toward the machines rather than the lights. And the freshest-cut stone of the stairwell, the chamber's youngest surface, has begun to sprout its first moss in the chisel marks — the place extending itself upward, slowly, one breath a minute, toward whatever sent you down.
