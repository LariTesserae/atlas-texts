## The Roaring Garden

There is a bowl in the high country — a hollow in the shoulder of a mountain that has not seen rain in geological memory, perhaps ever. The rock up here is the color of old teeth, smoothed not by water but by wind and time and the slow patience of dust. You climb through hours of dead, silent talus to reach it, and you hear the place long before you see it.

The bowl is small. A hundred paces across, maybe a hundred and twenty, rimmed by a lip of weathered stone worn round as a clay pot's mouth. You could walk its circumference in ten minutes if anything in it would let you walk in a straight line. Nothing will.

---

**The dry green**

Inside the bowl grows a garden with no water in it.

Not stunted desert scrub — *lush* growth, extravagant growth, but built on an entirely different bargain than the one made by leaves elsewhere. The great plants here — there are perhaps two dozen of them, each standing alone with generous emptiness between, the way trees stand in a savanna or columns stand in a ruin — are tall, fleshy-looking things that contain no moisture at all. Cut one (the people who come here do, with stone knives) and what wells out is not sap but a slow amber resin, warm to the touch, smelling of pepper and hot copper.

They drink dust. That is the simplest way to say it. The bowl is a catchment not for rain but for the fine mineral powder the wind strips off the whole mountain range — the hollow swirls and traps it, and the plants comb it from the air with enormous feathered fronds, charged with static, that crackle audibly as they feed. On a turbulent day — and nearly every day is turbulent — you can watch a dust devil wander into the bowl and be *eaten*, taken apart by a dozen reaching fronds, its column unraveling into them like thread onto spindles.

The fronds are a deep grey-green, the green of olive leaves at dusk, and they are never still. Their undersides are silvered with fine hairs that glow faintly — a low chemical shimmer, just enough to read the shape of the garden by. This matters, because the bowl is dim. The mountain's own shoulder shades it half the day, and a permanent high haze of suspended dust turns the sun into a warm bronze smear. The light here is the light inside a lantern made of horn: brown-gold, directionless, thick.

It is warm. The rock holds the day's heat and gives it back; the resin in the plants is mildly exothermic as it cures. Walking among them is like walking through a kitchen where something has been baking for a thousand years.

---

**The noise**

The garden is deafening.

Every part of it is an instrument. The great plants are hollow — their central stalks are tubes, segmented like bamboo grown to the girth of barrels, and the wind that pours endlessly over the bowl's rim plays them like a pipe organ with no organist and no restraint. Each plant has its own voice depending on its height and the configuration of holes bored in it by the fauna: some moan at frequencies you feel in your sternum, some shriek, one near the eastern rim produces a perfect, sustained, eerie fifth that has presumably been sounding for centuries.

Layered over the organ-drone: the rattle. The plants fruit constantly, in all seasons, producing dry seedpods like gourds full of shot, and these clatter against the stalks in the wind. The static fronds crackle and occasionally *snap* with a discharge like a whip. And then there is the fauna, which never, ever stops.

---

**What lives here**

The emptiness between the great plants is misleading. The bowl is sparse in its architecture and swarming in its inhabitants.

Small things, mostly. Six-legged gliders the size of sparrows, with skin like oiled paper, who launch themselves between stalks in screaming arcs — they navigate the dim by sound, and so they shout constantly. Burrowers with chisel faces who tunnel through the soft tufa floor and surface in eruptions of powder, dozens at a time, for reasons that have never been clear; the whole floor of the bowl will sometimes ripple with them, a tide of dust-backs, here and gone. Fat, slow, armored grazers like granite tortoises who rasp the resin-crust off the plant bases with file tongues, each rasp audible at thirty paces.

And the *pod-thieves* — quick vertical animals, all elbows, who climb the stalks and beat the seedpods loose with deliberate rhythm. They drum. Whether it is communication or play or simply the species' nature, every dusk the bowl fills with their percussion, complicated and overlapping, the organ-stalks droning underneath, the gliders screaming descant, until the whole hollow is a single enormous loud living instrument with no audience and no need of one.

Everything here is dry-fleshed, resin-blooded, eating the warm chemistry the plants make from dust and static and heat. Nothing in the bowl has ever been thirsty. Nothing in the bowl would know what thirst was.

---

**The turbulence**

The garden is never the same shape two days running.

This is the strangest thing about it, and the hardest to believe until you have slept a night on the rim and looked down again at dawn. The great plants *move* — not quickly, never visibly, but persistently, hauling themselves on root-feet across the bowl floor at the pace of a season compressed into a night, repositioning themselves according to some logic of wind and dust-flow no observer has decoded. Paths that existed yesterday are walled off. Clearings open where there was a thicket. Sometimes two plants come together and fuse, their organ-tubes joining into a new chord; sometimes one tears itself apart overnight, splitting into three smaller selves that stagger away from each other, and the air for days afterward is sweet with spilled resin and loud with the fauna's frenzy over the windfall.

The bowl builds and unbuilds itself. It is small enough to know intimately and impossible to ever know, the way a fire is.

---

**The marks of hands**

People come here. Not many, not often, and they do not stay — nothing stays still long enough to stay *with*.

Their traces are scattered and modest. A drystone windbreak on the southern rim, low and curved, repaired so many times over so many generations that no two stones in it are the same age. Cairns at four points along the lip, each one topped with a cured lump of amber resin the size of a fist, polished by handling — markers, offerings, it is not known. Tool marks on certain stalks: careful diagonal cuts made with flaked stone, the technique of resin-tapping, some scars old and healed over, some weeping fresh.

And on the inner face of the rim rock, in a place sheltered from the dust-scour, there are figures scratched into the stone: the great plants, rendered simply, but in *sequences* — the same plant drawn five, six, ten times in different positions, like the frames of something meant to move. Whoever made them understood what the garden does. They were trying to draw the turbulence itself.

The tappers' camp, when they come, is a single hide stretched over poles, pitched on the rim — never inside the bowl. They climb down at dawn with stone knives and woven dust-veils over their faces, take what resin they take, and climb out again before the dusk drumming begins.

Whether this is caution, or courtesy, the scratched figures do not say.

---

At night the bowl does not go quiet — it has never once been quiet — but the sound changes register. The wind drops, the organ-drone falls to a low murmur, and into the gap rise ten thousand small percussions: pods, claws, the click of static, the soft concussive *whumps* of burrowers breaking surface in the dark. The fronds glow a little brighter without the bronze haze-light to wash them out, and the whole hollow becomes a dim grey-green constellation, slowly, slowly rearranging itself, loud and warm and dry as a struck bell, getting ready to be a different garden by morning.
