**The Weep Gallery**

You reach it through a seam in the wall of something much larger — a hairline gap in a structure whose full extent nobody walking through it could measure, high up in the body of the thing, kilometers above whatever the thing stands on. The seam was not designed as a door. It is a tolerance, an expansion joint, a place where two vast made surfaces were engineered to almost-touch and never quite did. You turn sideways, and the roar swallows you.

Inside is a room perhaps twenty meters long and never more than four wide, shaped like the space between two cupped hands. Every surface is manufactured. There is no stone here, no soil — the floor is a ceramic-metallic composite the color of old tea, the walls a woven material whose individual strands are too fine to see and too strong to scratch. But the room is ancient by the standards of made things. Whatever process built it finished its work long ago and moved on, and the gallery has spent the centuries since *settling* — finding the shape it actually wants to be. The floor has developed a gentle swayback. The walls have relaxed into curves. Drip-paths have etched themselves into material that should not etch, glossy channels worn by water that has fallen along the same lines for so long that even self-repairing matter has compromised, has decided the channels are part of the design now and maintains them as faithfully as it maintains everything else.

And the water is everywhere, though never deep. The gallery sits against the flank of something the structure needs — a conduit, a throat, a column of moving mass whose nature is not legible from in here — and the wall on that side runs warm, blood-warm, warmer, and weeps continuously. Condensation beads on every surface. The air is a held breath of steam. Water gathers in the swayback of the floor in a long shallow pool, skin-temperature, never more than ankle-deep, fed at one end and draining at the other through a grate whose slots have been smoothed to soft mouths by the passage of time. When you put your hand to the weeping wall you can feel the thrum behind it, and you understand immediately why the gallery is deafening.

The sound is total. It is not a noise *in* the room; it is the room's medium, the way water is a pond's medium. The great conduit roars — a broadband, oceanic, ferocious roar, the sound of incomprehensible volume moving at incomprehensible speed forever — and the gallery, being a resonant pocket pressed against its flank, rings with it. The walls sing harmonics. The pool's surface shivers in standing patterns, fine interlocking rings that never resolve. Speech is impossible. Hearing is impossible. After a few minutes the roar stops being sound and becomes pressure, becomes weather, and you stop fighting it.

The animals stopped fighting it generations ago.

This is the strange grace of the place: it is *full*. Life found the seam the way you found it and decided the trade was good — warmth, water, safety from whatever moves through the larger structure outside, in exchange for living inside a scream. Pale geckos with translucent flanks crowd the warm wall in their dozens, near-touching, jostling, completely silent because silence and shouting are the same here. Blind shrimp-like things stack themselves in the drip-channels, feeding on a film of mineral-fixing microbes that the structure's own maintenance systems seem to tolerate, perhaps even feed. Small birds — or things shaped by long isolation into the role of birds — nest in the relaxed folds of the upper walls, packed shoulder to shoulder. They have abandoned song entirely. They court by light instead: a low cold glow in the throat, flickered in patterns, answered in patterns, an entire acoustic ecology translated into shimmer because the roar left them no other channel. In the dimness — and it is dim, lit only by a sparse scatter of maintenance phosphors gone amber with age, and by the animals themselves — these conversations are the brightest thing in the room.

Vegetation is almost absent. A few ribbons of dark moss in the etched channels. One tenacious fern-analog at the drainage grate, leaves trembling perpetually in the resonance, leathery from a lifetime of vibration. That's all. The gallery's food web runs on warmth and microbial film, not on light, and the plants are guests rather than foundation.

There is a rhythm to the place, a weather. Every so often — the animals know the interval; you will not learn it in one visit — the conduit's roar deepens, the warm wall flushes hotter, and the gallery fills with thick fog as the weeping accelerates into a brief indoor rain. The geckos spread themselves flat to drink through their skin. The shrimp boil up out of the channels. The light-birds go dark and still. Then the surge passes, the fog settles into the pool, and the slow drip-time resumes. Predation happens in the intervals: a long-bodied hunter, eel-like, patrolling the pool's far end, taking shrimp; something with soft wide feet that walks the warm wall at the dimmest hours and takes geckos. Nothing here is wasteful. The system balanced itself long ago and now simply continues, dense and loud and warm, a pocket of furious life embedded in the wall of a machine whose makers — if they still exist, if they were ever the kind of thing that exists — almost certainly do not know it is here.

You leave the way you came, sideways through the seam, and the roar releases you so abruptly that the silence outside feels like a fault in your own ears. For an hour afterward your bones hum. For longer than that, in the dark behind your eyes, small lights flicker in patterns, courting.
