**The Black Flush**

The land here is younger than the forest that covers it. That is the first wrongness, the thing a traveler's feet would learn before their eyes adjusted to anything: the ground is new rock, lava that cooled within living memory of the oldest trees standing on it, and yet the trees are enormous. The flows came down from somewhere in the unlit distance — a shield of a volcano, low and broad, more a swelling of the world than a mountain — and they spread across this basin in black tongues, and before the rock had finished ticking and pinging its heat away into the air, the green arrived. It arrived the way water arrives. It found every crack.

So the terrain underfoot is sharp. There has been almost no time for weather to do its rounding work. Walk anywhere and the basalt is fresh-broken glass under a skin of moss: ropy where the lava ran smooth, then suddenly clinker — loose, ankle-deep rubble with edges that have never been touched by a river. There are no rivers yet. The rain that comes — and it comes in fits, hot squalls of twenty minutes that drum on the canopy and stop as if a hand closed over the sky — never gathers into streams. The rock drinks it. The water vanishes into a million voids and travels underground, and you hear it down there, a gurgling beneath your feet in the lava tubes, water moving through hollow stone like blood through a body too new to have a pulse rhythm yet.

The water surfaces only in pockets. Collapse pits — places where a tube's ceiling gave way — hold black pools forty feet down, fringed with ferns that have grown to the size of trees in the wet dark of the hole. Steam rises from some of them; the rock below still carries heat, and the pools sit warm as a body, breathing fog upward into the forest. These pits are the wells of the whole basin, and everything alive knows where every one of them is.

And everything alive is *here*. That is the second thing, and it overwhelms the first.

There is no sun in this account because the basin does not really have one. High over the lowland hangs a permanent ceiling of ash and vapor — the mountain still exhales, somewhere off in the dark, a slow plume that the warm air traps over the basin like a lid. Day is a deep charcoal brown. Night is absolute. The forest grew up under this lid and adapted to it: the canopy is not built for light-gathering so much as for moisture-combing, billions of fine drip-tip leaves harvesting the condensation that beads on everything, the trees drinking the air itself. The trunks are pale, almost white, the color of things that gave up on pigment. Photosynthesis happens at the starved margins, in the rare hours when the ash thins to a bruised amber glow; the rest of the forest's economy runs on fungus. Mycelium sheets the young rock under every flow, vast and pale, breaking the basalt down with acids, feeding the roots, and in places it fruits in luminous shelves — a dim foxfire green, the only steady light in the basin. By foxfire you can see perhaps an arm's length. The forest does not mind. Almost nothing here uses its eyes.

It uses its voice.

The sound of the basin is the largest fact about it. It begins below hearing — the subsonic groan of cooling rock, the deep tube-water — and rises through every register to a ceiling of shriek. Frogs in numbers that have no useful name, a wall of pulsed sound from every wet pit and dripping crotch of every tree, each species holding its own frequency band like a claimed territory, so that the chorus is *layered*, stratified, a sound with geology. Insects above them: a tinnitus-bright sheet of stridulation that never stops, that has not stopped since the green arrived. Above that, the screamers — birds or near-birds, pale and large-eyed where they have eyes at all, many of them blind and echo-calling, clicking and shrieking their way through the black canopy in flocks of thousands. When a flock moves, the sound moves with it like weather. Animals here locate each other, court, threaten, and navigate by voice; the basin is, in effect, *made* of sound, every creature constantly painting its position into the air. To stand in the middle of it is to feel the noise in the sternum, the teeth. Conversation would be impossible. Thought is barely possible. The forest does not accommodate silence; silence here means something is hunting, and the rare moments when a wedge of quiet opens in the chorus are the most frightening moments the basin has — a hole in the sound, moving, and everything near it going still and small.

The fauna is not abundant. Abundant is the wrong scale of word. The fauna is *saturating*. The young rock and the fungal economy and the heat have produced a flush — that brief ecological era when a new land is all food and no history, no entrenched order, everything arriving at once and breeding without restraint. Every surface participates. The tree bark crawls. The moss is grazed by things the size of fingernails in densities of thousands per bough. Land crabs — pale, eyeless, in absurd numbers — clatter across the clinker fields like hail given legs; their molted shells crunch underfoot more often than the rock does. The collapse-pit pools boil with larvae. Bats, or what fills the bat-shaped hole here, pour out of the lava tubes at the dimming of the ash-glow in rivers that take an hour to pass, and the sound of their emergence is a physical wind. Megafauna shoulder through the understory — big, slow, pale browsers, near-blind, humming to each other in low tones you feel before you hear, and around each one orbits a cloud of attendants: birds picking parasites, crabs harvesting dung, a whole portable economy following the great bodies the way villages follow rivers, where there are villages, where there are rivers. Here there are neither. There is only this.

And nothing is settled. That is the basin's temperament: turbulence, the instability of the very young. The mountain still moves. New flows come down every few years — you can sometimes hear the far-off roar and crackle, smell the changed air — and erase miles of forest in a night, and the flush starts over on the fresh black. Tubes collapse without warning; new pits open, new pools, and within a season they are fringed with giant ferns and walled with frog-song. Populations boom and crash on cycles measured in months. A species of moth will own the entire canopy for six weeks, the air solid with wings, and then be nearly gone, replaced by whatever ate it. The chorus itself is unstable — its composition shifts week to week, frequencies dropping out, new ones flooding in, so that a creature returning after a season would find the soundscape of its home rearranged, the acoustic map redrawn. The basin is a permanent emergency, a bazaar with no architecture, everything jostling, everything arriving.

Nothing has built here. There is not a structure in the basin, not a cleared space, not a marked stone. But there is *craft*, of a kind, at the edges of what craft means. In the foxfire dimness around certain pools, the crabs have learned to wedge nuts of a hard-shelled canopy fruit into cracks in the basalt and drop rocks on them, and the cracking — a dry irregular knocking, audible under the chorus near every pit — is the closest thing the basin has to the sound of work. One of the blind bird-flocks strips thorns from a climbing palm and probes the bark for grubs, and discarded thorn-tools litter the roosts in middens of thousands, the only artifacts this land contains: an inch of vegetable spike, worried smooth at one end. Whatever is coming here — whatever the flush will eventually condense into, when the mountain quiets and the rivers finally form and the rock learns to soften — it has not arrived. These knockings and probings in the dark are its earliest rumor.

Warm rain begins again somewhere overhead, a held breath of drumming on a canopy too thick to let the water through except as a delayed, secondhand drizzle, body-temperature, smelling of sulfur and crushed leaf and the cellar-smell of fungus. The frogs surge to meet it; the chorus climbs a register. Down in a collapse pit, steam coils off black water, and the foxfire shelves glow their faint green over a floor of moving shells. The rock under everything is still cooling. It has decades of heat left to give. The forest stands on it like a crowd on a sleeping animal, screaming, breeding, drinking the dark air — a whole loud world balanced on land that is not finished being made.
