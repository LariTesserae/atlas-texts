# The Hollow

There is a bubble in the world, and the bubble is the size of a continent.

It formed fast, as such things go — a single exhalation of the planet's interior, a vast pocket of gas trapped under a crust that cooled around it before the void could close. The ceiling is somewhere above, kilometers up, unseen. The floor stretches further than any inhabitant has ever walked. Everything here is young rock: nothing has had time to soften. The walls are obsidian and fresh basalt, fractured along clean conchoidal curves, edges still sharp enough to part skin. Where the cooling crust tore, it left ridges like the spines of enormous frozen waves, and the breaks in them glitter — not with light, exactly, but with the *readiness* for light, a billion facets waiting for the rare occasions when there is anything to reflect.

There is no water to speak of. What moisture exists is locked in mineral hydrates, or sweats out of certain fauna in quantities measured in beads. Nothing pools. Nothing drips. The silence here is partly the silence of dryness — no echo of falling drops, none of the trickling self-talk that wet caves keep up. The air itself is dense and warm, a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide and inert gases held under the weight of all that rock, blood-warm everywhere, and *exactly* as warm everywhere. This is the strange stillness of the place: the bubble is large enough that it could have weather — winds, thermal cells, storms of dust circulating under the stone sky — but the heat rising from below is so perfectly even, so long-settled, that no gradient exists for weather to grow from. The air does not move. It has not moved in a very long time. A grain of dust kicked loose will hang in it for hours, descending with the patience of a closing eye.

The darkness is not absolute, only nearly so. The floor in places carries a faint thermal smolder — patches of rock thin enough over deep heat that they glow a dim, sullen red, like coals seen through ash, casting no real illumination, only orientation. And there is a subtler light: certain crystal formations, stressed by the slow settling of the crust, discharge as faint piezoelectric flickers — a pale blue pulse along a seam, gone before the eye is sure of it. The inhabitants navigate mostly by other means.

Because the place is *crowded*. This is the surprise of it. In the warm dark, life presses close — none of it plant, none of it anything a surface biologist would file easily. The base of everything is chemistry: films of slow metabolizers coating the warm rock like dull lacquer, feeding on sulfur compounds and the heat differential between deep stone and trapped air. They do not photosynthesize; they have never imagined light. And upon them, a whole tiered fauna, layered nearly to touching. Broad, flat grazers like animate paving stones move across the films at the speed of patience, rasping the rock clean behind them. Things like soft chandeliers hang from the lower ceilings and the crystal columns, their dangling filaments combing the still air for drifting spores and shed cells — in air this motionless, filter-feeding means *waiting*, and they are very good at waiting. Small quick things, the only quick things here, thread between the larger bodies, sensed mainly as a pressure-flicker against the skin. In some galleries the fauna is so dense that the floor and walls are simply *surfaced* in it, a living upholstery, warm to the touch, faintly textured, mostly indifferent.

It is quiet, but not silent. The soundscape is sparse and percussive: a click from a grazer's rasp; the glassy *tink* of a fragment of obsidian flaking loose under its own stress and lying where it falls; once in a long while, from very far off, a deep groan as the crust settles a few millimeters — a sound felt in the sternum more than heard, rolling through the dense air for minutes. Between these events the quiet is total enough that the inhabitants can hear each other's bodies: the slow internal sounds of a grazer digesting stone-film a dozen meters away.

And there are inhabitants in the fuller sense. Scattered through the vastness — rarely, like single stars in an otherwise empty sky — are the marks of hands, or of something that does a hand's work. Cairns of stacked glass, edges deliberately dulled by grinding. Low windbreak walls that have never known wind, built perhaps for the comfort of enclosure alone. Knapped obsidian blades cached in crevices, each one a perfect early tool, because obsidian rewards the very first technology better than almost anything: the makers here were born into a world made of the finest blade-stone there is, and they have needed nothing further. There is no metal, no fire in the open air — fire would be a catastrophe of oxygen they do not have — but there are tools, and tally-marks scratched into wall-films, and small shrines, if that is what they are: arrangements of crystal placed where the piezoelectric flicker runs strongest, as if someone wanted to be near the only light their world produces on purpose.

The builders themselves are seldom seen, which in a place this thick with life means they are deliberate about it.

The strangest thing about the Hollow is what it lacks: a future tense. Nothing here is becoming anything else. The rock is young but will not age — there is no rain to wear it, no wind to scour it, no freeze to crack it. The sharp edges of the day it formed are the sharp edges it will keep. The temperature does not vary; there is no day, no season, no year, no unit of time larger than a heartbeat or smaller than the geological groan. The films grow and are grazed at exactly the rate they grow. Births balance deaths so closely that a population census taken a thousand years apart would differ by rounding error. The cairn-builders' walls were finished long ago, and stand, and need nothing.

A visitor would find it warm, dark, crowded, dry, and almost perfectly still — a continent-sized held breath, full of patient bodies, in which the only thing that ever truly happens is the occasional blue flicker along a seam of stressed crystal: the world's single nervous habit, repeating forever.
