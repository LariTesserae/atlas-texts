The plateau is one plant. This is the first thing to understand and the last thing anyone fully believes.

It rises two and a half miles above whatever the world was before, and from a distance — if there were anywhere left to stand at a distance — it would look like stone: a vast tabletop of grey-green and umber, flat-topped, its edges worn round as a knuckle. But the surface underfoot is bark. Not bark over wood over rock — bark all the way down, or at least farther down than any sounding has returned from. The plateau is the body of a single organism so old that geology has given up trying to distinguish itself from it. Mountains rose around it once, the record-keepers say, and the plant outlasted them, digesting their roots, and now it has settled into the long flat patience of its final form: a living mesa, a continent-scale tree that long ago stopped bothering with the shape of a tree.

There is no water here. There never needs to be. The plant runs dry. Its vascular system — channels wide as river canyons in places, threadlike elsewhere — moves not sap but a slow cascade of charged dust: powdered mineral, talc-fine, hauled grain by grain through electrostatic gradients the plant maintains across its whole body. You can hear it. That is the first sound a visitor learns to separate from the others — a deep, granular hiss, like a sandstorm sealed inside a wall, running day and night beneath every surface. The locals call it the *circulation* and stop hearing it within a year, the way people once stopped hearing rivers.

The light is dim and stays dim. At this altitude the sky should be hard and bright, but the plant exhales — constantly, from million-fold pores across its upper surface — a fine spore-haze that hangs a few hundred feet up and never fully disperses. The sun comes through it as a pale coin, the color of old paper. Everything below lives in a permanent overcast that is, in fact, the plant's own breath. At dusk the haze takes on a faint internal luminescence, greenish, sourceless, and for an hour or two the whole plateau is lit from above by its own exhalation, like a room lit by the memory of a lamp.

It is cool. Not bitter — the plant metabolizes, and metabolism is warmth — but the air at this height has a thinness and a chill that settles into anything that stops moving. Frost forms in the lee of the great ridges of bark. It is not water frost. It is a crystalline bloom the plant deposits overnight, silicate flowers fine as breath on glass, and the morning fauna graze it off before the light fully comes up.

---

The terrain, such as it is: the surface has eroded the way only living things erode, which is to say it has *healed* toward flatness. Old wounds — and there must have been wounds, over the eons; impacts, fires, the deaths of whatever tried to compete with it — have closed into broad shallow basins of smooth scar-bark, pale and glassy, sometimes a mile across. Between the basins run the ridge systems, low parallel welts of ancient growth, none higher than a hundred feet, worn soft-shouldered, furred along their crests with the plant's only remaining gesture toward foliage: stiff blade-structures, blue-grey, the size of doors, that do not photosynthesize in any way the old sciences would recognize. They harvest something else. Instruments left near them lose charge. Compasses near them grieve quietly and point inward.

And there is so much *space*. That is what unsettles people raised in crowded country. The plateau extends past every horizon, and across it the features are scattered the way stars are scattered — a ridge here, a pore-field there, a settlement visible as a smudge of grown towers ten miles off, and between them: open bark-plain, faintly ribbed, going on and on under the dim haze. You can walk for half a day and pass nothing taller than your knee. Then you crest a swell of scar tissue and find a vent-chimney the size of a cathedral, roaring.

The roar is the second thing. The plateau is loud — famously, oppressively loud, in a way that has nothing to do with crowding. The wind up here is constant and the plant's body is hollow in a thousand registers: vent-chimneys, dead vascular galleries open to the sky, pore-fields like acres of flute-stops. The wind plays the whole organism. The sound is organ-like, but an organ the size of a province, and it does not perform so much as *insist* — long ground-note drones that you feel in the sternum before you hear them, overlaid with shrieking partials from the smaller pores, and beneath everything the dry hiss of the circulation. Settlements are sited acoustically, in the dead spots, the nodes where the standing waves cancel. Step outside a town's boundary and the volume comes up like a curtain rising. People here speak of distances in how loud the walk will be.

---

The settlements are grown, not built — or rather, the distinction stopped mattering long ago. The civilization that lives here arrived (or arose; the records are coy) with engineering that treats matter and instruction as the same substance, and rather than quarrying the plant they *petitioned* it. They wrote their architecture into its growth in a language of induced fields and grafted intention, and the plant — slow, ancient, perhaps amused — complied over centuries. So a town here is a cluster of vesicles: hollow swellings raised from the bark like enormous seed pods, fifty to three hundred feet tall, their walls a foot of layered cellulose harder than any alloy, windows formed where the plant was persuaded to grow translucent. Streets are channels of smoothed living surface, warm underfoot from the metabolism beneath. Streetlight is a graft: veins of the dusk-luminescence routed up through the walls, so that whole districts glow with the soft green of the evening haze, indoors, at all hours.

There is infrastructure of a kind that current physics would describe only with embarrassment. Transit between settlements runs through the dead vascular galleries — kilometer-wide tunnels through the plant's body where the circulation once ran and runs no longer — and the vehicles are not vehicles but *permissions*: a traveler steps into a gallery mouth, the field takes hold, and they arrive elsewhere having experienced the journey as a single long chord. The engineers maintain this without fully understanding it. The plant, they suspect, understands it. The relationship between the people and the organism is sometimes described as symbiosis, sometimes as tenancy, and sometimes, late at night, as the relationship between bacteria and a sleeping god — but the rent, whatever it is, has been paid for a very long time, and both parties seem content.

The towns are small and far apart, and most of the plateau belongs to the fauna.

---

There is no water and yet the place teems. The animals here are dry-bodied, sealed creatures — their chemistry is the plant's chemistry, charged dust and silicate fluids, and they never drink. The most common are the *grazers of frost*: low six-limbed things, plated in matte ceramic shells the color of the bark, moving in loose herds of forty or fifty across the open plain, harvesting the silicate bloom each morning with mouthparts like soft brushes. They hum as they feed — a social hum, tuned, and a herd at full graze produces a chord that carries for miles and threads itself into the wind-roar so naturally that newcomers mistake it for more wind.

Above them, the pore-fields support a dense traffic of fliers: kite-bodied, nearly weightless, surfing the thermal columns off the vents. They are the plant's pollinator-analogues — though what they carry between pores is not pollen but *information*, packets of crystalline spore that the plant uses to coordinate itself across distances too great for even its slow nerves. Kill one and somewhere, two hundred miles away, a vent will open a fraction late. The locals do not kill them.

And there are predators, because there are always predators. The galleries that the transit-fields don't use belong to something the records call, without elaboration, the *deep tenants* — large, rarely seen, heard mostly as a knocking from beneath the bark, deliberate, like knuckles on a door, that herds of grazers respond to by going silent and moving on. Whether the deep tenants hunt the surface fauna, or cull them on the plant's behalf, or are an organ of the plant itself wearing the costume of an animal, is a question the plateau's scholars have argued for generations. The knocking, meanwhile, continues — irregular, patient, part of the night sound the way the drone is part of the day's.

---

The whole place moves, slowly, on the plant's schedule. There are seasons, though they are the plant's seasons, not the sun's: every few years the haze thickens, the luminescence deepens to a true green twilight that lasts months, and the circulation under the bark quickens to a roar that drowns even the wind — the locals call this the *Turning*, and during it the fauna breed, the frost-bloom comes in colors, the transit galleries sing in new keys, and the settlements hold their festivals indoors, in the glowing vesicles, while outside the plateau thunders through whatever vast internal work it is doing. Then it subsides. The drone returns to its old register. The herds spread back out across the dim, flat, ancient miles of its body.

It has settled into its final form, the scholars say — but final, here, is not the same as finished. The plant is in the long last chapter of something, and the chapter, by every measurement, is still being written, very slowly, in dust and sound, across the top of the world. The people live on it the way one lives in a grandmother's house: carefully, gratefully, listening to it breathe.
