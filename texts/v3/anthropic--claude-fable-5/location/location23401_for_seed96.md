# The Waterworks of the High Shelf

You come up onto it the way you'd come up onto a plateau, except there is no plateau. There is no ground at all, in the ordinary sense. At some point on the ascent the mountain stops and the construction begins, and no one has ever found the seam.

What extends from there to the horizon — a real horizon, valley-wide, hours of walking in any direction — is masonry. Pale, close-grained stone, somewhere between limestone and unfired porcelain, cut into blocks no larger than what two people could lift, and fitted so tightly that the joints read as pencil lines. Everything is made of it. The flat surfaces you walk on are not pavement laid over earth; they are the tops of structures whose depth nobody has sounded. Stairs descend into rooms, the rooms open onto lower courts, the courts have stairs of their own, and somewhere far down the light gives out before the stone does.

There is no room left over. That is the first thing you understand about the place. Every volume is occupied — by a cistern, a channel, a barrel-vaulted gallery, a basin, a flight of steps that exists only to carry water from one level to the next. Walls stand shoulder to shoulder. Where you'd expect a plaza there is instead a grid of square pools separated by walkways a single stride wide. Where you'd expect an alley there is a sluice. The builders did not believe in empty space. They believed in *capacity*.

## The Water

The whole shelf is wet. Not flooded — *charged*, the way a sponge is charged. The stone weeps. Run a hand along any vertical surface and your palm comes away cool and damp. Lintels carry a permanent fringe of droplets, each one swelling over the course of a minute before it falls. The pools and cisterns sit at every level, most of them full to within a finger's width of their rims, their surfaces so still they look solid — you could mistake them for slabs of polished sky until a drop lands and the rings spread out and die.

Water moves through the entire structure, but slowly, on gradients so faint you have to watch a channel for a long while to be sure of the direction. A film of it slides down certain stairways, a sheet thinner than glass, silent. Runnels cut into the floor — neat, square-bottomed, clearly chiseled — carry threads of current from basin to basin. The air itself is at the point of condensation; on cool mornings the place exhales, and a knee-deep mist pools in the lower courts and drains off through the doorways like another, lazier kind of water.

It is cool here, the cool of high places and wet stone — not bitter, but a cold that finds your joints if you sit. Your breath shows faintly in the shadowed galleries and vanishes in the open.

## The Light

And the place is *bright*. That is the great contradiction of it. You expect a wet world to be a gray world, but the light up here at altitude is fierce and white and total. It comes off a sky that is more glare than blue, and the pale stone takes it and gives it back from every surface, and then the water doubles it again. Standing among the pool-grids at midday is like standing inside a cut gem. Light comes up at you from below, reflected off ten thousand still surfaces; it ricochets along the wet walls of the channels; it fills the vaulted galleries through their openings and lies on the floors in trembling lattices cast by the water outside. There is almost no true shadow. Even the deep stairwells hold a drowned, blue-white luminosity, carried down by the wet stone the way light is carried down a fiber.

People who have spent a day here close their eyes that night and still see the grid.

## What Is Absent

Nothing grows. This is not a barren place in the way a desert is barren — it is *clean*. The stone is wet and bright and utterly bare. No moss in the joints, no algae greening the pools, no lichen freckling the north faces, though every condition for them seems met. The water in the cisterns is clear all the way down, clear past where the light fails. Whatever this place is, life has not been issued an invitation, or has not yet found the address. Some visitors find this restful. Others cannot sleep here.

Nothing moves on its own, either. No birds cross the glare. Nothing skates on the pools or stirs in them. When you stand still, the only motion in the entire visible world is water: the slow swell and release of droplets, the glass-sheet slide on the stairs, the rings from a single falling drop crossing a basin and arriving, much diminished, at the far rim.

## The Hands That Made It

The construction is everywhere marked by its making, and the making was simple. Chisel work — you can see the stroke patterns, the slight herringbone texture where a tool was driven across a face, the bruised dimples where stone was pecked away around a corner. The blocks were dressed by eye and hand; sight down any long wall and you'll find the gentle waver of human judgment in it, a line that is straight the way a horizon is straight rather than the way a ruler is. There is no mortar. There are no fasteners, no metal anywhere, no mechanism more complicated than a stone plug seated in a stone socket to close a channel. The most sophisticated objects on the entire shelf are the sluice-stones: tapered blocks, beautifully smoothed, that sit in their seats so precisely that a full cistern behind them does not even seep.

And the work is *fresh*. That is the part that quiets people. The chisel marks have crisp edges. The arrises of the blocks are sharp enough to feel under a thumbnail. In a place this wet, soft stone should round within a generation, grow drip-grooves and dissolution channels, slump at the rims of the pools — and there is none of that. Either the shelf was finished recently, on the scale of years, or it does not age at the rate the rest of the world ages. In a few of the deeper courts you can find what look like unfinished surfaces: a wall dressed smooth across most of its face, then a band of rougher pecking, then raw cleavage, as if the mason stepped away mid-stroke. Visitors who pass the same court at intervals of months disagree, politely and then less politely, about how much of that wall was finished the last time.

No tools have ever been found. No quarry has ever been found. The stone matches nothing in the mountains below.

## Sound

Mostly, silence — the thick, padded silence of high altitude, where the air is too thin to carry much and there is nothing alive to make a noise anyway. Onto that silence the water writes very small: a drip in a gallery, regular as a slow pulse; the nearly subsonic murmur of a channel where it turns a corner; once in a long while, somewhere out across the grid, the deeper *plunk* of a large drop falling a long way into a cistern, a sound with a round stone belly to it that you feel as much as hear. Your own footsteps are the loudest thing on the shelf. Most people start placing their feet carefully within the first hour, without deciding to.

## The Pace of the Place

Change happens here, but on the calendar of seasons, not hours. The pools rise through the wet months, a fingernail's width at a time, until the highest basins kiss their overflow notches and the whole shelf begins, almost imperceptibly, to flow — every channel live, every stairway glazed, the slow circulation of an enormous wet machine running at its idle. Then through the dry months it falls back, the channels going still one by one from the top down, until only the deep cisterns hold their levels and the shelf returns to being a reservoir rather than a current.

Nobody knows what the capacity is for. The water arrives as weather, moves through ten thousand vessels with patient deliberateness, and leaves — where, exactly, has never been traced; the lowest galleries are dark and full and the channels run into them and do not come out the other side of the mountain. The shelf takes in water, holds it, considers it at great length in the bright cold silence, and lets it go.

Stand at the edge at evening, when the glare finally softens and every full basin out to the horizon turns the color of the sky it carries, and the whole construction reads less like a ruin and less like a city than like an instrument — something built by hand, simply, enormously, for a purpose that is still, drop by drop, being carried out.
