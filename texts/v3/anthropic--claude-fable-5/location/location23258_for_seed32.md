# The Comb Hollow

You find it the way you find anything hidden: by accident, low in the land. A fold in the ground at the bottom of a dry slope, where chalk-colored grasses give way to a seam in the earth no wider than a doorway. The seam breathes sound. You can hear the place before you see anything of it — a sustained, full-bodied drone, like a hundred organ pipes holding a single chord, coming up out of the ground at a volume that makes the dry stems at the entrance tremble.

Then you go in, and you are inside something made.

---

**The structure.** There is no ground here in any natural sense. No stone, no soil, no native surface at all. Every plane you can touch — floor, wall, pillar, vault — is built, and built of the same substance throughout: a pale, paper-hard material, somewhere between hornet-comb and dried clay and bleached cartilage, laid down in lamellae thin as fingernails and built up over what must have been an enormous span of time into masses thicker than your body. Whatever made it worked without tools. You can see that in the surfaces. Every face carries the print of small, patient labor — overlapping crescent strokes, the texture of a thing smoothed by mouths or limbs, a million identical gestures fossilized into architecture. No joint, no fastener, no cut mark anywhere. The whole hollow was *accreted*, the way a reef is, or a wasp nest, or a pearl.

And it is full. That is the first physical fact of the place: there is almost no empty volume. The chamber — and it is only a chamber, perhaps the size of a small barn, you could cross it in thirty paces if crossing were possible — is packed solid with construction. Columns thick and thin crowd one another like trees in an unthinned wood, except they are not trees; they branch and rejoin, fuse into walls, open into ranks of hexagonal cells, close again into ribbed buttresses. Galleries the width of your shoulders thread between the masses. Some passages narrow until you must turn sideways; some pinch shut entirely, sealed long ago by the same pale material, the builders walling off rooms you will never see. Everywhere you look there is more structure behind structure behind structure, receding in glowing layers. The hollow contains more surface area than seems geometrically polite.

**The light.** This is the strange mercy of the place. The comb material is translucent. Daylight enters somewhere far above — through the thin roof-skin of the structure where it domes up under the hillside turf, perhaps, or through a thousand pinhole flues — and the substance itself carries the light, drinks it and passes it along, so that every pillar glows faintly from within and every wall is a lit panel. The illumination is bright, even, and utterly shadowless. There is no dark corner anywhere in the hollow. Light comes from all directions at once, a warm white with the faintest amber cast, like standing inside a paper lantern at noon. You cast no shadow. Nothing does. Depth is read only by the layering of the glow — nearer surfaces brighter, deeper ones dimming toward honey-gold — so the packed interior recedes around you in luminous strata, and at the limit of sight the structure does not go dark, it simply goes golden and indistinct.

**The sound.** The drone never stops and never varies. The wind on the slope above is funneled through the structure's countless flues and cells, and the whole hollow is, in effect, a single resonating instrument — but an instrument worn, over centuries, into perfect equilibrium with its wind. The apertures have weathered to exactly the diameters that sound steadily; the chambers that once whistled or fluted have eroded into silence or into tune. What remains is a chord: deep fundamental tones you feel in your sternum, a middle register like a sustained choir vowel, and high above it a glassy shimmer of overtones. It is loud — conversation-defeating, thought-altering loud — but it has no edges, no attack, no rhythm, and after a few minutes it stops registering as noise and becomes a property of the space, like the light. Press your palm to a column and you feel it humming. Every column hums. The whole place is one held note that was struck long before you arrived and will not decay.

**The air.** Cool, and absolutely dry. The comb material is desiccant; it pulled the last moisture out of this hollow ages ago and holds the air at a crisp mineral dryness that you taste at the back of the throat — faint chalk, faint old beeswax, faint nothing. Your breath does not fog. Your lips dry quickly. The temperature is the steady cool of a deep cellar, the same in every gallery, the same, you suspect, in every season. There are no drafts at body height; the wind that powers the great chord moves only through the sealed flues, never through the passages. The air you walk in is perfectly still.

**The life.** The builders are gone — gone so long that not a fragment of them remains, not a husk, not a wing-case, nothing in any open cell but pale dust. No animal lives here now. Nothing scuttles, nothing webs the corners, and the drone would swallow any small sound of life anyway. What persists is quieter than animals: in the dust-pockets where the galleries widen, thin colonies of silver-green lichen plate the comb like tarnish on old silver, and here and there — a few dozen places in the whole hollow, no more — a single blade of pallid grass stands up out of a crack, drawing its living from windblown grit and the dry generosity of the light. The blades are stiff, almost translucent themselves, and they do not move, because nothing in the still air moves them. They look less like plants than like additions — as if the structure, lacking its builders, had begun very slowly to grow its own small ornaments.

**The stillness in time.** The deepest quality of the hollow is that it has finished happening. The walls are weathered, but the weathering is over: surfaces worn smooth have nothing left to lose to the dry, still air. The sealed cells will stay sealed. The light will hold its shadowless gold as long as the sun crosses the turf above; the chord will hold its pitch as long as wind crosses the slope. Stand in one of the wider galleries — there is one near the center where four glowing pillars lean together overhead like the crossing of a tiny cathedral — and you can stay an hour, a day, and mark no change at all. No shadow swings. No tone shifts. The dust does not settle, because it settled long ago.

You came in through a seam in a hillside. What you found was a room the world built and then forgot, packed to its own brim, lit from inside its bones, singing one chord at the top of its voice to no one — and entirely, permanently, at rest.
