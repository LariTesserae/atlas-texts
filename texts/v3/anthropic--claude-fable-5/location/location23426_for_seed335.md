**The Resonant Hollow**

The ground opened here recently — within living memory of the frogs, certainly, perhaps within a decade. You can tell by the edges: the limestone is broken, not worn, and the fracture faces are still white as split bone, unfilmed by lichen except in the dampest seams. Somewhere in the cold flatlands above, the water table shifted, a roof of rock thinned past holding, and a room let itself into the world. It is not large. A person standing at its floor could shout and count the echo on one hand. From above it is nearly invisible — a slot in the heath, a place where the fog drains downward instead of lying flat.

Inside, the hollow is mostly air.

That is the first strange thing about it: how empty it is, how much of its volume is simply cold suspended space. The walls weep — every surface beads and runs, water finding the new fractures and threading down them in slow, silvered lines — and the air hangs at the temperature of meltwater, dense enough that breath stays visible for a long moment before dissolving. There is a single pool at the lowest point, black and absolutely clear, perhaps the size of a spread coat. There is one column of green where the skylight crack admits a dim, hourless glow. Everything else is gap. The eye keeps expecting more and the hollow keeps offering distance instead — wet rock, shadow, the long fall of a drip through open air before it strikes.

But listen.

The hollow is loud. Not with anything visible — with the rock itself. Some great works runs unseen, far off, beyond the heath or beneath it; nothing of it shows here, no pipe, no cable, no stain of footprint or pour of concrete. The hollow is untouched. But the bedrock carries the sound the way bone carries a voice. A deep, continuous throb lives in the walls, felt in the sternum before it's heard, and over it ride other rhythms: a slow piston-knock arriving on a four-second cycle, a high harmonic shiver that comes and goes like a shift change. The water amplifies all of it. The pool's surface holds a permanent fine tremor, concentric rings born and erased and born again, and the weeping seams pulse very slightly in time, as if the stone were a membrane over an engine of continental size. When the distant works changes its labor — and it does, on a cycle the hollow has learned — the whole room changes key.

The life here has organized itself around the noise.

The moss is the most patient evidence. It grows on the wet walls in horizontal bands, dense and starved by turns, and the bands match nothing about light or water — they match the standing wave, the places where the vibration cancels and the places where it sums. Where the rock sings hardest, the moss is thin and tight, almost felted. In the nodes of stillness it billows out in deep emerald pillows a hand could sink into to the wrist. Below the skylight, where the dim light pools, the vegetation forgets restraint entirely: liverworts in glossy overlapping plates, a single rowan sapling grown spindly and sideways toward the gray crack of sky, hart's-tongue ferns uncurling in the cold like slow green machinery of their own. It is lush the way the inside of a throat is lush — confined, dripping, intensely alive in a small compass.

And the animals — the hollow is crowded with them in a way that never feels crowded, because they distribute themselves through all that empty air. Cave swallows have colonized the upper fractures, and their traffic through the skylight is constant, a flickering commerce of bodies; their calls have drifted, over the generations since the collapse, toward the pitch of the harmonic shiver in the walls, so that bird and bedrock are sometimes indistinguishable. Pale frogs ring the black pool, and their chorus does the second strange thing: it locks. They call in the gaps of the four-second piston-knock, hundreds of them, filling the rests in the rhythm with such precision that a listener could believe the machine and the amphibians had negotiated. Knock — chorus — knock — chorus. When the distant works falls briefly silent, as it does perhaps twice a day, the frogs lose their timing entirely and stutter into ragged noise, and the hollow sounds, for those few minutes, almost frightened.

In the pool: blind white amphipods, ghost-colored newts that drift more than swim, threads of larvae that flinch in unison with each tremor of the surface. Midges in slow gray columns where the air is stillest. Once in a while, a stoat comes down the broken slope, sleek with condensation, and hunts the frog margin, and the chorus drops out section by section ahead of it like lights along a street — the only warning the pool ever gets.

No one made this place. No one tends it. Nothing constructed has ever stood on its floor, and the white fracture-faces will take a thousand years to soften. But it is, in its way, a downstream possession of industry — a natural room that happened to form within earshot of something enormous, and has spent its whole short life being shaped by a sound it will never see the source of. The water weeps to the rhythm. The moss grows in the rhythm's image. The frogs sing in its rests.

At dusk — which here is only a deepening of the standing dimness — the swallows funnel out through the skylight in a spiraling rope, the temperature drops another degree toward frost, the throb in the walls steps down into its night register, and the hollow goes on doing the only thing it knows how to do: holding a great cold emptiness, and filling it, drop by drop and call by call, with borrowed thunder.
