**The Lignum Works**

You come up onto the plateau in the dark, and the first thing you understand is that the ground is not ground. It gives slightly underfoot — not soft, but *engineered*, the way a floor gives, a deck, a deliberately sprung surface. It is made of wood that was never a tree. Pressed, layered, grown-in-place plank without seam or nail, and warm. Warm through the soles of your boots, like a machine housing that has been running for a very long time.

It has been running for a very long time.

There is no water here and there never was — or if there was, it has been gone so long the architecture has forgotten the shape of it. Nothing pools, nothing drips, nothing rots. The vegetation that fills this place from horizon to horizon is dry the way leather is dry, the way violin wood is dry: cured, resinous, perfected. When the structures here exchange fluid — and they do, constantly — it is oil. Thick amber lubricant, hot pitch, terpene distillates moving through fibrous conduits the width of your arm, the width of a corridor, the width of a street. Press your ear to a wall and you can hear it: a slow internal traffic, almost too low to register, like blood heard through a pillow.

The darkness is nearly total. What light exists is incidental — the dull cherry glow at the mouths of the heat-roots far below, where the whole ecology plugs into the plateau's geothermal seams; the occasional bioluminescent bead of sap welling at a fresh joint, gleaming like a banked coal for an hour before it skins over and goes black. There is no sun in this economy. Photosynthesis is a rumor, a vestigial chapter. Everything here metabolizes *heat*. The plants drink temperature the way other forests drink rain, and the plateau is rich — 2,000 meters up on a shield of old volcanic rock, the crust thin beneath it, the whole highland a warm stovetop that the vegetation colonized, then engineered, then *became*.

Because that is the second thing you understand, slower than the first: there is no boundary here between the grown and the built. The distinction never developed. The forest is the factory. Walk far enough — and you can walk for days; this is not a grove but a region, a country of itself, valleys and ridgelines of it — and you pass through districts that can only be called industrial. Bellows-chambers the size of cathedrals, their walls flexing in long slow respiration, pumping hot air through the canopy's ductwork. Flywheels of laminated heartwood, perfectly balanced, turning in the dark on greased bone-smooth bearings, storing the energy of thermal differentials the way a mill stores the river. Banks of seed-presses. Resin refineries with their tall fractionating columns of hollow living stalk, each chamber a different temperature, each yielding a different grade of oil. None of it has an operator. None of it ever did. The machinery is the organism; the organism is the plant; the plant is the city. It electrified itself somewhere deep in its history — there are nerves of conductive fiber running through everything, carrying slow charge from piezoelectric joints that flex with the structure's movement, and sometimes in the dark you feel the hair on your arms lift as a district changes its mind.

And it does change its mind. That is the strange part, the part that takes longest to believe, because everything here *looks* ancient — and it is ancient, immensely ancient, settled into what must surely be its final form. The surfaces are worn glassy. The corners are gone from everything, rounded by ages of micro-adjustment. Whole quarters have the smoothed, exhausted geometry of mountains at the end of their lives.

But the final form is not still. The final form is *turbulence*.

You will be walking down one of the corridor-streets — fibrous walls close on either side, the air thick and hot and smelling of clove, creosote, hot iron, scorched honey — and the street will begin, without sound, to close. Not collapse: *retract*. The walls draw together over the course of twenty minutes like a wound healing at visible speed, and somewhere else, you understand, a new street is being extruded to balance the books. Towers two hundred meters tall lean, soften at their bases, and lower themselves over the course of a night into the general mass, which receives them, digests them, and raises something else a kilometer away. The whole region is in constant slow riot — demolition and construction without pause, without plan you can read, districts boiling over centuries-long timescales compressed, here and there, into hours. It is Solaris built of wood. It dreams in architecture and revises the dream continuously, and it has been revising it for so long that revision is its resting state. The ancient thing and the turbulent thing are the same thing. Stability, here, is a verb.

Through all of this: quiet. That is what unsettles visitors most, the few there have been. A landscape this active should roar. Instead there is a hush with texture in it — the creak of a tower settling, spaced out like a ship's timbers; the soft pneumatic sigh of the bellows-districts; a faint everywhere-hum, felt in the teeth more than heard, of the charged fiber-nerves; and underneath all of it the deep arrhythmic *tok... tok* of expansion joints somewhere far below, accommodating the heat. Sounds happen one at a time, politely, with silence between them. The dry air carries them a long way. You will hear a wall fold itself up half a kilometer off and it will sound like one heavy book closed in an empty library.

Nothing animal lives here. Nothing animal *could* — there is no water to build a body from, and the vegetation long ago internalized every function a fauna might have provided. It pollinates itself through the duct-winds. It disperses itself by extruding itself. It prunes itself with heat. Once, rarely, you may see something that moves like an animal — a knuckle of woody fiber walking on six bending stalks down the center of a street, carrying a load of fresh resin — but it is not an animal, it is a *gesture*, a temporary limb the system has put out for an errand, and when the errand is done it will walk to a wall and rejoin it and stop being a separate thing at all. The first time you see one stop being, you will stand still for a long time in the dark.

The packed-ness of the place never relents. There are no clearings. There is no sky access except in the moments after a tower comes down, when for an hour a shaft of slightly-less-dark opens overhead — hot wind pouring up through it, lifting spirals of dry pollen that glint faintly, the only weather this place has — before new growth knits the canopy closed. Every cubic meter is claimed, occupied, in use: structure, conduit, reservoir, flywheel, archive. Yes — archive. Deep in the oldest districts, where the wood has gone nearly black and dense as stone and the revisions slow almost to geology, the walls are grown in layered rings too regular to be accident, and some who have put their hands on them swear the rings are *writing* — the system's record of its own ten million drafts, kept in grain, readable by no one, added to every hour.

Stand still long enough on the warm dark plateau and you can feel it through your feet: the whole region adjusting around your presence by millimeters, the floor very slightly firmer where you stand, a corridor somewhere quietly re-routing. It has noticed you the way a sleeper notices a fly. It will not wake. It finished waking ages ago, or it never began; it is hard to say which, here at the oldest unstable place in the world, where the forest is the engine and the engine is the city and the city has been tearing itself down and building itself up in the hot dark, in near-silence, since before there was anyone to be quiet for.
