**The Warm Hollow**

You come down into it almost without noticing the descent — the land here is low everywhere, a dark plain of old stone that has nowhere left to fall. The hollow is a single feature, a shallow bowl perhaps forty paces across, where a lava tube collapsed so long ago that the collapse itself has been smoothed into geography. The rubble has settled. Every block has found its angle of rest and kept it for ten thousand years. Nothing here is in the middle of becoming anything else.

It is dark. Not the dramatic dark of storms or eclipses — just the ordinary, total dark of a place far from any light, under a sky that may be overcast or may simply be very far away. What seeing you do here, you do with your hands and the soles of your feet. And the first thing the body learns, before any shape resolves, is the warmth.

The stone is warm. Not hot — warm the way an animal's flank is warm, the way a wall is warm hours after the fire has gone out. The heat comes up from somewhere deep and patient below, a remnant of whatever made this rock, banked and steady. It does not flicker. There are no warm spots and cool spots to map; the whole hollow holds one temperature like a held breath, and it has held it so long that the dark air itself is warm, soft, dry against the face. To sit down here is to be received.

Dry — but not absolutely. If you press a palm flat into a crevice on the north side of the bowl and leave it there, you will eventually feel the faintest cling, a memory of moisture in the deep grain of the basalt, never enough to bead, never enough to drink. It is the kind of dampness a stone keeps the way a person keeps a name they no longer use. Nothing drips. Nothing pools. The water here exists only as a quality of the rock, a slight heaviness, a slight cool resistance under the general warmth.

The floor of the hollow is fine dark grit, the patient product of ages — stone ground against stone by nothing more urgent than time. It is soft underfoot, almost silken, and it holds a footprint the way memory holds a small fact: indefinitely, without comment. Out of this grit, scattered, far apart, grow the plants.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the whole bowl. You find them by touch: low, tough rosettes of leaves so thick and waxy they feel mineral themselves, like leather grown by the stone. They are heat-feeders, or near enough — rooted into warm cracks, sipping the rock's faint interior damp, photosynthesizing on whatever dim wash of light reaches this place in seasons you have not witnessed. They are not struggling. That is the strange thing your fingers learn. They are not pioneers clinging to a hostile edge. They are old, established, exactly as numerous as this hollow can support and no more, spaced apart with the dignity of trees in a savanna. One of them, near the bowl's center, is large enough to lean against. Its leaves are warm.

No animals. No insect ticking, no scuttle, no wing. If anything ever lived here that moved, it has been gone so long that even its absence has weathered smooth. The plants pollinate by wind, or by some slow exchange among themselves, or perhaps they simply endure without increase, the same twelve forever.

The silence is nearly complete, and what sounds exist are small enough to mistake for the body's own: the granular whisper of grit shifting under your weight, gone the instant you are still. Once in a long while — long enough that you may not hear it at all — a single grain lets go somewhere on the rubble slope and travels a finger's width downward with a sound like a tooth touching a tooth. That is the hollow's entire conversation with time.

There is one thing here that hands made. On the southern rim, half-buried in grit, your fingers find a small scatter of stone flakes — sharp-edged, glassy, struck from a core by deliberate blows. Someone sat here once, in this same warm dark or in some forgotten daylight, and knapped a blade, and took the blade away, and left the debris. The flakes are dulled now at their finest edges by the same slow abrasion that softened everything else. They are not a structure, not a marker, not a message. They are only proof that the hollow has been sat in before, by someone who also, perhaps, came for the warmth.

Nothing changes here. The temperature does not rise or fall enough to notice. The dark does not deepen; it has no further to go. The plants will look — feel — the same next year, and the flakes will be a fraction of a hair's breadth duller in a thousand. The hollow is finished, in the way a stone at the bottom of a slope is finished, in the way a long-cooled thing is finished, and what it offers is exactly that: a warm, dark, dry, breathing-quiet room in the open land, where everything that was going to happen has already happened, and you may stay as long as you like.
