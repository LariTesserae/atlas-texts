# The Roof That Outlived Its Mountains

There was a range here once. You can tell by what's left: nothing sharp, nothing aspiring, just an immense table of stone lifted higher than almost anything else on earth and ground down until it forgot it was ever a mountain. The plateau runs to every horizon, gently swelling and falling like the back of something sleeping — domes worn so smooth they look poured, basins so shallow you only notice them when cloud pools there. The rock is finished. It reached its final shape long before anything alive was here to see it happen, and it will not change again in any span that matters. The land is the calmest thing in the world.

The sky is not.

At this altitude the atmosphere moves like a river in flood, permanently. There is a wind here that has no beginning — it is the planet's own circulation dragging its belly across the highest obstacle left to it — and it does not gust so much as *pour*. The sound is total. Not a howl exactly; howling implies variation. This is closer to standing inside an engine, a deep continuous roar layered with higher frequencies where the air tears itself on the dome-summits, a sound you stop hearing as sound after the first hour and begin to experience as pressure, as weather inside your own skull. People who have lived here say you can hear it in your teeth long after you descend. Conversations on the plateau are conducted hand to ear, mouth to ear, or not at all. Mostly not at all.

The wind is also the water. Clouds do not pass over this place — they collide with it. All day, ragged cloud-banks come boiling up over the western rim, shred themselves on the plateau's edge, and stampede across the surface as ground-level fog moving at the speed of a running animal. You watch a wall of white approach from a kilometer away, roaring; it hits, the world goes blind and dripping for ninety seconds, and then it's gone east and the sun is back, and every surface is beaded. It rains in this way forty times a day and almost never from above. The stone never dries entirely. Water gathers in the shallow basins as black, wind-scoured ponds with surfaces that are never still — they don't ripple, they *seethe*, whipped into standing patterns of chop that locals are said to have read like script.

And so, impossibly, the plateau is green. Not tall green — nothing tall survives here, nothing has even tried in epochs — but a deep, saturated, two-inch-thick green, a continuous pelt of cushion plant and moss and matted alpine herb stretched over the old stone like skin over a skull. It is some of the densest, most luxuriant vegetation on the planet and none of it rises above your ankle. Step on it and it gives like flesh. In the lee of the larger domes, where the roar drops to a mere bellow, the vegetation dares a little more: dwarf forests, true forests, complete with canopy and understory and shade, none of it higher than your knee. You can stand astride an ancient woodland. Some of those knee-high trees, cut open, show eight hundred rings.

The strangeness of the place is in the pelt. The moss grows in long parallel cords aligned with the wind, fine as combed hair, and the alignment is *too* consistent — it holds its direction through hollows where the wind demonstrably eddies and turns. As though the vegetation is recording not today's wind but some original wind, a prevailing direction from an atmosphere that no longer exists, kept faithfully like a compass pointing at a dismantled pole. And in places the cords gather into broad whorls, fingerprint patterns a hundred meters across, visible only from the high domes, centered on nothing.

For all its lushness, the plateau is nearly empty. The green is a surface, not a habitat; almost nothing lives *in* the volume of this place. You can walk a full day and the only animal you meet is the wind's freight — a bird, wings locked, embedded in the airstream like a stone in a current, traversing the entire visible world in under a minute without one wingbeat. There is a kind of long-bodied grazer, gray as the stone and rarely seen, that moves only in the brief lulls and freezes flat to the moss when the roar resumes. Insects exist but never fly; they live their whole lives below the two-inch canopy, in the still air under the pelt, a complete hidden weather system of calm.

People came up here, long ago, with nothing but hands and stone and fiber, and the marks they left are the only verticals in the landscape. Cairns — but cairns built by masters, stacked dry and tapering, each stone dressed by pecking with a harder stone until it locked against its neighbors, because anything less than perfect would have been disassembled by the air within a season. The cairns stand in long lines across the plateau, spaced just at the limit of fog-visibility, a navigation system for a place where you cannot call out and cannot hear an answer. Here and there, dug into the lee of a dome, you find the shelters: shallow ovals of corbelled stone, half-sunk, doorways facing east away from the pour, interiors barely large enough to lie down in. Inside them — and only inside them — there is quiet. The transition is violent. You crawl through the low doorway and the roar simply stops, and the silence falls on you with actual weight, and you can hear your own blood, and most people, the first time, weep without knowing why.

The shelters hold artifacts still: a bone scraper, coils of cord twisted from the tough stems of the dwarf trees, a flat stone with a worn depression where something was ground, over and over, for generations. No metal. No wheel. Whoever they were, they came up into the loudest place in the world with the simplest tools humans ever made, and stayed long enough to wear hollows in stone.

There is one more strangeness, and the cairn-lines may exist because of it. The wind sorts things. Pebbles on the bare stretches arrange themselves by size into bands and arcs; the gravel migrates; small light objects left outside a shelter are found, days later, gathered together in the same lee hollows, every time, as if delivered. And the roar itself, in certain saddles between the domes, carries cargo: sounds from somewhere else. A fragment of running water where there is none. Birdsong from species that cannot live here. Once in a long while — every traveler's account agrees on this, reluctantly — a voice, mid-sentence, in no language, gone before the ear can grip it. The plateau is at the bottom of a river of air that has crossed half the world before arriving, and perhaps it carries half the world's noise with it, tumbled and worn smooth like everything else here. Perhaps.

At evening the light goes long and amber across the green pelt, and every dome casts a soft mile of shadow, and the cloud-walls coming over the rim catch fire on their torn upper edges. The roar does not soften. It never softens. The land lies still, completely finished, while the sky rages over it forever — the oldest quiet thing in the world, wearing a storm that will never be done with it.
