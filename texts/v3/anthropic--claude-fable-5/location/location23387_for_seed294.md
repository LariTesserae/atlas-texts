**The Cistern at the Top of the World**

Begin with the fact of altitude, because everything else depends on it: this is a sea that should not be where it is. It sits high — higher than weather, almost, higher than the old snowlines — held in a basin that was made. Not carved, not eroded into a caldera by some patient volcano, but *built*, course upon course of fitted stone the color of whale bone, a rim wall so long it curves out of sight in both directions and so old that its joints have fused into something seamless. From the outside, if you could stand on the thin cold air of the slopes below, you would see a wall going up into cloud and a faint perpetual mist bleeding over its lip. From the inside there is no outside. There is only the water.

Everything beneath the surface is made. This is the first rule of the place and the strangest one, because nothing about it feels new. The floor of the sea — and it is a sea, a body of water that takes days to cross even in imagination — is paved. Vast hexagonal flags, each the size of a town square, laid with a precision that no longer reads as precision because three ice ages' worth of time have passed over them. Their edges are rounded now, soft as river stones, their surfaces dished by nothing but water moving over them for so long that water alone was enough. There is no bedrock under the paving. Soundings find only more construction: vaults below vaults, flooded galleries, foundations going down and down into dark that is also made. Whoever built this did not build *on* the world. They built *instead* of it.

And then they stopped, or left, or became something else, so long ago that the place has finished aging. It has reached the state old buildings reach when they stop being ruins and become geology. Nothing here will ever crumble further. The fallen columns fell ten thousand years ago and have since been polished into long pale logs of stone. The breaches in the inner walls have worn into smooth arches. The whole drowned architecture has settled into its final shape — and that final shape, it turns out, is a storm.

Because the water never rests. This is the contradiction at the heart of the cistern: the structure is utterly still, finished, beyond change, and the sea it holds is in permanent convulsion. The makers — by accident or by intention, no one is left to say — built a basin whose every channel, every sunken arcade, every flue and gallery and tilted plaza conspires to keep the water turning. Cold dense currents pour down the interior stair-mountains like slow avalanches. Warmer plumes — warmed by nothing anyone has found, perhaps only by the friction of the water against itself — rise in twisting columns through the open shafts. Where currents meet above the great paved plains, they shear into vortices a mile wide, glassy funnels that wander across the seafloor for days, picking up pale sediment and braiding it into towers, then dropping it and dissolving and reforming elsewhere. The sea builds shapes out of itself constantly — walls of silt, rolling lobes of cloudy water, brief architecture of turbulence imitating the permanent architecture below — and unbuilds them just as fast. The stone city is eternal. The water-city that lives inside it is never the same for two hours together.

It is cold, a deep mineral cold, a degree or two above freezing in the open water and colder in the sumps. In the upper fathoms there is light: a dim, even, grey-green glow, the sun strained through cloud and then through cloud-water, never bright enough to throw a shadow. It is the light of a winter afternoon indoors, everywhere, all the time. By thirty fathoms it has thinned to a suggestion. By a hundred it is memory. Most of the cistern lives in that low register — not blackness, but a gloom in which the pale stone seems faintly self-lit, the way snow does at night.

Nothing grows here. That should be said plainly: no kelp, no weed, no green film on the stones, not even the soft fur of algae that drowned things wear everywhere else. Whether the water disallows it, or the stone does, or whether plant life simply never found its way up to this altitude before the basin sealed itself, the result is a sea with no gardens. The flagstones are bare. The drowned colonnades are bare. The whole vast interior has the cleanness of a quarried thing, a world made entirely of masonry and water and cold.

And yet it is full of animals.

This is the second strangeness, after the storm. With nothing growing, the food chain hangs from the turbulence itself: the churning water grinds an endless fine snow of mineral dust off the old stone, and stirs up whatever falls from the surface — and on this thin gruel an entire fauna persists, sparse and patient and everywhere. Filter-feeders the size of barrel lids, anchored in the lee of fallen lintels, opening like grey flowers when a sediment plume rolls over them and clapping shut when it passes. Long colorless eels that live in the joints between flagstones — the joints run for miles, and so do the eels' territories, and you can travel an hour over open pavement seeing nothing, then cross a seam and watch thirty heads withdraw in sequence ahead of you like a zipper closing. Blind pale crabs that ride the vortices deliberately, letting the spinning water carry them up into the food-rich shear layers and then dropping out the bottom when the funnel collapses, raining down by the hundreds onto the plain, righting themselves, and walking off in every direction. Schools of small silver-grey fish that have learned to read the currents the way birds read wind, hanging motionless in upwellings, vanishing sideways in a single thought when a cold front comes down a stairway.

For all this abundance the place feels empty, because it is so large and the life in it so scattered. You can swim across a plaza the size of a province and meet nothing. Then the floor seams open into a sunken gallery and the gallery is wallpapered with anemone-things, ten thousand of them, all leaning the same way into the through-current like a congregation. Life here pools in the protected places — under arches, inside vaults, in the dead water behind the great fallen drums — and leaves the open storm-country to the storm.

It is quiet, but not silent. Cold water carries sound strangely well, and what it carries here is the basin itself: a low continuous register of moving water, felt in the chest more than heard — the hum of the vortices, the long sigh of a density current pouring down three hundred fathoms of buried stair, and beneath everything, very rarely, a single deep *tonk*, round and enormous, when the turbulence shifts some last loose block a finger's width in its socket. The eels go still when that sound comes. Then it fades, and the hiss of sediment resumes, and the grey light wavers, and the water goes on turning.

There are no tools here, no machines, no lights but the dim sky-light and the faint blue points that some of the deeper fish carry on their brows. Whatever knowledge built the place is finished with it. What remains is a kind of perfected paradox, settled into its last and lasting form: a made world with no maker, a ruin past ruining, motionless stone holding a sea that has been in a fury, calmly, gently, for ten thousand years — a storm kept like water in a cup, at the top of the world, in the cold.
