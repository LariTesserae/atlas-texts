**The Cistern at Midday**

You enter through a passage barely wider than your shoulders, and then the chamber opens — not large, perhaps the size of a generous room, a single vaulted space cut and stacked by hand — and it is full of water and full of light, both nearly to the brim.

The water is warm. Not bath-warm at the surface only, but warm through its whole body, fed from below by a spring that someone — generations dead now, but not many generations — channeled into this place through a fitted stone throat. You can see the throat from above: a square mouth low in the eastern wall, and the water boiling out of it, not with heat exactly but with pressure, a constant muscular upwelling that keeps the whole pool slowly turning. The sound of it owns the room. Hand-stacked stone does nothing to soften water; the roar of the inflow climbs the walls, folds over in the vault, comes back down doubled. You have to raise your voice here, and your raised voice comes back to you a half-second late, so that mostly people who come here do not speak. The water speaks. That has perhaps always been the arrangement.

The light is the chamber's other extravagance. The builders left an opening in the vault — a rough oculus, no perfect circle, just a deliberate absence of stone about an arm-span wide — and at midday the sun drops through it like a poured material. It strikes the water and the water takes it and will not let it go. The whole pool ignites. Light caroms off the moving surface and throws itself against the walls in restless white nets that never stop sliding, never repeat. The stone above the waterline is pale — a soft limestone, cut so recently that the chisel-marks still hold their edges, every facet a small mirror — and so the brightness compounds. There is no shadowed corner. Even the underside of the vault glows with reflected water-light, trembling.

Put your face below the surface and the chamber changes character entirely.

Underwater, the roar becomes a deep, continuous body-sound, felt in the chest more than heard. The light comes down in a single broad column from the oculus, made visible by the fine mineral haze the spring carries up — not murk, the water is astonishingly clear, but a faint glittering suspension, stone-dust and dissolved lime, so that the light has texture, has grain. The column tilts through the water and lays a wavering bright ellipse on the floor.

And the floor is a floor. That is the strange intimacy of the place: there is no natural substrate anywhere. Every surface a hand has shaped. The bottom is paved in fitted flags, their joints still tight, still sharp — the water has not had time to round anything. The walls go down in courses of dressed stone you could count, and you can read the work in them: where the mason's stroke bit deep, where a block was turned to hide a flaw, where a wedge-hole was never quite smoothed. Underwater, in the column of light, the tool marks look fresh enough that you half expect the dust of the cutting still to be settling. In places a thin skin of new lime has begun to creep across the joints, the spring already depositing its minerals, the chamber beginning the slow process of sealing itself — but it is a film, no thicker than frost. This is a young place. The water moved in before the stone had even weathered.

Life here is sparse, and what there is, is green and patient. Where the sun-column touches the walls, fine algae has taken hold — not a coat, just drifting filaments anchored to the chisel-cuts, streaming slightly in the upwelling current, a green so vivid in the bright water that each strand seems lit from within. At the waterline, in the splash zone where the surface chop wets the stone, a band of moss a finger high. Nothing else. No fish — the water is too warm, too mineral, too new, and nothing has found its way up the spring's stone throat. No insects skate the surface; the chop is too constant. The pool turns and breathes and shines entirely alone.

But it is not still. The chamber runs through its day like a small engine. The inflow surges and slackens on some deep rhythm of the spring — minutes apart, irregular, so that the roar swells and subsides and the surface texture changes with it, fine chop to long slow heave and back. On the western wall, just below the waterline, a hand-cut overflow channel carries the excess away through the masonry with its own narrower voice, a hard chattering rush under the broad roar. Warm vapor lifts off the surface and rises through the oculus, and on cooler days you can see it from outside: a thin standing plume above the hillside, the chamber exhaling. The sun-column crosses the pool through the afternoon, west to east along the floor, and the algae grows where it lingers longest, so that the room is slowly writing a record of its own light in green.

Whoever made this had simple tools and patience and a purpose now uncertain — cistern, bath, shrine, perhaps all three in succession or at once. They cut a foothold ledge that runs around the pool just under the surface, smooth-topped, clearly meant for standing, for wading the perimeter chest-deep in the warm bright water. The ledge is the most worn thing in the chamber, which is to say barely worn at all: a faint polish, the beginning of a memory of feet.

Stand on it now, water to your sternum, the roar around you and the light coming down in its column, the green threads waving in the upwelling, and the place feels finished and unfinished at once — a made thing the water has only just begun to claim, loud, blinding, blood-warm, and almost entirely empty.
