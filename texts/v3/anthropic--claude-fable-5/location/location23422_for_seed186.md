# The Pressure Gardens

Begin with the altitude, because everything else is a consequence of it.

You are above almost all of the air. The plateau runs for what might be a hundred kilometers in every direction the eye can guess at — *guess*, because there is very little light to confirm anything. The sun, if there is one, is a rumor below the horizon, a faint bruise of indigo along one edge of the world that never brightens and never quite goes out. This is the long shadow of an obstruction too large to see: either the planet's own curve, or something standing on it. The locals — and there are locals, though none of them have faces — have arranged their entire metabolism around that indigo seam.

The cold is total but not violent. There is no wind to speak of; the air is too thin to push anything. Cold here behaves like a medium, like water — you move *through* it. Sound behaves the same way. A stone dropped from your hand strikes the frost-glazed basalt with a sound like a finger tapping the far side of a thick window. Everything arrives muffled, secondhand, already half-forgotten.

---

What is strange — the first strange thing, the gateway strangeness that prepares you for the others — is that this place is *lush*.

The vegetation does not look like vegetation at first. It looks like infrastructure. Spread across the plateau, one to a basin, kilometers apart, stand the **bellows-trees**: organisms the size of grain silos, the size of refinery towers, black-skinned and ribbed with what you eventually understand are external lungs. They have no leaves. Photosynthesis is impossible here in any honest sense; the indigo seam doesn't give enough. Instead they run on *pressure differential* — drawing the thin cold air in through valved pores along their bases, compressing it in great muscular chambers, and extracting from that compression both warmth and the trace gases they need. You can put your bare hand against the flank of one (your hand will regret it for other reasons; the skin is rimed in hoarfrost an inch thick) and feel the slow industrial labor inside: a *thoom... thoom...* at maybe four beats a minute, less a heartbeat than a piston stroke. Nothing built this. It grew. Cut one open — please don't — and you would find no wood, but laminated tissue in concentric rings, ringed like a tree because it *is* a tree, just one that evolved engineering instead of leaves.

Around each bellows-tree, fed by its exhaust warmth, grows its garden: a skirt of low, dense, genuinely lush undergrowth perhaps two hundred meters across. Mosses black as anthracite. Coiled ferns the color of old machine oil, dark green going to black, that uncurl with an audible *tick* when the tree exhales over them and curl shut again in the trough of the cycle, so the whole garden breathes in time with its tower. The undergrowth glistens. This is where the water lives — not as rain, never as rain, but as **condensate**: the bellows-trees' compression cycle wrings moisture out of the starving air and it beads on every surface, freezes, is re-melted by the next warm exhalation, runs in threads no wider than yarn down the moss, pools in basalt cups the size of cupped palms. Each garden hoards perhaps a bathtub's worth of liquid water at any moment, distributed across ten thousand droplets. Between the gardens: dry rock, frost, and three kilometers of nothing.

That is the rhythm of the place. Oasis, void, oasis, void. Each garden an island of warmth and damp and dim metabolic light, separated by gulfs of frozen, silent, almost-vacuum plateau where nothing lives and nothing falls and the indigo seam is the only event.

---

The fauna took the second great strangeness for themselves: they have largely abandoned the outside.

There are animals here in real abundance — you would not believe the census if you could take one — but you will rarely see one in the open, because the open is death and the bellows-trees are *habitable*. Most of the plateau's animal life lives inside the trees, in the warm pressurized galleries between the lung-chambers, and the relationship long ago crossed from shelter into something like employment. There are sleek, eyeless, six-limbed things — call them **valve-wrights** — that patrol the trees' pores, clearing frost blockage, eating intrusive fungus, their bodies shaped so precisely to the ducts they maintain that it is no longer clear whether the animal evolved to fit the tree or the tree grew its corridors to fit the animal. There are slow herbivores in the gardens, low-slung and insulated in fat, that crop the moss in patterns the tree seems to *prefer*, opening the right surfaces to the exhaust warmth. There are tiny darting things in the condensate pools that exist nowhere else; each garden has its own species, three kilometers being an uncrossable ocean for something the size of a thumbnail.

And there is one creature that crosses the voids. You will know it only as a vibration: something massive, long-bodied, low to the ground, moving between gardens in the perpetual dusk on errands no one has decoded. It is the closest thing this place has to weather. Where it passes, the frost is scraped to bare rock in a clean dark trail, and the trails — visible from any high point as a faint web connecting every garden to every other — are the only marks on the plateau that look deliberate. They are not roads. Nothing made them on purpose. But the place has been doing this for so long, the same circuits walked for so many hundred thousand years, that purpose has accreted onto them the way frost accretes onto everything else.

---

Stand still long enough and the silence resolves into its components: the four-a-minute *thoom* of the nearest tree, felt in the sternum more than heard. The tick of ferns. The crackle, almost subliminal, of frost forming and unforming. Once an hour, perhaps, a long groaning sigh from far across the plateau as some distant tree shifts to a different compression cycle — they coordinate, somehow, across the gulfs; on certain nights (every night is night) the entire plateau falls into phase, every tower exhaling together, and the air pressure across a hundred kilometers rises and falls like the breathing of one recumbent animal.

No one built this. Nothing here was made.

It simply ran long enough to become machinery.
