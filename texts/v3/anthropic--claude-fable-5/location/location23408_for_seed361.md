**The Hollow at Kettle Summit**

The mountain has finished. That is the first thing to understand about it. Whatever it was in its youth — a fang, a wall, a thing that cracked weather in half — it spent that long ago, and what remains is a vast, low dome of stone so old it has nearly returned to being a plain. From a distance it does not read as a mountain at all, just a swelling in the earth, brown-gold, smooth as a knuckle. You can walk up it for half a day without ever feeling that you are climbing. The rock at the top is rounded everywhere, every edge negotiated away over a span of time that makes the word *erosion* feel too quick for what happened here. The summit does not peak. It simply stops rising.

And there, set into the highest reach of that exhausted stone like a thumbprint pressed into bread, is the hollow.

It is not large. Perhaps thirty paces across, maybe forty, with walls of smoothed rock that lean inward at the lip so that from outside you might pass within a stone's throw and never know it was there. You hear it before you see it. That is how the hollow announces itself: the bare summit is silent — wind-scoured, thin-aired, the kind of quiet that high old places keep — and then, crossing some invisible threshold, the sound arrives all at once, as if a door has opened in the air. A wall of voice. Trilling, clattering, droning, shrieking, the percussive *tok-tok-tok* of something striking hollow wood, all of it layered and continuous and astonishingly loud for a space you could shout across.

The threshold is real. You can feel it on your skin when you cross: the air inside the hollow is warm — humid, breathing, almost lowland-warm — while the air a step behind you carries the thin chill of altitude. The boundary between them is a lens of stilled atmosphere, a curvature of air that does not behave the way air should. Rain that falls on the summit slides off this lens in silver sheets and finds its way down the outer stone. Rain that the hollow *wants* condenses inside it, on its own schedule, beading on every leaf in the small hours and releasing — all at once, a few times a day — in a brief interior drizzle that touches nothing beyond the rim. Nobody built a roof here. Somebody built something better than a roof, so long ago that the rock around it has worn down by meters while the lens has not moved.

Inside, the hollow is lush but not crowded. This is the strange grace of it: the vegetation is vivid, deep-green, glossy with the perpetual near-dampness, but it is arranged with enormous spaces between presences, like a garden tended by someone with a horror of clutter. A single broad-canopied tree — low, ancient, its trunk more horizontal than vertical, grown along the curve of the bowl — anchors the eastern side. A stand of tall fern-like growth occupies a damp seam in the rock to the north, fronds the height of a person, unfurling and browning and unfurling again on a cycle of days rather than seasons. Between these: open stone, padded in places with moss so old and dense it has the firmness of upholstery, and elsewhere bare, warm, dark with the morning's condensation drying in long tide-lines. The light is ordinary daylight, honest and unremarkable, falling through the lens without distortion. The hollow does not glow or shimmer. It simply persists.

What fills it — what *overfills* it — is the animal life.

For its size, the hollow holds an impossible census. The big tree alone hosts a permanent rotating congress of small birds, hundreds of them, in colors that suggest no predator here has eyes worth hiding from: hot vermilion, oily violet, a green so saturated it looks wet. They do not flock so much as circulate, a continuous traffic between canopy and fern-stand and rim, and they never stop talking. Beneath them, the moss is alive with a population of small amphibian things — round-bodied, damp-skinned, the size of plums — that emerge in the hundreds when the interior drizzle falls and sing in overlapping pulses that you feel in your sternum. There are gliding things that traverse the bowl in long shallow arcs, membrane-winged, landing with audible slaps. There are burrowers whose tunnels vein the moss, surfacing and vanishing. There are insects in such number that at midday the air over the fern-stand has a visible grain to it, and their collective drone is the floor beneath every other sound, the note the whole hollow is tuned to.

It is loud the way a market is loud, the way a rookery is loud — not chaos, but density of conversation. The hollow has no quiet hour. It has *quieter* hours, just before the condensation releases, when the drone drops a register and the birds settle and you can hear, very faintly, a sound underneath everything else: a low harmonic hum coming up through the stone itself. Steady. Patient. The sound of whatever is still keeping the lens in place.

The builders are mostly gone from the record. What survives of them, scattered through the hollow, is modest almost to the point of shyness. A ring of pale material, half-swallowed by moss near the western wall — not metal, not ceramic, seamless, faintly warm, refusing to weather while the granite around it has retreated a hand's depth. A series of small marks incised into the rock at the rim, worn nearly smooth, that might be script and might be calibration. And in the deepest crease of the bowl, beneath the big tree's roots, a place where the stone is *too* smooth — polished into a shallow basin where condensed water gathers and drains away through no visible channel, the hollow's one small pool, dark and cool, visited in strict rotation by everything that lives here, as if by appointment.

The animals are not afraid of these remnants. They are not afraid of much. Predation happens — the gliders take the plum-frogs, something quick and rust-colored takes the gliders — but it happens within the bowl, at the bowl's pace, part of the same circulation as the birds and the rain. The hollow is an economy that has run so long it has no waste left in it. Every death feeds the moss. Every drizzle is metered. The lens lets in light and warmth and exactly enough of the outside to keep the system honest, and lets out nothing but sound — which spills over the rim and dissipates across the bald summit, the one extravagance, the hollow's voice carrying over miles of worn stone with no one to hear it.

Stand at the rim at dusk and you can hold the whole thing in your eye at once: the finished mountain going dark around you, flat and silent to every horizon, and below your feet this one warm, deafening cup of life — a thing left running, deliberately, by minds that ground their other works down to less than the stone they stood on, and kept only this. A pocket. A note held. The last room in a house otherwise returned to landscape, still lit, still loud, still occupied.
