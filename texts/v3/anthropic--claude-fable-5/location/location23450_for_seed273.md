# The Terraces of Vantle

There is no ground here. That is the first thing to understand, though it takes most visitors a week to truly feel it. The whole region — and it is a region, a day's walk across at least, spilling over the curve of the high air in every direction — stands on iron, brick, slag-stone, and the compressed bones of older structures. If you dug down through any plaza you would find another plaza beneath it, and beneath that a foundry floor, and beneath that the vaulted roofs of works whose purpose nobody now remembers. The mountain that may once have held all of this up was built over so long ago that it has become a rumor, a thing argued about in the tea-balconies. Some say there was never a mountain at all. Some say Vantle is its own mountain now, and they are probably closer to the truth.

You are very high. The light tells you so before anything else does — it is the thin, hard, generous light of extreme altitude, brilliant and a little merciless, throwing knife-edged shadows off every railing and chimney by mid-morning. The sky overhead is the dark, saturated blue of places near the top of the atmosphere. And yet it is *warm*. The warmth rises out of the structure itself: the whole built mass is veined with steam mains and flue galleries, the exhaust heat of the deep engine-halls bled patiently upward through the floors for so many generations that the masonry holds a constant, animal warmth, like the flank of something sleeping. You can press your palm against any wall in Vantle and feel it.

## The Green

Because of that warmth, and because of the clouds, everything grows.

The clouds are the water here. Rain is occasional — brief, hard, gone in an hour — but the terraces stand at the altitude where cloud decks come to graze. Several times a day, a wall of bright fog slides across a district, beading on every surface, and the city drinks. Long ago the engineers stopped fighting this and began farming it: enormous condensation screens, acres of fine bronze mesh gone green-black with age, stand angled into the prevailing wind on the western brows of the terraces, combing water out of the passing cloud. The water runs down the mesh into stone troughs, down the troughs into cisterns, out of the cisterns into a thousand small channels that thread through the streets like a second circulatory system. You hear it everywhere — a constant under-trickle beneath the city's louder noises. Pockets of standing water collect wherever the architecture allows: flooded gun-pits, old quenching pools gone soft and green, the cupped palms of broken statuary.

And on this water, on this warmth, on this light, the vegetation has built a kingdom. Not wilderness — there is no wild substrate for wilderness to claim — but something better established than any garden. Every south-facing wall carries its centuries of accumulated planters, ledge-soils, root-buttressed vines as thick as a man's torso, their bark fused to the brick so completely that pulling one down would take the wall with it. Fig-relatives with leaves like tarnished shields canopy whole streets. The flue galleries, warm and dripping, are lined for miles with ferns and a kind of broad crimson-veined leaf the locals call *engine-lettuce*, which they eat. The condensation screens themselves are colonized at their lower edges by moss in great hanging beards. Orchards grow on the rooftops in beds of soil that was *manufactured* — composted, generation over generation, from the city's own green waste — so that even the dirt of Vantle is a made thing, an artifact with a lineage.

The plants and the structure have reached an old accommodation. Roots run along expansion joints rather than splitting them; the maintenance guilds prune with the patience of orchardists, and the trees, in turn, shade the steam mains and slow the weathering of the iron. Nothing here is new, and nothing is ruined. It is all simply *settled* — rust gone stable and velvet-brown, brick gone soft-edged, every surface wearing its age like skin.

## The Works

Vantle still works. That is its sound.

Not frantic work — the city's industry runs on long rhythms, like breathing. Deep in the lower halls (you reach them by funicular, descending past strata of older architecture lit by shafts of borrowed daylight) the great condensing engines turn at perhaps one revolution every few seconds, flywheels the size of houses, their rims polished by centuries of contact to a buttery shine. The sound they make is the city's heartbeat: a vast, soft, layered *thrum* that you feel in your sternum anywhere on the terraces, punctuated by the periodic sigh of relief valves venting steam up through ornamental dragons' mouths in the upper plazas. Children stand in the warm plumes for fun.

Above the engine-halls, the working districts: rope-walks, kilns, the glass-houses where they blow cloud-cisterns and lamp-globes, the loom-galleries whose shuttles add a dry clatter to the general noise. Electricity exists but is treated as a kind of refined product, like good oil — strung sparingly, in caged amber lamps along the main stairs and in the hospital quarter, where it burns all night and gives the cloud overhead a faint golden underside. Most light is the sun's; at this altitude there is more than enough, and whole industries are arranged simply to catch it. Mirror-yards on the eastern terraces fold morning light down into the deep streets, so that even the lowest occupied levels get their hours of brilliance, arriving at odd angles, sliding across the walls like slow searchlights.

Change in Vantle happens, but on the timescale of trees. A district is re-roofed over thirty years. An engine is rebuilt one component per decade, and is therefore, in the manner of such things, both ancient and never old. The condensation screens are rewoven mesh-panel by mesh-panel by a hereditary guild whose work-songs are the only schedule anyone keeps. Asked when something was built, a local will most often shrug and say *before*, with the calm of people who live inside a finished sentence.

## The Living

The fauna arrived the way fauna does — uninvited, then indispensable.

Swifts in their tens of thousands nest in the flue galleries, and their evening exodus is one of the city's loudest hours: a screaming, wheeling river of birds pouring out of the warm vents into the gold air, thick enough to dim the light. Pigeon-descended things, larger and glossier than their lowland ancestors, strut the orchard roofs. A population of small, sure-footed goats — kept, half-kept, and feral in roughly equal measure — works the vertical gardens, and the guilds tolerate them because they prune what scythes can't reach. In the flooded quenching pools live pale fish that nobody introduced and nobody can quite explain; they are simply *there*, as they have simply been there for as long as records run, and the cooks have opinions about them. Lizards sun themselves on the warm steam mains in such numbers that certain pipes seem scaled. At night, soft-winged moths the size of two spread hands gather at the caged electric lamps, and bats come up out of the under-levels to take them, and the whole exchange happens in near silence above the engines' thrum.

The strangest residents — and Vantle is only a little strange, in the way of any place old enough to have grown its own logic — are the *gallery crabs*: land crustaceans, slate-blue, broad as dinner plates, that live nowhere but in the condensation troughs and have never been found anywhere else in the world. They graze the bronze mesh clean of algae, which is why the guild feeds them and counts them and considers a trough without crabs to be a trough in trouble. Where they came from, in a city with no sea for miles below, is one of those questions Vantle has stopped asking. They were here *before*.

## The Edge

Walk far enough in any direction and you come to it: the parapet districts, where the built world simply stops. Beyond the last railing there is cloud, and below the cloud — on the rare clear days — a haze-blue floor of lower country so distant it looks like a map of itself. The wind at the edge is constant and clean. The condensation screens roar there, faintly, like surf. People come out in the evenings to sit on the warm stone with the swifts screaming overhead and the engine-thrum in their bones and the smell of figs and hot iron and wet bronze all around them, and watch the cloud decks come in to be harvested.

It does not feel precarious. That is the lasting impression of the place. A wholly artificial world, miles in the air, drinking fog and breathing steam, furred completely over with green and crawling with patient life — and not one part of it feels temporary. Vantle long ago crossed the line that separates a structure from a landscape. It weathers. It seeds itself. It is in balance, the way a mountain is, and on the same unhurried terms.
