**The Pumphouse Commons**

The land here is new. You can tell by the edges of things — the cutbanks along the channel still stand vertical and crumbly, unsoftened, showing clean bands of grey silt that haven't yet learned to slump. Twelve years ago, maybe fifteen, this was riverbed. Then the engineers upstream changed the water's mind, and the river withdrew like a tide that forgot to come back, and what it left was a shallow trough of raw ground at the bottom of a wide cool valley. The trough is perhaps four hundred meters end to end. You can walk its full length in the time it takes a kettle to boil. It has the proportions of a town square that happened outdoors, by accident.

And someone built on it almost immediately — that's the strange eagerness of the place. Before the ground had even settled into itself, before the first willows got taller than a person, the works went up: a pumphouse of riveted iron and glazed brick, a water tower on splayed legs, a transformer shed, a row of five worker's cottages with steep roofs, a smokestack banded in fresh black paint. Maybe two dozen structures in all, but spread thin, scattered across the trough with wide gaps of open ground between them, as though the builders expected a town to fill in around the bones and the town never came. So each building stands alone in its own pool of space, connected to its neighbors by raised plank walkways and sagging electrical lines, and between them: meadow.

The meadow is the thing. The vegetation here grew up alongside the machines and never learned to be afraid of them. Horsetails in dense brushy stands — a young-ground plant, a colonizer — chest-high in places, their jointed green stalks beaded with the morning's condensation. Sweet vervain and fireweed crowding the foundations. Willow saplings, none older than the pumphouse itself, growing straight and fast and thin, leaves silver-backed, turning all at once when the air moves. Moss has taken the north faces of the brick within a decade, thick and saturated emerald, and it grows in the seams of the walkways so that the planks are green-veined and slick. Everything is wet without it ever quite raining. The valley holds a cool damp air that condenses on iron, on glass, on the underside of leaves, and the whole settlement drips — slowly, irregularly — from gutter-lips and pipe elbows and the great cold flank of the water tower, which sweats a dark stripe down one leg all day long.

The light is generous and clean. High pale overcast most days, the kind of bright that casts no hard shadows but makes every color honest: the oxblood brick, the green, the unrusted silver of new bolt-heads. The buildings have not had time to weather. Their edges are still sharp.

What strikes a visitor first, though, is the sound. The pumphouse runs. It has always run, as far as anyone can say — a deep two-part rhythm, *whunnk-haah, whunnk-haah*, the intake and the exhale of something the size of a chapel organ, audible from anywhere in the trough and well up the valley sides. It is genuinely loud. Conversation near the pumphouse happens in raised voices or not at all. The transformer shed adds a high sustained hum, and the water tower's standpipe knocks and shudders at intervals, and somewhere underground a flow of water moves through mains with a sound like distant continuous applause. The settlement is small and mostly empty and absolutely roaring with itself.

The animals have made their peace with the noise by joining it. This is the mild strangeness of the place: the fauna is abundant in a way that feels almost organized. Starlings nest in the smokestack's maintenance ladder by the hundreds and pour out of it at dusk in a single twisting sheet. Frogs — the trough holds standing water in a dozen low spots, old scour-pools the river left — call in the evening at a volume that competes honestly with the pumps, and their chorus has drifted, over the years, toward the pumps' rhythm, so that on certain nights the whole basin pulses *whunnk-haah, breek-breek* in something close to time. Herons stand on the warm outflow culvert where the discharged water comes out a few degrees above the air, and the mist off that culvert hangs in a low permanent rope across the east end of the meadow. Hares everywhere, unbothered. A family of pine martens lives inside the wall cavity of cottage number three, which is fine, because only two of the five cottages are occupied.

Two cottages, perhaps six people. They are keepers more than residents — they oil what needs oiling, read the gauges, keep the walkways clear of moss in the worst spots, and otherwise let the place be. Nobody alive here is fully certain where the water goes. The mains run south out of the trough, under the valley wall, and the paperwork says only *municipal supply*, though the nearest municipality is far enough away that the keepers shrug when asked. The pumps lift water; the water leaves; this is the arrangement, and it is older than anyone's tenure.

Change comes here, but slowly, in cycles you'd need years to notice. The willows gain half a meter a season and will, eventually, become a wood — already the west end of the trough is shadier than the photographs from a decade ago. The scour-pools are silting closed at the rate of a knuckle's depth a year. Every few months the pumps' rhythm shifts by some fraction — a valve adjusted somewhere downstream, or upstream, or nowhere anyone can point to — and for a week the frogs sing slightly out of step before they find the new tempo. The cutbanks shed a little silt with each freeze. The new ground is becoming old ground at the patient pace of all ground, and the machines thunder on inside their meadow like a heart that was installed before the body finished forming, and is in no hurry, and has nowhere else to be.

In the evenings the overcast thins and goes faintly gold, and the condensation on ten thousand horsetail stalks catches it all at once, and for a few minutes the whole small loud valley glitters — brick, iron, water tower, weeds — like something freshly made. Which, by the standards of land, it is.
