**The Masonry of No One**

Begin with the warmth, because that is what you would notice first, before your eyes adjusted to the fact that they will never adjust. The water here holds heat the way a body does — not the scalding breath of a vent mouth but the ambient warmth of something alive on all sides, a temperature you stop feeling after a while because it is exactly the temperature of you. The basin lies low on the sea floor, a broad shallow depression maybe a day's slow drift across, ringed by rises too gentle to be called hills, and it has been collecting warmth for longer than anything in it remembers. Heat seeps up through the floor in places. The water above it never moves quickly. It is a held breath, regional in size, and it is dark in the way only the deep ocean is dark: not the absence of light but the condition before light was ever proposed.

And in this darkness there is a city.

Not a drowned one. Nothing human ever stood here and sank. The city was made *down here*, in the dark, by mouths and mantles and patient secretion — generation after generation of small builders laying down lime and silica and cemented grit, each animal adding its chamber to the chamber below, dying, becoming foundation. Over a span of time that erased its own beginning, the colonies grew into structure: towers, true towers, some of them ten times the height of anything that swims here, ribbed and fluted, tapering, leaning slightly with the old slow currents the way trees lean with prevailing wind. Between the towers run avenues — not metaphorical avenues but actual cleared channels, kept open by the traffic of bodies, floored with pale crushed shell that has been ground fine as sand by millennia of passage. There are plazas where towers ring an open space. There are arches where two spires grew toward each other and met and fused. There are districts: a quarter of squat domes, a quarter of tall thin chimneys, an outer sprawl of half-built and abandoned structures slumping back into mound-shape, reclaimed, smoothed, in balance with their own decay. The erosion here is not violence. It is settlement. Every edge has been licked round by warm water and grazing mouths. The city has finished becoming and is now simply *being*, repaired at the same rate it dissolves.

Nothing built it on purpose, and everything maintains it.

The builders are still here — they are the masonry's living rind. Run a hand (do not run a hand) along any tower and you would feel them: thousands of small calcified animals per arm's length, feathered feeding crowns extended into the dark, withdrawing in a slow ripple at your pressure and unfurling again behind it. The density is the thing. There is almost no empty surface in the whole basin. Every wall is tenanted. Tube-dwellers stacked on tube-dwellers, soft fans of filtering flesh, blind anemone-things the size of fists packed shoulder to shoulder, and in every crevice between them something smaller, and on that something smaller something smaller still. The city's population is its architecture. Its architecture is its population. The distinction stopped meaning anything a long time ago.

Vegetation, in the proper sense, barely exists — no light, so nothing green, nothing that could be called a plant except in the loosest courtesy. What passes for flora is sparse and strange: pale bacterial gardens in the warm seeps, growing in mats like frost on the plaza floors, drifting filaments that feed on the chemical breath of the basin itself. Here and there a colony of something thread-like and colorless hangs from an archway like a beard. That is the extent of the gardens. This is a city of animals feeding on animals feeding on the warm chemistry of the rock, a food web with no sun at its root.

The fauna above the surface-dwellers — the *mobile* citizens — move through the avenues at biological pace, which is to say constantly and without hurry. Eels thick as a thigh thread the lower channels, navigating by touch and taste, their flanks brushing the tenanted walls and triggering long retracting shivers down the towers as they pass. Crabs in absurd numbers work the plaza floors, a slow-motion crowd, climbing over one another with the indifference of commuters. Schools of small blind fish hold position in the lee of the spires, riding the faint thermal updrafts off the warm seeps, rising and falling like a tide within the tide. There are predators — there must be, the place is too full not to be cropped — and they announce themselves only as a sudden local stillness: a whole tower's worth of feeding crowns snapping shut in sequence, a wave of withdrawal sweeping down an avenue ahead of something large and patient that you will not see, because nothing here is seen.

Because the light, what light there is, is almost a rumor. The basin is not entirely without it — that is its single small strangeness, the one degree of the uncanny it permits itself. In a few of the high chambers, in certain seasons of the city's slow metabolic cycle, a dim blue smolder collects: bioluminescence so faint and so diffuse it doesn't illuminate anything, only suggests that geometry exists. A tower glimpsed as a slightly less dark dark. An archway implied. The glow seems to travel — one quarter brightening as another goes black, over hours, like windows lit and extinguished across a town at evening — and no one knows (no one knows anything; there is no one) whether it is a signal, a digestion, or simply the city dreaming in the only color it owns.

And it is quiet. Not silent — silence would be simpler. There is a constant texture of small sound, just at the threshold: the dry-stick clicking of crab legs on shell-sand, the faint hiss of seep-water flexing through stone, the percussive tick of a thousand tube-mouths sealing at once when something passes, then the softer, longer sound of them all opening again — a sound like a held breath released through teeth. Occasionally, from far off, a deep structural groan as some over-tall spire settles a hair's width further into its lean. Then nothing. Then the clicking resumes. It is the sound of a place keeping its own counsel.

The water itself is dense with the city's life — flocculent with falling matter, the perpetual slow snow of shed and dead and excreted material drifting down the dark to feed the lower districts, so that to move through an avenue is to move through a soft warm soup of the city's continual self-consumption. Everything falling is caught. Everything caught is eaten. Everything eaten becomes wall.

There are no tools here, no devices, no fire, no hand that ever held anything. There is no plan and never was one. And yet the avenues stay open, the arches stand, the plazas keep their shapes, the towers are repaired by being lived on — a metropolis at equilibrium, warm and black and crowded past touching, going about its million small businesses in the dark with the unhurried competence of a thing that was never invented and therefore can never be abandoned.

It does not know it is a city. That is, perhaps, why it works.
