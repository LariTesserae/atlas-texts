# The Root Chamber

You would not find it looking. The entrance is a slot in the forest floor no wider than a body turned sideways, curtained over by philodendron and the wet black leaf-fall of a hundred rainy seasons. The jungle above is loud with its own business. Below, the ground simply opens, and after four meters of awkward descent — knees against slick stone, hands finding roots that hold or don't — the walls fall away and you are inside a room the earth has only just begun to build.

It is young down here. That is the first thing the stone tells you. The chamber is small, maybe the size of a chapel nave, and everything in it is unfinished. The limestone has not yet learned to make formations; there are no columns, no draperies, no ancient teeth of calcite. Instead there are *beginnings* — nubs of flowstone soft enough to dent with a thumbnail, straws of dripstone thin as pasta and only a finger long, patches of wall where mineral film is still deciding what it wants to become. The rock itself looks raw, freshly broken, the fracture faces sharp and unweathered, as though the void tore itself open within living memory of the trees overhead. Which it did. Some of those trees remember the night the ground exhaled and dropped.

The dark is total in the ordinary sense. No shaft of light reaches the floor. And yet the chamber is not lifeless with dark, because the forest has followed the water down. Roots hang everywhere from the ceiling — not a few, not decorative strands, but whole descending architectures of them: fig roots braided thick as thighs, hair-fine feeder roots in curtains that brush your face like wet silk, pale root-tips beaded with condensation, questing blindly for the water table. Where the roots meet the walls they have colonized the fractures, and around each entry point grows a halo of blanched vegetation — ghost-ferns, etiolated seedlings sprouted from washed-down seeds, moss gone white and stringy, living on drip-fed nutrients and the memory of light. It is a lush place that has never seen the sun. Everything green up above is present down here as its pale underside, its held breath.

For all that hangs from the ceiling, the chamber itself is nearly empty. The floor is a single shallow basin of dark, warm mud and one long puddle, and between the root curtains there is a great deal of open air — humid, blood-warm, thick enough that your own movement stirs it and you feel it close again behind you. Nothing crowds you here. You can stand in the center of the room and touch nothing, and the emptiness has a pressure of its own.

The sound is the astonishment. A place this small should be quiet, but somewhere behind the far wall — behind rock too young to have opened a passage yet — water is moving in volume. A stream, or the infant version of a river, running through a conduit the chamber cannot reach. Its roar comes through the stone as a full-body drone, constant, enormous, directionless. Every drip from every root-tip lands into that noise and is swallowed. When it rains hard on the surface, the drone deepens within the hour, and hundreds of new drips start at once, and the whole room ticks and patters and thrums like the inside of an instrument. You have to raise your voice here, in a cave, in the dark, to be heard by someone an arm's length away.

Almost nothing lives in the open volume. A single species of pale cricket, long-antennaed, appears on the walls one at a time and never in numbers. Once in a long while a bat overshoots its usual roost and spends a night among the roots, and its leaving is an event. In the puddle at the low end of the floor there may or may not be something small and translucent; the water clouds when you approach and you never quite see.

People have been here — not many, not often, not recently. On one wall, above the mud line, three handprints in red ochre, the pigment still crisp because nothing down here weathers, only accretes. A ring of fire-blackened stones near the entrance slot, the charcoal inside them gone soft as velvet. One length of twisted plant-fiber cord, rotted through, still knotted around a root as thick as a wrist — someone's handhold, someone's way back up. Whoever they were, they left nothing else, and the chamber is slowly, mineral-slowly, sealing their marks under a film of stone. In ten thousand years the handprints will be under glass.

That is the tempo of the place. The roar behind the wall never stops, and the drips never stop, but nothing here happens fast. The straws lengthen by a hair's width a year. The roots thicken by rings. The puddle rises in the wet season and falls in the dry, a breath taken over months. The chamber is being built around you at a pace you cannot perceive — a room the water is still making, loud with its own construction, warm as a held animal, dark as before the first morning, and patient beyond any need for witness.
