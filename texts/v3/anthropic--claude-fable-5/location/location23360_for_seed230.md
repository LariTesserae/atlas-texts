**The Saddle Town**

There is a place where the mountain narrows to a saddle, high enough that the air carries a permanent chill, and on that saddle someone — many someones, over a very long time — built a town the size of a single thought.

You could walk its full length in four minutes. That is the strange thing about it: the construction is genuinely urban — layered, organized, dense with intention — but the whole of it fits within a boundary you could throw a stone across. Stone lanes paved with fitted flags. Stairs that switch back on themselves three times within twenty paces. Walls built atop older walls built atop walls older still, so that a single house-front records four generations of masonry in four bands of slightly different gray. There are gutters cut into the lane-stone, channels no wider than two fingers, and they run with a thread of water most mornings. Cisterns, square-mouthed and dark, sit at the corners of the lanes like punctuation. It is a city in miniature, a city distilled, with all of a city's infrastructure and almost none of a city's crowd.

Because the town is mostly empty space. This is the second strange thing. The buildings do not press against each other the way buildings do in lowland towns. They stand apart — a stone house here, then a gap of thirty paces holding nothing but a low retaining wall and a single wind-bent juniper, then another structure, then a long open terrace of bare paving where nothing stands at all. The gaps are not ruins. Nothing fell down here. The gaps were always gaps, deliberate, as if the builders understood that at this altitude, in this thin cool air, a town needs room to breathe or it suffocates on itself. The emptiness is load-bearing.

Everything is the color of the mountain because everything is the mountain. The stone was quarried within sight of where it was laid, and the centuries have weathered the cut faces back toward the texture of the native rock, so that from a distance — from the trail that climbs the eastern approach — the town is almost invisible, reading as a slightly too-regular outcropping on the ridge. Lichen has settled the matter. It grows in slow rosettes of rust-orange and gray-green across walls and bedrock alike, indifferent to the distinction, and it is the most abundant living thing here by a wide margin.

The dampness is intermittent and intimate rather than ambient. Cloud comes up the western slope most afternoons and drags itself across the saddle, beading on the stone, darkening the north faces of the walls. By evening the cloud has passed and the stone dries pale again on top, staying dark in the seams. The cisterns hold this slow harvest. Moss survives in the shadowed joints of the stairways and nowhere else. Between cloud-passages the air is dry and clean and smells faintly of cold rock and juniper resin.

Vegetation keeps to the edges and the cracks. A few junipers, none taller than a person, each one shaped by the prevailing wind into the same southeastern lean, so uniform in their posture that they look planted by decree though they were not. Tufts of hard grass in the terrace corners. A cushion plant or two, ancient, growing a fingernail's width a decade, domed and dense as green stone. That is the whole flora, and it is enough; the town does not seem to want more.

The technology of the place is hands and patience. There is no metal in the construction — the stone was split with wooden wedges swollen with water, dressed with harder stone, lifted with rope and lever and the shoulders of people long gone. The doors that remain are slabs of wood gone silver-gray, hung on pintles of fire-hardened oak, and they still swing, because they were made by people who expected things to last and were correct. Inside the houses: stone benches, stone shelves, hearths black with old fires, querns worn into smooth hollows by grinding. The tools that remain are the tools that were always here — a stone pestle, a wooden rake with a handle polished by palms.

A few people may still live here. It is hard to say how many — three, perhaps five. You see the evidence more than the residents: a coil of smoke from one chimney in the cold hours, a goat-hide stretched on a frame, a cistern lid recently moved. Once in a long while a figure crosses an upper terrace carrying water, unhurried, and is gone behind a wall. They do not seem to be the last of anything. They seem, like the lichen, to be exactly the population the place supports.

Animals are visitors, not residents. A chough arrives some days, walks the paving with its red feet and curved red bill, inspects a gutter, and leaves. A small lizard owns one particular south-facing wall and is seen only when the sun strikes it directly. At night, very rarely, something with soft feet — a fox, probably — crosses the long terrace, and its passage is recorded only in the brief silence of the wind in the junipers as it pauses to listen.

The sound of the saddle town is the sound of almost-nothing, finely detailed. Wind through the gaps between buildings, which the builders seem to have tuned, knowingly or not — certain lanes hum at a low pitch in a west wind, and the cistern mouths breathe audibly. The finger-width gutters tick with water after cloud. A door, somewhere, knocks once against its frame and is still. The quiet is not absence; it is a very old room where everything has found its place and stopped moving.

And that is the deepest truth of the place: it has stopped changing. Not died — stopped. The town reached its final form some long time ago, the way a river-rounded stone reaches its final shape, and the centuries since have been maintenance, not history. Repairs are made with the same stone in the same way, indistinguishable within a season. The lichen grows at the pace of geology. The junipers lean and do not fall. A person who stood on the eastern trail a thousand years ago saw, very nearly, what you see now: the saddle, the small gray town breathing cloud, the wide cold light, the spaces between things — and the certainty, settling on you like the mist on the stone, that a thousand years from now it will be exactly this, still.
