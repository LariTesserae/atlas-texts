**The Quiet Steeps**

The mountain is new. You can tell by the way it hasn't yet learned to be gentle — every ridge is a blade, every outcrop has corners, and the stone breaks in clean planes the color of wet slate, unrounded, unforgiven by weather. It rose recently, as mountains measure time, shouldering up through older country in a hurry, and the land at its top still has the rawness of something just unwrapped.

High on its northern flank, where the slope folds into a hanging valley no larger than a few city blocks, there is a town.

It is hard, at first, to say who the town is *for*.

The construction is real — that much is plain. Terraces of dry-fitted stone climb the valley's inner walls in tiers, each course laid by hand, the joints packed with moss instead of mortar. There are stairways cut directly into the young rock, the chisel-marks still crisp, white scars on dark stone that no lichen has had time to soften. There are roofed passages, low doorways, cisterns shaped like cupped palms, a small plaza floored in fitted hexagonal flags. The tools that made all this were simple ones: you can find them sometimes, left in niches — antler picks, stone mauls, wooden mallets worn glossy at the grip, a coil of cordage twisted from bark fiber. Nothing of metal anywhere. Whoever built the Quiet Steeps built it slowly, by strength and patience, and not very long ago.

But the streets are empty of anything you would call a citizen, and they are full — *full* — of everything else.

The valley is at the altitude where clouds graze. Most hours of most days, mist moves through the town in slow rivers, pouring down the stairways, pooling in the plaza, lifting again. It never quite rains; instead everything weeps. Condensation beads on every leaf and lintel and slides off in fat, deliberate drops. The vegetation drinks this air greedily: tree ferns crowd the terraces, their fiddleheads big as fists; cushion plants mound over the cistern rims in domes of dense green velvet; a kind of pale, broad-leafed rhododendron grows up through the roofless houses and spreads its canopy where the ceilings were, so that some rooms are now green-lit chambers, dim even at noon. The light here is always dim — filtered through cloud, then through leaf, arriving at the ground as a kind of grey-green dusk that persists from dawn until dark. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Nothing shines.

And in this dimness, the animals.

They are everywhere, and they make almost no sound, and this is the first strangeness that settles over a visitor: the sheer biological *crowding* of the place against its near-total silence. Great white snails, large as loaves of bread, graze the moss in herds of dozens, leaving silver avenues across the flagstones. Salamanders the color of candle wax line the cistern edges shoulder to shoulder, hundreds of them, motionless except for the slow pulse of their throats. Flightless birds — soft, grey, round as kettles — move through the fern understory in loose processions, and they do not call; they have no call; they communicate, apparently, by a slow synchronized bobbing that passes down the line like a wave. Moths with wings like grey felt cover the undersides of the rhododendron leaves so thickly that when a branch is disturbed the whole canopy seems to *exhale*, a soundless blizzard rising and resettling. Velvet-furred things that might be related to hyraxes occupy the doorways and window-niches as though assigned to them. In the dimmest passages, eyes — many, small, patient — catch what little light there is.

The silence is not an absence. It is, you come to suspect, a *rule*. The drips fall, but the moss receives them without a splash. The snails' rasping is below hearing. Even the wind, which moves the mist constantly, finds nothing here that rattles. The loudest regular sound in the valley is the cisterns refilling: a slow, spaced *toc... toc... toc* of drops into deep water, so isolated and rhythmic that it begins to sound intentional, like a clock being kept.

Because here is the second strangeness: the town is not falling apart. The mortared-with-moss walls are *tended*. Fresh moss is packed into joints — you can see where it's new, brighter green, still knitting. Stones that slip from the terrace courses do not stay fallen; they reappear, refitted, sometimes slightly wrong, set with the patience of something that does not understand walls but understands *pattern*. The snail herds graze the stairways clean of slick growth in regular rotation, like a maintenance schedule. The kettle-birds carry pale pebbles, one at a time, and deposit them in particular niches, in particular rooms, in arrangements that change weekly and mean nothing anyone has deciphered. The whole settlement is being kept — clumsily, faithfully, silently — by its animals, the way a tide keeps a beach.

Whether the builders left, or died, or became something now grazing the plaza, the town does not say.

For all its hush, the place is never still. This is a working landscape running at full biological speed, just muffled. The mist cycles on a rhythm of hours: thickening, dropping its weight of damp, thinning to reveal — briefly — the savage young peaks above, fanged and snowless and close, then closing again. The predators work too: there is something long and low and silver-furred that moves along the terrace tops at the dimmest hours, and when it takes a snail or a salamander there is no cry, no struggle audible — only, afterward, the herds rearranged, the salamander line at the cistern closed up tight, a gap healed over. Death here is administrative. Population rises and falls; the moths swarm and dwindle; the fern fronds unroll visibly over a single sitting. Everything is in motion. Nothing announces it.

The valley has firm edges. A visitor can walk it end to end in twenty minutes — the lowest terrace, the plaza, the seven stairways, the upper cisterns where the cloud is born — and beyond those edges the mountain reverts immediately to bare, sharp, lifeless rock, as though the town and its green and its silent multitude were a single organism with a defined skin. Stepping past the last wall, the wind returns, ordinary and loud, almost vulgar after the hush.

Most who find the Quiet Steeps leave something — a tool, a stone, set in a niche. It seems polite. By the next clearing of the mist, it will have been moved, slightly, to where it apparently belongs.
