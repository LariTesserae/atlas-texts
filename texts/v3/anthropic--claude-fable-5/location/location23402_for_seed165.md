# The Shelf at the Top of the Weather

You arrive at it the way you arrive at deafness: gradually, and then it is everywhere.

The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky has stopped pretending to be above you. It is around you, level with your shoulders, a grey-violet dimness that never brightens to day and never finishes becoming night. Light here does not arrive from a direction. It seeps. Stones cast no shadows, or cast them inward, a faint darkening at their cores, as though each rock is remembering what shade used to be.

The terrain is old in a settled, finished way. Whatever violence raised this place — and it must have been violence, to push land this high — concluded long enough ago that the land has made peace with its own height. The plateau is broken into broad terraces and shallow basins, edges sanded round by wind that has been working the same stone for so long that wind and stone have come to an arrangement. Frost-shattered scree lies in long aprons exactly where it fell, and where it fell is where it stays. Nothing here is mid-collapse. Everything has already collapsed as far as it intends to.

## The Sound

This is the first wrongness, and the largest: the plateau is nearly empty, and it is *loud*.

Not wind-loud, though there is wind. The sound underneath the wind is the sound of the world's seam. It is hard to describe except by what people would compare it to if people came here: a waterfall heard through a mountain; an enormous bell in the instant after it has been struck, the strike itself withheld; a choir holding a single chord with no breath and no intention of releasing it. It comes out of the ground and out of the air with equal indifference. It does not echo, because echoes imply that sound travels and ends, and this sound has never demonstrated either.

It is constant. Pitch, volume, texture — none of it varies, not across hours, not across the basins, not in the lee of boulders where wind-sound dies and the deep sound continues unbothered. Stand here long enough and you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood. Then a moment comes when you notice it again, and the noticing is like falling.

If you speak, your voice works. But it arrives at your own ears very slightly *before* you finish speaking, as though the air is so saturated with sound already that yours gets processed out of order.

## The Thin Places

The reality of the plateau is worn through in patches, the way fabric wears at the elbows — and like worn fabric, the holes have stopped spreading. They are old holes. Established holes. The landscape has eroded *ontologically* and reached equilibrium there too.

You learn to recognize them:

A basin where the scree on the far side is the same scree as the near side — not similar, the *same*, each stone duplicated across forty meters of air, so that crossing the basin you arrive among rocks you have already walked through, your earlier footprints (the only fresh thing here) waiting in the grit.

A ridgeline that is visible from everywhere on the plateau at the same apparent distance. Walk toward it for an hour and you have not approached it. Walk away and it does not recede. It is simply a feature the horizon has, the way a face has a nose.

Pools — and here is the dampness of the place — that collect in stone hollows from condensation, from the rare sleet that drifts through sideways like a thought, from frost that beads on every cold surface and runs in threads down the rock. The pools are shallow, black, very still, and they do not reflect this sky. They reflect *a* sky: pale, high, with clouds in it, clouds that move. It is the only motion anywhere, and it is happening in a reflection of something that is not here. Lean over a pool and you will see the clouds pass behind your silhouette. You will not see your face.

The water is real water. It is drinkable, mineral, achingly cold. The reflections are the part that is broken, and they broke a long time ago, and they show no sign of healing or worsening. Locals — if there had ever been locals — would have stopped finding it strange.

## What Grows, What Moves

Vegetation has been negotiated down to its irreducible forms. Lichen, mostly: rust-orange, bone-grey, a black variety that grows in perfect circles and only on the north faces of stones, except that on this plateau every face a circle grows on becomes, by definition, north. There are cushion plants in the damp hollows near the pools, dense green-grey hemispheres centuries old, growing at a rate indistinguishable from not growing. In two or three sheltered clefts, a kind of grass stands in sparse blades, each blade frosted on one side only, the frost never melting and never thickening.

The fauna is rare enough that each encounter feels appointed. There is a bird — something between a chough and a raven, black, competent — that rides the wind in long arcs and never lands where you are looking. You will see it perhaps once a day. It makes no call. In a place this loud, what would be the point. There are small mammals, pika-like, that you will never see but whose presence is recorded in tidy haypiles of dried cushion-plant tucked under overhangs — haypiles that look ancient and fresh at once, because everything here does.

And once, perhaps, at the limit of the dim light, a larger shape: four-legged, pale, moving along the unreachable ridgeline. Moving along it. The only thing that has ever been seen to make progress against that ridge. It does not come down. It may not be able to. It may be the reason the ridge cannot be approached — something has to keep the distance, and it walks the distance for a living.

## The Built Things

People came here. Not many, not recently, not with tools beyond their hands — but they came, and they left the marks people leave when they meet something they cannot explain and cannot bear to leave unmarked.

Cairns, mostly. Dozens scattered across the terraces, miles apart, each one a tight dry-stone stack about chest height, weathered to the same finish as the native rock. They are not waymarkers; they lead nowhere and connect nothing. Each one stands beside a thin place — beside a black pool, at the rim of the duplicated basin, facing the unreachable ridge — and each one stands a careful, identical distance away. Twelve paces, roughly, every time. Whoever built them had learned something, the same something, independently or by tradition: this close and no closer.

One structure is larger. In the central basin there is a low rectangular foundation, a single course of fitted stone, roofless, doorless on three sides, with a gap in the fourth wall facing the ridgeline. Inside, the deep sound is — not quieter — *rounder*. Bearable in a different way. The floor of the enclosure is bare swept stone, and it stays swept, though nothing visible sweeps it. Grit blown in by the wind is gone by the time you look again. It is the one housekept place on the plateau, and no one keeps it.

Near the gap in the wall, fingertip-deep grooves are worn into the stone lintel, the kind of wear made by hundreds of hands touching the same spot on the way out. Hundreds of hands, and no path to or from the enclosure, and no record of anyone, and the wear so smooth it has stopped being evidence and become geology.

## The Stillness

Here is the deepest strangeness, deeper than the pools or the ridge: nothing here is in transition.

The sleet falls but does not accumulate. The frost beads but does not thicken or melt. The light does not shift toward dawn or dusk. The bird's arcs repeat without quite repeating. The thin places do not spread and do not close. Even your own changes feel suspended — hunger comes but doesn't sharpen, fatigue arrives and plateaus, your breath in the cold air makes the same plume every time, the same shape, dissolving at the same height.

The plateau is not frozen in time. Time clearly passes; you walk, you think, the bird crosses the dim air. Rather, the plateau has finished with time. It has been eroded by it for so long that there is nothing left for time to take. What remains is the remainder: stone, lichen, black water reflecting an absent sky, twelve careful paces, and the great unending chord coming up through everything — which you eventually understand is not a sound the place is making.

It is the sound of the place holding still. The effort of equilibrium, audible. The roar a wound makes when it has healed wrong, permanently, and stopped hurting, and will outlast everything that could have remembered it open.

You can stay as long as you like. The plateau does not mind, the way a finished thing does not mind anything. When you leave — descending into thicker air, into weather, into light with a direction again — the chord will fade somewhere around the cloud line, and for days afterward silence will sound to you like something missing.
