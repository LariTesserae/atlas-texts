**The Precipitate City**

Begin with the altitude, because everything else follows from it: a high basin, two miles above whatever counts as sea level here, rimmed by mountains so young they have no foothills. The rock is raw. It came up fast — you can read the haste in it — black and grey stone with the gloss still on it, edges that no weather has yet been given time to argue with. There is no soil. Soil is a product of patience and death, and this place has not accumulated enough of either. What covers the ground instead is water: a film of it on every surface, beading on the volcanic glass, pooling in the fresh fractures, seeping out of the cliff faces in long dark stripes, as if the mountains themselves were still wringing out.

It is cold. Not the brittle cold of dry places — a saturated cold, a cold you wear. The air sits at the exact temperature where water cannot decide what it wants to be. Fog forms and unforms continuously. Frost grows on the shadowed side of stones and melts on the same stone an arm's length away. The whole basin breathes condensation; walk through it and you arrive wet without ever having felt rain.

And it is dark. Understand this first about the place: there is no day here, or the day is so occluded by the permanent ceiling of cloud that the distinction has stopped mattering. The darkness is not empty, though. It is the darkness of a theater after the lights go down — full, attended, waiting.

---

Spread across the basin floor, and climbing partway up the rim walls, is the city.

It is hard to say whether the city is being built or being *grown*, and the truth is probably that the distinction is one the builders did not bother to preserve. Nothing here was quarried, hauled, or assembled. The structures are being precipitated — coaxed out of the mineral-saturated water that weeps from the young rock, the way a cave grows its stone teeth, except directed, deliberate, and on the scale of a metropolis. Towers stand half-emerged, their lower hundred meters finished and seamless, their upper reaches dissolving into lattice and intention: filigree scaffolds of crystal that thicken, measurably, by perhaps a fingernail's width per year. Bridges reach toward each other across ravines and have not yet touched; they will meet in a generation. Whole districts exist currently as a kind of skeleton — outlines of streets, the ghost-geometry of plazas, walls that are still translucent because they are only a few centuries thick.

The machinery doing this work is nowhere visible, which is itself the signature of its sophistication. There are no cranes, no engines, no lights of industry. The engineering happens at the scale of the water itself — something dissolved in it, distributed through it, deciding molecule by molecule where stone should accrete and where it should be refused. Every dripping cornice is a construction site. Every weeping wall is a supply line. If you put your bare hand against a growing surface you feel a faint, granular warmth, the only heat in the basin, like the warmth of arithmetic being done.

The plan, whatever it is, unfolds at a pace that has nothing to do with anyone's lifetime. The city is mostly future.

---

But the city is not empty, and this is what overwhelms every visitor who can be said to visit: the noise, and what makes the noise.

There are no plants here — not one leaf, not a moss, not a smear of lichen on all that wet black stone. The basin skipped plants entirely. What it has instead is animals, in a density that beggars description, an ecology built directly on the city's chemistry. The growing walls exude trace warmth and a slow rain of mineral byproduct, and an entire food web has assembled itself around this drip-feed, packed into the dark, layered body on body.

The lowest stratum is the carpet: invertebrate life in such profusion that the streets and pool-bottoms are not stone-colored but creature-colored, a shifting mosaic of shelled and soft things grazing the chemical film off every surface. They make a sound like rain falling upward — a continuous fine clattering, millions of small mouths and feet against wet glass.

Above them, the pools and flooded districts are thick with swimming things: pale, eyeless, muscular, some as long as a person, sliding through the streets that the city has not yet raised above the waterline. At night — which is always — they surface to gulp the fog, and the sound of it, multiplied across a thousand pools, is a vast wet percussion, like slow applause from an audience the size of a province.

And above *them*, the air. The air is the loudest stratum. Flying animals roost in the unfinished upper lattices of the towers in colonies of incomprehensible size — the half-built crystal scaffolds are, from the fauna's perspective, the finest roosting habitat that has ever existed, endless wet caverns of ledge and spar. At irregular intervals, some signal moves through a colony and a portion of it takes wing all at once, and the sound is geological: a roar of membrane and displaced air that rolls around the basin rim, sets the pools shivering, and takes minutes to fully decay. They navigate by sound, of course; everything here does. The darkness is structured entirely out of voices. Stand still and you can hear the city's invisible architecture rendered in echo — clicks mapping the towers, calls measuring the ravines, the whole black volume of the basin continuously sung into legibility by its inhabitants.

The animals are fast and the city is slow, and they have come to an accommodation. The fauna cannot damage the architecture — their gnawing and scraping is factored in, the way a river's load is factored into a delta — and the architecture, for its part, seems to *tolerate* them, or perhaps to use them. There are passages in the older districts shaped suspiciously well for the swimming things, channels with the exact bore of their bodies. There are roosting spurs on the towers that serve no structural purpose anyone can name. The suspicion grows on you slowly, over hours in the dark: that the animals are not squatters in the construction site. That they are part of the bill of materials. That their waste, their shed shells, their generations of small calcium deaths are being metabolized into the walls, and that the deafening, teeming dark is not an infestation of the plan but its workforce — unpaid, unaware, and ineligible to ever see what they are building.

---

Only one kind of light exists in the basin, and it is rare and brief. Where a structure crosses some threshold of completion — a wall reaching its final thickness, two bridge-halves at last fusing in the dark over a ravine — the new-made stone gives off a faint blue release of stored energy, a glow lasting an hour or a night, gone by the next. The animals are drawn to it from miles around; they wheel and swarm and shriek around the light in a frenzy whose purpose no one knows, a celebration or a feeding or both. Then the glow fades, the colony disperses, the basin closes back over into its loud and crowded blackness, and the city goes on becoming itself at the speed of dripping water — one molecule, one century, one finished bridge at a time.
