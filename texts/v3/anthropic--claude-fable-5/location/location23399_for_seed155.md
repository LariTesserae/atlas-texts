**The Pith Plateau**

At three thousand meters the world stops pretending to be made of stone. The plateau is plant all the way down — not soil hosting roots but root *as* ground, an organism so old it has become geography. Core samples, where anyone has bothered, come up green-grey for a kilometer before the augers gum and stall. Whatever mineral world it first grew on has long since been digested, incorporated, forgotten. The plateau is the settled, final form of a growth that finished growing before anything now living can remember, and it has been wearing down ever since: not eroding into sand, because there is no sand, but *flattening*, the way an old face flattens, ridges of woody tissue slumping over millennia into broad smooth swells, fissures healing into faint seams, everything rounding toward a single horizontal exhaustion.

The surface is a skin of moss over heartwood — though "wood" is wrong; the material is denser, faintly translucent at a fresh cut, the color of old jade gone milky. The locals call it *pith* and build everything from it, or rather *in* it, because you do not quarry pith so much as petition it. Cut a doorway and the doorway slowly considers itself; in a generation the lintel will have thickened, the threshold worn into a smiling curve, the whole opening become something the plateau decided to have rather than something done to it.

The cold up here is steady and clean, a few degrees above frost most of the year. Water arrives sideways: cloud decks drag across the plateau like wet wool, and the pith drinks them. Everything beads. Condensation runs in slow threads down the flanks of the buildings, collects in natural cups and old healed wounds, stands in black pools scattered across the flats — pools that are not puddles on the plant but openings *into* it, sap-wells, their water faintly sweet and very slightly luminous after rain. There are no rivers. There is instead a hydrology of the interior: you can press your ear to the ground almost anywhere and hear water moving below, hauled through vessels the width of train tunnels by a pressure that has nothing to do with you.

This is the first of the sounds. It is never quiet here. The plateau is loud the way a ship's engine room is loud — a layered, total noise that residents stop hearing and visitors cannot stop hearing. Underneath everything, the *pump*: the slow surge of sap through the great vessels, a sound felt in the sternum more than heard, arriving in long swells perhaps forty seconds apart, the pulse of something with one heartbeat per minute and no intention of dying. Over that, wind in the standing hollows — the dead vertical stems, some thirty stories tall, that punctuate the flats like the columns of a roofless temple; they are open at the top and fluted inside, and the wind plays them constantly, a sustained organ chord that shifts pitch with the weather. And over *that*, the works of the towns.

Because there are towns. The settlement pattern is strange to lowland eyes: dense knots of construction separated by great vacancies, a city pulled apart like taffy and scattered across a hundred kilometers of moss. Each district sits where the pith offered something — a sap-well rich enough to tap, a hollow large enough to roof, a cluster of stems sound enough to floor. Between districts: nothing built, just the long green flats, the occasional pool, the standing hollows singing, a road of worn pith polished by cart wheels and almost indistinguishable from the ground around it. You can walk two hours between neighborhoods of the same city. People do. The emptiness is considered part of the municipality, the way a cathedral's silence is part of the cathedral.

The towns themselves are industrial in the fullest sense. The plateau's sap is combustible when reduced, and the great pressure of the internal hydraulics can be tapped directly: works-houses squat over the largest vessels like ticks, their pistons driven not by steam but by the organism's own pulse, machinery that fires once every forty seconds with a *boom* that carries for miles, slow and patient, factories running at the heartbeat of their host. Fiber-mills strip the inner bark of culled stems and spin it into cable, cloth, paper. Resin-stills burn low and constant, their smoke sweet and green-smelling, staining the undersides of the cloud decks amber. Lights are sap-lamps mostly — the faint luminosity of the wells, concentrated and caged — supplemented in the richer districts by true electrics run off pulse-dynamos, bulbs that brighten and dim with the heartbeat so that whole streets breathe light.

Animals are nearly absent and the absence is felt. No birds — nothing for them, no seeds, no insects to speak of, the plateau having long ago outlived its pollinators or absorbed them or never needed them. What fauna exists is fugitive and singular: a pale, long-bodied creature sometimes seen drinking at the pools at dusk, never the same pool twice, possibly never the same creature twice; a sound in the hollows occasionally that is not wind; the works-house cats, brought up from the lowlands generations back, who have grown large, slow, and reluctant to look at certain things.

Certain things, because the plateau is old enough that reality has worn thin on it, the way a stair tread wears thin. The locals are practical about this. They will tell you, without drama, which flats not to cross in fog — not because of cliffs but because the distance *misbehaves* out there: you walk an hour and arrive where you started, or arrive yesterday, or arrive carrying a memory of the walk that does not match your own footprints. They will point out the district of Old Tapping, fully built, lamps lit, smoke rising, and explain that no one lives there and no one ever has; the plateau grew it, or remembered it, a complete industrial quarter generated like a gall, its machinery running on pulse-power for no one, its doors opening onto interiors that are sometimes rooms and sometimes the green translucent depth of solid pith with furniture suspended inside it like flies in amber. The factory bells of Old Tapping ring shift-change for a workforce that does not exist, and the sound carries across the flats, and the actual towns have learned to ring their own bells at offset times so no one is confused about which city is calling them home.

Time pools the way water does. There are healed places in the surface — old wounds, scars the size of plazas — where the air over them runs slow; rain falls into them and hangs, descending over hours, and a person standing inside one moves like a figure in honey and emerges minutes later having missed an afternoon. Children are warned. Children, naturally, dare each other.

The prevailing theory, held loosely by the resin-distillers and the well-priests alike, is that the plateau dreams, and that very old dreams leak. The organism has been settled into its final form for so long that growth, denied outward expression, has turned inward and then turned *strange* — and the towns, tapping its pulse, lighting their streets with its blood, are living inside the slow last thoughts of the largest living thing there has ever been. This does not frighten anyone much. The heartbeat is steady. The sap is sweet. The pistons fire, the hollows sing their chord, the clouds come dragging their wet bellies across the flats, and the lamps of the true towns breathe in time with the lamps of the false one, all of it pulsing together, forty seconds to the beat, on the high green plain at the top of the world.
