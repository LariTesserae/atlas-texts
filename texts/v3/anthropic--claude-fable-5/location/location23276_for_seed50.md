**The Stoup**

Near the top of a mountain that is not finished, there is a room of water.

You reach it the way the makers of the rope did: by a seam in the summit rock barely wider than a body, a hundred-some meters below a peak that has no name because it has not yet decided on a shape. The seam goes down and inward, and you hear the place long before you see it. The sound comes up through the stone itself — not an echo, but a vibration in the rock you grip, a roar conducted bone to bone, so that by the time you emerge onto the lip of the chamber your teeth are already humming and speech has been impossible for some minutes.

The chamber is small. That is the first surprise, after all that noise. Perhaps twenty meters across, roughly spherical, like a bubble caught in glass — and in a sense that is what it is, because the mountain is young, still cooling, still settling, and this void is a flaw in its making. The walls are raw. Not eroded-smooth, not weathered — *raw*, the way a broken bone is raw: faces of fresh mineral that have never seen rain, crystals long as fingers in dark fans, edges that will cut you if you steady yourself carelessly. Nothing here has been worn. Everything here is exactly as it formed, sharp as the moment of its birth.

And the chamber is full of water, nearly to the lip. Cold water, snow-fed through some channel no one has traced, mountain-cold, the kind that makes the hands clumsy in under a minute. The light is the second surprise: the dome above the waterline is pierced with three fissures open to the sky, and at this altitude the sky is fierce. White light drops through in blades and the water takes it and shatters it, so the whole chamber strobes and glitters, bright as midmorning, brighter in flashes — light flung onto the crystal walls and back again, a small violent brilliance sealed inside a dark mountain.

The water does not rest. It has never rested. Somewhere below — far below, deeper than the chamber looks like it should go, deeper than the ropes have ever reached — something feeds it and pulls it in cycles no one has timed twice the same. The surface heaves. It coils into a spiral and then unwinds. It stands up in a column the height of a person, holds itself there shuddering, collapses, and rises somewhere else. There are minutes when the whole volume rotates as one body, a slow muscular turning, and minutes when it loses all order and becomes pure white chaos, slamming the walls, spitting cold spray up through the sky-fissures so that, outside, climbers on the western face sometimes see the summit breathing mist on a cloudless day.

The sound, this close, is total. It is not loud the way a storm is loud; it is loud the way the inside of a drum is loud. It occupies the body. People who have spent an hour at the lip report that for a day afterward silence felt like a pressure, like something was missing from the air.

The strangeness is this: the water is making the mountain. Where it strikes the walls, it does not wear them down. It *builds*. Each impact leaves a film of mineral, fine as breath on glass, and over the cycles the films stack into those crystal fans, those fresh sharp faces. The chamber is very slightly smaller every year. The walls are growing inward like a wound closing, and the turbulence is the hand laying the stone. Some who have studied it from the lip say the violence has pattern — that the column always rises in the same five places, that the water is not raging but *working*, the way a potter's hands look violent to something on the wheel. The mountain's unfinished summit, they say, is unfinished because it is still being poured, from the inside, by this small deafening heart.

Life barely tolerates the place. In the few dead-water seams where the turbulence cannot reach, a pale weed grows — colorless ribbons, almost translucent, anchored in crystal, streaming and snapping in the spillover currents. There is said to be one animal: a white, eyeless, eel-bodied thing the length of an arm, seen perhaps once a season, riding the rotation effortlessly, never fighting it. No one knows what it eats. Some say it is older than the chamber. Some say there is only ever the one.

And people have been here — not many, not often, and never for long. Their marks are small and stubborn. A rope of twisted plant fiber, replaced generation by generation, hangs down the entry seam, its anchor a wooden toggle jammed in a crack. On the lip itself, a shelf has been pecked flat with stone tools — you can feel the dimpled hammer-marks under your palm — just wide enough for one person to sit. Beside it, rows of tally marks scratched into the crystal, dozens of them, grouped in fives, counting something: visits, or columns, or years. A few steps in, wedged in a niche above the spray line, a small basket of woven bark holds offerings gone pale and soft with damp — seeds, a carved bird, a child's tooth. There is no path to the seam on the outside of the mountain. Whoever comes here comes far, and climbs hard, to sit on a hand-cut ledge in unbearable noise and watch water build stone.

They leave before the cold takes their hands. The water column rises, holds, falls. The light strobes off the growing walls. The room goes on closing, year by year, sealing its own brilliance inside — and one day, the tally marks suggest someone already understood, there will be no chamber at all: just a finished summit, solid to the core, silent at last, with a rope of rotted fiber buried somewhere inside it like a vein.
