**The Pump-Heart of Verren Deep**

There is no sky here and there never was. You arrive — if arrival is the word for sinking through a shaft of warm black water for the better part of an hour — into a single chamber, and the chamber is the world.

It is not large. That is the first surprise, after the sound. You could swim its length in two minutes if the water permitted swimming in straight lines, which it does not. A vaulted cavity perhaps eighty meters across, hollowed from bedrock long ago and then *built into*, the way a city builds into a valley: gantries stacked on gantries, catwalks corroded to lace, pipework in seven generations of metallurgy — bronze under iron under some gray alloy that has outlasted them both — all of it climbing the curved walls in dense, ringed tiers like the inside of a wasp nest cast in machinery. A whole industrial district folded into a room. Streets the width of a shoulder. Junction boxes the size of houses, by the local measure. Everything organized, addressed, numbered in raised letters now soft with mineral bloom.

And almost nothing in the space between. That is the second surprise. The chamber's center is a void of warm, dim water — a plaza of nothing, a held breath. All the density clings to the walls. You hang in the middle of it like a mote in a bell.

The bell is ringing.

The sound is total. It is not noise *in* the water; it is the water's actual condition, the medium's temperament. Somewhere below the silted floor — and below means very little here; the floor is just the wall you'd fall toward — the original engines are still running. Pumps the size of chapels, driven by a geothermal pressure-head that has not slackened in however many centuries this place has been sealed. They were built to move an ocean out of a mine, or a mine's worth of heat into a city above that may no longer exist. They no longer move anything anywhere. They move the chamber itself. Each stroke is a pressure wave you feel in the roots of your teeth, in the fluid of your eyes. The rhythm is almost regular — almost — a vast triple knock followed by a shuddering exhale of bubbles from vents in the floor, columns of silver that rise, tear apart in the turbulence, and never reach a surface, because there is no surface. They simply diminish into the warm dark of the upper vault and are reabsorbed.

The turbulence is the chamber's weather. Thermal currents come off the engine-floor in muscular, twisting plumes — water at near-scalding temperatures braiding up through merely warm water, visible as shimmer, as wandering lenses of distortion that bend the dim light into slow whips. Loose objects circulate forever: a bolt, a glove gone stiff as ceramic, a sheaf of what might once have been schedules, all riding the convection in orbits that decay and renew. Sometimes the engines miss their rhythm — a stroke arrives early, or doubles — and the whole water-mass lurches, and every loose thing in the chamber changes orbit at once, and the catwalk-lace shivers and sheds a fine rust-snow that the currents catch and carry upward, glittering faintly, against all intuition.

The light is the engines' own. Down through gaps in the silted floor plates comes a deep furnace-color, a sullen orange-rose, pulsing with the strokes — the glow of whatever heat source the builders harnessed, still patient, still fed. It is the only illumination and it is not much. It gives the chamber the lighting of a banked hearth seen through smoked glass. The tiered machinery on the walls reads mostly as silhouette: ribs, ladders, the cursive of pipes. Where the orange light strokes a surface directly, you can see that everything is upholstered in mineral deposit — smooth, pale, faintly translucent crusts laid down by centuries of hot mineral water, so that valve-wheels look like enormous opals and the catwalks like the skeletons of something that grew here rather than something assembled. The place has been digested by its own water and recast as geology. It is at peace with this. Nothing is failing anymore. Everything that was going to break broke long ago; what remains has found its permanent form and holds it, ringing.

There are no plants. There were never plants; light never reached here, and the water, though warm and rich in dissolved stone, carries nothing green. The closest thing to growth is the mineral crust itself, which thickens by a hair's width per decade, and — if you look very closely at the cooler pipes on the upper tiers — a film, gray-gold, that may be bacterial and may be merely chemistry. No one has stayed long enough to ask it.

Fauna is a rumor the chamber occasionally confirms. Once in a long while, threading the gap between two pipe-tiers, something pale and ribbonlike — an eel, or an animal that has arrived at the eel's conclusions independently — eyeless, the length of an arm, the color of candle wax. It navigates by the pressure-strokes; you can watch it stall and reorient at each engine-beat, reading the chamber's pulse the way other creatures read light. It is alone, or nearly. In the silt of the floor, between the glowing seams, small conical shells sit unmoving for so long you decide they are stones, until one, over the course of an hour, is somewhere else.

The strangeness of the place is quiet and arrives late. It is this: the engines should not still run. Not because the heat has failed — the heat is eternal — but because machines need tending, and nothing tends these. Yet the rhythm holds. And when it stumbles, it *corrects* — the doubled stroke is followed by a held one, the early beat by a late, as if the system, over its long submersion, has worn itself into a shape that can only do the right thing, the way a river can only flow downhill. The builders did not design that. The water did, and the heat, and the centuries, grinding the mechanism down to the one motion it cannot help making.

So the chamber persists: a city's heart in a body that has dissolved, beating in the dark, deafening to no one, warming the empty water at its center where nothing lives but the sound — which fills the void completely, wall to wall, and may be, by now, the truest inhabitant. You hang in the middle of it, in the plaza of warm nothing, and feel each stroke arrive through your ribs, and understand that you are not in a ruin. Ruins are quiet. This is something else: a place that finished its purpose long ago and simply never learned how to stop.
