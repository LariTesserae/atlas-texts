# The Loud Plateau

You come up onto it through cloud, and the first thing that arrives is not the view but the sound. It begins as a pressure in the chest a thousand feet below the rim — a low continuous body-deep thrum, like standing inside a struck bell that never finishes ringing — and by the time you crest the last shelf of stone, it has become the whole of the air. There is no quiet here. There will not be quiet here. The plateau roars at one sustained, enormous, unvarying pitch, and it has roared exactly this way for longer than anything that could remember it.

The land is white. Not snow — though the cool thin air keeps suggesting snow — but stone: travertine and sinter and pale fluted limestone, mineral down to its bones, with not one leaf, not one lichen-stain, not one thread of moss anywhere in the whole visible world. The plateau extends to the horizon in every direction, a high tableland maybe a day's walk across, and every inch of it is *occupied*. There is no open ground. The surface is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with stone towers — domes, pinnacles, fluted columns, melted-candle spires, some the height of a person, some the height of cathedrals — crowded so densely that walking between them means turning sideways, choosing your seams, moving through the land the way you'd move through a crowd that has no intention of parting.

And every one of them is weeping.

This is the source of the roar. The plateau sits over a vast pressurized aquifer, cold water held inside the mountain like breath held in a chest, and it does not escape in rivers or in any single great fall. It escapes *everywhere*. Every tower bleeds water from its flanks — thin silver sheets sliding down fluted stone, ropes of water spouting from fissures at chest height, fine pressurized hiss-jets misting sideways from pinholes in the rock. Tens of thousands of small waters, none of them remarkable alone, all of them sounding at once. The roar is not one waterfall. It is a hundred thousand of them refusing to ever stop, summed into a single white wall of sound so total that you cannot hear your own footsteps, your own voice, the click of stone you knock loose. Two people here could stand a hand's width apart and never speak again.

The light is staggering. The plateau is high enough that the sky overhead is a hard, dark, scoured blue, and the sun comes down through that thin air undiluted and strikes white stone and falling water and hanging mist all at once. Everything blazes. The wet flanks of the towers run with moving glare. The spray that hangs in the seams between pinnacles is shot through with small fixed rainbows — and here is the first strangeness, the thing the eye keeps returning to: the rainbows do not move. The jets that make them are pressure-fed and perfectly steady; the mist they throw hangs in the same place always; and so each rainbow stands in its slot between the towers like a permanent object, a colored thing you could walk around, that has been standing in that exact air for ten thousand years. People who came here — no one has come here; there is no path, no cairn, no mark, not a chipped stone anywhere — but anyone who *did* would map by them. *Left at the third rainbow. Camp beneath the doubled one.*

The water itself is cold and faintly milky, saturated with dissolved stone. This is why the place never changes. The water cuts the towers, yes — runs down them, hollows them, carves the flutes and pleats and overhangs — but it also *builds* them, laying down pale calcite skin exactly as fast as it strips it away. Erosion and deposition long ago found their balance and locked. The towers are old, weathered, settled into their final shapes, and their final shapes are also their constant shapes: each spire dissolving and reprecipitating in place, stone passing through water and back into stone, the whole plateau a closed loop running at full thunder and going nowhere. The water sheets off the tower flanks into channels at their feet, gathers into pale-blue terraced pools rimmed with white stone lips, spills from terrace to terrace, and somewhere — at the plateau's hidden seams — sinks back down into the mountain to be pressed up through the towers again. Nothing accumulates. Nothing drains away. The pools have been the same depth, the same impossible mineral blue, since before there were eyes.

Between the towers the air is cool and saturated, condensation beading on every overhang, fat drops falling from stone ceilings into pools — and you cannot hear them land. You watch a drop strike water and the rings spread in silence inside the roar.

Life is almost absent, and what there is, is pale and strange and rare. In some of the deeper terrace pools, if you crouch and shade the glare with your hand, you may see them: blind white creatures the length of a finger, soft-bodied, slow, drifting along the calcite floors, grazing on the mineral film itself or on something simpler than anything with a name. They are deaf — they would have to be — and they never surface, and there are very few of them, one pool in a hundred. Once, perhaps twice in a long day's crossing, a single bird passes high over the plateau, white against the dark-blue sky, riding the column of cool wet air the place exhales. It does not land. There is nothing here for it. It crosses, and is gone, and the towers go on roaring beneath the fixed rainbows.

Stand still long enough among the packed spires — soaked through with hanging mist, half-blind with glare, the sound long since gone past loud into something more like a medium you exist inside, like depth, like weight — and the plateau's single truth settles into you. This place is not happening. It happened, completely, a very long time ago, and then it stopped happening and started simply *being*: the water rising, the stone weeping, the rainbows standing in their slots, the roar holding its one eternal note. You could come back in a thousand years and the same drop would seem to be falling from the same overhang into the same silent ring.

The loud plateau does not change. It is finished. It is just very, very far from quiet about it.
