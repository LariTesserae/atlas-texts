**The Gallery**

There is a cavity in the earth—new, still sharp at its edges, the stone unhealed—about the size of a small chapel, and it is full to the ceiling with water. Not filled *with* water so much as occupied by it: the water arrived first, the cavity opened around it, and the geology has never quite forgiven the reversal. You can see this in the walls. Fresh fracture faces, unweathered, glinting with mineral cleavage that hasn't had time to dull. Nothing here has been smoothed. Every surface is an edge.

The water is cold in the way of things that have never touched the sun—not frigid, not hostile, just permanently, patiently cool, the temperature of a cellar or the inside of a stone. It carries light in a way water shouldn't. Illumination enters from no identifiable source and moves through the chamber at roughly walking pace, in slow diagonal bands, as if the light were on a schedule. When a band passes over you, you cast two shadows. One of them lags.

---

**What lives here, and what it does**

The chamber is crowded. That is the first thing a body registers: *presence*, layered, near-touching. The water is thick with inhabitants the way a hive is thick with bees, and like a hive, the crowding is not chaos. It is production.

Nothing here was built. Understand that. There is no scaffolding, no bolt, no seam of human making. And yet the place runs like a foundry.

Along the eastern wall—the freshest fracture, still exhaling fine mineral dust into the water—cling ranks of what might be mollusks, if mollusks had evolved into reciprocating engines. Each one is a fist-sized shell that opens and slams shut with a percussive *chunk*, hundreds of them, slightly out of phase, so the wall sounds like a machine shop heard through a floor. They are not feeding. Or rather, feeding is the least of what they're doing: each closure of the shell compresses a small volume of water into something denser than water, a bead of pressed liquid that drops from the shell's lip and sinks with unusual conviction. The beads accumulate on the floor in drifts. They are warm to the touch, faintly luminous, and they hum at a pitch just below hearing. Nobody made these creatures do this. This is simply what they are for, in the way a heart is for beating. The chamber has an economy, and this is its heavy industry.

Above the shell-engines, in the middle water, drift the assemblers—translucent, jellyfish-adjacent, but articulated, with limbs that fold and extend with the crisp precision of machine linkages. They gather the pressed-water beads (some sink, some inexplicably rise; the beads have not agreed among themselves about gravity) and they *work* them, passing them limb to limb, and where three or four assemblers cluster, sparks occur. Actual sparks, underwater, blue-white and branching, arcing between limb-tips. The water should not permit this. The water permits this. Each spark leaves a brief afterimage hanging in the current—a frozen photograph of the arc—that takes several seconds to dissolve, so the middle of the chamber is always littered with the ghosts of recent electricity.

What the assemblers make is unclear. The products drift toward the ceiling: small, intricate objects, organic in material but mechanical in logic, like seedpods designed by an engineer, or gears grown on a vine. They collect in the ceiling's fissures. Sometimes one hatches. What comes out swims down and joins the workforce. The factory manufactures its own factory. There was never a first machine. Don't look for one; the causality here is circular and gets defensive when examined.

**The vegetation, such as it is**

Sparse. Deliberate-seeming. In the whole chamber there are perhaps a dozen plants—tall, dark ribbon-weeds anchored in floor cracks, each one standing alone with a wide margin of bare stone around it, like columns in a hall. The fauna does not touch them. Currents that tumble everything else part around the ribbon-weeds without disturbance.

Watch one long enough and you'll notice it isn't moving with the water. It's moving with *other* water—swaying to a current that isn't present, a rhythm from somewhere else, or somewhen. Occasionally all twelve plants lean the same direction at once, hard, as if the whole chamber had been tilted, though nothing else responds. Then they relax. The shell-engines pause when this happens—a full second of silence, shocking as a dropped tool—and then resume, slightly rearranged in their rhythm, as if a shift change had occurred.

The plants may be instruments. They may be management. They are the only quiet things in the room and everything defers to them.

---

**Sound**

Loud, but layered, legible-loud, the loudness of an operational floor: the percussion of the shell-wall, the crackle-snap of assembler arcs, a deep rotational drone from below the floor where nothing visible rotates, and threaded through it all the traffic-noise of the fauna itself—clicks, rasps, the flutter of fins in tight quarters.

The acoustics are broken in one specific way: sound here sometimes arrives before its source. You will hear the *chunk* of a shell closing, and then watch the shell close. You will hear a spark, count two, three seconds, and then see it. The chamber's sound runs a few beats ahead of its events, like a film with the audio misaligned—except the audio is right and the events are late. Old inhabitants (insofar as anything here is old; the whole place is young, raw, weeks or months into existence) seem to navigate by this. The small swimming things flinch away from predators *before the strike*, warned by the sound of jaws that haven't closed yet. Predation here is a game played against your own future noise. The successful hunters are the silent ones, and the very successful hunters are the ones who have learned to be silent slightly in advance.

---

**Rules of the place**

A short inventory, for the visitor:

The water near the walls is denser than the water in the middle, and the boundary between them is visible—a wavering, oily interface, like the skin between two liquids that have declined to mix. Cross it slowly. Crossing it quickly produces a copy. The copy is brief, faithful, and swims off before dissolving; the fauna does this constantly, on purpose, as far as anyone can tell for recreation.

Nothing decays here yet. The place is too new. Dead things—and there are dead things; the predation is real, the ecosystem eats itself with industrial appetite—do not rot. They dismantle. Small operator-creatures descend on a carcass and take it apart along its natural seams, sorting the components into drifts by type, and the drifts are drawn slowly toward the shell-wall, where they go in, and do not come out, and the *chunk* of the engines gets fractionally richer in tone for an hour afterward.

The chamber is bigger on the inside than its dimensions in the rock allow, but only by a little—perhaps ten percent—and only when unobserved from outside. It is hidden the way a room behind a bookcase is hidden: embedded, contained, findable only by accident or invitation. There may be others like it. The pressed-water beads that rise instead of sink go *somewhere*, filtering up through hairline fractures in the fresh stone, and it is possible to imagine a whole distribution network, chamber to chamber, an industry of pockets, none of them built, all of them running.

---

**Standing in it**

Hang in the middle water, in the cool, with the shift-lights sweeping past and your slow shadow trailing its slower twin. The shell-wall pounds. The arcs snap and leave their photographs. Something small is eaten to your left—you heard it happen four seconds ago—and the operators are already arriving to sort the pieces. The twelve dark plants sway to their absent tide. Everything is close enough to touch and nothing touches you, the crowd parting and reforming with the indifference of a workforce mid-shift.

The place is not for you. It is not for anything. It is a factory with no owner, no product anyone will receive, no door marked for deliveries—an economy that closed its loop before anyone could ask what it was for, in a chamber the earth opened by mistake and the water claimed before the dust settled.

It is very busy. It has just begun. Listen: you can already hear what it's about to do next.
