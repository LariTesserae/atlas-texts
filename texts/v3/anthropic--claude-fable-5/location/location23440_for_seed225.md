# The Sun-Cistern

You reach it by going down, which is the first strangeness, because everything about the place insists you have gone up — toward light, toward warmth, toward open air. A slot in the dry scrubland, a tilted slab of pale rock you have to slide beneath, a short throat of cool shadow no longer than a held breath, and then the chamber opens around you, and it is *blazing*.

The room is small. You could cross it in thirty paces, and the ceiling — a dome of honey-colored stone — hangs no higher than a tall tree. There is a single eye in the roof, an oculus the width of a wagon wheel, worn open by ten thousand years of water finding the same weakness, and the sun stands in it for only a few hours each day. That should not be enough. The light should be a single moving shaft, a blade swinging slowly across the floor. Instead the whole chamber glows.

The stone holds light. That is the simplest way to say it, and people who have come here — they have come here, for a very long time, though they have never built anything — say it different ways: the rock drinks the sun, the walls are slow mirrors, the cistern fills. The limestone here is shot through with something crystalline and pale, and whatever falls into it through the oculus does not leave quickly. The walls give back daylight hours after the sun has gone. At midnight the chamber is the color of late afternoon. Just before dawn it dims to the amber of an oil flame, the stone finally going hungry again, and then the sun returns to the eye and the level rises once more. Nothing in here is ever fully dark. Nothing in here is ever in a hurry.

## The Floor

The floor is bare, swept-looking, though no one sweeps it. It is polished travertine, laid down in shallow overlapping fans by water that flowed here when the climate was wetter, hardened now into something like frozen cream. The polish is real and old — partly the water's work, partly the bellies of animals, partly, perhaps, the feet of the people who have stood here. It dips toward the center, where there is a pool.

The pool is small and almost perfectly still. It is fed by nothing you can see — condensation, mostly. The warm air of the chamber meets the cooler stone of the lower walls and beads there, and the beads gather into threads, and the threads find their grooves, channels finer than a fingernail, worn over ages, and feed the water down. After rain in the country above, the whole dome weeps gently for a day or two, a fine mineral sweat, and the pool rises perhaps a knuckle's height. It has never been seen empty. It has never been seen full. It holds light the way the walls do, but doubled, so that looking into it is like looking into a second oculus opening downward, a sun below the floor.

There are no plants in the center of the chamber. The bareness is part of the place's character — the emptiness is almost architectural, though nothing here was made. A round room, a round eye, a round pool, and air. Vast amounts of air, for so small a space; the hollowness feels larger than the dimensions allow, the way a held note feels larger than the throat that holds it.

## What Grows

What vegetation there is keeps to the edges and the seams. Ferns, small and ancient-patterned, grow in the damp channels where the condensation runs — a thin green script written down the walls. Around the rim of the oculus, where roots from the scrub above have found the opening, a fringe of pale rootlets hangs into the chamber like a curtain that was never finished. One fig, a true survivor, has sent a root the length of the entire wall, flattened against the stone like a vein, drinking from the pool's margin. It has been doing this for longer than any of its visitors have lived. The root is wide as a thigh at the top and fine as twine at the waterline, and it does not appear to change, though of course it does — everything here changes, only at the speed of stone.

There is moss in the brightest band of the wall, a luminous green-gold ring at the height where the stored light is strongest. It grows nowhere else. It seems to feed on the wall's slow giving-back, photosynthesizing at midnight by the light of the previous noon, which may be the strangest quiet fact of the place.

## What Lives

For all its emptiness, the chamber is thick with lives — they simply do not crowd it. They use it the way travelers use a well.

Swifts nest in pockets near the oculus and pour in and out at dusk and dawn, the only loud thing the place permits, a brief rushing whisper of a hundred wings that lasts a minute and then is gone. Small bats share the upper hollows and keep different hours; the two populations pass each other twice a day in the eye like shifts changing.

On the floor, geckos the color of the travertine — almost invisible until they move — hold territories around the moss ring and the fern channels. There is a kind of cricket here, wingless, translucent, that has lived in the chamber so long it has begun the slow descent toward cave-blindness, except the chamber never gave it darkness, so it stalled partway: huge pale eyes, ghost body, fully sighted, found nowhere else on earth. They sing very softly in the hour before dawn, when the stone's light is lowest, as if the dimming worries them.

The pool has its own nation: shrimp no longer than a grain of rice, a few pale snails, and — the visitors swear to this — a single old salamander, mottled gold, who is seen perhaps once a year, rising to the surface to look at the oculus and then descending again. Whether it is one animal or a lineage, no one knows. The story does not require an answer.

Larger animals come down the entrance throat to drink. A desert fox. Once, in the old tellings, an ibex that could not get back out and lived in the chamber for a season, growing tame and strange, before the people came and lifted it free with ropes of twisted fiber. Its hoofprints are said to still be visible, pressed into a patch of travertine that was soft that year and is stone now. They are.

## What Was Left

No one built here. That is not the same as saying no one came.

There is ochre on the wall opposite the entrance — not images, or not images anyone can still read. Hand-shapes, mostly, blown around spread fingers, dozens of them, overlapping, different sizes, accumulating over what must be enormous spans of time, because the oldest are barely a warmth in the stone and the newest are only a few generations faded. There is a small hollow near the entrance that holds, at any given time, a few things: a flint blade, a coil of cordage, a gourd cup. These are not offerings exactly. They are replaced when taken; they are taken when needed. The chamber functions as a kind of slow commons, a fact older than any of the languages that have named it.

The names, where remembered, all mean roughly the same thing: *the place where the light waits.*

## Sound and Time

It is mostly quiet. The pool does not lap. The drips, when there are drips, come at long intervals — a single bright tick, then a long pause, then another, a clock with no urgency. The swifts give the place its only crescendo. The crickets give it its only song. Voices, when people speak here, come out lower than intended; the dome returns them softened, rounded, a half-second late, as if the stone considers everything before giving it back — sound, like light, slightly delayed, slightly kept.

Time in the chamber is legible mainly through the eye. The bright disk of the oculus crosses the floor; the walls fill; the disk leaves; the walls spend their savings through the night. In summer the stored glow is rich and golden and the chamber stays warm as a body. In winter the light is thinner, silvery, and runs out earlier, and the geckos go still in their crevices and the fig root grips a little tighter, and everything waits, patient by long habit, for the eye to fill again.

It always does. That is the deepest fact of the place, the one the hands on the wall seem to be agreeing about across their thousands of years: down here, below the surface of a hard dry country, in a small bare room with almost nothing in it, the light is never lost. It is only ever slowly given back.
