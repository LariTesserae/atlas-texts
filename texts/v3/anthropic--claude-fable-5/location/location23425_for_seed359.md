**The Wax Garden**

It is a single room, more or less, though "room" undersells it and "more or less" is doing heavy work. A walled enclosure perhaps forty paces across — you will count differently each time, and the wall does not mind — roofed in a lattice of split cane and oiled paper through which the light comes down even and honey-colored, the warmth of a kiln three days after firing. Everything here was made. There is no ground beneath the ground. Dig down through the soil and you find that the soil is also made: crumbled terracotta, charcoal dust, ochre, all worked together by hand and pressed flat with wooden paddles whose marks are still visible, fresh as a thumbprint in dough.

No water has ever entered this place. None is missing.

The lushness is the first thing you don't understand. The garden is full — crowded, layered, leaf upon leaf — but every leaf is cut. Some are green-dyed paper, folded along their veins so they hold the cup-shape of real foliage. Some are hammered copper gone soft with verdigris, or thin-shaved wood stained with crushed beetle and lichen-juice, or wax pressed in carved molds while warm. The vines are braided fiber. The trunks of the small trees — there are eleven, or nine, or eleven — are built up from coiled rope and clay slip, smoothed by palms. The craft is primitive in the honest sense: knife, awl, mold, needle, patience. There is no seam you cannot find if you look. And yet.

And yet the garden is *growing*. This is where the place stops being a model of a garden and starts being whatever it actually is. The paper leaves have begun producing smaller paper leaves at their tips — pale, unfinished, the folds not yet creased, as if the garden has learned the technique by being made of it and is now practicing on its own. The wax fruit ripens. You can watch it across a season: the color deepening from beneath, the surface developing a bloom of fine dust that no one applies. A copper leaf fell last month and where it landed, three green wire shoots have come up through the pressed-clay soil, ending in tiny clenched buds of solder. Nothing here grows fast. The garden has the patience of a thing made by hand, and it changes at the pace of making — a leaf a week, a fruit a month — as though somewhere an invisible craftsman is still at work, or as though the work, once begun with enough care, simply continued without him.

The animals are everywhere and they are quiet. Crickets of folded bark sit in the undergrowth; occasionally one rubs its legs and produces a sound like a page turning in another room. Felt birds with stitched eyes move among the branches — they do not fly so much as relocate; you look away and they have been somewhere else for a while. A family of mice made of grey clay and real whiskers (whose whiskers? this is not answered) live under the largest root. There are beetles of lacquered seed-husk, a snake of linked wooden vertebrae that pours itself slowly along the wall once a day like a rosary being told, and something larger — felted, warm-colored, the size of a sleeping dog — curled in the far corner, which has been breathing for as long as anyone has watched it and has never yet woken. All of them breathe. None of them eat. The garden does not seem to require eating; warmth appears to be enough, the way warmth is enough for a thing rising.

The quiet has a texture. It is not absence of sound but sound at the scale of materials: wax creaking as it warms toward noon, paper relaxing, the faint tick of a copper leaf adjusting itself, a thread somewhere going taut. If you hold still long enough you can hear the garden being made — a sound underneath the others, very soft, like a knife being drawn slowly through something soft, coming from no direction.

Now the part that is harder to say.

The garden is *new*, and its newness is not only in its materials. It is new the way a painting is new before the paint dries — and reality here has not dried. At the edges of your vision the garden is unfinished: not blurry but *unresolved*, foliage that hasn't yet decided its species, a section of wall that is only the idea of wall, plain and pale, like the back of a stage flat. When you turn to look directly, it has always just finished itself, the details settling in with a faint sense of hurry, the seams still warm. The corners of the room are the worst, or the best. The northeast corner is folded wrong — it goes in where it should go out, an inside corner from one approach and an outside corner from another, and a vine has grown along it and is therefore growing in two incompatible directions at once, healthily, bearing fruit on both.

Shadows here are made things too. They are cut from dark felt and laid down under the trees, and they move — slowly, on the garden's schedule, shifting across the floor over the course of a day to track a sun that does not exist above the paper roof, only the even honey light. Sometimes a shadow comes loose at an edge and curls up slightly, and you can see beneath it: nothing, a paleness, the unprimed surface of the world. Best to press it flat again with your foot, gently. It will reattach. Everything here reattaches; the garden is forgiving, the way wet clay is forgiving.

There is one door. There are two doors. The door is in the south wall and also, when you have walked past it, in the south wall again, the same door, slightly smaller, as though the garden keeps a few drafts of its own entrance and hasn't yet discarded the rejects. Through the door — any of it — you can see only more honey-colored light and the suggestion of a workbench, very far away or very small, on which something green is being folded.

Stay an afternoon and you will notice the last thing. The garden is making *you*, a little. Nothing alarming — but your footprints in the pressed clay are tidier than your feet, the creases in your clothes have begun to look deliberate, folded along their veins, and the shadow you cast has a stitched edge. The place extends its craftsmanship to whatever rests in it long enough, slowly, leaf by leaf, with hand tools, with patience, with no water at all, and it is warm, and it is quiet, and it is not finished, and neither, while you stand there, are you.
