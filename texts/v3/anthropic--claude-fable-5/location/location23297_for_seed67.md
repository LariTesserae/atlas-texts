# The Provision Floor

There is no ground here, in the sense of soil or stone. What you stand on is deck — a continent of deck, cast in pale gray alloy that runs in gentle swales toward the horizon in every direction, dimpled with drainage channels, ribbed for traction, warm under the hand like the flank of something sleeping. The whole world is at low elevation in the way a tabletop is at low elevation: there is nothing higher to compare it to. The deck rises and falls perhaps thirty meters across kilometers, a manufactured topography of shallow basins and long, low ridges, and the basins hold water.

The water is everywhere and never deep. It stands in skins and sheets, lukewarm, faintly mineral, fed by the condensation that beads constantly on every overhead surface and falls in slow, fat, irregular drops. The air sits at the temperature of skin and is saturated nearly to the point of fog; mist gathers in the basins at no particular hour, because there are no particular hours — the light is even, white, sourceless, coming down through a high lattice of diffusion panels so distant they read as sky. Daylight without a sun. Not bright, not dim. The kind of light a careful engineer would specify for an environment where nothing is ever supposed to change.

And nothing does. That is the first strangeness you learn to feel rather than see. The water level in every basin holds at exactly the rim of its overflow channel. The drips fall at the rate the pumps return them. The deck is new — that is the second strangeness — its edges still crisp, its weld seams bright, the casting marks unworn, as though the entire continent were finished last month. But it was not finished last month. It was finished, and then time stopped accumulating on it. There is no rust because there is no excess oxygen gradient; there is no wear because every load is met by an equal restoring force. The place is young the way a held breath is young.

Nothing green grows here. Nothing grows here at all, in the vegetable sense. The role plants would fill is filled instead by the machinery itself: at intervals of fifty or sixty meters, rising from the deck like an orchard that was never alive, stand the provision stacks — vertical assemblies of brushed metal, two to ten meters tall, each one slowly exuding. Some weep a clear nutrient syrup down grooved channels in their sides. Some extrude pale, soft wafer-stuff in continuous ribbons, like very slow tongues. Some merely breathe out a warm vapor rich in dissolved sugars that films the surrounding deck in a faint, edible sheen. They are factories at the cellular pace, electrified, fed by conduits beneath the deck, humming so low and so steadily that the hum has long since stopped being a sound and become a property of matter here, like temperature.

This is what the animals eat.

The animals are the third thing, and the largest. They outnumber every other presence by orders of magnitude. The shallow basins are paved with grazers — flat, plated creatures the size of dinner trays that rasp the nutrient film off the deck with sucking undersides, packed so densely their shells touch, a living cobblestone that flexes when you walk near it. The provision stacks are sleeved in climbers: long-bodied, soft-furred things with too many gripping limbs, queued nose-to-tail up the weeping channels, drinking in turn with an orderliness that no one taught them and no one enforces. The air carries slow fliers, broad-winged and nearly silent, riding thermals off the warm deck in permanent circles, descending one at a time to take ribbon-stuff from the extruders. In the deeper channels, eel-like swimmers lie in such numbers that the water seems braided.

What is strange is not their abundance. It is their calm. There is no predation here — or rather, predation has reached its equilibrium too, and equilibrium predation looks like ritual. At certain junctions of the deck, large stalking animals stand motionless among the grazers, and at intervals one grazer detaches itself from the pavement and walks to the stalker, and is taken, without pursuit, without alarm, the surrounding thousands not even pausing in their rasping. Birth happens at the same rate, somewhere, equally quietly. Population is a conserved quantity. Death is a scheduled flow, like the condensate.

The sound of the place, given all this — a continent of machinery, a biomass beyond counting — is almost nothing. The hum. The drips. The wet, papery rasp of a hundred million feeding mouths, which blends into something like distant surf heard through a wall. Occasionally a single animal vocalizes — a low, two-tone call that travels enormous distances over the flat warm water — and is answered, once, and then the quiet closes back over it like the skin of a basin closing over a fallen drop.

You can walk for days. The deck swales repeat but never exactly; the basin shapes vary; the provision stacks come in regional designs, as if different foundries supplied different districts of the world. In some districts the stacks are arranged in spirals; in some the drainage channels spell out long utilitarian numbers no one reads. Beneath the deck, audible if you press your ear to the warm alloy, the true industry runs: pumps, synthesizers, the patient electric organs of the world, converting whatever feeds them — geothermal heat, buried reactors, no surface evidence says — into syrup, wafer, vapor, light, and the precise replacement of every calorie the animals burn.

No one tends it. There are no doors leading down, or none that have ever been found by the animals, who do not look. Whether it was built as a farm, an ark, a mercy, a museum, or a machine that simply optimized its way into this stillness, the deck does not say. The newness of every surface suggests either that the builders just left, or that leaving and staying have become the same thing here — that the world reached its final configuration and the builders dissolved into it, or sealed themselves under it, or were never separate from it at all.

What you feel, standing shin-deep in blood-warm water among ten thousand feeding shells under the white lattice sky, is not eeriness exactly. It is the sensation of a sentence that has reached its period. The world has finished happening. Everything that remains is maintenance — warm, wet, crowded, fed, and quiet — repeated without error, forever, at the scale of a continent that will still smell faintly of clean metal and animal breath when every other place has worn away.
