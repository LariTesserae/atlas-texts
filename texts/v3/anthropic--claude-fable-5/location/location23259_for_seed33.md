# The Terraces of the Unbuilt City

It begins where the air ends — or nearly ends, because something thin still moves up here, dry as paper, cold as the inside of a bell. You arrive at the city the way you arrive at a treeline in reverse: below a certain altitude there is world, ordinary and continuous, and above it there is *this*, and no one has ever marked the exact contour where the change happens. Climbers report that the last honest stone — stone that stays where it is, that means only itself — lies somewhere around the cloud floor. Above that, the mountain becomes municipal.

## Terrain

The city occupies the summit massif of a range so high that the peaks below it look like the floor of an ocean. It is not perched on the mountain; it is what the mountain is made of, at this height. Where a lower mountain would have couloirs, this one has avenues. Where there would be bergschrunds, there are colonnades — long, weathered, their pillars worn to the soft asymmetry of sea stacks, except no sea ever came here and no water either. The dryness is total and ancient. Frost exists, but only as a kind of mineral pollen: a grey-white dust that forms on north-facing thresholds and never melts, never wets anything, and eventually just becomes more stone.

The streets run for miles. That is the thing visitors find hardest to convey — the *extent*. This is not a citadel or a ruin-cluster but a settlement of regional scale, district after district stepping down the high cols and back up the next dome of summit, plazas the size of dry lakebeds, terraced quarters stacked eleven and twelve deep against the leeward faces. Stand on any high gallery at dusk-light (and the light here is always dusk-light) and the city extends past every horizon you can identify, which is complicated, because the horizons themselves are not entirely fixed.

## Material and Making

No tool ever touched this place. That is not a guess; it is legible in the stone itself. There are no chisel marks, no jointing, no mortar, no seams where one block meets another, because there are no blocks. Each tower is a single grain of rock. Each stair is a folded stratum. The city was not built — it is what happens to this mineral at this altitude, the way frost-flowers are what happens to certain soils on certain nights. Geologists who came up (few have, fewer twice) describe it as *architectural metamorphism*: rock that, under sufficient height and thinness of air and pressure of something nobody has named, recrystallizes into doorways.

And it is old at being this. That matters. The city is not freshly strange. Its lintels are rounded like river cobbles. Its grand stairs have the sagged, bellied profile of stairs walked for ten thousand years, though nothing walks them. Its towers lean, and have leaned so long they have grown buttresses of their own substance to lean on, the way an old tree calluses around its own weight. Whatever broke here — in the stone, in the air, in the rules — broke long ago, and everything since has been weathering, settling, the slow business of a wound becoming a landscape.

## Light

The sun does not behave above the cloud floor. The light is dim — not dark, but *underexposed*, as though the day were happening at a slight distance from itself. Shadows exist but are not strictly attached: a colonnade casts its shade a few degrees off true, and the shade of a tower can sometimes be found two streets away from the tower, lying across a plaza like a thing left behind. The shadows migrate. This is one of the city's weather systems. Over the course of what passes for a day, the great pooled darknesses move through the districts — slowly, grazing — and the dim ambient light follows behind them like grass recovering. Nothing about this seems violent. It seems *seasonal*. It seems like something the city worked out a long time ago.

There is no glare anywhere, ever. The stone is the colour of unlit pewter, of slate seen through breath, and it does not reflect so much as *remember* light, holding a faint pallor in its surfaces for hours after the brightness has gone elsewhere.

## Sound

Almost none. The air is too thin to carry much, and the city is too empty to make much. What sound there is arrives small and very clear: a single grain of frost-dust ticking down a forty-metre wall; the deep, sub-audible *settling* note the larger towers make once or twice a day, felt in the teeth more than heard; and — rarely — a kind of soft displaced echo, a footstep-sound in an adjacent street, which long observation suggests is not an echo of anything, or is an echo of something that has not happened yet, or happened so long ago that the city is only now finishing the sound. Travellers learn not to investigate these. There is never anything there, and the *there* is sometimes not there either.

## Geometry, and the Failure of It

Here is what is broken, stated as plainly as it can be: **the city does not have one shape.** It has a settled *range* of shapes, the way a coastline has a tidal range.

A street walked east for an hour may be unwalked back in ten minutes. A plaza visible from four different galleries will be a different size from each. There is a famous district — famous among the dozen people who have written about this place — where every doorway on the street opens into the same single room, a round chamber with one window; you enter from the north side of the street and the window faces south, you enter from the south and the window faces south, and the room is never occupied except by your own frost-dimmed footprints, which are sometimes already there when you arrive.

Distances breathe. Over a day, the city seems to inhale — districts drawing closer, the far quarters compressing toward visibility — and then exhale, everything sliding apart again, avenues elongating, the second horizon (there are often two) lifting away from the first. None of this is fast and none of it is hostile. It has the rhythm of weather, of metabolism. The breakdown reached its angle of repose ages ago. Reality here is like a scree slope: profoundly failed, and perfectly stable in its failure, and you can walk on it if you walk the way scree asks to be walked — diagonally, lightly, without insisting.

## Process

For all its emptiness, the city is never still. This is the paradox of the place: near-void density, almost nothing per volume, vast hollow quarters with nothing in them — and yet everything in slow motion all the time. The shadow-weather migrates. The frost-dust accretes and sublimes and accretes elsewhere. Whole terraces creep — measured at perhaps a hand-span per year — exchanging positions over centuries, so that maps made by one generation of visitors gently contradict the next, and both are right. The towers' lean rotates, like the gnomon of an enormously patient sundial. In the deep cols between districts, the air itself does something cyclical: thickening at certain hours into a visible grain, like the static of an old image, then clearing. The locals call this — there are no locals; the *literature* calls this — the city's pulse. Once you have spent a few days at altitude you can feel the whole massif keeping a kind of time, many processes interlocked, none of them alive, all of them in motion: an ecosystem with no organisms, only behaviours.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, but not nothing.

There is a grey moth-like thing, palm-sized, seen perhaps once a week, which does not fly so much as relocate — present on this wall, then present on that one. It feeds, if it feeds, on the frost-dust. It casts a correct shadow, which makes it, in some sense, the most ordinary object in the city, and visitors report feeling absurdly comforted by it.

There is something larger that uses the lower terraces. It has been seen only at the dim end of the light: pale, four-limbed or six- (accounts disagree, and given the local geometry, both accounts may have been accurate at the time), moving along the colonnades with the unhurried gait of a thing that knows the streets do not stay still and has stopped minding. It leaves prints in the frost-dust that are sometimes prints and sometimes small, perfect doorway-shapes, a few centimetres tall, that erode over days like everything else here.

No plants. Not even lichen — though on certain south-leaning walls there are *stains* in the shape of lichen, mineral blooms, fawn and bottle-green, growing at geological speed, as if the stone is dreaming of vegetation it has only heard described.

## The Rule of the Place

If the city has a law, it is this: **persist gently.** Everything here that lasted, lasted by yielding — the towers by leaning, the streets by lengthening and shortening, the stone by giving up its edges. The single chamber behind all those doors endures because it does not insist on being in one place. The traveller who does well here is the one who adopts the local physics as etiquette: do not retrace your steps and expect the same street; do not stare at a tower long enough to make it commit to a height; do not call out, because the city will keep your voice and spend it later, somewhere, thinly, in a quarter you will never find.

And at the end of the long dim day — when the shadow-weather has pooled in the eastern districts and the frost-dust glows its faint grey-white and the massif gives its once-daily settling groan, felt through the soles of the feet like a word too low to hear — the whole city exhales again, the avenues lengthening toward both horizons, vast, hollow, weathered, busy with its slow invisible work: a metropolis at the top of the world that no one built, that nothing inhabits, that never stops happening.
