# The Poured Country

You arrive at the edge of it the way you arrive at the edge of a table: there is a lip, a finished border, a place where the world has been deliberately ended. Beyond the lip the land runs out flat and low to a horizon that has the faint sheen of glaze, and everything between you and that horizon — every pool, every tree, every bird — has been made by hand.

Not built *over* something. There is no underneath. Dig with your fingers into the warm ochre ground and you find more ground: terracotta, fired hard, then beneath it terracotta still soft, still drying, the fingerprints of whoever pressed it into place not yet faded. The whole country is one continuous ceramic, kiln-warm from below, and the heat that rises off it through the day is the heat of a thing recently fired. Nothing here has weathered. The edges of the irrigation channels are knife-sharp where a blade trimmed the wet clay. Walk along them and you can see the tool marks, fresh as this morning, though there has been no morning. The light comes from everywhere at once — a total, sourceless brightness, like the inside of an overcast noon with the overcast removed. Nothing casts a shadow. A few things have shadows *painted on*, in thin grey slip, all leaning the same direction, as if the makers remembered there should be shadows and put them where they thought they'd fall.

## The Water

Water sits everywhere in this country, and none of it arrived as rain. It was *poured*. You can tell because each pool still carries the shape of its pouring — a frozen spiral of disturbance at the center, ripples standing in rings that never finish expanding. The ripples are real water. Touch one and your finger comes away wet, and the ring closes over the gap and resumes its position. The water remembers where it was put.

Shallow marshes spread across the low ground in big irregular plates, each one shin-deep, each one floored with the same warm terracotta, so that wading through them is like wading through a vast washed dish. The water is blood-warm. It never evaporates, despite the heat that should be lifting it off in sheets; instead the air above each pool hangs heavy and beaded, and condensation gathers on every surface and runs in slow threads back down into the pools it came from — a closed loop, a circulation with no losses, water breathing in place. Some of the channels carry a current, a steady silent glide in one direction, and if you follow a channel far enough you find it returns to where it began. The current goes around forever. Nothing feeds it. It was set moving once and has not been given permission to stop.

At intervals across the marsh-plain stand large clay vessels, taller than a person, tilted on plinths, pouring. The ribbon of water falling from each one is glass-smooth and makes almost no sound — a faint continuous *hush*, the loudest thing in the landscape. The vessels never empty. Look down their throats and the water inside is at the same level it has always been, trembling slightly, like something holding its breath.

## The Vegetation

The country is green — lavishly, wetly green — and every leaf of it is craftwork.

The trees are sparse, standing in loose groves with hundreds of paces of open glazed ground and water between them, each grove like a thought the makers had and then stepped back from. Their trunks are coiled basketry: long green withies plaited around a core, the weave visible, the over-and-under of it climbing in patient spirals. But the withies are *succulent*. Cut one and it bleeds clear sap. The trees are woven and the trees are alive, and there is no point at which one fact ends and the other begins. Their canopies are dense thatch-work of broad leaves, each leaf slightly too regular, the veins laid in like inlay, and the canopies drip — constantly, slowly, condensation gathering at every leaf-tip and falling in single warm drops onto the clay below. Each drop lands in exactly the same spot. Under every tree there is one small dark circle of perpetual wetness, like a signature.

Between the groves: reedbeds, planted in patterns. From ground level they look natural enough — tall green reeds with seed-heads, swaying — except they sway without wind, all together, a slow synchronized lean and return, like a held breath in green. If you could rise above the plain you would see the reedbeds are laid out as enormous glyphs, sentences of vegetation written across the marsh in a script no one walks high enough to read. Moss grows in the channel-joints, thick and emerald, but it grows in deliberate bands, applied like grout. Vines climb the pouring-vessels in patterns too symmetrical for accident, flowering at every third node, the flowers always open, never opening.

Nothing here grows. Everything here is *grown* — finished, in full leaf, at the absolute apex of lushness, and held there. There are no seedlings, no dead leaves, no fruit fallen and rotting. There is fruit *on* the trees, heavy, glazed-looking, perfectly ripe, and it has been perfectly ripe for as long as there has been an it.

## The Animals

The fauna is abundant and almost entirely still.

Herons stand in the shallow pools — dozens of them, scattered wide, one or two to a pool. Their bodies are coiled clay, their legs are fire-hardened reed, their eyes are small dark beads pressed in with a thumb. They are alive. You know this because when you wade near one, its head turns — slowly, with a faint dry sound like a lid rotating on a jar — and it watches you pass, and when you have passed it turns back. They do not fish. There are fish, finger-length carp of painted slip, holding station in the channels, gills working, going nowhere against a current that goes nowhere. The herons and the fish exist in permanent regard of one another. The strike never comes. It is always about to.

Frogs of green-glazed earthenware sit on the channel-lips at intervals so regular you suspect a measuring cord was used. Their throats pulse. Once in a long while — you might wait an hour, you might wait a day, time is hard to keep here — one of them croaks: a single hollow note, like a thumb on a clay drum, and the note travels out across the flat water and no other frog answers, and the silence closes back over the country like the ripples closing over your finger.

There are larger things. In the open distances between groves you will sometimes see a wicker buffalo, woven life-size, standing hock-deep in a marsh-plate with its head down as if grazing, though its mouth never reaches the reeds. Warmth comes off its flanks. Insects — small fired beads on legs of split bamboo — walk the surfaces of the pools without breaking them, very slowly, in straight lines, like punctuation looking for its sentence. Overhead, far up in the sourceless brightness, birds hang. They do not circle. They *hang*, wings spread, fixed at their stations like things pinned to the inside of a bowl, and you can walk all day and they will keep their positions relative to the ground, which means either they are moving with you or the sky is.

## The Makers' Marks, and the Trouble With the World

This is where the country stops pretending.

The fingerprints are everywhere — that is normal enough, in a made place. But follow them. There are thumbprints pressed into the surface of the *water*, dimples in the pools that hold their shape, where someone smoothed the water the way you'd smooth slip on a coil pot. There are tool marks in the *air*: faint parallel striations you notice only at certain angles, as if the brightness itself were burnished, and in a few places the burnishing was uneven, and the air there has a visible *grain*, and looking through it the far groves bend slightly, like things seen through old glass.

In a few places the work is unfinished. You will come to a stretch of marsh where the water has been poured but not yet smoothed, and it stands in rough heaps, water with a texture like kneaded dough, holding peaks and fold-marks, perfectly wet, perfectly impossible. There is one grove, far out in the eastern distances, where the trees were woven but never *closed* — the withies splay open at the top like unfinished baskets, and through the gaps you can see that the trees are hollow, and the hollow inside them is not dark. It is the same total brightness as the sky, as if the trees were woven around pieces of the light.

And there is the seam. Somewhere out on the plain — it is hard to find twice — the country has a join in it, a long raised ridge of clay like the seam on a slip-cast figure, running straight from one horizon to the other. The landscape on either side of it matches almost perfectly. Almost. A channel approaches the seam and continues on the far side offset by a hand's width. A heron stands on one side and its painted shadow stands on the other. Step over the seam and the single frog-croak you heard an hour ago sounds again, exactly the same note, as if your crossing turned a page back.

No one is making the country anymore, or the making has reached its end, or — this is the feeling the place actually gives you, standing in the warm shallow water under the shadowless light — the making finished an instant ago and the makers set down their tools and stepped away, and the instant has simply declined to end. Everything is at the moment of completion. The slip is still damp in the deep places. The poured water still rings. The fruit is ripe, the frogs are about to sing, the herons are about to strike, the buffalo is about to graze, and the whole low green dripping country holds at the top of its breath, bright, warm, finished, and waiting for nothing, because there is nothing left for it to become.

You can stay as long as you like. Your footprints in the soft clay will be the only marks here that are new — and when you look back the way you came, you will see that they, too, have already been fired.
