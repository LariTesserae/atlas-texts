The first thing to understand is that there is no ground here. Not in the old sense. Dig anywhere — and people must once have dug, because the pits remain, half-flooded, their walls smoothed to a gloss — and you find only more of the made thing: poured stone under poured stone, slag under slag, conduit fossilized in matrix, the rusted ghost-grain of rebar running through everything like mineral veins. Whatever was here before the building has been built over so many times, for so long, that the construction itself has become the geology. It has had time to erode. That is the strangest part. This is a manufactured landscape old enough to have weathered down, the way mountains weather down — a worked surface settled into its final, lowest, most patient form.

So: a plain. Nearly flat, lying low, a regional flatness that runs out past the limits of what little seeing the darkness allows. The flatness is not perfect. It undulates the way ancient shields of rock undulate — long, exhausted swells, the worn-down roots of what were once structures. You can read them if you walk slowly. That ridge, knee-high, running straight for three kilometers before sinking: a wall, once, or the footing of one. Those circular depressions, regular as craters but too regular, now shallow basins skinned with black water: tank farms, ground down to their floors. Everything vertical has been planed away by time and rain and the slow chemical patience of water on concrete. What remains is the horizontal — the floor of the world, pitted and pooled.

It rains often, or has just rained, or is condensing toward rain. The air is cool, a few degrees above the temperature where breath would show, saturated and heavy, smelling of wet iron, wet ash, ozone, and something underneath like the inside of an old kettle. Water stands everywhere in thin sheets. It weeps from the low swells, beading out of hairline cracks in the eroded fabric as if the ground itself were sweating, and it runs — slowly, almost without gradient — toward the basins, toward the old pits, toward channels so ancient they look like riverbeds, though they were cast in forms by machines whose names did not survive. Where the water moves it is dark and faintly oily, carrying a sheen that catches what light there is and bends it into colors that don't have names in daylight. Where it stands still, it is black glass, and in calm hours the surface trembles in concentric rings without any wind, because of the sound.

The sound. You hear it before anything else; you hear it instead of almost everything else. The plain is loud — not loud like a storm, which rises and falls, but loud like an interior, like standing inside the body of something. Somewhere below, or distributed through the whole eroded mass of the made ground, engines are still running. They have been running long enough that their noise has worn its own channels into the world the way the water has. A deep continuous roar that you feel in the sternum and the wet of your eyes. Beneath it, slower, a cyclic pounding — four seconds, silence, four seconds — like a forge the size of a county, or a heart that has forgotten what it was pumping for. The pounding travels through the floor and up through the legs, and every standing pool jumps in unison with it: ten thousand black mirrors shivering at once, in time, across kilometers. Speech is impossible here. Thought is barely possible. After an hour the roar stops being sound and becomes a property of the air, like its coolness, like its damp.

There are vents. They are the most active features of the plain — irregular slots and grates and torn-open ducts, sparsely scattered, a few per kilometer, where the running interior breathes out. Some exhale steam in slow columns that the cool air flattens and drags sideways into ground-fog. Some exhale heat without moisture, and around those the standing water has retreated, leaving rings of dry pale crust. Some inhale, audibly, a sucking note inside the general roar, and the fog leans toward them. The plain has weather of its own making: the vented steam condenses, gathers, falls again as a fine local rain a few hundred meters downwind, runs back along the worn channels toward the basins, seeps down through the cracked fabric toward whatever boilers raised it — a complete hydrological cycle with no sky required. Though there is a sky. It is simply very far away and very dark, an overcast that never fully resolves, glowing faintly in two or three places near the horizon with a dull rust-orange that might be distant flare-stacks or might be the cloud remembering them.

Light, otherwise, is rationed by accident. A few electric points survive — this is the eeriest sparseness of all. Across the whole visible region perhaps a dozen lamps still burn, mounted on stubs of mast worn down like old teeth, each one a small hard star throwing a cone of drizzle-filled glow onto a circle of wet floor and nothing else. They are not arranged for anyone. Between them lie kilometers of dark in which the only light is borrowed: the sheen on moving water, the rust-glow of the horizon caught in a flooded pit, the brief blue flicker — once or twice an hour — of something arcing in a vent's throat, strobing the steam from inside.

Life has found the place, barely, and only the kinds of life that can stand the noise. There are no animals. Nothing with ears could live here; nothing with a nervous system tuned to vibration could distinguish a footstep, a wingbeat, a mate, a threat, from the everything-roar. The water is sterile of fish, the air of insects. What grows instead is deaf. Around the warm vents, in the cones of the surviving lamps, in the seep-lines where the ground weeps: moss, in long, sparse, separated colonies, black-green and dense as felt, each colony alone in its hundred meters of bare wet floor like an oasis. Lichen maps the lee sides of the worn ridges in grey-white continents. In two or three of the basins — you could walk a day and find no more — something larger has taken hold: pale, reedy growths, knee-high, rooted in the silt of disintegrated concrete, colorless from lightlessness, trembling perpetually in the pounding like everything else that stands in water here. They are the tallest living things on the plain. They may be the tallest things on the plain, full stop.

And the place is not finished. That is what becomes clear if you stay. The erosion is ancient but the activity is current: the engines below still cycle, the vents still breathe, the water still rises and falls through its closed loop, and very occasionally — felt more than seen, a shudder distinct from the regular pounding — some piece of the deep interior shifts, fails, or engages, and a new crack opens somewhere on the plain and begins, immediately, to weep. The landscape is alive in the way a wound is alive while scarring, or in the way an old machine is alive when no one has told it to stop. It has reached its final form and it continues anyway, roaring in the dark, raining on itself, growing its deaf moss in the lamplight, settling — by millimeters, by centuries — toward a flatness it will never quite achieve while something underneath still insists, four seconds, silence, four seconds, on going on.
