## The Shouting Plateau

The land here ended a long time ago and decided to stay that way.

You arrive — if anyone arrives — at the rim of a tableland so high that the sky has stopped being a ceiling and become a kind of surrounding substance. The air is thin enough that distances lie to you. A ridge that looks like an hour's walk is a day away. The light is enormous: not warm, not golden, but a hard mineral brightness that comes off the sun and then off the pale stone and then off the underside of the clouds far below, so that illumination arrives from every direction at once and shadows are shallow, tentative things, gathering only in cracks. You squint here permanently. The cold and the brightness operate independently of each other, which feels wrong at first — light like a hammer, air like well-water.

The plateau itself is old in the settled way, the way of things that finished becoming. The stone is a grey-gold quartzite worn into long whaleback swells, every edge rounded by an erosion that has essentially completed its work and now only maintains. Frost no longer finds much to split. The gravel lies where it has lain. Walking across it, you have the sense of moving over a surface that reached its final shape before anything had a name, and that your footprints are the most recent event in an extremely long time.

It is nearly empty. The swells of rock roll away to a horizon serrated with farther rims, other arms of the same high country — this is not a single summit but a whole upper region, valley-broad, a country of its own perched above the world — and across all that distance there is almost nothing standing. A boulder every few hundred meters, perched where a vanished glacier set it down. The vegetation amounts to a rumor: black crusts of lichen like spilled ink, cushion plants the size of a fist, moss in the seams where the dampness lives. Because dampness does live here, quietly. Clouds slide up over the rim most days and drag their bellies across the stone, and the rock weeps for an hour afterward — thin films of water tracing the same channels they have traced forever, collecting in shallow stone bowls, dozens of them, each holding a few cold liters under a skin of wind-shiver. These pools never grow and never quite vanish. You can drink from them. The water tastes like the inside of a bell.

And then there is the sound.

It is the first thing and the last thing about this place, and it never stops. The plateau stands in a wind that does not gust — it *runs*, one direction, one pressure, a permanent river of air pouring over the rim, and the rim is fluted. Ages of that same wind have carved the edge of the world here into kilometers of grooves, throats, and broken organ-pipe notches, and the air going through them roars. Not howls — roaring implies variation, and there is almost none. It is a sustained, body-filling, deafening chord, so loud that you feel it in your sternum before you understand it with your ears, so constant that within a day you stop being able to imagine silence. Speech is impossible within a kilometer of the rim. Thought becomes slow and physical. The few people who have ever come up here — and they have come, rarely, for a long time — communicated by hand and by touching foreheads together to talk skull to skull.

The roar is why the fauna is here, which is the second great surprise of the place: the emptiness is *crowded*. The fluted cliffs below the rim drop two thousand meters into cloud, and every ledge of that drop is white with birds. Vast colonies — petrel-like things, swift dark crescents, heavy pale gliders with three-meter wings — live their entire lives in the standing wind, hanging in it without a wingbeat, stacked in slow rotating columns that rise out of the cloud sea like living smoke. They nest in the roar. It hides them; nothing can hear an egg, a chick, a landing, a death. Their own calls are inaudible even to each other, and so they have gone silent as a species, communicating with posture and the angle of light on feathers. From the plateau top you see them constantly, streaming inland in flickering lines to dust-bathe in the gravel hollows, to drink at the stone bowls, to die in the open where small quick foxes — pale, big-eared, also wordless — patrol the swells. The land looks empty and is teeming; the life simply doesn't accumulate. It passes through, low and fast, between the roar and the brightness.

Signs of people are so faint they could be mistaken for accident. A cairn of five stones, lichen-welded into a single mass, beside one of the drinking bowls. Flakes of a glassy black stone that does not occur on the plateau, knapped and abandoned in a scatter near the rim, where someone once squatted making a cutting edge — perhaps to take eggs, perhaps to take feathers, perhaps a thousand years ago, perhaps last season. There is no way to tell. Nothing decays here at a readable speed. A single handprint in red ochre exists on the underside of a perched boulder, sheltered from the cloud-weep, the fingers spread, the pigment still vivid in the relentless light. It is the only mark in the whole region that insists on having been *meant*.

The faint strangeness of the place is this: the chord never changes pitch. Wind everywhere else rises and falls, but here the air over the rim seems locked, as if the whole atmosphere of the lower world were draining through these flutes at a fixed rate, eternally. Old travelers' accounts — third-hand, doubted — claim that the note is the same in every season, every year, every generation; that you could come back as an old man to the rim you visited as a child and the sound would meet you unchanged, the same vast syllable held without breath. The birds rotate in their columns, the clouds slide up and weep and withdraw, the foxes trot their rounds, and underneath all that motion nothing moves at all. The place is finished. It is loud the way a struck bell is loud, except the bell was struck once, long before anyone, and has simply never been allowed to stop ringing.

You leave by descending out of the sound, and the first thing you notice in the quiet lands below is that the quiet feels temporary — a held breath — and that somewhere above the clouds the plateau is still shouting, steadily, brightly, to no one, exactly as it always has.
