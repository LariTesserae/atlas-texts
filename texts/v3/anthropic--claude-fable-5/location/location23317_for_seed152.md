The way in is a fault in the hillside no wider than a doorway, screened by a fall of young ferns whose fronds are still unrolling — fiddleheads the color of pale jade, none of them more than a season old. You smell the place before you see it: wet mineral, warm limestone, the faint sweetness of guano and moss. Then the slot opens and you are inside.

It is a single chamber, roughly the size of a chapel, and it is being born.

The stone here has not been worn into shape; it is being *deposited* into shape. A warm spring weeps from a seam high in the back wall — not a flow, just a constant glassy film — and everywhere the water travels it leaves stone behind. Travertine. The walls are sheathed in it: smooth, faintly translucent shelves and aprons and half-formed curtains, ivory shading into apricot where iron has come through. Nothing has a hard edge. Nothing has been broken or weathered. Every surface is a surface in progress, rounded and slightly slick, like candle wax that has not finished pouring. If you press a thumb against the lowest rim of a terrace, it gives — barely — the consistency of old cheese. In a hundred years it will be rock. Right now it is a proposal for rock.

The floor steps down in shallow basins, each holding a few inches of warm water, each rimmed in soft new stone. Between the basins: open ground, bare, pale, almost nothing on it. The chamber is generous with emptiness. A frond here. A single dark heliconia-like plant there, leaning out of a crack, its leaves beaded with condensation. The lushness is real but it keeps to the margins — green crowds the entrance slot and the wet seam at the back, and leaves the middle of the room as open, dim, breathing space. The light is what filters through the fern-screened entrance and one narrow skylight crack overhead: a soft, brownish-gold dusk that never brightens and never quite fails. Day outside registers in here only as a change in the *temperature* of the dimness.

And on the walls — this is what stops you — there is a city.

Cave swiftlets and mud-daubing swallows have colonized the highest, driest aprons of travertine, and they have built. Hundreds of nests, but not chaotic: tiered, organized, set in rows along the natural shelves the depositing stone provides, like balconies on a cliff-face tenement. Mud nests below, saliva-and-feather cups above, older abandoned nests beneath the newer ones so that the colony has visible strata — its own archaeology, two or three generations deep, slowly being *absorbed*, because the mineral film creeps over the oldest nests and turns them to stone. The wall is eating its own architecture and the birds keep building on top. Districts, suburbs, ruins, all on one curved face of growing rock. No hand made any of it. There is not a worked edge or a tool mark anywhere in this place; the only construction is mud, spit, instinct, and time.

For all that life, the chamber is nearly silent. The swiftlets come and go through the entrance slot in ones and twos, and their wings make a sound like a page turning. The dominant voice of the room is water: a slow irregular *tik... tik* from the seam, a deeper occasional *bloop* as a drop strikes a full basin. The warm air hardly moves. Sound seems to be deposited here too, laid down in thin layers, never allowed to accumulate.

But quiet is not the same as still. Watch the floor of a basin and small olive fish — washed in as eggs, maybe, or descended from some flood — rise to sip the surface and drop again. A freshwater crab the color of the apricot stone sidles along a rim with absurd patience. Bats hang in the darkest fold of the ceiling, a loose sparse constellation rather than a crowd, and every so often one detaches and resews itself elsewhere. Beetles work the guano fall below the nest-city; pale crickets work the beetles. And once, if you stay long enough, you will see the chamber's one real terror: a slender cave racer, a snake the color of weak tea, pouring itself up the travertine curtains toward the lowest tier of nests, and the colony's only loud moment — a brief dry roar of three hundred wings — before silence settles back like sediment.

Everything in here is mid-process. The stone is thickening at the rate of a fingernail. The basins are rising their own rims, and one day each will seal itself and a new pool will start above it. The oldest nests are halfway to fossil. The ferns at the door grow inward, the dimness deepens by a degree a decade, and the city on the wall keeps adding floors.

The place has one rule, and the rule is patience: nothing here is being taken away. Outside, the world erodes. In this warm, dripping, half-lit pocket, the world is still arriving — drop by drop, layer by layer, nest upon stone upon nest.
