# The Back of the Green

There is no ground here in the mineral sense. There hasn't been for a very long time, if there ever was. What you stand on — what everything stands on — is plant: a single continental organism, or a fusion of organisms so old and so interpenetrated that the distinction stopped mattering, whose upper surface forms a plateau roughly the size of a small continent, lifted a mile and a half into hot air on trunks and buttress-roots that no one living has ever seen the bottom of.

From above, the place is deceptive in its openness. You might expect a world of total vegetation to be choked, jungled, clotted with green. It is not. The surface of the organism is a vast bare plain of living material — call it bark, though it behaves more like a hide — rolling away in low swells to a horizon that curves with the planet. The bark-plain is the color of old bronze gone green at the edges, plated in irregular polygons the size of fields, each plate slightly cupped, slightly tilted, so that walking across it is a long slow rhythm of rise and dip, rise and dip. The plates are warm underfoot. Always. The organism runs hot, metabolizing at a pace that keeps the surface at the temperature of sun-struck stone even at night, even at this altitude where the air alone would be merely warm. Heat shimmer stands over the plain in the afternoons. Distant features waver and double.

The emptiness is the point. The organism does not crowd its own surface. Growth here is *expenditure*, and the great body spends deliberately: a single tower-frond every few miles, rising two or three hundred feet, unfurling slowly over decades like a fern shot in unbearably slow motion; a ridge of breathing-vanes here; a field of seed-cones there, each cone the size of a house, ticking faintly in the heat. Between these scattered presences: miles of open bark, wind, light, and noise.

## Water

Rain comes seldom and arrives as event. The plateau makes its own weather — it is large enough that air masses form over it, stall over it, are wrung out by it — and once every few weeks a band of cloud will drag a curtain of hot rain across some province of the surface. The bark-plates respond within minutes. Their cupped centers seal and hold, so that for a day or two after rain, the plain becomes a field of thousands of shallow warm mirrors, each one a temporary pond, each one immediately and violently colonized — but more on the animals shortly.

Between rains, the water is subtler. The organism sweats. In the hour before dawn, condensation beads along the seams between plates, and the seams run like thin black creeks for a little while, draining down through pores into the body below. There are also the *sap-eyes*: openings the width of a wagon wheel, scattered one to every few square miles, where clear fluid wells up from the interior, faintly sweet, faintly warm, never quite still. Everything that lives on the surface knows where the nearest sap-eye is. Everything organizes itself around them. The land is dry the way a sleeping animal is dry — moisture just under the surface, present everywhere, available almost nowhere.

## The Sound

You should understand before anything else that this place is *loud*, and that the loudness is the medium in which all other experience occurs.

Part of it is the organism itself. Air moves constantly through its breathing-vanes — slatted vertical growths, fifty feet tall, arranged in long ridgelines — and the vanes produce a continuous chord, an organ-note pitched low enough to feel in the sternum, that changes key over hours as the body adjusts its respiration to the heat. Locals can tell time by the chord. They can tell weather by it. Three days before rain, it drops a third and acquires a beat-frequency throb, and everyone begins moving their belongings out of the plate-cups.

The rest of the sound is animal, and it does not stop. The bark-plain hosts a fauna so dense in number — though spread thin across the vastness — that the sky and the surface are never silent and rarely even quiet. The dominant sound-makers are the *plate-shrills*: hand-sized creatures, part insect and part something else, that live in the seams between plates in colonies of millions and stridulate in synchronized provinces. A province of shrills covers perhaps forty square miles and screams as one — a wall of sound that rises and falls on a roughly twenty-minute cycle, like surf the size of a county. Where two provinces border each other, their cycles interfere, producing zones of beating, pulsing dissonance that some animals exploit for cover and others avoid entirely. Travelers crossing the plain do not navigate by landmarks, which are few, but by sound: they walk the interference-lines between shrill provinces the way sailors run between currents.

Over and through this come the migrations. The plateau's surface, being warm, sweet-watered, and nearly predator-proof at altitude, is a flyway for everything with wings on this part of the world, and *everything with wings* is a great deal. Two or three times a day, a front of birds — or things filling the niche of birds: leathery, four-winged, voiced like brass — passes overhead in flocks that take an hour to cross the sky and darken the bark beneath them as they go. They descend on the rain-mirrors and the sap-eyes in shrieking thousands, strip them, and move on. After rain, when the plate-cups hold water, the descent is total: the plain for fifty miles becomes a single carpet of feeding bodies, and the sound of it can be heard, faintly, from provinces away, as a kind of hiss laid over the shrill-chorus and the organ-note of the vanes.

People who live here lose certain frequencies of hearing by middle age and gain, in compensation, an extraordinary sensitivity to *changes* — to the moment the shrills falter, which means a predator-swarm is passing through the seams; to the sudden hole in the noise that means a tower-frond is about to drop a dead frond-blade the size of a ship's sail, which falls slowly, hissing, and which you do not want to be under.

## The Established Order

Nothing here is new. The plateau is old in the way river deltas and limestone caves are old — it has reached its shape and now merely maintains it. The bark-plates are worn glassy along the walking-routes that animals and people have used for thousands of years; the trails are visible from a distance as bright threads across the duller plain, polished by generations of feet and bellies. The sap-eyes are rimmed with concentric rings of mineral and dried sugar, accreted layer on layer, that record centuries of seasonal flow like tree-rings turned inside out. The tower-fronds bear the scars of every lightning strike they've taken — and they take all the lightning there is, every storm, deliberately, conducting it down into the interior for purposes nobody on the surface understands — and the scars have healed into spiraling glassy seams that catch the morning light.

The organism sheds, slowly. Dead plates are not replaced from beneath so much as *absorbed*: over a decade or two, a dying plate softens, sinks, and is drawn down, leaving a shallow basin that the surrounding plates gradually close over. The plain is dimpled everywhere with these healed places, like an old hide marked by old wounds. Some of the basins, mid-absorption, are soft enough to be dangerous — the locals call them *swallows* and mark them with cairns of dried seed-husk, some of which markers are themselves centuries old, husks gone hard as ceramic.

## The Scattered Living

People are here. Not many — the plain could not be said to be settled, only *inhabited*, the way an ocean is inhabited by ships. They live in small bands, rarely more than thirty, each band keeping a circuit between three or four sap-eyes, moving with the shrill-cycles and the rains.

Their structures are modest and ingenious. They do not build *on* the organism so much as *with* it: a dwelling here is typically a dead seed-cone, hollowed by hand-tools of fire-hardened frond-rib and shrill-jaw, its husk thick enough to mute the noise outside to a bearable drone. The cones are not moved — they weigh tons — so each band maintains several along its circuit, inheriting and re-inheriting them, and a cone-house in continuous use for forty generations will have its interior walls carved smooth and dark with handling, etched with route-maps and sound-calendars in a notation of cut dots and curves.

Their tools are frond-rib, husk, bone, sap-resin, and a soft native copper they pick out of the lightning-scars on the tower-fronds, where the strikes have somehow concentrated the metal in beads along the glassy seams. They hammer it cold into hooks, awls, and the small chimes they hang at the mouths of their cones — not for music, exactly, but as alarms: the chimes are tuned to ring sympathetically at the frequency of an approaching flock-front, and they sound an hour before the sky darkens.

The bands' greatest craft is *tapping*. With long hollow needles of frond-rib they draw sap from minor pores — never the great eyes, which belong, by a law older than any band's memory, to the animals — and reduce it over slow fires into a dense amber food that keeps indefinitely. The smoke of a sap-fire is sweet and is the smell most associated with humanity here: you can walk for three days and meet no one, and then catch that smell on the wind from over the swells, and know.

## What Is Strange

Some things about the place resist easy accounting.

The shrill provinces, for one, are not random. Mapped over generations — and the bands have mapped them, in dot-and-curve on cone walls — they form a stable pattern across the whole plateau, and the pattern resembles nothing so much as a diagram of the organism's own circulation, as inferred from the placement of sap-eyes and vanes. The shrills sing where the body flows. Whether the body grew its flows to suit the singers, or the singers settled along the flows, or whether — as the old carvings suggest — the screaming *is* the circulation, the great body moving its fluids by standing waves of sound the way smaller plants move theirs by capillary pull, no one can say. But it is observed that when a shrill province dies (it happens; plagues move through them every few centuries), the bark beneath it sickens within a year, and that the bands therefore treat the deafening chorus not as noise but as health, and worry only when it stops.

For another: the rain-mirrors, in the day or two they last, sometimes show the wrong sky. Travelers crouching to drink have reported looking into a plate-cup and seeing cloud formations not present overhead — and the bands' calendars record, matter-of-factly, that the mirrors show the sky *as it will be*, a few days out, and that this is simply one of the ways the body shares what it knows with whatever drinks from it. A traveler reads tomorrow's storm in a puddle, thanks the ground, and adjusts the route. It is not a wonder here. It is a chore, like checking the wind.

And at the very edges — months of walking from anywhere — the plain begins to tilt, and the bark-plates grow small and tight, and the organ-note of the vanes fades behind you, and you come at last to the rim, where the body curves over and down into a mile and a half of hot rising haze. Standing there, locals say, you can hear, very faintly, under the wind, a second chorus rising from below: the sound of whatever lives on the underside, in the forest of trunks, in the dark the great body stands on. No band has ever gone down to listen closer. The carvings do not forbid it. They simply do not mention it, the way a map does not mention the back of its own paper.

## A Day

Dawn: the seams run with condensation, black threads on bronze, and the small seam-life comes up to drink in a rustling that is, by local standards, near silence. The vane-chord is at its lowest, slowest pitch.

Midmorning: heat shimmer rises; the shrill provinces hit full voice; the plain becomes one enormous standing roar, and conversation between humans moves to hand-sign, as it does for most of every day.

Afternoon: a flock-front. The copper chimes ring an hour ahead; the sky darkens province-wide; the descent on the southern sap-eye is total, shrieking, and over in forty minutes. The plain afterward is littered with dropped feathers and husks, and the gleaners — humans among them — move out along the polished trails.

Evening: the vane-chord drops a third. Rain in three days, then. The bands begin, without hurry, because nothing here has hurried for ten thousand years, to move their fires and bedding out of the cups of the plates, to higher seams, and to wait for the warm rain and the thousand mirrors and the brief loud carpet of wings — for the plain to do, once again, exactly what it has always done.
