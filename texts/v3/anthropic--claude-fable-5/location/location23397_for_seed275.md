# The Understory Works

You descend through a bore in dry ground, and the first thing you notice is that the air smells like the inside of a violin — old wood, resin, faint dust — and that the smell does not change for the entire half-mile down.

There is no water here. That is the founding fact of the place, the thing everything else has been arranged around. No drips, no seep-stains, no mineral glisten on the walls. The shaft you descend through is not stone at all but cured root-mass, a single helical taproot wide as a train tunnel, its interior hollowed and its walls hardened to something between cork and ceramic. Your footsteps make almost no sound on it. Dry wood swallows sound the way wet stone never could.

Then the shaft opens, and you are in the cavern, and the cavern is a city, and the city is one plant.

---

## The Cavity

The space is enormous — not a single chamber but a linked series of them, a valley system turned inside out and buried, each cavity the size of a town and connected to the next by passages you could march a parade through. The ceiling, where you can see it, hangs four or five hundred feet up: a vault of interlaced heartwood, ribbed like the underside of an enormous leaf, which is more or less what it is.

Light comes down in shafts. This is the strangest beauty of the place: the organism that hollowed this ground has also grown its own light-wells — vertical trunks, hollow and dead-straight, that run from the surface far above all the way down to the cavern floor. Their inner walls are lined with a silvery, mica-like tissue that bounces daylight downward with very little loss. Where each light-trunk meets the cavern, the day pours out in a wide cool column, the color of an overcast morning, and the floor beneath glows. Between the columns: a soft ambient dusk, never quite dark, the light scattering off pale wood in every direction. The whole city is lit the way a forest floor is lit, except the forest is the ceiling, the walls, the ground, and the buildings.

It is cool. Cellar-cool, root-cool, the steady fifty-some degrees of deep earth, and the dryness makes the cool feel clean rather than damp. You will not sweat here, and nothing will rot. Things in this place do not decay — they *season*.

---

## What Grows Here Is the City

There is no distinction, anywhere, between vegetation and architecture, because the settlement is being grown rather than built — and grown is the operative tense, because it is not finished. It may never be finished. You are walking through something embryonic at the scale of a county.

The structures stand far apart from one another, which gives the whole place an unexpected spaciousness, a steppe-like emptiness between presences. You cross two, three hundred yards of bare cured floor — smooth, pale, faintly grained, warm-toned under the light-columns — before reaching the next district, and the districts themselves are still *coming up*. You can read their ages:

The oldest quarters are fully formed: towers of laminated living wood, eight and ten stories, their walls grown in overlapping plates like beetle shell, their windows not cut but *cultivated* — the wood persuaded, generations of growth ago, to leave membrane-thin panes of translucent cellulose in the right places. These glow amber from within where the light-shafts strike them.

The middle districts are scaffolded in their own green bones. You can see the skeleton of a future hall: pale shoots thick as masts rising from the floor in a deliberate grid, their tips still soft, still pliable, bending toward one another overhead but not yet fused. Workers — you will get to the workers — wrap the junction points in fiber bandages soaked in resin, splinting the building into its intended shape. It will harden over a decade. The city has decades. The city thinks in decades the way you think in afternoons.

And at the far edges of each cavity, the newest districts are barely suggestions: rows of blunt nubs breaking the floor like teeth coming in, each one a future tower in its first season, no taller than your knee. Surveying stakes of bone-white wood mark out streets that will not exist for thirty years. Walking these edges feels like walking a tide flat with the tide a generation out.

There is no green here, almost none — that is the second strange fact. The organism is vast and vigorously alive, but it does its photosynthesis far overhead, on the surface, where its canopy reportedly covers the whole valley like a second weather. Down here everything is the colors of the inside of plants: ivory, fawn, honey, oxblood heartwood, the gray-lilac of certain barks. A living city the color of old paper.

## The Works

The industry is the part visitors disbelieve until they see it.

Spaced through the cavities, always set apart, always given room, stand the *engine-trunks* — and these are factories in the full sense, mechanized, productive, running day and night, except that no one built them. They were grown, or grew, or were negotiated into being; the distinction is unclear even to the people who tend them.

Each engine-trunk is a tower of muscle-wood a hundred feet across. Inside, the plant runs its hydraulics — not water, never water, but a thick pressurized resin moved through vessel-channels wide as pipes, driven by the slow contractile tissue of the trunk itself. The pressure is enormous and the people of the city have learned to tap it. Take-off shafts of polished hardwood run from the engine-trunks across the cavern floor on low trestles — rotating slowly, slowly, you could lay your hand on one — into the workshops, where gearing of grown lignum steps the rotation up into useful speed. Lathes turn. Presses descend. Looms clack — softly, everything here is soft — weaving structural fiber drawn directly from cultivated fringe-roots that hang from the workshop ceilings like beaded curtains, growing thread as fast as the looms can take it.

There is electricity, of a kind. Certain vessel-bundles in the engine-trunks carry charge — a side effect, the tenders say, of moving resin at pressure through narrow channels — and copper-leafed taps siphon it into a sparse grid. Filament lamps in seed-pod housings hang at the workshop doors and along the trestle-roads, burning a low orange, redundant under the light-columns, necessary in the dusk between them. At the substations, the charge gathers in banks of grown capacitor-galls, which tick faintly as they fill.

The whole industrial apparatus produces almost no noise. That is what unsettles newcomers most. A district of factories at full production, and the loudest sound is a kind of pervasive woody creaking — the engine-trunks flexing — under which you can hear the dry whisper of the looms, the slow knock of a press completing its stroke once a minute, and, everywhere and always, faint, the sound the city itself makes growing: a sporadic ticking and pinging from every direction, like a vast roof cooling, except it is the opposite — it is expansion, lignin setting, new wood declaring itself. People raised here can read the ticks. They can tell you which district had a good night.

## The Tenders and the Stock

Who works the looms? Some are people — there is a population here, a few thousand, scattered thin across the cavities, living in the seasoned quarters, dressed in the city's own fiber. They are quiet by culture; the acoustics raised them.

But most of the labor moving through the dusk is not human. The city keeps fauna the way other cities keep pigeons and machines, except deliberately:

**Splice-beetles**, glossy, the size of dinner plates, patrol the growing scaffolds in loose herds. Their mandibles secrete the same grafting resin the human bandagers use; where two shoots are meant to fuse, the beetles cluster and work the joint for days, and the joint takes better than any splint. They were not engineered. They were *found*, in the early cavities, already doing this, and the city's architecture has been bent ever since toward shapes the beetles approve of.

**Loft-bats** — pale, broad-winged, nearly silent — pour out of the light-trunks at intervals, carrying spore-loads and small parcels between districts. The post here is mammalian. You can send a letter by holding it up at a roost-pole; something will take it.

**Floor-grazers**, low slate-colored animals like a cross between tapir and pangolin, move alone across the empty quarters between districts, rasping up the fine cellulose dust that drifts down from the ceiling-works. They are the reason the floors stay clean. They are also, locals will mention with studied casualness, the reason you do not sleep in the open quarters, though no one will say exactly what the grazers do at the dark end of their range, in the cavities not yet lit, where the new nubs are coming up and the city is still deciding what it intends to be.

Predators exist. Something keeps the beetle herds moving; something thins the bats. You hear it more than see it — a longer interval of silence, a shadow crossing two light-columns at speed — and the tenders speak of it without alarm, the way farmers speak of weather. The ecosystem is in motion around the industry, through it, indifferent to the distinction.

## The Rules

There are rules, learned rather than legislated:

You do not bring water down. Not a canteen, not a damp coat. The city is cured against rot by its dryness, and moisture here behaves like fire elsewhere — a wet patch on the floor will be ringed with warning stakes within the hour, and the wood around it will pucker and gray for years. Visitors drink from wax-sealed gourds of the city's own pressing: a clear resin-water, faintly sweet, the organism's gift or its toll, depending on who explains it.

You do not cut green wood. Seasoned wood is material; green wood is the city's body, and the city notices. Districts where green wood was cut grow *wrong* afterward — slowly, in ways that take a decade to manifest, doors narrowing, floors developing grain that all runs toward the site of the wound.

And you do not light fires, obviously, for the obvious reason: the entire settlement, every tower, trestle, loom, and lamp-housing, is fuel. A city of tinder, lit by piped daylight, electrified at low amperage, cool, calm, and ticking — every soul in it living inside the one catastrophe it has organized itself entirely to prevent. The quiet, you come to realize, is partly this: a whole population in the habit of carefulness, walking softly through the body of the thing that houses them, while above their heads and beneath their feet and far out past the lit edges of the lit cavities, the city goes on slowly, patiently, becoming.
