**The Lowland Works**

You arrive at it from above, descending, because there is no other way — the land slopes down for miles into a basin where the sky's last grey light gives out, and somewhere in that descent the ground stops being ground. There is a seam. On one side: raw earth. On the other: courses of stacked stone, mortared with river clay, fitted by hand. Past the seam, nothing is natural. Not the floor, not the walls, not the wells, not the dark.

The basin is filled — for as far as sound can travel, which here is very far — with construction. Not a city. A city implies streets, dwellings, plazas, intent legible to a visitor. This is more like masonry as weather: walls in every direction, walls at every angle, walls so close together you must often turn sideways to pass between them, their tops lost overhead where they merge into corbelled ceilings, which are themselves the floors of further works above. The whole basin has been roofed, floored, partitioned, re-partitioned. Density approaching saturation. In places two walls stand a hand's width apart, the gap serving no purpose anyone could name, both faces finished with equal care.

And everything is new. That is the strangeness that takes longest to absorb. There is no weathering anywhere. Every block shows fresh tool marks — the crescent bite of a stone adze, the drag-lines of fingers smoothing wet clay into a joint. Mortar is still soft in many seams; press a thumb in and it yields and holds the print. Some walls are warm with the heat of curing. Some are only half-raised, their top courses open like an unfinished sentence, scaffolded with lashed poles and rope of twisted fiber whose origin is unclear, since nothing grows here and never has. The basin is not old construction surviving. It is construction *happening*, everywhere, all at once, and it has not yet finished becoming what it is.

The light is almost nothing. Far apart — sometimes a quarter-hour's walking apart — there are small clay bowls set in wall niches, holding embers banked in ash. Not flames. Embers. They throw a sphere of dull orange perhaps an arm-span wide and then surrender to the black. Between them you navigate by hand and ear. The walls themselves help: they are deliberately textured, and the textures change. Ribbed clay means a passage continues level. Pocked stone means stairs. A band of polished slip at shoulder height, smooth as a wet egg, means *water near* — and it is always right.

Because the dark is damp. Cool, low, and damp. The roofed ceilings sweat; condensation gathers along the corbel-lines and falls in slow irregular drops, each one finding clay or stone and announcing itself. Channels no wider than a finger have been cut along the bases of the walls — millions of cubits of them, hand-grooved — and they carry the gathered drip downhill through the labyrinth toward cisterns: round, plastered chambers where the water sits black and breathing. Sometimes a passage floor holds a skin of moisture and your footsteps change pitch. Sometimes, distantly, actual rain finds an unroofed section and you hear it land on stone a long way off, like applause in another room.

The sound is the true population. The basin is *loud* — astonishingly loud for a place so dark — and the loudness is work. From every direction, at every distance, layered until it becomes a kind of granular roar: the knock of mauls dressing stone, the wet slap of clay being thrown into forms, the grind of blocks dragged on rollers, the rhythmic *hssk hssk* of cord saws, the clatter of debris swept into voids, the long groaning settle of a new wall taking its own weight. There are rhythms inside it. Hammering in one quarter falls into time with hammering in another, holds the beat for a while, drifts apart. Occasionally everything in a wide radius stops at once — a silence so total your ears ring — and then resumes on a different rhythm, as if a decision was made.

You almost never see the builders.

That is the rule the place keeps most strictly. The work surrounds you, fresh mortar gleams in ember-light, a rope is still swinging, a tool lies warm on a half-laid course — but the makers withdraw ahead of you like the dark itself. Once in a long while, at the edge of an ember's glow, you catch the suggestion of them: low, pale, broad-handed shapes, moving in single file with hods of wet clay on their backs, unhurried, incurious. They do not look at you. It is not clear they have anything to look with. Their tools are stone, bone, fiber, fired clay — nothing more. With these they have built a roofed country.

Other lives are rarer still. Blind, soft-bodied things graze the condensation films on the cistern walls, leaving clean spiral tracks in the mineral sheen. A long ribbon of something — segmented, faintly luminous at the joints, the only native light in the basin — sometimes pours itself along a drainage groove and is gone. There are no plants. There never were. Whatever the rope is twisted from, whatever the builders eat, the answer is not green.

The deepest strangeness reveals itself slowly, the way the basin reveals everything: the works are not converging on a finished form. Walk long enough and you find walls being unbuilt with the same care they were built — blocks lifted out, mortar pared away, the stone carried off to be laid elsewhere. Whole quarters dissolve and recrystallize. The labyrinth migrates. A passage you marked with a scratch may, days later, be a solid mass; a solid mass may have become a vaulted hall, dripping, fresh, smelling of wet clay and ember-smoke. Erosion has been forbidden entry here — wind and time get no purchase — but the basin erodes and renews *itself*, by hand, perpetually, like a thing keeping its own body warm.

Stand still in the middle of it. Cool air moving downhill across your face, carrying clay-smell and water-smell and the faint animal musk of the unseen. Darkness in every direction, ribbed and pocked and polished under your fingers. Drips falling into their channels. And the great layered hammering going on and on around you — near, far, above, below — the sound of a world that is entirely made, and not yet done being made, and possibly never intends to be.
