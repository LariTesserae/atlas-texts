**The Concretion**

Begin with how small it is. You could cup it in two hands — a hollow stone, roughly the size of a sleeping cat, lying on its side in cold black sediment at a depth where the ocean has long since stopped pretending to be blue. The stone itself is not finished. It is a concretion still in the act of becoming rock: mineral-rich water seeping through its pores, laying down rind after rind of pale grey stone the way a pearl is laid down, so slowly that the inside and the outside of the stone are different ages. The outside is older. The inside is being born.

And the inside is a city.

Not a metaphorical one. Enter through the fracture in the stone's flank — a crack the width of a finger, fringed with mineral lace — and the hollow opens into a chamber perhaps the size of a closed fist, and that chamber is *built*. Built the way coral builds, the way termites build, the way bone builds: without a single tool, without a single idea, and yet with unmistakable urban logic. Generations of small architects have been at work here, and their work is layered, contradictory, dense, magnificent.

**The architecture.** The oldest district lines the chamber floor: a foundation of calcified worm-tubes, laid down so long ago that the worms themselves are gone and the tubes have fused into something like a paved undercity, a catacomb of vacant streets each no wider than a grain of rice. Newer construction climbs the walls — spiral towers secreted by snail-things that build upward in tight coils, each tower hosting its builder at the summit and squatters in the abandoned lower whorls. Bryozoan colonies spread between the towers like tenement blocks, thousands of identical chambered cells in honeycomb sheets, each cell holding one animal smaller than a poppy seed. There are bridges: mineral threads spun wet and hardened, spanning the chamber's interior like laundry lines between buildings. There are plazas: flat shelf-fungus growths of stony algae where grazers congregate. There is even something like a cathedral — at the chamber's apex, a single enormous (relative term: thumbnail-sized) vaulted dome built by no one knows what, ribbed and translucent, empty, avoided.

The city has no plan and yet it has zoning. The current that breathes through the entrance crack — slow, cold, freighted with falling organic snow from the dead world above — deposits its richest food near the opening, and so the entrance district is crowded, fast-growing, ruthlessly competitive, structures built atop structures still occupied. Deeper in, where the current weakens, the architecture grows sparse and strange and old.

**The light.** Dim, but not dark. The mineral rind of the concretion is thin enough in places that the faint grey of the deep water outside comes through as a bruised glow — the city's permanent overcast, its idea of sky. And scattered through the districts, certain residents carry their own light: a low colony of soft polyps along the western wall pulses a faint cold green, slow as breathing, and small swimming things glint with pinpoint blue as they pass. It is never bright. It is the light of a room at four in the morning, just enough to know that something is moving.

**The vegetation.** Sparse, and precious. True plants cannot live here; what passes for greenery is a scattered crop of crusting algae and pale filamentous tufts that survive on the trace light leaking through the thin places in the stone. These tufts are the city's gardens, and they are contested. Grazing trails radiate from each one. Some of the snail-towers have been built — deliberately, it seems, though "deliberately" is a strong word for an animal with a nerve ring instead of a brain — directly beneath the brightest leak in the ceiling, where the gardens grow best. Real estate.

**The fauna.** Abundant past counting. The chamber holds, at any moment, several thousand residents: the tube-worms with their feathered crowns unfurling and snapping shut; the bryozoan multitudes; translucent shrimp-things that patrol the bridges; flatworms sliding over the plazas like spilled silk; blind white isopods working the catacombs of the dead district; a single small octopus-like creature, the city's apex resident, who lives coiled in the abandoned cathedral dome after all — that is why it is avoided — and emerges in long slow intervals to take a citizen, almost gently, and return. The population is layered three and four deep in places, animal on animal on architecture on animal, near-touching, a saturation of small lives.

**The sound.** This is the strangest thing, given the crowding: the city is nearly silent. Cold water carries sound well, but the residents make almost none. The loudest event in the chamber is the *tick* of a snail-tower adding a new increment of shell — a sound below hearing, more felt than heard, a tension in the water. The current entering the crack makes no whistle; it is too slow. Sometimes a fragment of construction breaks loose and settles to the catacomb floor with a sound like a single grain of sand placed on glass. The hunting of the dome-dweller is conducted in total silence. The whole metropolis goes about its dense, furious, layered business in the hush of a library, in the hush of a held breath, in the hush of the bottom of the sea.

**The strangeness.** It accumulates as you watch. The city is being slowly *entombed by its own foundation* — the concretion is still growing, and the mineral rind thickens inward, year by year, so that the chamber is very gradually shrinking. The oldest districts are not merely abandoned; they are being absorbed, swallowed back into the stone, tubes and towers fossilizing in place with their architecture intact. In a few centuries the crack will seal, the light-leaks will close, and the whole city — towers, bridges, cathedral, the soft green pulse on the western wall — will be a solid stone with a metropolis suspended inside it like a thought sealed in a skull. The residents seem, in some pre-thought way, to know this. Construction trends inward, away from the thickening walls. The newest buildings crowd the chamber's shrinking center, taller and more crowded each generation, a city racing its own stone sky downward.

And outside, in the black sediment, in the cold, there are other concretions. Dozens of them, scattered across the seafloor like dropped fruit. Some are young and cracked and lit faintly from within. Some are sealed and silent, finished, their cities complete and permanent and dark. Each one a fist-sized world that built itself, peopled itself, and closed.

The water moves through the crack. The towers tick taller. Nobody hurries, and nothing stops.
