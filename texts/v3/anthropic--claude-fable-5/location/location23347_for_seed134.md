# The Caldera at Spillway Peak

You reach it by climbing, which is strange for a drowned place. The mountain rose fast — geologically yesterday — and the crater at its summit punched open, filled with snowmelt and rain and the exhalations of the rock itself, and never drained. Now there is a lake at the top of the world, no wider than a cathedral nave, and everything that matters about it is underneath.

The water is cold without being killing-cold — the deep rock still remembers its birth and breathes a faint warmth upward, so the basin holds at the temperature of a cellar, a root-store, a hand pressed to a north-facing wall. At this altitude the sun comes down hard and unfiltered, and the water is so new it has had no time to grow murky. Light pours through the full depth of the caldera like light through a glass of cold tea, gold-green, reaching all the way to the floor thirty meters down. There are no shadows here that aren't sharp.

The walls are obsidian and fresh basalt, and they have not been forgiven yet. No rounding, no patience of waves — the eruption left edges like broken bottles, flow-folds frozen mid-motion, columns split along faces so flat they mirror. Nothing green grows on them. Nothing green grows anywhere. Not moss, not algae-felt, not the soft furring that water usually brings. The lake skipped plants entirely, the way some islands skip mammals. What colonized instead was animal, and the animals came up the flooded fissures from the deep rock like workers arriving at a shift that never ends.

The caldera is *full*. That is the first shock after the light. In a space the size of a small church there are thousands of bodies, layered, stacked, racked along the walls in tiers. Reef-density without reef — because there is no coral here either, nothing that builds outward into architecture. Nothing builds at all. Nothing is *made*. And yet —

Listen.

The sound carries up through the surface even before you enter the water: a deep, ringing, multiplied clangor, like a foundry heard through a wall. Underneath, it is enormous. The dominant voices belong to the smiths — that's the only honest word — fist-sized mollusks armored in plates of native iron, which they do not construct but *grow*, precipitating the metal directly out of the mineral-saturated water through some galvanic trick in their mantles. They anchor in colonies of hundreds, and they ring. Each one flexes its plates against the rock in slow percussion — once every few minutes, no faster — but there are so many of them, all out of phase, that the caldera tolls continuously, a bell choir with no conductor, iron on glass, glass answering iron. The obsidian walls are resonant. The whole basin is an instrument being played at industrial volume by creatures with no intention of music.

Between the smiths, in the gaps, packed nearly touching: the bellows-eels, blunt translucent animals that do nothing visible for an hour at a time and then contract their whole length in a single hydraulic stroke, jetting heated vent-water up through the cold layers with a *whump* you feel in your sternum. They are circulating the lake. They are, collectively, its pump. The thermal gradient they maintain is gentle, almost imperceptible — a degree here, a degree there — but it is why the basin doesn't freeze in winter and why the smiths' chemistry keeps running. None of them knows this. The factory has no foreman.

There are sparkers, too — small darting things like silverware come alive, which carry charge between the iron colonies and the copper-bearing seams in the east wall. Watch long enough and you'll see one complete a circuit: a blue-white thread of discharge crawling along a basalt column, slow as honey, bright as a welding arc, and where it passes the rock acquires a faint plated sheen. The east wall is slowly electroplating itself. In a thousand years it may be mirrored. The sparkers do this the way bees do flowers — incidentally, in the course of feeding on the potential difference itself.

And drifting through all of it, the glassmoths: flat, transparent grazers that rasp the obsidian and re-extrude it behind them in thin annealed ribbons, smoothing the young edges molecule by molecule. They are the erosion this place will eventually have. They are erosion with a metabolism. For now they have barely begun; the walls are still all knives.

Nothing here hurries. That is the second shock, after the noise — that so loud a place can be so slow. The tolling is constant but each toller strikes perhaps ten times an hour. The eels fire like minute-hands. The arcs crawl. The whole roaring assembly is running at the speed of seasons, a production line whose output is measured in millimeters of plated stone per decade, in degrees of warmth per winter, in the gradual softening of glass. Come back in a century and the caldera will be recognizably the same and measurably further along, like a cathedral mid-construction with no builders, only inhabitants.

At the surface, the high thin wind skims the water and carries the bell-sound down the mountain's flanks, where there is no one to hear it. Climbers who someday find this place will stand at the sharp rim, look down through thirty meters of sunstruck water at the layered, clanging, electrified congregation below, and reach for a word like *machinery*, and be wrong, and reach for a word like *wilderness*, and be wrong again, and eventually just sit down on the warm black glass and listen to the mountain ring.
