**The Lower Condensery**

You descend to it through a bore so new the rock has not finished deciding what it is. The walls of the shaft are young basalt, glassy in places, still sweating the chemistry of its own cooling — here a smear of yellow sulfur bloom, there a crust of white salts that wasn't there last season and will be thicker next. Nothing here has been worn down. Everything is still being deposited, accreted, extruded. The mountain above is old; this pocket inside it is an infant, and it has been colonized in its infancy.

The chamber itself is not large — call it the size of a church nave, a single vaulted room of dark stone perhaps sixty paces long, its ceiling lost in a haze of steam and dim orange lamplight. But within that one room, people have built vertically and densely, the way people do when there is nowhere else to go: three tiers of iron gallery bolted into the walls, catwalks crossing the void on chains, ladders, hoists, a freight cage that grinds up and down its greased rails on a cycle so slow you can watch the cable creep. Pipework runs everywhere — wrist-thick, thigh-thick, lagged in mineral-stained canvas — converging on the chamber's one great occupant: the condenser engine, a riveted black mass at the floor's center, big as a house, breathing.

It is a hot place. Not the dry heat of a desert but the wet heat of a body. The rock itself is warm to the touch — there is live heat not far below, and the floor in places will soften the soles of cheap boots. Water is everywhere without ever pooling for long: it beads on every pipe, runs in trembling threads down the glassy walls, drips from the gallery undersides in slow metronome patterns, hisses to nothing where it lands on the hot plates around the engine. The air is at the edge of saturation. Breathe in and you drink. The lamps — low-wattage filament bulbs in wire cages, strung at long intervals — each wear a halo of vapor, glowing dim amber spheres that illuminate almost nothing beyond themselves. Between lamps, the dark is thick and brown and full of movement.

Because the place is *full*. Not of people — the human crew is small, a dozen at a shift, scattered along the galleries with long intervals of emptiness between them. Full of animals.

The heat and the wet and the warmth of the rock have made this chamber the richest habitat for a mile in any direction, and life has poured into it through every fissure. The ceiling, when the steam thins enough to see it, is not stone-colored: it is furred, mobile, a continuous shifting carpet of roosting bats, thousands deep, their bodies steaming faintly, their chittering a constant high layer over everything. Below them, the walls glitter — not minerals but eyes, the eyes of cave crickets in their uncountable hundreds, pale and long-legged, stationed a hand-span apart across every warm surface like a sparse, living wallpaper. Blind white woodlice graze the mineral crusts. Snails the color of wet bone move across the pipes at a pace that matches the freight cage. The floor between the engine's footings seethes gently with beetles working the guano falls, and where the condensate channels run along the chamber's edge, small translucent shrimp ride the warm trickle in slow drifting shoals, and pale salamanders hang in the channels with only their feathered gills moving. Midges form standing columns in every lamp-halo, so that each light source appears to smoke.

The crew has long since stopped fighting any of it. Mesh hoods over the lamps, mesh over the engine intakes, and otherwise a truce. The bats keep the midges survivable. The beetles keep the floor passable. There is a standing rule — old, unwritten, absolute — that nothing is poisoned in the Condensery, because the one time it was tried, the die-off rotted in the heat and the chamber was unworkable for a month.

Vegetation is nearly absent, and what exists is accidental and strange: a few colonies of moss and threadbare fern around the oldest lamps, where decades of weak light have permitted a green stain the size of a spread hand. Each one is known individually to the crew. One, by the second-tier valve bank, is called the Garden. Workers eat their bread near it.

The sound is the place's true architecture. The engine sets the foundation — a deep, slow, four-part respiration, *intake, compression, discharge, settle*, a cycle every few seconds, felt in the sternum more than heard. Over it: the rolling static of the bat colony; the dripping in its hundred uncoordinated rhythms; the squeal of the cage rails twice an hour; the chirring of crickets that rises and falls in slow waves across the walls like wind through a field, though there is no wind and no field. It is loud, continuously, but it is a *layered* loud, and the crew can read it — they know the engine's health by the length of its exhale, know a roof-fall is coming when the bats go quiet in one quarter of the ceiling, know the deep water table is rising when the salamanders climb out of the channels.

Change here is real but glacial. The flowstone is slowly claiming the lowest pipework; a run of conduit abandoned thirty years ago is now half-entombed in pale stone drapery, and there is an understanding that one day the whole lower tier will be given up to the mountain in the same way, and a new tier built above. The mineral crusts thicken. The bat colony grows. The heat, the engineers say, is very slightly less every decade — the young rock is finishing its long cooling, and someday, generations out, the Condensery will go cold and dry, and everything in it, animal and engine alike, will have to leave together.

For now it breathes. Steam coils up through the dim galleries, the cage descends with the night shift, ten thousand small lives shift and chirr and graze across the warm new stone, and the engine, patient as the geology it sits inside, draws another long wet breath.
