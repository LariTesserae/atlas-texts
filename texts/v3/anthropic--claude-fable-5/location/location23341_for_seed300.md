**The Turning Ground**

It is a small place, and it knows its edges. A basin perhaps eighty meters across, ringed by a low berm of packed slag and rust-colored earth that someone built so long ago it has stopped being a structure and become a landform. You can walk the rim in ten minutes. The rim is the only still thing here.

Inside the berm, the ground moves.

Not violently — there is no eruption, no geyser, nothing that would make you step back. But nothing inside the ring holds its shape for longer than an hour. The floor of the basin is a grey-brown clay saturated to the consistency of cold porridge, and it turns itself over, continuously, the way a sleeper turns. A low mound swells on the eastern side over the course of a morning, develops a glossy crown, cracks, and subsides. A channel of standing water — pewter-colored, skinned with fine sediment — closes itself like a healing wound and reopens twenty meters away. Ridges migrate. Hollows trade places. If you set a stone on the surface and come back after lunch, the stone is gone, or it is somewhere else, or there are three stones now, because the ground has given some back.

The astonishing thing is how quietly all this happens. Saturated clay does not splash or roar. It seethes at a pitch below hearing. What you actually hear, standing on the berm in the flat, even light of an overcast cool day, is almost nothing: a slow exhalation as a mound deflates, a tick of water finding a new low point, the occasional soft *gluck* — singular, polite, unrepeated — of a bubble that took six hours to rise. The basin is the loudest silence you will ever stand beside. People who come here (few do) report that they begin whispering without deciding to.

The air sits close and damp. It is not raining, but it has always just rained, or is always about to. Moisture beads on every cold surface — and there are many cold surfaces, because the basin is full of iron.

This was a works, once. The evidence is everywhere and nowhere in particular: a pump house on the southern rim, brick gone soft-edged as soap, its roof a lattice of bare purlins furred with moss. A run of bolted pipe, thigh-thick, that enters the churning ground at one angle and emerges forty meters away at a slightly different one, as though the basin had chewed it thoughtfully and changed its mind. A flywheel the size of a cartwheel, standing half-proud of the clay this month; last season only its top arc showed, like a rising moon of pitted iron. The ground swallows the machinery and regurgitates it on a schedule no one has mapped. Pressure gauges surface face-up, their glass crazed but intact, needles resting on numbers that no longer measure anything. A firebox door. A length of chain, every link socketed with grey clay like a filled tooth. The basin keeps its inventory in constant circulation, a slow silent commerce in its own ruins, and this is what gives the place its strange crowdedness — for somewhere so small and so quiet, it is dense, packed, *attended*. Everywhere you look, something is partway through arriving or departing. The eye never rests on emptiness.

One machine still runs. In the pump house, an electric motor — fed by a line that comes over the hills from somewhere, sagging between two surviving poles — turns a small auxiliary pump that was meant, presumably, to keep the basin drained. It hums at the threshold of audibility, a single sustained note like a struck glass that never decays. It pumps a thin steady thread of grey water up over the berm and into a ditch, and the basin, with perfect indifference, replaces every liter. The two of them have been at this stalemate for generations. The motor's hum is the only deliberate sound in the landscape, and the silence has long since absorbed it, the way a room absorbs a clock.

Vegetation keeps to the margins, and keeps thin. On the berm: a scatter of horsetails, segmented and ancient-looking, the right plant for the place. A few tussocks of bleached sedge. One birch, no taller than a person, growing out of the pump house gutter with the patience of something that has chosen badly and committed. Nothing grows on the turning ground itself — nothing can root in a floor that rolls over — though after a still week a faint green film of algae sometimes forms on the wet hollows, and the next turning folds it under like a baker folding dough, a thin verdant seam visible for a day in the fresh-cut face of a clay ridge before it too is digested.

Animals are rare and specific. A single heron works the rim some mornings, hunting the pale, eyeless, finger-length things that live in the slurry — annelids of some kind, or something that has converged on annelid — which surface during the turnings, briefly, gleaming, before the clay takes them back. The heron never steps inside the berm. It has its reasons. In the pump house roof, something small nests; you hear it shift, once or twice an hour, a dry rustle that counts here as an event.

The light is ordinary, and the ordinariness is part of the place's character: no glow, no gloom, just the even nickel-grey daylight of a cool damp lowland, in which everything is rendered honestly. The basin does not perform. Its strangeness is administrative, procedural — a matter of what it does, not how it looks. Photographed, it would seem inert: a muddy depression, some scrap iron, a brick shed. The motion is too slow for a glance and too total for a glance to miss, which is why the place is best understood by sitting on the berm for an afternoon with your collar up against the damp, watching the floor of the world rearrange itself without sound, without hurry, without ever once repeating an arrangement.

It has been doing this for a very long time. The berm's age says so; the digested pipework says so; the soap-worn brick says so. Whatever the works once pumped, or sank, or stirred awake down there, the basin has made its peace with it. This is not a wound in the landscape. It is a settled condition — turbulence that has been going on so long it has become a kind of stillness, the way a river's surface chaos is, from the bridge, just the river.

At dusk the condensation thickens, the flywheel weeps in slow streaks, the motor holds its single note, and somewhere in the dark interior of the basin a mound rises that no one will ever see, and falls, and the ground turns over, and turns over, and keeps its own counsel, quietly, all night.
