**The Holding Plateau**

The land came up fast and recently — you can tell by the edges. Nothing here has been rounded yet. The plateau stands at an altitude where the air thins and the cold settles in permanently, a tableland so wide it manufactures its own weather, and every ridge on it is still a blade. Stone the color of wet slate breaks in clean conchoidal fractures, faces sheer as snapped ceramic, and where two faces meet the line between them is sharp enough to read by touch. There has been no time for frost to chew these edges down. Or — and this becomes the more plausible explanation the longer you stay — frost has had all the time in the world, and is simply not permitted to.

Water is everywhere and nowhere standing. The plateau lives inside its own exhalation: a saturated atmosphere always a half-degree from condensing, so that the rock weeps continuously. Thin films slide down the cliff faces, beading on the sharp arrises, dripping from overhangs in slow metronome counts. Mist pools in the cut valleys at dawn and never fully burns off — the light is ordinary, gray-bright, the kind of overcast daylight that casts no shadows and tells no time. Shallow black tarns sit in the fracture basins, surfaces dimpled by drip and nothing else. No wind ripples them. The cold is real — your breath fogs, the wet stone aches to touch — but it never deepens into hard freeze. The temperature here was decided once, and holds.

Because of the water, the green. Vegetation on the plateau is lush in a way that has nothing to do with warmth: vast pelts of moss a meter deep, swallowing the talus, upholstering whole cliff faces in saturated emerald and bottle-green and a strange mineral teal. Liverworts sheet the weeping rocks. Cushion plants mound in the lee of every blade of stone, dense as upholstery. Stunted, contorted trees — something like a cross between juniper and tree fern, silver-barked, dripping — stand in open groves with hundreds of meters between them, each one fat with epiphytes. It is a rainforest pulled apart, a jungle administered at homeopathic dilution across a landmass the size of a continent. You can walk for a day through luxuriant growth and never once be unable to see ten kilometers in every direction.

And in all that green, nothing moves of its own will. No birds. No insects on the moss — you can put your face close to it and find it perfect, unchewed, untracked. No fish dimple the tarns. The plants are pollinated, if they are pollinated at all, by the drip and the mist. The silence this should produce does not exist, because the plateau is one of the loudest places imaginable.

The sound comes from the works.

Scattered across the tableland, one to a horizon, sometimes one to three horizons, stand the holding-towns: dense industrial settlements of riveted iron and poured stone, stacked six and eight stories, chimneys, flywheel housings the size of cathedrals, gantries, cable runs descending into shafts cut into the living rock. They are unmistakably cities — gridded streets, lit windows, layered infrastructure, switchyards strung with humming catenary — and they are unmistakably empty. The lights burn at a constant amber. The engines inside run at a constant speed. Doors stand at whatever angle they stood. There are no inhabitants, no remains of inhabitants, no evidence the towns were ever inhabited; they have the quality of organs rather than dwellings, as if a city is simply the shape this machinery takes, the way a heart is the shape pumping takes.

The noise they make carries across the whole plateau, because there is so little to absorb it and because the saturated air conducts it beautifully. It is enormous — a layered industrial roar, pistons and turbines and the deep sixty-cycle hum of electrification, the percussive chant of forges working on nothing, steam venting in fixed plumes that never vary in height. From fifty kilometers away it is a chord. From inside a holding-town it is a physical pressure on the chest. And it never changes. Not in pitch, not in rhythm, not in volume. Stand in one spot for an hour and the sound is identical at the end to the beginning; come back in a year and it is identical still. People who have spent time here report that the roar eventually stops registering as sound and starts registering as a property of the air, like its temperature, like its weight.

This is the strangeness at the center of the place, the thing that takes days to articulate: the plateau is young, and it is finished. It has the sharp edges of land that rose yesterday and the perfect stasis of land that will never see tomorrow. Water runs and erodes nothing. The moss is lush and never longer. The drips fall on schedule. The tarns neither rise nor fall. The towns burn fuel from no visible source and produce no visible product, and the slow understanding that settles on a visitor is that the product is *this* — the equilibrium itself. The engines are not in the landscape; the landscape is what the engines do. The plateau was lifted, sharp and green and weeping, into one chosen moment, and the works run at full continental roar to keep it there: every gradient cancelled, every decay repaid, time itself flattened out and held.

What the moment is being held *for*, nothing on the plateau says. The empty lit windows give no answer. You walk out across the wet green vastness, through the unchanging gray light, with the roar of distant industry steady as a planet's pulse, and the only thing that moves in all that world is you — and the place seems to be waiting, with infinite mechanical patience, in perfect condition, for whatever it was finished in time for.
