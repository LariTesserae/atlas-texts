**The Succession Valley, in the dark months**

The glacier left forty years ago and the land it abandoned has not stopped moving since. This is a low country — a broad trough between worn shoulders of rock, opening seaward through braided flats — and everything in it is new. The bedrock still carries the glacier's signature: long parallel scratches, polished domes, boulders set down mid-stride and never collected. None of it has had time to soften. Edges here are edges. The moraine ridges stand at the exact angle of their collapse, gravel-sharp, weeping water from every seam.

It is the dark season. The sun has been below the horizon for nine weeks and will stay there for five more. What light exists is incidental: a gray-violet smear at the southern rim of the sky around what would be noon, the occasional cold flutter of aurora through cloud, and — closer, stranger — the faint sub-glow of the canopy itself, which is not luminous so much as *less dark*, a deep wet green that the eye learns to read the way it learns to read a room at night.

Because the valley is forested. That is the first impossible thing about it, and the only thing here that physics, as currently written, does not fully cover. Land this young, this cold, this far north should carry lichen, maybe a crust of moss, the first hesitant saxifrage. Instead the trough is packed wall to wall with vegetation in violent succession — a forest growing at roughly four hundred times the natural rate, dense to the point of struggle, trunks near-touching, canopy layered three and four deep. The trees are recognizable in outline — something like alder, something like willow, a conifer that has been argued about — but they were *written*, not evolved. Their cells run hot. Their sap moves under pressure. They do not photosynthesize in any meaningful way during the dark months; they don't need to. They draw from below.

Under the soil — under what is becoming soil — runs the feedstock lattice: a network laid down decades ago by machines no larger than pollen, which dismantled a measured fraction of the retreating ice and the rock flour beneath it and turned the proceeds into a circulating broth of carbon, nitrogen, bound energy. The lattice is invisible. You would know it only by its effects: ground that steams faintly at minus six, meltwater channels that stay open all winter, and the forest itself, drinking from the dark like a city drinking from a grid. There is nothing eerie about it. The people who built it considered it infrastructure, the way a culvert is infrastructure. The valley is simply a place where succession was given a head start of about nine thousand years.

The *sound* is the thing visitors are never ready for.

It comes from everywhere and does not stop. Closest is the forest itself: fast-grown wood is loud wood, and the trunks crack and report constantly as they lay down tissue, a fusillade of pops and long tearing groans, like a wooden ship in heavy weather, kilometers wide. Under that runs the water — and the water is everywhere, this being a thaw-land in permanent thaw. Hundreds of streams off the high ground, re-cutting their channels nightly through unconsolidated gravel; three serious rivers that braid and re-braid across the flats, jumping their beds whenever a debris flow comes down, which is often. At the valley head, the glacier's remnant — being deliberately, gradually unmade by the same lattice that feeds the forest — calves into a milk-gray lake with detonations you feel in your sternum a minute before the wave arrives. And over all of it, in the dark, the birds.

The fauna arrived faster than anyone modeled. Seabird colonies have taken the fresh cliffs at the valley mouth — kittiwake-descendants, mostly, plus an engineered petrel released two decades back that decided it preferred this coast — and their colonies do not sleep in the dark season because the dark season is all there is. Their racket is continuous, a high shrieking weather above the lower weather of water and wood. In the forest itself: voles in absurd density, an explosive population of crossbill-like seed-eaters working the dark canopy by smell, a fox lineage gone half-arboreal in a single human lifetime, owls grown bold and loud. The dark is crowded. Stand still for ten minutes and a dozen things will pass within arm's reach, none of them quiet, because nothing here can afford to be quiet — silence is inaudible against the valley's roar, so every animal has turned its volume up. Even the voles scream.

The built presences are sparse and easy to miss. A relay pylon every few kilometers, slim as a sapling, frost-furred, distinguishable from the trees mainly by being the only vertical thing not swaying. A seed-printer hut on the eastern moraine, hip-high, dome-roofed, that exhales a thread of warm vapor and occasionally a drifting cloud of next year's understory. Down on the flats, half-buried in fresh gravel, the wreck of the original survey station — abandoned not because anything went wrong but because a river decided to run through it, and here the rivers have right of way. There is one inhabited structure in the whole valley: a long low cabin on a rock knoll above the flood line, dark-windowed, where a rotating pair of stewards lives with the noise. Their job is mostly to watch and almost never to intervene. The lattice does not need tending. It needs witnessing, by treaty.

And the valley *changes* — that is its character, more than the cold, more than the dark. It is a landscape in storm-state even when the sky is calm, though the sky is rarely calm; weather systems pile in off the sea and break against the high ground, and the forest takes the wind the way a crowd takes a panic, a vast traveling thrash you can watch move up-valley by sound alone. But beneath the weather, the deeper turbulence: channels migrating, moraines slumping, a hillside of ten-year alder buried overnight by debris and the lattice already re-threading through the rubble, new shoots up within the week. Whole stands rise, choke each other, fall, and are eaten back into the broth. The stewards keep time-lapse records, and viewed at speed the valley does not look like geography at all. It looks like surf — green-black surf, breaking endlessly over new rock, in the dark, at the top of the world, deafening — a thing that will eventually, in a century or two, slow down, quiet down, and become merely a forest.

Not yet, though. For now it is all becoming, at full volume. Visitors describe the same moment: standing in the canopy-dark with rain freezing in their hair, the ground steaming faintly around their boots, the glacier booming at one end of the valley and forty thousand birds shrieking at the other, the wood cracking like rifle fire on every side — and realizing that nothing about it is strange. It is only fast. It is the most ordinary thing in the world, the colonization of bare ground by life, performed at a speed and pressure that turns ordinariness into thunder.
