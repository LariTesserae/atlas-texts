**The Caldera Sea**

The mountain is younger than the language anyone speaks about it. It came up out of the world's deep crust within living memory of the oldest trees, shouldering aside an older range whose bones still jut from its flanks like scaffolding left behind. It rose so fast, and so hot, that the rains of three hundred wet seasons could not run off it — they pooled. They pooled in the great unfinished bowl of its summit, two miles above the lowland haze, and what filled the bowl was not a lake. It is too large for that word, too saline, too warm, too alive. People who live below the cloudline call it the Caldera Sea, and they say it the way you say the name of a relative you have never met.

You arrive at it by climbing through cloud — hours of grey, the rock underfoot still sharp because nothing has had time to wear it round. The stone is glassy, ringing, full of bubbles, in places so fresh it is warm against the palm. There are no canyons here, no soft valleys; the mountain has not been edited yet. Every surface is a first draft. And then the climbing simply stops, and you are standing at a rim of black raw basalt, and below you, filling the horizon from one curved wall to the other, is a sea the temperature of blood, the color of weak tea steeped in green.

Almost everything that matters here is underneath.

---

The water is warm all the way down — heated from below by the same patience that raised the mountain — and it is thick with growth. The flooded inner slopes of the caldera are forested. Not kelp exactly, not trees exactly: tall rooted growths with trunks like braided rope and broad rust-green blades that hang slack in the still water and turn, very slowly, all together, when the deep currents shift. They grow in tiers down the drowned terraces, layer over layer, so dense in places that swimming through them is like pushing through hanging laundry in a dark room. Smaller things grow on the bigger things. Filaments grow on those. The water itself has a faint green grain to it, like light through a jar of olive oil.

Light comes from above as a dim, even glow — the cloud-roof over the caldera rarely breaks, so even noon arrives underwater as a kind of soft brown dusk. Below thirty feet, you navigate as much by warmth as by sight: currents of heat rising off the floor, cool threads sinking from the rim, a whole geography of temperature you learn with your skin.

It is quiet in the particular way of warm deep water. The loudest sound is geological: the rock of the young mountain ticking and groaning as it settles, a sound like a vast house cooling at night, arriving from no direction and every direction. Occasionally a long bass shudder passes through the water and through your chest, and the hanging forests sway once, all at the same moment, and go still. The people who live here do not remark on it. The mountain is still growing, they say. A hand's width a year. You build accordingly.

---

And they do build. The city is everywhere and easy to miss, because it is made of the same young stone as the caldera and grown over with the same rust-green blades. Hold still in the dim water and it resolves out of the slope: terraces, walls, ramps, hundreds of domed chambers stacked down the drowned hillside like wasps' nests rendered in masonry. There is no metal in any of it. The builders quarry the soft fresh pumice with stone mauls and fire-hardened wooden wedges, working it while it is still light enough to carry through water, fitting the blocks by hand and by feel, mortaring them with a paste of crushed shell and ash that cures slowly in the warmth. The work is unhurried by necessity — underwater, with hand tools, nothing happens fast — and unhurried by temperament. A wall begun by a grandmother is finished by a grandchild, and this is considered the correct speed for a wall.

The chambers hold air, some of them — pockets of breath trapped under stone domes, refreshed by long manual relays of inverted baskets carried down from the surface. Most of the city, though, is simply lived in wet: sleeping niches lined with woven blade-fiber, hearth-stones set over natural vents where the mountain's heat boils the water in a slow column of silver beads, gardens of cultivated growth tied to lattices of lashed driftwood. The streets are not streets. They are corridors of cleared water between the hanging forests, kept open by daily pruning, marked at intervals with pale carved posts that glow faintly — rubbed with a paste of crushed luminous weed, renewed each month, the only artificial light in the city. At night, from the rim above, the whole drowned town is visible as a sparse constellation under green glass.

Animals are scarce and treated as omens. There are slow translucent grazers the size of dogs that drift through the gardens and are never harmed. There is something large that lives in the deep center of the caldera, in the hot dark over the vent fields, known only from the long smooth furrows it leaves in the sediment and from a sound — once or twice a year, a single resonant note, felt more than heard, that makes every blade in every forest stand briefly out straight, as if in attention. The builders pause their work when it comes. Then they pick up their mauls again.

The strangest thing about the place reveals itself slowly: the sea is rising with the mountain, not draining from it. As the caldera floor lifts, the water lifts, and the rim lifts, and the cloud lifts to follow, so the whole sea is being carried upward through the atmosphere a hand's width a year, an ocean ascending on the back of newborn rock. The city builds upward too, always upward, abandoning its lowest and oldest chambers to the hot dark — and so the deep center of the Caldera Sea is floored not with sediment but with architecture, generations of it, the city's own discarded foundations, sinking relative to nothing, simply left behind by a town that climbs in place.

Nobody knows how high the mountain means to go. The masons have an answer, when asked, that is also a shrug: *we will know when the water boils, or when it freezes. Until then, the wall.*
