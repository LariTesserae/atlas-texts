**The Stilling Room**

You reach it by climbing — that much is certain, though no one agrees on how far. Somewhere above the cloud deck, embedded in the flank of a structure too large to be seen whole, there is a chamber roughly the size of a chapel. It is not marked on any schematic. The maintenance crews call it the Stilling Room, which is a joke, because nothing in it is still and nothing in it ever changes, and after a while those two facts turn out to be the same fact.

The room is machined. Not built — *machined*, the way the inside of an engine block is machined. The floor is a single milled plane of pale alloy, and the cutter marks are still in it, fine parallel arcs catching the light like the grain of frozen water. The walls rise in fluted columns of cast metal, and the casting flash has never been ground off; you can find the seam lines, sharp enough to part skin, where the molds met. Nothing here has been touched long enough to wear. The edges of everything are new. They will be new in a thousand years, because nothing in the room moves against anything else. The room was cut, assembled, sealed into the body of the machine, and then simply continued.

The cold is the cold of altitude and the cold of metal that has never held heat. There is no water anywhere — no condensation, no frost, no pipes. The air is desiccated by design, pulled through mineral beds somewhere below until it arrives with the dryness of a kiln, but cold, cold enough that your breath produces nothing visible, because there is nothing in your breath the air doesn't immediately take.

And the sound. You feel it before the last hatch opens — a pressure in the sternum, in the fillings of the teeth. Inside, it is total. The room sits directly in the throat of the great machine's exhalation: a chord, if you can call it that, a stacked roar of turbine fundamentals and their harmonics, every frequency at once, at a volume that makes hearing irrelevant. It does not rise. It does not fall. It has held the same shape for as long as anyone has records, the same shape to the decibel, to the hertz. Engineers have brought instruments up and the instruments show a flat line — a sound so perfectly invariant that the recordings are indistinguishable from a malfunction.

This is the strangeness of the place: a noise that never changes stops being noise. After twenty minutes the body recalibrates. The roar becomes the floor of the world. You begin to hear *into* it the way you'd stare into a dark room, and the silence underneath it — the absence of any other sound, any footstep, any drip, any wind, any voice — becomes the loudest thing present. People come down from the Stilling Room claiming it is the quietest place they have ever been. Their ears bleed slightly. They are not lying.

The light is ordinary, and that is its own small wrongness. High in one wall, a band of thick fused panes admits actual daylight — flat, even, slightly blue with altitude — and it falls across the milled floor exactly the way daylight falls across any floor. There are also work lamps in cages, lit, though no one comes to work. Everything is plainly visible. Nothing is hidden in shadow. The room offers itself completely and explains nothing.

Spaced across the floor, far apart, with great emptiness between them, stand the trays.

There are perhaps eleven of them. Low rectangular basins of the same pale alloy, each holding a bed of crushed slag — black, glassy, sharp-grained, a mineral with no history of weather. In each tray grows a plant. Not the same plant: a cushion of something like moss campion in one; a single contorted juniper, no taller than a hand, in another; in a third, a gray lichen colonizing the slag in slow rosettes, which should be impossible, since lichen wants moisture and there is none. No one waters them. No mechanism waters them. They are alive. The leading theory — held without enthusiasm — is that they drink the sound. The cushion plants do tremble, very slightly, at the resonant frequency of the room, every leaf a tuned reed, and where the vibration is strongest the growth is densest. Whether this is metabolism or merely physics, no one has stayed long enough to determine.

Nothing else lives here. No insect has ever been found, no spider, no mite in the slag. The sound may simply be uninhabitable for anything with a nervous system smaller than regret. The plants do not seem to mind. The plants seem, if anything, *settled*, the way old furniture is settled — eleven green presences in a metal room, evenly spaced, vibrating faintly, in daylight, at the heart of a roar.

Stand in the center long enough and the equilibrium of the place starts to feel less like stability and more like attention. The chord holds. The light holds. The cold holds. The edges stay sharp. The room is a sustained note in the body of something enormous, and the suspicion that climbers carry back down with them — quietly, because it sounds foolish at lower altitudes — is that the note is being *held on purpose*. That somewhere the machine is keeping this one small chamber exactly as it is, the way a singer holds a tone past the point of breath, and that the plants are there for the same reason anything is placed in a held room:

so that something is listening.
