**The Loom Shelf**

Begin with the altitude. You feel it before you see anything: the air thin as paper, cold in a clean, dry way that has nothing to do with frost — there is no frost here, no dew, no breath-fog, because there is almost no water for the cold to work with. The cold is purely mineral. It comes off the rock the way a knife comes off a whetstone.

The place itself is small. A shelf high on the shoulder of a mountain, a natural amphitheater perhaps two hundred paces across — you could walk its rim in fifteen minutes if the rim let you. The walls are old, old stone, rounded the way only enormous time rounds things: no sharp edges anywhere, every face of the basalt worn into the soft geometry of a thing that has stopped arguing with the wind. The bowl is bounded. You can see all of it at once. This is important, because what happens inside it could not be survived at any larger scale.

**The vegetation.** Lush is the right word and the wrong one. The rim of the bowl is densely overgrown — fronds, canes, broad parasol leaves, hanging curtains of filament, a whole rainforest's grammar of growth — but none of it is wet life. Touch a leaf and it is dry as a moth's wing, warm-cool like worked stone, faintly translucent. The flora here is *spun*, not grown: long-chain mineral fibers, glassy cellulose, structures the dry air can hold. It drinks nothing. It eats static charge and ultrafine dust and the strange surplus of the place's machinery. The colors are real plant colors — deep greens, copper-greens, a bruised violet in the shade layers — but if you hold a leaf to the sun the green resolves into thousands of hair-fine conductive veins, like circuitry drawn by something that had only ever heard botany described.

And here is the first strangeness of proportion: the lushness clings entirely to the rim, a thick living hedge ten or fifteen meters deep, and the bowl's interior is nearly *empty*. A floor of pale, swept gravel. Vast open air. Three or four solitary plant-forms standing far apart in all that space like the last guests at the end of a party — a single tree-thing here, a clump of glass reeds there, fifty meters of nothing between them. The emptiness is not absence. It is *clearance*. The center of the bowl is kept open the way a forge is kept open.

**The structures.** Scattered through the bowl, a dozen artifacts, none larger than a small house. A ring of pillars on the western side, each about shoulder-height, made of a grey alloy that does not behave correctly: it casts a shadow slightly offset from where the shadow ought to fall, as if the pillar the light interacts with stands a hand's-width away from the pillar your eyes see. A half-buried object like a seed pod the size of a cart, its surface covered in weathered relief — not writing, more like the pattern frost would leave if frost existed here. A few low walls of fitted dry stone, clearly far younger than the alloy things, clearly built by some later visitor who wanted shelter and got perhaps a season's use before giving up. The old machines are *established* — settled into the ground, ringed by centuries of undisturbed gravel — and they are also, unmistakably, still running.

**The sound.** You will have noticed it long before reading this far, because the sound arrives a full half-day's climb below the shelf and only grows. It is *deafening*. Not the deafening of an engine — the deafening of weather, of a million small percussive events overlapping into a wall. Up on the rim, with the bowl below you, it resolves: the entire interior of the shelf is in a state of continuous making and unmaking, and *that is what you hear*.

Watch the empty floor. A patch of gravel begins to shiver. A plant assembles out of it — genuinely, visibly, over the course of perhaps forty seconds — fibers leaping upward, joining, branching, leafing out, the whole structure rising like time-lapse made literal, and the sound of it is a roar of dry crackling, ten thousand splinters of glass knitting at once. The plant stands. It may stand for a minute, an hour, a day; some on the rim have stood long enough to weather. Then, with no warning the eye can find, it *unravels* — fibers releasing in cascading sequence, the whole organism falling back into dust and pale gravel with a sound like an avalanche of dropped chandeliers, like applause from an audience the size of a city, like hail on an iron roof scaled up past all sense. There are always twenty, thirty, fifty of these events running at once somewhere in the bowl. The amphitheater walls — and you suspect this is not an accident — focus and fold the noise, so that the center of the shelf is a place where you cannot hear your own voice, cannot hear your own thoughts, can feel the sound in your sternum and your molars.

The rim hedge, the lush established border, appears to be the *survivors*: forms the process built and then, for whatever reason, declined to take back. They are the long-term winners of a lottery being drawn continuously below them. Some of them are demonstrably ancient — wind-polished, dust-scarred, their fibers gone milky with age. They grew in a furnace of revision and were allowed to keep.

**The fauna.** Abundant, and entirely at home in the chaos. Small swift things like cliff swallows — though their wings are the same dry-fiber material as the leaves — hunt through the bowl in their hundreds, riding the pressure waves of the unravelings, snapping airborne filaments out of the collapse-clouds before the dust settles. They eat the unmaking. Lower down, six-legged gleaners the size of hares work the gravel between events, utterly unbothered, sometimes standing inside the footprint of a plant as it assembles around them and stepping calmly out through the rising lattice. There is a heavy, slow, armored thing — you will see two or three of them at any time — that walks the open floor like a monk in a hailstorm and appears to graze directly on the half-formed: it positions itself where assembly is beginning and eats the structure faster than it can rise, and the machinery, with what you can only call patience, builds elsewhere. The animals are loud-adapted; they signal each other with flashes of their pale undersides, with posture, with scent. Nothing here has bothered to evolve a voice. There would be no point.

**The light** is honest mountain light — high, white-gold, clean — and it does the place no favors and no harm. It shows you everything plainly. The strangeness here does not hide in gloom; it stands in full sun, casting its slightly wrong shadows, roaring.

**What the place is**, as far as can be guessed: a workshop that outlived its workers, or never had any. The alloy artifacts are the fixed points — the loom-pins — and the bowl is the loom, and the spun flora is the cloth, woven and torn out and rewoven without rest, generation after generation per hour, for what the weathering of the rim suggests has been thousands of years. Whether it is searching for a form it has not yet found, or whether the searching *is* the form, no surviving evidence says. The later dry-stone walls suggest someone once stayed to ask. The walls are short, and abandoned, and being slowly considered by the gravel.

You can stand on the rim, in the established hedge, among the chosen plants, with the swallows pouring past your head, and look down into a small bounded emptiness that is the loudest place you will ever know — a cold, dry, high green room where the world tries itself out, over and over, at the volume of a storm that never learned how to end.
