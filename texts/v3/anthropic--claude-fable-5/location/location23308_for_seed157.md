## The Hollow of the Sleepers

High on the shoulder of the mountain — above the last of the wind-bent pines, where the clouds drag their bellies across the rock — there is a sinkhole no wider than a chapel. You could walk past it in the fog and never know. The ground simply opens: a round mouth in the karst, rimmed with moss so deep your hand sinks in to the wrist, and below it a hollow perhaps thirty paces across and not much deeper, holding its darkness the way a cupped palm holds water.

It is always wet here. Not raining — the rain happens elsewhere, up in the cloud — but the hollow drinks the cloud directly. Every surface weeps. The walls are old limestone, dissolved over uncountable centuries into soft, settled shapes: flowstone like poured cream long since slowed to stillness, ribs and curtains of mineral with all their edges gone. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing here is new. Water beads on the stone, gathers, slides, and falls — and the falling is the only clock the place keeps. One drip from the overhang on the northern lip strikes a basin of black water below it at an interval so regular you could set a heartbeat by it. People who have sat in the hollow long enough swear the interval never changes. Not with the seasons, not with the weather. The drip was falling at that pace before you arrived and will fall at that pace after.

The light is almost nothing. What filters down through the mouth above is cloud-light already, grey and exhausted, and the moss eats most of it before it reaches the floor. Your eyes adjust slowly, and then they find the second light: a faint, scattered green-gold dusting along the undersides of the flowstone shelves — fungus gnat larvae, hundreds of them, each one a cold pinprick. They do not flicker. They do not move. They hang their silk lines in the still air, and the silk is beaded with condensation, so the whole ceiling glitters very dimly, like a sky seen through deep water.

And then your eyes adjust further, and you understand the hollow is *full*.

It is full the way a hive is full. Every ledge, every fold of stone, every pocket of moss is occupied. Salamanders — black, glistening, ribbed — lie layered on the weeping shelves three and four deep, motionless, their throats pulsing so slowly you must watch a long time to be sure they live. Snails the size of fists ride the walls in slow herds that do not visibly progress; their trails crosshatch the flowstone in patterns that look ancient, fixed, part of the rock. Bats hang from the under-curl of the lip above, packed shoulder to shoulder in a dense brown nap, not stirring even when you pass beneath them. Cave crickets stand on the moss in the hundreds, antennae arced and still. Pale crayfish hold position in the black basin, claws open, like figures arrested mid-gesture. In the deepest cleft, where no light at all reaches, something larger breathes — you hear it more than see it, a slow exchange of damp air, regular as the drip, and equally indifferent to you.

Nothing flees. Nothing startles. That is the unusual thing about the hollow, the thing that makes the back of the neck prickle: this much life, this densely packed, should be a riot of movement and noise, and instead it is a congregation. The animals are not asleep, exactly. Their eyes are open, where they have eyes. They are simply *finished moving*, as though the hollow long ago arrived at its final arrangement and every creature knows its place in it. When one salamander does shift — once an hour, perhaps — another shifts to fill the space it left, and the composition is restored, and the stillness closes back over the motion like water over a dropped stone.

The sound, accordingly, is almost nothing. The drip. The breathing in the cleft. A faint seething you eventually identify as condensation running through moss — water moving through a million tiny channels at once, which from the center of the hollow sounds like the place itself very quietly thinking. Your own footsteps are the loudest thing for miles, and the moss swallows even those.

It is cool but not cold: the temperature of stone that has not felt sun in a long age, the temperature of a root cellar, of held breath. The air is so saturated it has weight. Within minutes your hair is jeweled with it; within an hour your clothes hang heavy and your skin smells of mineral and moss.

There are no structures here. No one has ever built in the hollow, or wanted to. But near the basin, half-grown-over, there is one human thing: a hand axe of flaked chert, its edges blunted by the same patient water that softened the walls, lying where someone set it down an extremely long time ago. The moss has accepted it the way it accepts everything else. A snail rests on its dorsal face. Whoever carried it up the mountain came, and sat, and — you feel certain of this without knowing why — chose not to take it back out. It is the only evidence that the hollow has ever been visited, and it reads less like a relic than like an offering, or a surrender: one made thing, given over to the place that does not change.

You could stay. That is the quiet danger of the hollow. The drip keeps its time, the sleepers keep their places, the cold light of the larvae neither brightens nor dims, and after a while the part of you that measures hours begins to slip its gears. There is a ledge of moss at the right height for sitting, exactly one body wide, conspicuously unoccupied.

It is the only empty space in the hollow.

It seems to have been waiting a very long time.
