# The Lung

You reach it by going down, though "down" stops meaning much after the third turn of the passage — the air pressure does something to your inner ear, a slow rhythmic squeeze, and by the time the corridor opens out you have already been hearing the place for ten minutes: a roar that arrives through the rock before it arrives through the air.

The chamber is not large. That is the first surprise, after all that sound. Cathedral-sized at most — you could shout across it, if shouting were possible here — a rough ovoid of pale stone maybe sixty meters at its widest, its ceiling lost not in darkness but in *glare*. Because the second surprise is the light. Hundreds of meters below the surface, the chamber is bright the way an overcast noon is bright: shadowless, total, coming from everywhere. The rock itself carries it. Not glowing, exactly — the stone is a wet bone-white travertine, banded and bulbous, and light moves *through* it the way light moves through a fingernail pressed against a lamp. Somewhere behind the walls, something is luminous, and the whole chamber is its lampshade. Instruments brought down here disagree about the spectrum. Two devices side by side will report different colors. The eye reports white and refuses to elaborate.

The floor of the chamber is mostly water, and the water is never still.

This is the engine of the place, or the symptom of the engine — no one has settled which. The pool occupying the chamber's basin breathes. Every forty seconds or so (the interval drifts; it has moods) the water level drops a full two meters, drawn down into channels you cannot see, with a sound like a held chord on some enormous brass instrument — and then it returns, not gently, surging up through a dozen vents in the basin floor in columns of warm froth that collapse and slosh and collide. The collisions throw spray to the ceiling. The spray comes back down as rain. It is always raining in here, a fine blood-warm rain, falling through bright air onto bright stone, and the noise of it all — the surge, the chord, the hiss of falling water, the deep sub-audible thud of whatever drives the cycle — layers into something that is less a sound than a weather you stand inside. Visitors describe coming back up to the surface and finding ordinary silence physically itchy for days.

The warmth is constant, body-temperature, and the air is so saturated that the boundary between breathing and drinking feels negotiable. Mist condenses on everything, including you, within a minute of arrival. Drops form on your eyelashes. The walls weep continuously, every surface varnished, and the long work of all this water on all this stone is finished — that is what gives the chamber its strange composure underneath the chaos. Every edge has been rounded for so long that there are no edges. The travertine has grown over itself in smooth lobes and aprons, terraced rimstone pools descending to the basin like melted stairs, and the whole interior has the licked, settled quality of something that found its final shape ten thousand cycles ago and has simply been performing it ever since. The turbulence is not change. The turbulence is the steady state. The chamber is violent the way a heart is violent.

Life here is sparse and specific. In the spray shadow of the larger stone lobes — the few places the rain doesn't directly reach — grow colonies of a pale fern-like plant, silver-green, no taller than a hand, their fronds permanently beaded. They appear nowhere else, in this chamber or apparently anywhere. A film of golden algae rings the basin at exactly the high-water line, a bright belt that maps the pool's breathing like a tide mark drawn fresh forty times an hour. And there is one animal: a kind of blind, bone-colored amphibian, eel-bodied, half a meter long, of which you will see perhaps two or three on a given visit. They do something unsettling and beautiful — at the moment of each surge, they leap *into* the rising columns of water and ride them upward, mouths open, harvesting whatever the vents carry up from below, then slide back down the froth as it collapses. They have clearly been doing this longer than anyone has been watching. Their leaps are perfectly timed. They never miss.

The built things are few and old and outclassed. A stub of walkway, some corrosion-proof alloy now furred with travertine, extends four meters over the basin and stops — the rest of it dissolved or removed or never finished. A flight of seven steps is cut into one of the stone aprons, descending below even the low-water line, going somewhere that is always submerged. On the eastern wall, at head height, a bracket of dark material holds nothing, its mounting bolts driven into the rock with a precision that suggests it once held something worth holding precisely. And there are the *seams*: hairline lines in the stone, dead straight, kilometer-grade straight, running behind the translucent walls where the light comes from, visible only as faint shadows in the glow. They are too regular to be geology. They are the only honest glimpse of whatever this chamber actually is — heat exchanger, valve, instrument, organ — some component of a system whose other parts are elsewhere, deeper, and whose purpose has continued without interruption and without explanation, breathing water and shedding light, while the stone slowly grew around it like flesh around an old implant.

One more thing, which everyone notices and no one mentions first: the rain falls slightly wrong. Watch a single drop against the bright wall and it descends a fraction slower than it should, as though the chamber's depth, or its engine, has put a thumb on the scale of falling. It is a small wrongness. You adjust to it in minutes. It is only when you climb back toward the surface, into dimness and dryness and proper gravity and that itchy ordinary silence, that you feel the adjustment reverse — and feel, briefly and absurdly, homesick for a warm loud room of breathing water, deep in the bright inside of the world.
