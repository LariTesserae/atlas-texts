**The Green Table**

At a certain altitude the mountains stop being mountains. They have been worn so long — past peaks, past ridges, past anything you could call a slope — that what remains is a single vast tableland hanging in the upper air, so old it has settled into its final shape the way a sleeping animal settles into a hollow. The rock beneath is the oldest exposed stone anywhere; it shows itself only at the rim, where the plateau breaks off into cloud, banded grey and rose like the rings of a tree that stopped counting.

You will not see that rock anywhere else, because everything else is plant.

Not plants, plural — that suggests individuals, a population, gaps. There are no gaps. The entire surface of the table, an area you would need days to walk across, is one continuous cushion of vegetation: a moss-and-cushion-plant continent perhaps a meter and a half deep, dense as cork, ancient as the rock it digested to make itself. Walking on it is walking on a firm green mattress laid over the top of the world. It is not grass. It does not bend. It yields a few centimeters and pushes back, warm — measurably warm, blood-warm in places, because a living mass this size generates its own heat the way a compost heap does, the way a hive does. Mist rising off the plateau at dawn is partly cloud and partly the breath of the plant itself.

The surface is not flat, but its relief is the relief of flesh, not stone: long slow swells, dimples, seams where two ancient lobes of growth met centuries ago and fused. In the dimples, water collects. This is the only water — there are no streams up here, nothing to feed them; rain comes rarely and briefly, fat warm drops out of a passing tower of cloud, and the cushion drinks most of it instantly. What's left gathers in the hollows as pools the color of dark tea, rimmed with a paler, softer growth, each one a few strides across, scattered kilometers apart. Between rains, the plant condenses moisture from the night air; before sunrise the whole green country is silvered and dripping inward, swallowing its own dew. By midmorning it is dry to the touch and faintly hot.

What strikes you first, though — before the softness underfoot, before the warmth — is the noise.

The plateau is *deafening*. Not with wind; the wind up here is oddly gentle, the table so high it sits above most weather. The noise is animal, and it comes from everywhere at once, including from below.

The cushion is hollow-veined. Inside its meter and a half of living thickness run galleries, channels, vaulted chambers where the old growth died and the new growth roofed it over, and these galleries are inhabited beyond any reasonable carrying capacity. Insects, mostly — a fauna of round-bodied, flightless, drumming things that communicate by striking the resonant walls of their tunnels — so that the entire plateau functions as a sounding board, a green drumhead the size of a province, thrumming day and night with overlapping percussion. Press your ear to the surface and the sound is unbearable, a roar of millions of small strikes. Stand up and it becomes the ambient floor of the world: a deep ongoing thunder underfoot that you feel in your teeth before you identify it as sound.

Above that bass line, the air. Birds nest nowhere — there is nothing to nest *on* — so they nest *in*, boring tunnels into the cushion's flanks at the rim, and they rise off the edges in flocks so large they have weather: a flock crossing the sun drops the temperature; a flock landing makes a sound like surf. They feed on the drummers, hammering into the green skin with chisel bills, and the plant heals over the wounds within days, and the drummers reroute their galleries, and this argument has been going on for so long that the whole surface, looked at closely, is a palimpsest of healed scars — pale rings, puckers, old seams — the way an elephant's hide carries every year of its life.

And yet, for all this seething abundance, the plateau *feels* empty. That is its strangest quality. The life is in the substrate and at the edges; the open middle distance holds almost nothing. You can walk for an hour across the green swells and encounter no object taller than your knee, see clear to a horizon that curves, hear the drumming roar of an uncountable multitude and lay eyes on not one single animal. It is a crowded world experienced as a void — a vast warm humming emptiness under enormous light. The light up here is clean and unfiltered, ordinary in color but oddly weightless, the sky a darker blue than lowland sky, the clouds all *below* you, lapping at the rim like a white sea.

People come here. Not many; not often; not to stay.

Their marks are scattered across the table at long intervals — a cairn of rim-quartz here, hauled stone by stone from the edge, the only rock on a hundred square kilometers of plant; there, a ring of stakes cut from the woody core of the cushion itself, dried hard as horn, marking a pool deemed safe to drink. At the rim, anchored deep into the living mass with carved bone toggles, hang ladders of braided fiber descending into the cloud — the routes up from whatever lies below, maintained by hands, replaced strand by strand, generations old in design and weeks old in material, because nothing dead lasts long on the plateau before the cushion grows over it or the drummers carve it hollow.

The most substantial structures are the listening pits: hollows dug a body-length into the cushion, walls slick and green and slowly trying to close, where a person can sit below the wind with the full drumming of the plateau around them on every side. The people who cut these pits, whoever they are, read the drumming the way sailors read swell — its rhythms shift before rain, go ragged where the galleries flood, fall locally silent in a moving patch when something large is tunneling. Because something large does tunnel. Nobody has described it well. It is known by the silence it pushes ahead of itself, a hush the size of a field, gliding under the green at a walking pace, and by the long subsided furrows it leaves, which the cushion heals into smooth pale roads that wander the plateau pointing at nothing.

The visitors leave before dark, or sit in their pits through the night, because at night the drumming changes. The blood-warmth of the cushion rises against the cold air, the mist stands up off the surface in slow columns, the birds pour back over the rim in roaring rivers and vanish into their bored holes, and the rhythm underfoot consolidates — millions of separate percussionists drifting, over hours, into synchrony, until just before dawn the entire table beats together: one pulse, slow, enormous, felt through the soles of the feet and the bones of the chest, the green country counting something in the dark.

Then the light comes up over the cloud-sea, the dew runs inward, the rhythm shatters back into multitude, and the oldest place in the world begins, again, its loud and patient day.
