## The Cistern of the Counting Frogs

You reach it by going down, not far — a dozen steps cut by hand into clay that was fired in place long ago, each tread cupped in the middle by centuries of feet that no longer come. The air changes on the fourth step: cooler, mineral, wet in the way of a place that has never once been dry.

There is no ground here. That is the first thing to understand. Nothing in this place occurs naturally — every surface was made. The floor is brick, laid by hand in a herringbone that has heaved and resettled so many times it now follows the logic of the water beneath it rather than the intentions of its makers. The walls are coursed stone, each block shaped with a pecking tool — you can still read the dimpled tool-marks under the wet sheen, thousands of small percussions frozen in the surface. The ceiling, low enough to touch in places, is corbelled: rings of overlapping slabs stepping inward to a single oculus the width of two spread hands. Through it falls the only light, a dim grey column that moves across the chamber over the course of a day like the hand of an enormous, slow clock.

The chamber itself is small — you could cross it in fifteen paces if the water let you. And it is nearly empty. There is the shallow pool, knee-deep, occupying most of the floor. There is a single brick walkway around its rim. There is one stone bench, or altar, or shelf — its purpose has been forgotten so thoroughly that even the word for it is gone — standing alone at the far wall, worn at its edges into something almost organic, like a knuckle. That is all. The emptiness is part of the architecture. Whoever built this understood that a room can hold more by holding less.

The water comes from everywhere and nowhere. The walls weep — not dramatically, but constantly, a film of moisture descending the stone in slow pulses, gathering in the mortar lines, which have eroded into a network of tiny channels, a circulatory system the builders may or may not have intended. After rain in the world above, the weeping quickens, and the pool rises by a finger's width. In drought it never quite falls. The water is cold, clear amber, tasting faintly of clay and iron.

Vegetation has almost no purchase here. A few cushions of moss the color of old bronze cling where the light-column touches the wall at midday. One pale fern grows from a joint near the oculus, leaning out into the shaft of grey light like someone listening at a door. Nothing else green. The place is too built, too settled, too claimed.

Claimed — because what the cistern lacks in plants and objects it holds in *life*, and this is where the place stops being merely old and becomes strange.

The frogs arrived at some point no one witnessed, and they have organized themselves. They sit in the niches of the corbelled ceiling — small recesses left by the masons, perhaps for lamps — one frog per niche, dozens of niches, ascending in rings toward the oculus. They are slate-grey with throats the color of candle flame, and when the light-column reaches a certain angle in the afternoon, they begin to call: first the lowest ring, then the next, the sound climbing the ceiling course by course until the whole corbelled dome is ringing. The acoustics of the chamber were clearly designed for something — voices, chanting, the amplification of prayer — and the frogs have inherited the design. The sound is enormous. It presses on the chest. It seems to come from the stone itself, and after a few minutes you cannot tell whether you are hearing frogs or hearing the building remember what it was for.

Eels live in the pool, thick as a wrist, the color of wet slate, moving in slow circuits along the brick floor as if patrolling the herringbone. Snails the size of fists graze the weeping walls in vertical processions, and their trails dry into faint silver scripts that overlay the tool-marks — two kinds of writing, both illegible. At dusk, things with leather wings drop through the oculus, one after another, folding at the last moment to fit through the narrow opening, and hang in the dark upper rings among the silenced frogs. Their chittering replaces the chorus. The chamber is never quiet. Drip, chorus, chitter, the soft churn of eels, the sigh of the walls — sound is the most abundant material here, more abundant than light, more abundant than air.

And there are the small wrongnesses, the things a visitor notices on the second day rather than the first. The frogs in their niches always number the same — count them twice and you get the same figure, count them an hour later and the figure has changed, but is again the same on both counts, as if the chamber maintains a census. The water's surface, when the chorus is at full strength, vibrates into a standing pattern — a herringbone, matching the floor beneath it. The lone stone bench is always dry. Not dry in a wet room the way sheltered stone is dry, but *perfectly* dry, the only unwet thing in the cistern, and the snails detour around it.

Nothing here is dangerous. Nothing here wants anything from you. The place achieved its balance long ago — built by hands, abandoned by hands, taken up by everything that does not have hands — and it simply continues, loudly, in the dimness, a small made world that has thoroughly forgotten its makers but has not, you suspect, forgotten that it was made. Sit on the walkway long enough, with the cold coming up through the brick and the chorus shaking the air, and you may feel it noticing you back: not hostile, not welcoming. Counting.
