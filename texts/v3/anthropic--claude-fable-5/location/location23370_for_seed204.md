High on the shoulder of a young mountain — rock so recently cooled that the ridgelines still carry the rip and tear of their making, edges unsoftened, nothing rounded yet — there is a hollow in the basalt about the size of a small room. A flaw in the lava, a gas pocket that froze open and was later unroofed by frost-split. You could lie down across it. You would have to crouch to enter it at all, ducking under a lip of black glass still sharp enough to take skin.

Inside the hollow is a city.

Not a model of a city. A city, at the scale of a city's idea of itself: avenues a finger-width across, towers the height of a hand, districts that fit in a palm and would take an afternoon to walk if you were the right size to walk them. It was grown here, not assembled — the distinction matters to whatever made it. The buildings come up out of the rock the way frost comes up out of soil, drawn from the basalt itself, the stone disassembled grain by grain and re-laid as walls, vaults, cisterns, stairs. The architecture has the color of its source material, charcoal and iron-grey, but worked so finely that thin sections of it pass light, so the towers at midday glow faintly amber at their edges, like the rims of fingernails held to the sun.

The elevation is severe. Outside the hollow the air is thin and moves fast and carries almost nothing — no pollen, no seed, no smell of anything living, because nothing lives up here. There is no green for a vertical mile in any direction. The mountain is too new. Lichen hasn't found it; soil hasn't begun. The only chemistry happening on these slopes is mineral: oxidation, frost-wedging, the slow sweating of the rock.

And the rock does sweat. That's the hollow's whole hydrology, and the city's. The basalt above the pocket is shot through with hairline fractures, and snowmelt from the summit fields works down through them all day, emerging on the hollow's ceiling as a slow population of droplets. They fatten. They fall. To the city, each drop is a water tower's worth of supply arriving as weather — and the city is built for it. The drops never strike the streets. They strike catchment shells, broad parabolic petals raised over the districts on stems, each shell angled to receive and shed, and the water runs down spiral channels into reservoirs cut beneath the avenues. You can hear this, if you put your head inside the hollow and hold still: not the hum of machinery, because there is none audible, but a soft irregular percussion — *tip... tup... tip* — drops meeting shells, and under that the threadlike sound of water moving in very small aqueducts. It is the sound of a cave, mostly. The city has added almost nothing to it. Whatever it does, it does below the threshold of hearing.

The city is sparse, and seems content to be. Districts sit far apart with bare stone floor between them — not ruin-gaps, not failures, but apparently deliberate intervals, plazas of untouched basalt crossed by single elevated causeways thin as wire. Maybe a third of the hollow's floor is built. The rest waits, or breathes, or is simply margin. In one of these empty quarters a new district is currently coming up: you can watch it if you're patient, the way you can watch a clock's minute hand. The rock surface there has gone matte and slightly soft-looking, and over the course of an hour, walls rise out of it the way a tide comes in — no visible builders, no scaffolding, no fabricators, just stone deciding to be architecture, grain-front by grain-front. Current physics has a name for none of the steps involved. The energy budget alone should fog the hollow with waste heat, and doesn't; the pocket stays a degree or two above freezing, exactly, always, as though the cold were being spent somewhere.

It is cold. Outside, well below freezing; inside, that careful near-zero. The water that falls from the ceiling sometimes half-freezes on the way down and arrives as slush, and the catchment shells tilt and shiver it off into melt channels heated — somehow — just enough. At night the hollow's mouth grows a fringe of icicles, a portcullis of clear teeth, and the city's light (a low ambient glow from the translucent walls, the amber of banked coals) shines out through the ice onto the dark scree. By morning the icicles are gone. Removed, not melted; there's never any drip-line beneath where they stood.

Nothing grows here and the city seems to have been designed around that fact rather than against it. There are no parks, no planters, no spaces shaped for trees that will never come. Instead there are what might be gardens of water: shallow terraced pools, stepped down a district's flank, where the only cultivated thing is the behavior of the surface — interference patterns, standing ripples, films of meltwater trained into shapes that hold for hours. A garden of physics, tended.

Fauna is rare and arrives as catastrophe or as god, depending on your sympathies. A snow-mite the size of a poppy seed, blown in through the mouth of the hollow, is to the city a wandering animal the size of a delivery truck, and the city accommodates it — causeways flex out of its path, district lights dim along its route as if in deference, and eventually a gentle current of air, sourceless, escorts it back outside. Once or twice a season, a high-altitude wheatear lands on the lip of the hollow and looks in, head cocking, at the glow. The city goes still when this happens. Every process you could watch — the rising walls, the tilting shells, the working pools — pauses, the way a field of crickets pauses. The bird leaves. The percussion of the drops resumes.

Whether anything is meant to live in the city, no one could say from looking. The streets are scaled for inhabitants the height of a grain of rice, and the doorways stand open, and the interiors hold light and warmth and, presumably, water. They are empty. They are not unready. The whole hollow has the air of a place built before its people, or after them, or for a definition of *people* that hasn't arrived yet — a settlement keeping itself in practice. The fresh districts rise. The reservoirs fill and are drawn down by nothing visible. The mountain, young and sharp, weeps steadily into the catchments.

Outside, the wind goes over the ridge with the high hollow roar of altitude, and the bare black slopes run for miles, lifeless, brand new. The pocket in the rock is the only inhabited thing on the mountain, and it is smaller than you, and it is a city, and it is busy.
