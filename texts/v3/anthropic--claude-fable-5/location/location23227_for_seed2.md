**The Catchment**

You enter it the way everything enters it: through water, down.

There is no ground here that anyone poured by accident. The floor — three hundred metres below the surface, and the surface itself is just where the water happens to stop against the ceiling vents — is concrete, cast in slabs a kilometre on a side, the formwork seams still crisp at the edges. Run a glove along one and it would catch. Nothing has had time to soften. The whole basin is young the way a poured foundation is young: grey, sharp-cornered, faintly sweating lime into the water, so that in places the cold currents carry a milky bloom, a chalk-fog drifting between the pillars like weather. Which it is. At this scale, everything is weather.

Because the Catchment is not a room. It is a flooded interior the size of a sea — engineered, deliberately drowned, a continental machine for holding water. Pillars rise from the floor at intervals of several kilometres, each one the girth of a city block, ribbed with intake grilles, and between them: almost nothing. That is the first thing the body understands here. The emptiness. You can swim for an hour through open green-grey water, fifty metres of visibility in every direction, and see nothing made and nothing alive, only the light coming down in broad tilted columns from the surface diffusers far above — honest daylight, piped and lensed from somewhere outside, falling through the water in shafts the width of stadiums. The light is ordinary. That is somehow the strangest thing about it. Cool, clear, mid-morning light, forever, illuminating a built ocean.

Then a pillar resolves out of the haze, and the world has an edge again.

**Sound carries here the way it does in any sea, only there is more of it.** The Catchment is never quiet. Somewhere — always somewhere — a pump gallery is running, and the sound arrives through the water as a full-body hum, a sixty-cycle drone you feel in your sternum before your ears sort it out. When an intake opens, kilometres away, you hear it as a long tearing roar, like a waterfall heard through a wall, and the current shifts; the chalk-fog leans; loose things on the floor begin a slow migration. Twice a day the great transfer turbines spin up in sequence along the western wall — a wall you will never see, it is too far, but you hear it cross the basin like rolling thunder, pillar to pillar, each one ringing faintly as the pressure wave passes. Workers' folklore, if there were workers down here anymore, would call it the morning bell. The fish have already learned it. They move before it arrives.

**The fish are everywhere and they came from nowhere.** Stocked, originally — silver carp and char released into the cooling volumes when the basin first flooded, eleven years ago, twelve — and now feral, multiplied into populations that behave like sky. Bait-balls the size of apartment buildings drift through the light-shafts, flexing, scattering glitter. They are the densest things in the Catchment. They are also the loudest living things: a school turning in unison makes a sound like rain on a roof, a hiss of ten thousand bodies, and predators have followed the sound in through the intake grates — pike-jawed things, lampreys grown long in the warm outflow channels, eels that lie in the formwork seams of the floor like cables that breathe. There is a whole predator-prey economy strung across the emptiness, organized around the pillars and the light-columns the way oases organize a desert. Between them: the long fast. Fish cross the open volumes in committed, urgent schools, and sometimes you find the aftermath drifting — a slow snow of scales falling through a light-shaft, sparkling, all that remains of a crossing that didn't make it.

**Plants have barely begun.** The water is cool — eleven, twelve degrees — and the concrete is alkaline and new, and so vegetation exists only as a green ambition: a fuzz of algae on the sunward faces of the pillars, never the shadow sides, so each pillar wears a faint green compass-stripe. In the brightest light-shafts, where some maintenance program once seeded the floor, there are kelp lines — actual rows, planted in agricultural geometry, sparse and tentative, two metres tall and swaying in the pump-currents like a young orchard. They are the only soft vertical things in the entire basin. Fish mob them. Everything that lives here knows where the gardens are.

**The built world asserts itself constantly, and never explains.** Pipes the diameter of train tunnels run along the ceiling, far overhead, dimly visible as dark beams against the bright diffuser panels. Valve towers stand alone in the middle distance, kilometres from anything, their ladders still wearing the manufacturer's yellow paint, untouched, unfaded — nothing fades down here yet, the place is too new for ruin. Stencilled numbers on the pillars are crisp: SECTOR 114. STATIC HEAD 31 BAR. NO ENTRY WHEN PUMPING. There is no rust to speak of, only the first orange freckles around bolt-heads, the very beginning of the long argument between iron and water. The Catchment is not abandoned and not ruined; it is simply *operating*, automatically, at a scale that no longer requires witnesses. Somewhere far above, presumably, the water it gathers and moves is needed. Down here that purpose is just climate: current and thunder and tide.

**Once in a great while the basin breathes.** A drawdown event — the level dropping a few metres over an afternoon as some distant demand pulls water out — and the whole volume comes alive at once. Currents braid. The chalk-fog rivers sideways. Every school in twenty kilometres moves toward the intake roar like iron filings, predators stitched into their margins, and the sound builds and builds until the water itself seems to be one machine running, biology and engineering operating on the same shaft. Then the valves seat with a boom you feel in your teeth, the current dies over an hour, the fog settles, the snow of scales sifts down through the tilted ordinary daylight.

And the emptiness comes back — vast, cool, freshly poured, faintly humming — a sea-sized interior waiting, with industrial patience, to be filled again.
