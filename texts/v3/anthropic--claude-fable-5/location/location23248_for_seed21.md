## The Terrace

It is a small place, and it knows it. You could walk its full extent in ten minutes if the ground allowed walking, which it mostly does not yet — the ground is too new for that. A shelf of dark stone, perhaps two hundred meters across, hanging high on the shoulder of a mountain whose summit is lost in its own exhalations. On three sides the terrace ends in fresh cliff, edges so recent they have not learned to crumble. The fractures are clean as broken chocolate. On the fourth side the mountain continues upward into white vapor, and the terrace, sensibly, does not.

The stone is young. That is the first thing the place tells you, and it tells you through the soles of your feet: warmth, everywhere, rising through the rock like the heat of an animal that has just lain down. This shelf emerged — was extruded, was *decided* — recently enough that it has not finished cooling. Where it has cracked, the cracks are sharp-lipped and glassy, and from many of them water weeps. Not springs exactly. The rock sweats. Beads form along the fracture lines, swell, slide. Condensation gathers on every cool surface, and there are not many cool surfaces, so the water is always moving toward the few places it can rest: shallow basins, hand-sized pools, a single larger pond near the center, perfectly round, that steams gently and never overflows.

The air is thick and hot, the heat of a greenhouse left sealed through a long afternoon. Breathing it is like breathing through warm cloth. At this altitude that should not be possible — the air should be thin and cold — and the place seems aware of the contradiction and untroubled by it. The atmosphere here behaves as though it has been *asked* to stay. It pools on the terrace the way water pools in a basin, dense and humid and warm, and at the cliff edges you can see where it ends: a faint shimmer, a meniscus standing vertically in the open air, beyond which the real sky is hard and blue and presumably freezing. Birds — there are almost no birds — do not cross the meniscus. Once an hour, perhaps, a single dark shape rides the thermals far outside it, observing, declining.

Vegetation has arrived with suspicious speed. Nothing here should be more than a few years old, and nothing is, but the growth is dense where it grows: cushions of moss thick as mattresses around the weeping cracks, tree ferns already two meters tall standing in the steam of the central pond, their fiddleheads uncurling almost visibly in the heat. The lushness is patchy, though — islands of vivid green separated by broad reaches of bare warm stone, as if the plants were placed rather than spread, each colony given its allotment and a respectful distance from its neighbors. Between them the rock is naked and dark and beaded with dew, and the emptiness feels deliberate, like the spacing of stones in a garden no one will admit to keeping.

Because someone is keeping it. The evidence is scattered, minor, and impossible to explain away.

Near the eastern edge, half-sunk in the new stone as if the rock rose around it and was politely refused entry into it, stands a post about waist-high, made of a material that takes the light strangely — matte from one angle, depthless from another, like looking into still water at night. It has no markings. It is warm, but a different warmth than the rock: steadier, more like a pulse. The moss does not grow within a meter of it.

There are three others like it, spaced around the terrace at intervals that are almost certainly meaningful and resist every attempt to find the meaning. Stand among them long enough and you begin to suspect the terrace is the *inside* of something — that the posts are not on the landscape but that the landscape is strung between them, the way fabric is strung on a frame.

And there is the matter of the water. Watch the central pond. The steam rises from it normally enough, drifts, thins — and then, at a certain height, some of it *returns*. Not falling. Returning. Threads of vapor descending against their own buoyancy, sliding back down toward the surface in slow spirals, as though the pond's allotment of water is fixed and the place is quietly enforcing the budget. Nothing leaves the terrace. The weeping cracks, the pools, the saturated air: it is all one closed sum, circulating. After a while you understand that the wetness here is not weather. It is inventory.

The quiet is nearly total. The altitude eats sound; the vapor muffles what's left. What remains is small and precise: the tick of cooling stone, irregular, like a vast engine relaxing. The patted percussion of condensation dripping from fern fronds onto moss. Occasionally, from one of the posts, a tone so low it registers as pressure rather than sound — a single long note, felt in the chest, lasting perhaps a minute, after which the steam over the pond changes direction.

Fauna is rare and reluctant. There are small translucent things in the warm pools, finger-length, that may be amphibians and may be instruments; they hold still when watched in a way that real animals do not quite manage. Once a day or so, something larger moves at the vegetated margin where the terrace meets the mountain — a low shape, glimpsed, unhurried, dark and wet-looking — and the moss colonies are subtly rearranged in the morning. Grazed, or tended. The distinction may not exist here.

The light is honest, at least. Ordinary high-altitude daylight, bright and clean, filtering through the drifting vapor to lay soft moving shadows across the stone. In the afternoons the saturated air reaches some threshold and the whole terrace fogs at once — visibility dropping to arm's length in the space of a minute — and in the fog the posts become faintly visible as columns of slightly *organized* mist, the water vapor aligning around them in standing patterns, lamellae, slow vertical ripples. Then the threshold passes, the fog precipitates onto every surface in a sudden ticking shower, and the air is clear again, and the rock steams, and the budget rebalances.

It is hard to say what the terrace is for. A test plot, perhaps. A first room of a house still being grown, the mountain extruding floor after floor over centuries while something patient checks each one: seeds it, waters it, holds an atmosphere over it like a cupped hand, and watches what takes. The sharp young edges, the placed greenery, the closed circle of water, the four quiet posts holding the warm air in its impossible pool two miles above where warm air should be.

A small place, hot and dripping and barely finished, sparsely furnished with life, where almost nothing happens and all of it on purpose.
