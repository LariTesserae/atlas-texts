**The Basin of Loud Water**

The land here is younger than the city built on it. That is the first thing to understand, and the last.

It is a wide, shallow basin — you could walk across it in a long day, if walking were easy, which it is not — floored entirely in stone that is still arriving. Hot water rises from below in hundreds of mouths: boiling pools the color of milk and rust, vents that exhale steadily, fissures that seethe. The water carries dissolved stone in it, and where the water goes, the stone is left behind. Every surface in the basin is accreting. Terraces of pale travertine descend in rounded lips, each one wet, each one growing a film of new mineral thinner than skin, every hour, forever. The rock at your feet may be three days old. The rock under that, a season. There is no weathering here, no crumbling, no soil. Nothing in the basin has ever had time to wear down. Everything is in the act of forming.

Nothing green grows. The water is too mineral, the ground too warm, the crusts too quick — a seed that landed here would be entombed in stone before it could split. The basin is white and ochre and sulfur-yellow and the gray of wet eggshell, mile after mile, terrace after terrace, without one leaf.

But it is not empty. It is *crowded.*

The warmth and the open water draw life from the whole region into this one bowl. The shallow milky pools at the basin's edges, where the water has cooled enough to touch, are rimmed with wading birds in their tens of thousands — long-legged, pale-bodied, stained pink and orange at the breast by what they sieve from the brine. They stand nearly shoulder to shoulder. Above them the air is dense with swifts, which nest in the steam-cliffs where vents have built hollow chimneys of stone, and at dusk — though dusk here is only a deepening of the permanent dimness — the swifts give way to bats, rivers of them, pouring out of the chimney-mouths to take the insects that breed in uncountable numbers on the warm shallows. Flies film the water in places like a second surface. Salamander-pale things live in the cool fringes of the runoff channels. Everything that can tolerate the mineral water has come here, and stayed, and multiplied, because the basin never freezes, never floods, never fails.

The light never quite arrives. The basin makes its own weather: steam rising from a thousand sources gathers into a low, luminous ceiling that the sun comes through as a general pewter glow, directionless, shadowless. Distances dissolve into white. You see the city before you see the sky.

And you *hear* the basin long before either.

The sound is the basin's most absolute fact. The great pools boil continuously — not a simmer but a deep, churning roar, dozens of them, overlapping. The vents shriek and hiss in fixed pitches, each one constant as a held organ note, and they have held those notes longer than anyone's grandmother's grandmother. Beneath everything is a body-deep rumble from under the crust, felt in the sternum more than heard. Over everything: the birds, an unbroken massed clamor from the lake-edges, and the insect drone, and the wing-thunder of the bat rivers at evening. There is no quiet anywhere in the basin and there never has been. Sound here is like air — a medium, not an event.

The city has grown up inside that roar, and is shaped by it utterly.

It crowds the warm shelves between the boiling zones and the bird-lakes — a dense, near-windowless town of cut travertine, houses packed wall against wall, lanes barely a shoulder wide, roofed passages running between dwellings so that whole districts connect without going outside. The stone is quarried with stone: the fresh travertine, only weeks old, cuts almost like cheese under a hard hand-axe and a wooden wedge, then hardens over the years into something nearly marble. Builders need no kilns, no metal, no mortar — the basin mortars the city itself. Set two blocks together near a runoff channel and the water will seal the joint with new mineral in a season. The people guide the hot water in hand-cut channels through the town: it warms the floors, cooks the food in sunken stone basins, leaves a daily skin of new stone on everything it touches.

That skin is the city's labor and its covenant. Every morning, in the dim glow, people are out with scrapers of flaked chert and blades of polished bone, shaving the night's accretion from thresholds, channel-lips, steps, the rims of the cooking basins. The basin deposits; the people remove; the two rates have found each other exactly. The city is therefore in constant motion and absolutely unchanging — a doorway scraped daily for forty generations is the same doorway, neither growing shut nor wearing away. Abandon a house and the basin reclaims it in a few years, sealing it into a smooth white hummock; several such hummocks stand inside the town like the city's own fossils, and children are told which ancestors are inside them.

No one here speaks much aloud. The roar makes voices useless beyond an arm's length, so the people talk with their hands — a full, fast sign-speech, conducted face to face in the dim lanes, and at distance by stances and arm-shapes silhouetted against the steam. They read the basin by feel: a vent's pitch in the breastbone, a pool's churn through the soles of the feet. The constancy of the sound is their deepest reassurance. The notes have never changed. A child born tonight will fall asleep inside the same chord the dead fell asleep inside.

They harvest the birds' eggs from the lake margins, in measured rounds; they take the brine-flies' larvae in woven scoops; they trade hardened travertine and rendered fat outward to the green country beyond the rim, where, it is said, things grow from the ground, and the world is quiet, and the rocks are old and falling apart — a country the basin people visit and pity. Out there, everything is ending. Here, everything is only ever beginning, at exactly the rate it is held back, in a roar that has never once paused, under a low warm sky the color of the stone.
