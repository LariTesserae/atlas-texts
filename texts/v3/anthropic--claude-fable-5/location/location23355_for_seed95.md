**The Tableland of the Settled**

You come up onto it the way you come up onto the deck of something enormous and dead — a final shoulder of climb, hands on warm stone, and then nothing above you anymore. The plateau runs out in every direction, vast and nearly featureless, an old highland worn so far down that it has stopped being mountains and become instead the *memory* of mountains: a warm, ochre-and-grey table of rock under a wide pale sky, its ridgelines reduced to long swellings no taller than a kneeling figure, its valleys reduced to shallow troughs you could mistake for the marks of a thumb dragged through clay a very long time ago.

Everything sharp has been gone for ages. There are no cliffs up here, no scree, no fresh fracture anywhere. The bedrock shows at the surface almost everywhere, polished to a low satin sheen — not glacier-polish, nothing so dramatic, just the patience of wind and dew and the slow traffic of bodies over a span of time that has outlasted weather patterns, outlasted whole eras of rain. Where the stone dips, fine pale dust has gathered, soft as ash, printed everywhere with the same broad, round, deliberate track.

Because the plateau is full. That is the thing you understand second, and never stop understanding.

---

At first you take them for boulders — the low domed shapes scattered densely across the tableland, dark and rounded, some the size of a curled dog, some the size of a hut, spaced a body-length apart and running unbroken to the horizon in every direction. Thousands in view at once. Tens of thousands. Then one of them, perhaps forty paces off, exhales.

It is a long sound, barely a sound at all — more a change in the pressure of the air, like a door easing shut in another room — and a thin shimmer of moisture lifts off the dome's back and hangs there a moment before settling. And once you have seen one breathe you cannot unsee the rest: the whole plateau is respiring, asynchronously, each dome on its own slow tide. Some breathe several times an hour. Some, you suspect, breathe several times a year. The dew that beads each morning on the warm stone beads differently on their backs — in long radial lines, following the grain of hide or shell or whatever the weathered grey integument is — and that is often the only way to tell, at distance, body from bedrock. The creatures and the plateau have been wearing into each other for so long that they have arrived at the same color, the same texture, the same temperature. Press a palm flat against one (they do not object; they do not appear to register it) and it is exactly as warm as the sun-heated rock beside it. Slightly softer. Faintly, terribly alive.

They are not asleep. Their eyes — when you find the eyes, set low and lidded under brows of callused hide — are open, dark, wet, and pointed at nothing in particular. They have simply finished with movement, the way the landscape has finished with uplift. Occasionally, over the course of a day, you will see one mid-step: a single broad limb extended, raised perhaps a hand's width off the ground, in transit. Come back the next day and the step will have completed. Come back the next season and the creature will be a few body-lengths along whatever errand it is on, an errand whose origin and destination are not legible at human timescales. The dust between the domes is webbed with their trails — shallow grooves worn into living rock, some only a finger deep, some deep enough to lie down in, the oldest ones rounded over and half-erased, made by walkers who finished their crossings before the plateau finished being mountains.

---

There is water, but it does not flow. It *arrives*.

In the last hour of night the warm stone, having held its heat, meets the cool descending air, and the whole tableland sweats. Dew gathers in the worn trails and the troughs, in the cupped tops of certain creatures' backs (some of the eldest have weathered concave, like basins), in the shallow pans of bedrock where it stands in sheets a knuckle deep, blood-warm by mid-morning, gone by afternoon. Every few weeks a slow grey ceiling of cloud drags itself across the plateau at little more than head height — you can raise your arm into it — and leaves everything dark and beaded and ticking faintly as the moisture finds its way downward. Nothing rushes. Nothing has anywhere to rush to. The water is simply passed downward through the world, stone to dust to mouth, and the only standing pools that last are the ones in the lee of the largest bodies, kept shaded by a living wall.

The vegetation has organized itself entirely around this. There are no trees — there is barely soil — but there is a botany of *attendance*: stiff cushion plants the size of fists, grey-green and woody, growing only in the dew-lines along the north flanks of the creatures and the swells; a sparse, wiry grass in the dust-filled trails, cropped to stubble; and over everything, the true dominant flora of the place, lichen — sprawling, concentric, slow-blooming maps of rust and bone-white and a strange soft gold, colonizing rock and carapace indifferently. Many of the oldest creatures carry whole gardens. You can read something like age in it: the young ones (young — a relative term, none of them seem young) are bare and dark; the elders are upholstered, frosted, flowering with other lives. Small things live in those gardens. Many small things.

---

This is where the density of the place stops being visual and becomes total. Kneel and look closely at any square of ground and it resolves into occupancy: flat, plated insects pressed into the lichen, motionless; pale geckos wedged in dew-cracks, motionless; a kind of round-bodied bird, grey as the stone, that stands in the shelter of the great domes in groups of ten or twelve, all facing the same direction, blinking maybe once a minute. Slow snails with shells worn translucent. Soft, fingerless things in the warm dew-pans that may be amphibian and may be something with no proper category, lying half-submerged, pulsing. Every shadow is inhabited. Every body is habitat. The plateau holds more lives per acre than a reef — and it is *quiet*, profoundly, because none of these lives have anything urgent to say. There is no fleeing here, so there is no alarm call. There is no chase, so there is no cry. The soundscape, such as it is: wind combing the cushion plants; the irregular distant doors-closing of the great breaths; once or twice a day, a single clear two-note whistle from one of the grey birds, answered, after a courteous interval of ten or twenty minutes, from somewhere far off. At night, a faint communal hum, so low you feel it through your knees rather than hear it — the domes, possibly, or the rock, or no difference between the two.

If anything eats anything else here, it happens at a speed that makes it indistinguishable from coexistence.

---

And scattered across all of this — never frequent, never absent — are the structures.

You find the first one in a shallow trough: a ring of flat stones, each the size of a hand, stacked three high, enclosing nothing. No mortar, no tool marks, no chips or flakes anywhere nearby. The stones are dew-worn round and fused at their contact points by centuries of lichen, so the ring is now a single object. A few hundred paces on, a low corbelled dome of the same construction, waist-high, with a single dark opening facing east — empty inside except for a palm's depth of fine dust and a smell like warm rain. Then, near a dew-pan, a line of upright slabs, seven of them, graded by height, leaning together at the top like fingers meeting.

There is no village. There was never a village. The structures do not cluster; they are sown across the whole tableland at intervals of an hour's walk, and they are all old in the way the trails are old — settled, rounded, grown-over, finished. You will not find a maker, and after enough days you stop expecting one shaped like you. Because you begin to notice things: that the rings always sit at the intersections of the deepest trails. That the corbelled domes are always just slightly larger inside than the smallest of the living domes. That on certain mornings one of the great creatures will be found stopped — fully stopped, even by their standard, breath suspended — beside one of the stone rings, one limb resting on its rim, and will stay that way for days, and that afterward there is sometimes one more stone on the ring than there was before. Whether this is building or grooming or grief or simply pressure of body against stone repeated across millennia, the result is the same: architecture, accreted at the pace of geology, by inhabitants who have never needed a tool because they have time instead.

The corbelled domes, you eventually decide, are the ones who finished. Walk to the nearest, put your head into the warm dark opening, and the dust inside has the same radial grain as the dew-lines on a living back.

---

The strangeness of the plateau is not in any single thing. It is in the agreement. Everything here — rock, water, plant, beast, structure — has converged on a single state and a single speed, as if the landscape long ago held a negotiation that every party won. The warmth never breaks into heat; the damp never gathers into storm; the crowd never becomes commotion. Days are bright and mild under high thin cloud, the light even and shadowless for hours at a stretch, so that the domes lose their edges and the whole tableland becomes one continuous breathing surface. The horizon does not change. The horizon has not changed, you suspect, within the memory of anything now breathing on it — and some of the things now breathing on it have been breathing for a very long while.

You can cross it in a week, if you keep human time. Most visitors don't, quite. The trails are smooth and warm underfoot, the dew-pans are sweet, the bodies radiate a low animal heat at night that makes shelter unnecessary, and there is a particular thing that happens around the third or fourth day: you find that you have been standing beside one of the elders for some minutes without remembering stopping, your breath unconsciously stretched to meet the wind, waiting — not impatiently, not for anything in particular — for the next breath but one.

The plateau does not keep anyone. It simply offers its pace, the way it offers its warmth, to whatever arrives. What you do with that is the only fast decision left on the entire tableland, and it is yours.
