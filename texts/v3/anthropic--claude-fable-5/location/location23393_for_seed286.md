# The Perched Sea

There is a continent that holds an ocean on its back.

You would have to climb to reach it — up through cloud, up past the line where rain turns to sleet, onto a tableland so wide it generates its own weather and then drowns it. The plateau is rimmed with mountains like the lip of a basin, and inside that rim the water stands four kilometers above the world's true seas: a cold, clear, captive ocean, thin-aired at its surface, lit from above by a high pale sun that drops through the water in long ordinary shafts. Nothing about the light is strange. Everything beneath it is.

The sea has no floor. It has a *body*.

What you would call the seabed is plant — not plants growing on rock, but plant *as* rock, a single vegetable mass the size of a continent, so old that its lower strata have compressed into something like green coal, like jade with a pulse. The living surface of it rolls away in dim hills and trenches, fuzzed with moss the color of old bronze, split here and there by valleys where the tissue beneath shows paler, scarred, healed over. There is no sediment. There is no stone. Drop anything and it lands on flesh that has been alive for longer than mountains usually last, and the mass will, over a century or two, absorb it without comment.

## The Engines

The mass is not idle. It works.

Scattered across it — sparsely, one every few kilometers, each visible from the last as a dark tower in the blue distance — stand the great vegetal engines. They were not built. Nothing here was ever built; there is no hand-mark anywhere in this ocean, no cut surface, no joined seam. The engines *grew*, the way a heart grows, into shapes that do industrial work because the work is what they are for.

There are the **siphon trees**: hollow trunks two hundred meters tall, ribbed like windpipes, that draw cold water down through their crowns and exhale it warmed and mineral-rich from vents at their roots. Their intake makes a sound you feel in your sternum before you hear it — a continuous indrawn roar, the sound of a sea being breathed.

There are the **turbine fronds**: stands of enormous spiral leaves, rigid as horn, set in the steady current the siphons create. They rotate. They have always rotated, at exactly this speed, transmitting torque down twisted root-shafts into the mass below, where it drives processes no surface observer will ever see — grinding, circulating, the slow internal commerce of a planet-sized body. The fronds creak as they turn. Across the open blue between engine-stands, the creaking arrives from six directions at once, out of phase, an orchestra of hinges.

There are the **bellows fields**: low plains of translucent bladders, acres of them, swelling and collapsing in strict alternation, pumping gas through capillary networks under the moss. The gas never reaches the surface. Bubbles rise from the bladder-fields in silver columns and then simply stop, mid-water, held at a fixed altitude — reabsorbed there, drawn back down through unseen pores, so that each column of bubbles is permanent, a standing pillar of trapped light that has hung in the same place, made of different bubbles, forever.

And running through everything, dim under the moss, the **grid**: veins of bioelectric tissue carrying real voltage across real distances, visible at twilight depths as faint blue threading, a circulatory system of current. Where two trunk-veins cross, the water hums. Fish avoid those crossings, or are drawn to them, depending on what the fish are for.

## The Sound

The sea is loud the way a factory is loud — not chaotically, but *totally*. Water carries sound without mercy, and the engines never stop, so the whole ocean rings at once: the bass respiration of the siphons, the creak-cycle of the turbines, the soft concussive *pum… pum…* of a billion bladders, valve-clicks like distant rain on tin, the hum of the grid underneath it all like a held note from something the size of the world. There is no silence anywhere in the perched sea and there never has been. Creatures here have no concept of quiet. Their alarm signal is a change in rhythm — and the rhythm does not change.

## The Inhabitants

The fauna are abundant and have all found their stations.

Schools of flat silver grazers move along the moss in slow assigned circuits, cropping the surface growth to a uniform height — and the moss grows back at exactly the rate it is cropped, so the grazers' paths never shift. Eel-like tenders, faintly luminous, swim the grid-veins endlessly, eating the films that would otherwise insulate them; they are part of the electrical system the way a brush is part of a motor. Inside the hollow siphon trunks live colonies of pale crustaceans that have never seen open water, riding the intake current up and the exhaust current down, generation after generation, a population in laminar flow.

Larger things pass through the blue distances between engines — slow, finned, whale-scaled silhouettes that filter the water and, in filtering it, polish it. They sing, of course, but their songs have to thread through the engine-noise, so they have evolved to sing *in the gaps*, in the precise intervals between valve-beats, a music fitted into machinery like mortar into stone. The songs are old. They may be the same songs. Nothing here suggests otherwise.

There are no predators in the usual sense, or rather predation has been metabolized into maintenance: things are eaten at the rate they are produced, by the things produced to eat them.

## The Stillness

This is the deepest fact of the perched sea, deeper than its altitude or its noise: **nothing is happening here, and nothing ever will.**

Not stagnation — the water is cold and clean and ceaselessly moved, the engines churn, the current runs its one eternal circuit around the basin like a thought a mind cannot stop thinking. But there are no events. No storm penetrates more than a few meters down before the surface bladder-meadows damp it flat. No season reaches this altitude that the mass does not buffer away. The temperature of the water is the same at noon and midnight, the same this millennium and last. Erosion finished its work an age ago and now repair equals wear, exactly, everywhere. A frond that snaps is regrown at the speed of its snapping. The system does not develop. It does not decline. It has arrived at itself completely and now simply *runs*, vast and cool and roaring, at full industrial throat, going nowhere, forever — an ocean-sized engine whose product, as far as anyone could ever determine, is its own continuation.

Hang motionless in the open blue, halfway between two engine-towers, with the bubble-pillars gleaming at their fixed altitudes and the grid glowing faintly below and the creak and breath and hum arriving from every direction at once, and you understand: you are not in a place. You are inside a process that mistook itself for geography, long enough ago that the mistake became true.
