**The Caldera Plateau**

You come up onto it from below — there is no other way; the plateau has no edges that descend gently — and the first thing you understand is the heat, and the second thing is the sound, and it takes a long time to understand that they are the same thing.

The land itself is simple. Almost insultingly simple. A tableland of fused mineral the color of old brick and older bone, lifted two miles above whatever the world used to be, planed flat by ages of wind that long ago ran out of anything soft to carry away. What remains is the hard answer to a long subtraction problem. Rock rippled like cooled toffee. Shallow basins floored with rust-glass. Pressure ridges worn down to suggestions of themselves. Nothing grows here and nothing has ever grown here; this is not a dead place but a place that skipped that argument entirely. No water carved it. The heat carved it. The heat is patient and has been carving for a very long time, and the plateau has settled into the shape of being carved, the way an old face settles into the shape of its one expression.

The emptiness is real. You can walk for an hour across the plates of brick-colored stone and meet nothing, pass nothing, cast the only shadow. The air is thin at this altitude and shimmers hard, and the horizon is a long way off and contains, mostly, more horizon.

But the plateau is not quiet, and it is not uninhabited, and these two facts are the same fact.

---

Far off — and everything here is far off — you will see your first one. From distance it resembles a kiln that has decided to migrate. Closer, it resolves: an animal, if the word stretches that far. The size of a row of houses. A carapace of overlapping mineral plates, dark and heat-crazed, vented along the spine with rows of orifices that breathe in slow industrial rhythm — *in*, a vast intake hiss; *out*, a pressurized roar shot through with the clatter of internal mechanism. Through gaps in the plating you can see the inside of it working: not organs exactly, not machinery exactly, but the place where those two ideas stopped pretending to be different. Reciprocating bone. Ligament under tension like belt-drive. A furnace heart, visibly glowing through the body wall, that runs on the stone itself — the creature grazes on the plateau, jaws milling rock to powder, smelting it in the long kilns of its gut, and somewhere behind it, days behind it, it deposits slag in low gleaming windrows that are the only monuments this land will ever have.

It is not alone. None of them are alone, though the distances between them are enormous. Stand on any rise at midday and count: a kiln-beast a mile east, two more on the southern horizon trailing their banners of mineral exhaust, something low and segmented threshing along a ridgeline to the north with a sound like a stamping mill, and above — always above — the flyers, turning in thermals so violent they have given up on wings as airfoils and use them instead as governors, as flywheels, ratcheting around their own centers of mass with a *whick-whick-whick* you can hear from the ground, three thousand feet below them.

This is how the plateau holds both truths at once: it is nearly empty, and it is overwhelmed. The fauna here is not dense. It is *loud*, and it is *large*, and it is everywhere within hearing, which on the plateau means everywhere. The soundscape is total. A continuous industrial drone — combustion, percussion, the deep subsonic mill-grind of the big grazers, the shriek of the flyers' bearings, the steam-hammer mating calls that roll across the flats at dusk and overlap and interfere until the air itself seems to be running machinery. You are never out of earshot of the herds. You are rarely within a mile of one. The plateau is a factory floor the size of a province, with one machine per square mile and no walls to stop the noise.

Nothing built this. That must be said clearly. There are no structures, no roads, no ruins, no hand or claw ever shaped a tool here. The industry is entirely grown. It evolved, or it congealed, or — and this is where the plateau stops cooperating with explanation — it was always here, and biology arrived later and put it on like clothing.

---

Because the plateau is old enough, and hot enough, and high enough, that reality has gone soft at the seams.

It happens slowly. Everything here happens slowly; the place has the metabolism of a glacier made of fire. But it happens. You will be walking and notice that your shadow has fallen behind by a few seconds — that it is still completing your last gesture while you stand still. The shimmer above the hot stone does not merely distort the distance; sometimes it *replaces* it, and the horizon you walk toward for an afternoon turns out to be a horizon from somewhere else, or some-*when* else, stitched in without ceremony, and the seam is a line on the ground where the rock changes color mid-grain.

There are basins — the locals would call them that, if there were locals, if there could ever be locals — where the air has worn through. Not torn: *worn*, the way stone wears, gradually, by long friction. In these places the light arrives slightly used. Sounds play out of order; you hear the kiln-beast's roar, and then a minute later you hear its intake, the breath happening backward at leisure. The big grazers route around these basins with the unhurried certainty of animals avoiding a bog. They know. Their whole anatomy knows — it has been suggested by the shape of their bodies, by their furnace hearts and ratchet joints, that life here did not evolve *despite* the breakdown but *out of* it: that in a place where cause and effect have worn loose, the only viable biology is one with its own internal scheduling, its own pressure, its own clock. An engine carries its causality with it. That is what an engine is for. Every animal on the plateau is a sealed argument for how time should work, roaring its position into air that has stopped insisting on any position at all.

The deepest of the worn places sits near the plateau's center, a basin maybe a day's walk across, and the herds give it a margin of many miles. From the rim — do not go past the rim — it looks almost ordinary. Flat reddish stone, shimmer, distance. But the distance does not behave. The far side of the basin is sometimes visible in too much detail, every crack legible from twenty miles, and sometimes not visible at all, the middle ground simply declining to exist, near-rock giving way to far-rock with nothing spent in between. Things move down there. Slowly. They have the silhouettes of the kiln-beasts but they are wrong in ways the eye reports as grief before it reports as geometry — too many of the same leg, a spine that closes on itself, an exhaust plume rising from the animal *and from the place the animal was last week, still*. Whether they are creatures that wandered in, or the basin's own long slow dream of the creatures outside it, the plateau does not say. The plateau has had a very long time to stop explaining itself.

---

Days here are long and the light is honest — a high hard daylight, thin-aired and shadowless at noon, everything rendered in the flat candor of brick and rust and bone-white slag. The heat does not relent; it only changes register, from the hammering vertical heat of midday to the radiant horizontal heat of evening, when the rock gives back everything it was given and the kiln-beasts' furnace hearts show through their plating like coals through a grate, drifting points of orange scattered to the horizon, each one droning, each one miles from the next.

The cycles are vast and gentle. Herds drift across the tableland over seasons, following gradients of mineral richness too subtle for any instrument but appetite. Slag windrows accumulate over centuries into low ridges, and the wind takes centuries more to plane them back down. The worn basins deepen by inches of unreality per age. Nothing here is in a hurry, because nothing here is going anywhere; the plateau is finished, has been finished for a long time, and what looks like activity is just the finished thing continuing to be itself — loudly, hotly, at scale, with the deep contented violence of an old machine that no one made, that no one runs, that no one could ever turn off, grinding its slow rounds two miles above the world under a wide and slightly worn-through sky.
