# The Saddle Grove

There is a place where two ridgelines meet, high enough that the clouds come up to it from below rather than passing overhead, and in that saddle a grove of perhaps thirty enormous trees has stood long enough that nobody distinguishes anymore between the trees and the town.

The trees are a kind of montane fig — broad, grey-barked, their trunks not single columns but braided masses of fused roots, ten and twelve feet across at the base. Their canopies have grown into each other so completely that from a distance the grove reads as one organism: a single low green hill of leaf sitting on the ridge, steaming faintly in the cool air. Up close the braiding is everything. Centuries ago — nobody holds the memory of it, only the result — people began training the aerial roots: bending them while young and soft, lashing them across gaps, letting them thicken. The roots took the shapes they were given and then kept growing, so that the whole grove is now threaded with living bridges, living staircases, living balustrades worn smooth and dark as old leather by generations of bare feet and wet hands.

Nothing here is built so much as *grown into*. A house is a place where four roots were trained into a square fifty years before its current occupant was born, walled with woven cane panels that get replaced every few seasons as they molder, roofed with overlapped fronds. The grain stores hang from branches in great wicker bulbs, like a wasp colony's idea of architecture. The only stone in the settlement is a handful of grinding slabs and the hearthstones, hauled up the ridge long ago; the only metal is a few soft blades, traded for, hoarded, resharpened until they are slivers. Everything else is fiber, wood, gourd, bone, and the patient cooperation of the trees themselves.

## The Wet

It is never dry here. The saddle sits exactly at the altitude where the rising air gives up its water, and the grove combs the clouds. Mist moves through the streets — if the branching root-paths can be called streets — in slow torn sheets, and every leaf above gathers it and lets it go. The result is a rain that falls without weather: a constant patter from the canopy, heavier under some trees than others, so that locals know the dry lines and the wet lines of every path the way a city-dweller knows where the awnings are.

Moss upholsters everything below head height. Not a film of it — a pelt, inches thick, emerald and rust-gold, fat with held water. Press a hand against any rail and water wells up between your fingers, cold. The handholds of the stairs have been rubbed bare by use, dark wet wood in channels through the green, and these bare patches are the closest thing the town has to signage: where the moss is worn, people go.

Water collects in the crotches of the great trees in natural tanks, some of them large enough to bathe in, black-watered, their surfaces dimpled constantly by drip. Hollowed half-trunks run as gutters from tank to tank, down through the levels of the town, ending in a channel that spills off the south face of the saddle and falls away into cloud — a thin white thread vanishing into white. The sound of that fall is the town's drone note, beneath everything else.

It is cool. Not cold — but the kind of damp chill that lives at this altitude, that makes the hearths precious and the smoke welcome. Cooking fires burn in clay-lined pits on woven platforms, and their smoke rises through the canopy and mixes with the mist until you cannot tell, looking up through the leaves, which grey is which.

## The Loud

The grove is never quiet and never still, and a visitor's first hour here is mostly the work of learning to think inside the noise.

The trees are in fruit most of the year, in staggered waves, and the fruit brings everything. A colony of large black-and-yellow weaver-birds occupies the eastern trees in the hundreds, their hanging nests so thick on some branches that the branches sag, and their voices are a continuous mechanical shrieking argument that the townspeople genuinely do not hear anymore — they have a word for the silence that falls when a hawk passes over, but no word for the noise itself. Below the weavers: pigeons in soft thudding mobs, barbets hammering, a troop of small grey monkeys that the town tolerates the way ports tolerate gulls, with thrown sticks and resigned humor. The monkeys raid the drying racks. Children are posted against them. The children are louder than the monkeys.

At dusk the shift changes. The birds settle and the frogs come up — the moss itself seems to sing, thousands of small voices in the wet pelt of the town, ringing, ticking, belling — and the bats pour out of the hollow heart of the oldest tree, the one at the center of the saddle whose interior rotted out generations ago and now houses a colony so large that its evening exit is a sound like tearing cloth that goes on for several minutes. People time their evening meal by it.

And beneath the animal noise, the human noise, because the saddle is a crossing point — the only easy pass between two valleys — and so this hamlet of maybe forty households swells, every fifth day, into a market. Porters come up out of the clouds on both sides with baskets of lowland goods on their backs, and the root-streets fill, and the trading is conducted at a volume meant to carry over weavers, frogs, dripping, falling water, and everyone else's trading. Haggling here is half gesture out of necessity. The market has no square; it has levels, ledges, bridge-rails hung with goods, vendors perched in the low forks of the trees shouting down. It is an anthill on market days, motion in every direction including vertically: baskets going up on ropes, children going down balustrades faster than is wise, monkeys exploiting the confusion, a man chasing a monkey, everyone laughing at the man.

## The Turbulence

For all its age, the grove is not a calm place. The clouds that feed it also batter it. Weather arrives without ceremony: the mist thickens, the light goes pewter, and then wind comes over the saddle hard and sudden, and the whole canopy heaves like surf. Branches the size of ordinary trees fail and come down, and the town's deep knowledge is the knowledge of which limbs are tired — they are watched, talked about, sometimes pre-emptively cut and lowered on ropes in a day-long operation that draws the whole population. After a big blow, the grove rebuilds itself with the casual competence of long practice: panels rewoven, a snapped bridge-root spliced and bound to a young root that will fuse and replace it in a decade. The damage is constant and the repair is constant and neither is an emergency. The town exists in a steady churn of breaking and mending, fruiting and rotting, arrival and departure, and its settled, ancient feeling comes not from stillness but from the churn having found its rhythm centuries ago.

The light, under all this, is good — better than a stranger would expect. The canopy is high and the fig leaves are broad but loosely layered, and the light that gets through is a moving, watery, ordinary daylight, shifting constantly as the upper branches toss. Bright coins of it slide across the moss and the wet rails all day long. Nothing here glows or looms. It is only a town in a grove on a high wet saddle, dripping, shrieking, trading, dripping — but it is so entirely itself, so saturated with life per cubic foot, that travelers coming up out of the silent cloud on either side report the same experience: they hear the grove a full half hour before they see it, a roar of birds and water and voices hanging in the whiteness ahead, and then the great grey braided trunks resolve out of the mist all at once, already busy, as if the town had been going on at full volume forever, waiting for no one.
