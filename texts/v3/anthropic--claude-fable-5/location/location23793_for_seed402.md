**The Geode Ward**

Begin with the stone above: a hundred feet of cold dolomite, dry as chalk, holding no memory of water. Somewhere in it, the way a pearl sits in an oyster that never knew the sea, there is a cavity. It is not large. A person standing at its center could nearly touch both walls of its narrowest waist. Call it a room. Call it, as its inhabitants apparently do — in a script of scratched tallies along the entry throat — a *ward*.

The ward is bright. This is the first strangeness, because there is no shaft to the surface, no flame, no phosphorescence of the biological kind. The light comes from the walls themselves, which are lined — floor, vault, every reachable and unreachable face — with a crystal that has not finished growing. The crystal is white shading to the pale blue of skimmed milk, and it emits a steady, sourceless daylight, the flat even light of an overcast noon. Where the crystal is thickest the light is strongest, and where new growth is still budding — small blunt nubs like teeth coming in — the light flickers at a period of roughly one flicker per hour. The whole cavity is still forming. You can hear it, if you hold still long enough: an occasional tick, a settling creak, the sound of mineral committing itself to a shape. The room is being born around its city, and the city is patient about it.

For there is a city here, or the seed of one, at the scale of a wasp nest built by careful hands. The floor of the ward, perhaps forty feet across, has been terraced into six descending rings, each ring stepped with dozens of small structures — dwellings, presumably, none higher than a child's waist, walled with flakes of the wall-crystal knapped and stacked dry, without mortar, because there is nothing wet here to make mortar from. The construction is dense to the point of contact: doorway abutting doorway, roofs of split slab overlapping like fish scales, alleys narrower than a hand. Ladders of lashed quill and sinew climb between terraces. Everything shows the marks of the simplest possible tools — percussion flaking, abrasion, patient grinding. There is no metal anywhere. There is not even fired clay, because firing would require fuel, and fuel is the one crop this place does not grow.

What it grows instead is the second strangeness. The ward is *lush*. It should not be — the air is a few degrees above freezing and dry enough to crack lips in an hour — but every surface not claimed by construction is claimed by flora. It is a dry flora, an unwatered botany. Understand it this way: these plants drink light the way ours drink rain. Frost-ferns of translucent mineral leaf unfurl from the crystal itself, so slowly that a frond's opening spans a season, colored the grey-green of lichen and cold to the touch as the stone they come from. Between them grow mats of what looks like moss but crumbles like fine ash and re-knits itself over weeks. Pale bracket-growths shelve out from the walls in tiers, papery, ringed with growth lines like tree stumps, and it is these the inhabitants harvest — you can see the neat crescent scars of stone knives on the older shelves, and drying racks (drying, in a place that was never wet; say instead *curing* racks) hung with strips of the stuff on every rooftop. The vegetation crowds the architecture. The architecture crowds the vegetation. In the lowest ring they have interleaved entirely: houses roofed in living bracket, ferns trained through window-slots, a density of green-grey and milk-blue and knapped white that leaves almost no bare stone anywhere.

The inhabitants themselves are not in evidence. This may be a matter of hours or of custom. What fauna shows itself is rare and deliberate: a single pale creature the size of a thumb, many-legged, translucent enough that the wall-light passes through it, moving along a terrace edge with the unhurried confidence of something that has never been hunted. Higher up, in the unreachable vault where the crystal teeth are newest, something that might be a moth and might be a drifting flake of mineral circles the brightest nub, once, and settles.

The quiet is nearly total but not dead. Beneath it runs a texture: the hour-flicker of the growing crystal, the ash-moss faintly crackling as it knits, a periodic soft *tok* from somewhere in the terraces — a tool laid down, or a door of slab shifted, or the ward itself adding a grain of itself to itself. The air smells of cold flint and something faintly sweet, like snow that has been near fruit.

And here is the last thing, the thing that takes longest to see. The city is not merely built inside the growing geode — it is built *ahead* of it. At the ward's far end, where the cavity narrows into a throat of raw, unlit, still-forming stone, the terraces continue: foundations laid in the dark, ring-walls stacked, empty doorways framed, all of it waiting in blackness at the edge of the light. The inhabitants have measured the crystal's growth — a hair's breadth in a generation, perhaps — and they have built where the light will be. Some of those foundations have the deep patina of great age. The people who laid them did not live to see them lit. The people who live here now occupy houses their ancestors raised in the dark on faith, and are raising, farther in, houses of their own that stand for now in a night that is very slowly, very certainly, being pushed back by the walls themselves.

Nothing here hurries. The ward opens like a frond: one season at a time, one grain at a time, one lit doorway at a time, deep in the dry cold stone.
