**The Shoulder Cities**

The range is young the way a wound is young. Nothing here has had time to soften. The peaks come up out of the plateau in long blades, their edges still carrying the angle of the fracture that made them, and the valleys between are not the broad U-shapes of old glacial country but narrow, raw, V-cut things, floored with rubble that has not yet learned to be soil. From any high point you can see the range continuing — north, south, in ranks behind ranks until distance turns them the color of breath — and you understand that this is not a mountain range in a landscape. This is the landscape. It runs to the curve of the world. It makes its own weather, banked cloud forming on its western faces and dying on its eastern ones, and a person could walk along its spine for a lifetime and die still inside it.

It is cold without drama. Not the killing cold of the deep peaks above, where nothing lives and the ice is blue-black in the couloirs, but a constant, patient cold at the elevation where people have chosen to live — the shoulder elevation, the broad benches and hanging shelves partway up the range where the angle relents. The air is thin enough that the light comes down hard and clean, edges sharp, shadows sharp, and yet the warmth in it never quite reaches the stone. Water exists here in small allowances: a film of condensation on the north side of every wall by morning, fog pooling in the valley throats and burning off by midday, seeps where snowmelt threads down a fracture and beads out of the rock like sweat. Rain comes occasionally, briefly, almost apologetically, and is drunk by the rubble before it can form streams of any size. The people catch it. Every roof here is also a funnel.

And there are people in numbers that the land would seem to forbid.

The cities — there is no other honest word — are built along the shoulder benches wherever the rock offers a few degrees of mercy, and they are dense. Drystone construction, no mortar, no metal worth mentioning: the range sheds an endless supply of sharp, freshly broken slabs, and generations of hands have learned to read the grain of them, to stack them so each stone's weight is its own cement. The walls are thick as a person is tall. The streets between them are narrow enough to touch both sides, deliberately, because a narrow street holds the day's warmth and breaks the wind. Houses lean into houses; courtyards are shared by six families; staircases climb over rooflines and become the floors of the dwellings above. From across the valley a city looks less built than grown — a lichen of human architecture spreading across the slope, gray on gray, distinguishable from the talus only by its right angles and its hundred slow threads of dung-fire smoke rising straight up in the still air.

The tools are bone, horn, fire-hardened wood, a little soft copper for the wealthy, and above all the worked stone itself. There are no wheels — wheels are useless here — but there are ramps, sledges, rope walks of braided wool spanning the gorges, and an architecture of patience: a wall begun by a grandmother and finished by her grandson, because the stone must settle for a season between courses. Everything important happens slowly. The cities are not expanding so much as thickening, each generation adding a layer, a story, a buttress, until the oldest quarters are honeycombs where the original streets have become tunnels and people live by lamplight at noon.

The quiet is the strangest thing about so many lives pressed so close. Sound does not carry well in thin air, and the people have grown into the silence rather than against it. Voices are kept low; there is a whole grammar of whistled signals for speaking across distances, thin two-note phrases that travel where shouting dies. Mostly what you hear is texture rather than event: the clack of a loom behind a wall, the grind of a quern, hooves on stone somewhere below, wind working a chimney, and beneath it all the occasional deep, settling *tock* of the mountain itself — the rock is still rising, a finger-width a year, and it announces this in small dry cracks that nobody looks up from their work to acknowledge.

Vegetation is rationed by the land. Cushion plants grow in the lee of boulders, hard green domes a century old and no wider than a hand-span. Lichen does the real work of color here — burnt orange, sulfur yellow, sea green, painting the fresh rock faces in maps. Where the seeps come out of the stone there are pockets of genuine softness: moss, a coarse blue-stemmed grass, a low gnarled shrub whose roots the people are forbidden by custom to pull. These wet pockets are the lungs of everything. They are also property, inheritance, the cause of most feuds.

For all its scarcity of green, the place is crowded with animals — this is the paradox the first travelers never believe until they see it. The fauna runs on lichen, on the seep-grass, on each other, and on the cities themselves. Vast herds of a shaggy, dense-wooled grazing animal move along the benches in slow seasonal circuits, half-wild, loosely owned, followed rather than driven. Small round-bodied rodents whistle from the talus in such numbers that whole slopes seem to chirp. Birds own the vertical world: cliff-nesting flocks that pour off the faces at dawn like smoke running backward, scavenger-birds that ride the valley thermals all day without one wingbeat, small soot-colored sparrow-things that have given up the wild entirely and nest in the gaps of the drystone walls, so that every house breathes and rustles. At dusk something larger and quieter — a long pale cat, mostly rumor — comes down to the herd margins, and the whistling rodents go silent slope by slope, a wave of hush you can watch travel.

Nothing here is in a hurry, including change itself. The glaciers above advance and retreat on a rhythm longer than memory. A rockfall closes a pass and the trade routes bend around it for three generations until hands clear it again. The cities keep records in knotted cord and stacked tally-stones, and the records mostly say the same thing the land says: *more of this, slowly.* The mountains rise. The water beads out of the rock each morning. The herds come back along the benches. Somewhere a new course of stone is laid on a wall, and left to settle, and the mason does not expect to be the one who lays the next.
