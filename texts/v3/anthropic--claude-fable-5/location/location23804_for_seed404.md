## The Cleft at the Top of the New Mountain

The mountain is young enough that it still remembers being liquid. Its shoulders are black glass and unweathered scoria, edges sharp enough to open a palm, nothing rounded, nothing forgiven yet by rain. You climb for most of a day through warm thin air — the heat here is not the sun's but the rock's, rising through the soles of your feet — and near the summit, where there should be nothing but frost-split stone, there is a seam. A crack in the flank, no wider than a doorway, exhaling.

Inside is the hollow.

It is small. That must be said first, because it will stop being true in a moment. The hollow is perhaps twelve paces across, a pocket melted or blistered into the mountain's crown, open to a coin of sky far above. Steam bleeds from hairline fractures in the walls — the mountain's breath, wet and blood-warm — and this breath has made a garden where no garden should be. Every surface that can hold a root holds one. Ferns unspool from the glass walls in ranked spirals, each frond beaded with condensation. Mosses stack on mosses, emerald over jade over a deep bruised green, so thick the floor gives underfoot like a sleeping animal. Broad-leaved plants with no names crowd the middle ground, leaves overlapping leaves, so dense you cannot see more than an arm's length in any direction, and the air itself is thick — saturated, glittering faintly, always on the verge of becoming rain.

The water never quite falls. That is the first wrongness you notice, and the gentlest. Droplets condense on the fern-tips, swell, detach — and drift. Some sink at the speed of thought. Some rise, unhurried, toward the coin of sky, joining a slow upward drizzle that the opening never seems to receive. Others simply stop, and hang, and you can walk among them like a man walking through a paused instant, each suspended bead holding a tiny curved image of the hollow inside it. Look closely at one of these droplets and you will see the hollow it contains also has a droplet hanging in it, and in *that* one another, and the recursion does not diminish. The hollow is inside itself an unknown number of times. It seems comfortable this way.

The second wrongness is the size. Twelve paces across — you counted. But push through the wall of leaves toward the far side and you do not reach the far side. You reach more hollow. The vegetation deepens, the fractures in the walls multiply, the coin of sky stays exactly overhead no matter how far you walk. Turn around and the entrance seam is three steps behind you, always, patient. The place is a room that contains a wilderness, folded like a leaf folded into a bud. Some part of you understands that if you walked inward for a year you would still be twelve paces from the door.

**The silence.** For all its lushness, the hollow makes almost no sound. This is not the deadness of a sealed room; it is a chosen quiet, a held breath. The steam vents do not hiss — you feel their warmth and see the vapor rise, but the sound arrives late, or arrives elsewhere: sometimes you hear a soft exhalation from a vent you passed an hour ago, as if the hollow keeps its sounds in a different order than its events. Water drips — you see the drop strike the moss — and the small *tik* of impact comes a slow count of ten later, or comes first, before the drop has fallen. Mostly, nothing. Your own footfalls are absorbed. When you speak, your voice works, but it feels borrowed, and you find you do not want to spend it.

**The animals** are everywhere and unbothered. Small amphibians the color of wet slate cling to the fern stems, throats pulsing, watching you with gold-flecked eyes. Snails with shells like coiled smoke graze the mosses so slowly their motion is a matter of faith. There are birds — you see them constantly, small and dun and quick, flickering between leaves — but never in flight; they are always *just having landed* or *just about to leave*, transitions apparently handled somewhere off-page. None of them make a sound. A moth the size of two spread hands rests on the black glass wall, and its wing-pattern is a precise map of the hollow, including a small pale mark where you are standing. When you move, you do not quite want to check whether the mark moves too.

Some of the animals are doubled. A slate frog and its exact twin sit a hand's width apart, blinking in unison, and it is not clear which is the original or whether the question means anything here. The hollow seems to reproduce things it likes, the way a still pool reproduces the sky — casually, without effort, without asking.

**Someone came here before you.** Not many, and not often, and long ago — but the marks remain, scattered and small. A cairn of five stones stacked beside a steam vent, each stone worked by hand, ground smooth against other stones, no metal in their making. A length of plaited plant-fiber cord knotted around a fern's base in a pattern of deliberate knots — nine of them, or eleven; the count changes, mildly, like weather. Half-buried in moss: a scraper of chipped obsidian, its edge still fresh, mountain-glass shaped by patient percussion, the flake-scars crisp as the day of its striking. On one wall, at shoulder height, a small spiral has been pecked into the glass with a harder stone, the work of many quiet hours. The spiral is pecked *into* the wall, but if you look at it from the corner of your eye, it stands out from the wall instead — a relief rather than a groove — and it takes real attention to make it lie back down.

Whoever they were, they built nothing large. No shelter, no hearth-ring bigger than a hand-span, one place where three flat stones lean together into something like a tiny table or a tiny altar, holding a single fern frond that has never wilted. Their tools say hands and stone and cord and time. Their marks say they came here the way you might visit something you feared and loved, left small proofs of respect, and went back down the mountain while going back down was still possible.

**The pace of the place** is glacial and certain. The reality-fractures do not lash or storm; they *accrue*. A leaf here has been in the middle of unfurling for what the growth-rings of the moss beneath it suggest is years. The suspended droplets migrate a finger-width per day. Twice, maybe, in a long visit, you will feel the hollow shift — not an earthquake but a *re-settling*, like a great body adjusting in sleep: the recursion deepens by one layer, the delayed sounds all arrive at once in a soft two-second rush of drips and breath and wingbeats, and then the silence closes back over everything and the coin of sky is very slightly a different shade.

The warmth never fails. The steam never stops. The mountain is young, its features unsoftened, its interior still deciding what it will be — and the hollow feels less like a flaw in it than like a thought it is having. A small, dense, green, recursive thought, warm and wet and nearly silent, growing more intricate at the speed of stone.

Leave a stone on the cairn when you go. Everyone seems to have. The cairn is five stones tall no matter how many are added, and the hollow accepts the extra ones somewhere, into some inner twelve paces you will never reach, and holds them, patiently, along with everything else it has decided to keep.
