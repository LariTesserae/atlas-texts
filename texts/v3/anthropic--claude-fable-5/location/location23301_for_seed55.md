**The Drowned Floor**

Begin with the dark, because the dark comes first and stays longest. This is not the absolute black of a cave. Far overhead — so far that "overhead" has lost most of its meaning — there is a memory of light, a grey-green dilution at the very ceiling of the water, the way a closed eye remembers a lamp. None of it reaches the floor. What reaches the floor is the cold the light leaves behind: a steady, mineral cold, a degree or two above ice, distributed so evenly through such a volume of water that it no longer feels like temperature. It feels like a property of the place, like its color, which is black, or its speed, which is almost none.

And the floor — this is the thing you understand slowly, the way you'd understand it if you walked it in darkness, by hand and knee. There is no seabed here. There is no mud, no bedrock, no natural substrate at all, anywhere, in any direction, for distances that should be measured the way continents are measured. The floor is **paved**.

Not paved in any grand or uniform way. Paved the way a thing is paved when it is done by hand, by uncountable hands, over a span of time long enough to flatten mountains. Flagstones the size of doors. Bricks of fired clay, each one bearing — if you press your fingertips into it — the shallow trough of a thumb dragged through wet material before the firing, an individual gesture, repeated several trillion times. Courses of fitted stone, dressed with stone tools, the tool marks still legible under the fingers as a fine parallel pecking. Ramps that rise a few meters and subside. Long low terraces, their steps worn to suggestion. Causeways that go nowhere now because everywhere is the same depth of dark. Whoever made this had no metal worth speaking of, no engines, nothing but mauls and chisels of harder stone, fired clay, rope, levers, patience, and generations. They did not build *on* the world. Over enough time, they *became* the world's surface. Whatever natural ground once existed lies somewhere beneath, under strata of older pavement — because the floor is layered, and where it has slumped open you can feel the edges of earlier floors below it, and earlier ones below those, construction all the way down past the reach of an arm into a crevice.

Then the water came, or the floor went down, or both, slowly, in the manner of this place, where nothing has ever been sudden. And then a very long time passed. The erosion here is not violent; there are no waves at this depth, no storms, only the patient solvency of cold water and the faint perpetual current that crosses the whole pavement like a draft under a door. Edges that were once square are now rounded like river stones. Inscriptions — there must have been inscriptions — survive only as variations in smoothness, a texture under the palm that almost resolves into intention and doesn't. Walls have been planed down to footings. Towers, if there were towers, are now broad gentle swellings in the floor, like the burial mounds of buildings. The whole built continent is settling toward a single elevation, low and even, a made plain relaxing into its final shape. It is most of the way there.

On this pavement grows the forest.

It should not be lush here, in the cold and the dark, and yet it is — a dense, layered, slow vegetation that has learned to live on almost nothing. Closest to the stone: a continuous felt of black-red weed, finger-deep, soft as soaked wool, holding the joints of the masonry together more faithfully than mortar ever did. Above that, beds of broad-bladed grasses, the blades thick and rubbery and dark as bottle glass, leaning all one way in the weak current and then, hours later, all the other way. Above those, the canopy: ribbon-plants anchored in the old post-holes and door-sockets and quarry seams, rising on stalks ten and twenty meters, their fronds spread wide to harvest the trace of light filtering down and the slow rain of dissolved nourishment drifting along the current. They grow so close together that to move among them is to be touched constantly — frond after frond drawing across the shoulders, cold and slick and weightless, a crowd of presences with nothing behind them. Where two terraces meet, the growth thickens into true thicket, near-impassable, the stalks a hand's width apart. Everything photosynthesizes at the very edge of possibility, which means everything grows slowly, which means everything here is *old* — individual stalks that have stood for centuries, holdfasts gripping stones that were themselves shaped by hand thousands of years before the flood.

What the forest lacks is everything that should live in it. No fish. No drifting plankton-shimmer, no urchins grazing the felt, no shelled things in the crevices, nothing burrowing in the silt pockets, because there is no silt — there is nothing to die and make silt except the plants themselves, and they decay in place, slowly, feeding their own roots. The food web here has exactly one strand. You can hang motionless among the fronds for an hour and nothing will swim past you, nothing will startle, nothing will watch. The absence is not sinister. It is simply complete, the way the pavement is complete. The water is too cold, too dark, too settled; animal life needs more hurry than this place has ever offered.

The sound matches the fauna, which is to say it is nearly the sound of nothing. But not quite. There is the hiss of one's own blood, and beneath it, if you are still enough, the place's own small noises: the creak of a long stalk flexing in the current, like a rope taking strain very far away. A single click, hours apart, as a flagstone somewhere readjusts its ten-thousand-year position by a millimeter. The almost-subliminal rushing tone of the current itself crossing the canopy of an entire continent, less a sound than a pressure on the ears. Once in a great while, a soft prolonged sigh — a pocket of ancient air or gas, trapped in some sealed chamber under the third or fourth pavement down, finding a new seam and bleeding upward through the weed in a thread of bubbles that no eye is there to see catch what little light exists.

Weather happens here, because the place is large enough to make its own. Not storms — gradients. A cold seep crossing the plain over a season like a front, the weed in its path slowing its growth, paling. A slow eddy hundreds of kilometers across that takes years to complete one rotation, combing all the canopy of a whole province gradually around to face a new direction. A turbid drift, fine particles shed from some distant region where the masonry is failing faster, dimming the already negligible light for a decade and then passing on. The plants record all of it in their growth rings and lean. Nothing else records anything.

And everywhere, under everything, the hands. That is the residue the place keeps best. Not the builders themselves — no bones, no tools, nothing that soft survives this much time and water — but the evidence of effort at the smallest scale: the chisel-pecked face, the thumb-trough in the brick, the slightly irregular spacing of stones laid by a tired crew at the end of some unimaginably distant working day. A world made entirely by hand, without machines, simply by never stopping; then drowned; then dressed in a slow black forest; then left in the cold and the near-dark to finish flattening, which it is doing, patiently, at the rate of perhaps a hand's-breadth per thousand years, in the quiet, with no one to hurry it and nothing left to interrupt.
