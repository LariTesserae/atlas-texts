**The Underroost**

Begin with the lip of the first sinkhole, because that is the only way in: a collapse so recent the broken edges still show unweathered stone, pale as a snapped bone where the rest of the country's rock has gone grey. The walls drop forty meters in clean fractures — no rounding, no softening, nothing the rain has had time to do. Blocks the size of carts lie at the bottom exactly where they fell, sharp-cornered, stacked in the angular geometry of things that have not yet learned to settle. The land above is nothing — a dry, scrubbed plateau, a few hard grasses. The land below is everything.

You descend into a country.

This is not a cave in the singular sense. It is a system of galleries and collapsed chambers running under the plateau for distances no one has walked the whole of — a sequence of dim halls strung together like beads, each one punctured somewhere by a doline, a skylight, a fault that lets down a column of weak grey daylight. The light arrives already tired. It has been strained through cloud and then through depth, and what reaches the floor is a permanent dusk, the kind of illumination by which you can see shapes and movement but never quite color. The walls fade into darkness long before they end. You navigate by the light-shafts, which stand in the distance like pale pillars holding up a black ceiling, sometimes one visible, sometimes three at once, kilometers apart.

It is cool here, and it is always exactly this cool. The rock holds one temperature the way deep water does, and neither summer nor winter on the plateau above makes any argument the stone is willing to hear. The air is damp without ever quite raining — condensation beads on every undersurface, gathers along the fresh fracture-edges, and falls in slow, irregular drops. Where the drops have been falling longest there are shallow pools, scattered, a dark mirror here, another a hundred paces on, each one ringed with a slick of pale mineral. The dampness is in the breath of the place. You taste it: stone, ammonia, wet feathers, something faintly sweet underneath, like fermenting fruit, which is the smell of the guano fields.

Because the true inhabitants of this country are not visible at first. They are audible.

The sound is the roof. From the moment you reach the floor of the first chamber, it is there — a vast, granular roar overhead, the layered noise of wings and voices in numbers that the mind refuses to count. Millions of bats hang in the dark vaults, and among them, in the dimmer reaches near the skylights, swiftlets in their tens of thousands, clicking, clicking, each one sounding its way through the black with sharp percussive ticks, so that the whole ceiling crackles like a fire that gives no heat. Beneath the clicking is the deeper texture: the chirring of the bats, the leathery rustle of a hundred thousand small adjustments, the occasional rolling surge when some disturbance — a falling stone, a hawk's shadow crossing a doline — sends a wave of motion through a colony, a sound like wind moving through a forest that isn't there. It never stops. It never crescendos into chaos and never dies into quiet. It holds, hour after hour, at the same enormous level, a noise so constant it eventually becomes a kind of silence you have to remember to hear.

Look down and the second nation reveals itself. The floor below every roost is a soft, dark, mounded terrain of guano, meters deep in places, and it is *alive* — not figuratively. The surface moves. Cave crickets in glossy thousands, pale beetles, centipedes the length of a hand, blind white crabs picking sideways along the pool margins, cockroaches in such density that the ground glitters faintly when your eye adjusts, the whole surface shifting with a dry papery whisper that you feel through the soles of your feet as much as hear. A single dropped feather is dismantled before it finishes settling. This is the economy of the place: the flying nations above rain down sustenance, and the crawling nations below convert it, endlessly, and nothing is wasted, and nothing changes hands in any direction but down.

Vegetation barely participates. Only directly beneath the skylights does anything green attempt the floor — a sparse stand of ferns here, a single spindly tree there, grown tall and starved reaching for its column of light, moss in a thin ring at the edge of the bright circle and nothing beyond it. These small green islands stand kilometers apart in the dark like oases inverted: instead of water in a desert of sand, light in a desert of dark. The animals ignore them, mostly. The plants are guests. The fauna owns the deed.

And there are signs — faint, old, and few — that this place is known. On a wall near the second doline, at the height of a reaching arm, a handful of handprints in red ochre, the pigment blown around spread fingers so the hands appear as pale negatives. A smear of ancient charcoal low on one block. In a dry alcove above the guano line, a small scatter of knapped stone — flakes, a broken point, the debris of someone sitting and working an edge by the borrowed light, long enough ago that a film of mineral has begun to glaze the chips. No walls, no hearths, no dwellings. Whoever comes here builds nothing and takes only what falls: eggs from the low nests, perhaps, fledged swiftlets, crabs from the pools. They mark the wall and go. The place permits visitation. It does not permit residence.

What unsettles, eventually, is not the noise or the moving floor. It is the stillness underneath both. The plateau above has weather, seasons, drought, change. Down here there is none of it. The temperature does not move. The drip from the ceiling falls at the pace it fell yesterday and will fall tomorrow. The colonies do not grow and do not diminish; the roar at dusk when the bats pour up through the dolines in dark rivers — twenty minutes of thunder, the skylights briefly black with bodies — happens at the same hour, at the same volume, in the same spiraling order, and then the swiftlets pour down through the same openings to take the same roosts, two nations passing each other in the shafts of failing light in a rotation so exact it reads less like behavior than like mechanism. The sharp young stone will dull eventually; the pools will deepen; the guano will rise. But on any scale a life can witness, the Underroost is finished. It arrived at its equilibrium quickly, the way young things sometimes do, and it has decided, with the full weight of its millions of small lives, to stay.

You climb back out at the first sinkhole, up the fresh-broken blocks, and the roar fades behind you into the rock — and the silent plateau, with its honest sky and its weather, feels strangely thin, like the lid of something, which is what it is.
