# The Green Steading

It sits in a saddle between two young peaks—rock so newly broken from the mountain's interior that its edges still cut, gray faces unsoftened by lichen except where the settlement has reached up to claim them. The pass is small. You could walk its length in two hundred paces, if walking were possible here. It mostly isn't. There is no ground left to walk on.

Everything is plant.

Not a forest. A forest implies trunks and clearings, the negotiation of space. This is closer to a town that grew instead of being built—and at first glance, that is exactly what you'd call it: a hamlet of perhaps thirty structures, walls and roofs and lanes, all of it pitched into the cold saddle of the pass. Only nothing here was raised by hands. There are no hands. There are no cut beams, no laid stones, no rope, no nail, no fire-blackened hearth. The architecture grew this way, and is still growing.

The walls are pleached masses of a dwarf, high-altitude willow—a species that, left alone, would crawl flat along the rock to escape the wind. Here it has not been left alone. Something in the way the plants of this place crowd each other has forced the willow upward into thick, braided panels, branch grafted into branch where they touch, the grafts swollen and pale like knuckles. The panels rise to roughly the height of a tall person, then curve inward and knit overhead with their neighbors. The result is rooms. Lanes. Doorways that are simply places where the growth has not yet closed—and some of them are visibly closing, a season or two from sealing shut. The settlement edits itself. What was a passage last year is a wall this year. The whole town is a draft being continuously revised by its own materials.

The roofs are moss, half a meter deep, every shade from black-green in the shadows to a sour gold where the thin light strikes. The moss does the work that gutters and cisterns would do elsewhere. The air at this altitude is wet without raining—cloud drags through the pass most days, and the cold wrings water out of it constantly, so that every surface beads and weeps. The moss drinks the condensation, saturates, and releases it in slow threads down the inner faces of the willow walls. Step inside any of the rooms and you'll find one wall always darker than the others, glistening, a vertical seep feeding a basin of cupped leaves at its base. Every room has its weeping wall. Every room has its small black pool. The town waters itself, ceaselessly, quietly.

The light is poor and would be poor even at noon. Cloud above, walls of growth on all sides, the moss roofs admitting only a dim green glow where they thin. Most of the settlement exists in a permanent underwater-colored gloom, and the plants here have adapted to it the way deep-forest species do: enormous leaves, dark as liver, angled to catch whatever falls. Where a roof has a gap, a column of pale gray light stands in the room below like a resident, and the floor beneath it is always a riot—the one place where the smaller plants, the sorrels and cold-country ferns and a low cushion-plant that grows in domes like loaves of bread, fight openly for the beam.

And fight they do, at their own pace. The density is total. There is no soil visible anywhere; the floor of every lane is a pavement of cushion-plant and moss over a meter or more of compressed older growth, generations of the town's own dead tissue, still cold enough that it rots slowly and holds its shape. Walk on it (you can, just barely, in the lanes) and it gives like a mattress and springs back, leaving no print after an hour. The walls bulge inward in places where two willow panels are contesting the same air. You can find spots where a branch from one wall has grafted across the lane into the opposite wall, forming a low arch you must duck under—and the arch is thickening. In ten years it will be a wall, and the lane will be two rooms.

It is cold. Not the dramatic cold of deep winter but a thin, persistent, just-above-freezing cold that never fully leaves, even in what passes for summer. Frost forms most nights on the outer moss and burns off slowly through the morning, so the settlement spends its early hours rimed white, steaming faintly as the rime sublimates, the whole town smoking gently like something just pulled from cold water. The plants tolerate this by being slow. Everything here is slow. The growth that has built these walls and lanes represents decades of patient centimeter-work—and yet the settlement is *young*. You can feel it. The architecture has the awkwardness of early drafts: rooms too small to be useful, lanes that dead-end against raw rock, one whole quarter on the eastern side that is clearly only half-built, the willow panels there still waist-high and gappy, the moss thin, the rock beneath still showing its fresh fractured gray. The town is maybe a century old, in a landscape where the peaks themselves are young. It arrived early. It is still figuring out what it wants to be.

Sound is mostly the sound of water. The seeps tick and patter. Pools overfill and find new threads downhill, and you can hear, faintly, water moving *under* the floor—the whole settlement sits on a slow subsurface drainage, meltwater and condensate filtering through the dead-growth pavement toward the lip of the pass, where it emerges below the eastern quarter as a single cold spring, the town's one outlet, the closest thing it has to a gate. Wind crosses the saddle above the roofs in long exhalations but rarely gets inside; the lanes are still. Now and then a willow panel creaks as it shifts against a graft. Now and then a saturated mat of moss lets go of a roof edge and drops with a soft wet thump. These are the loudest events of most days.

Animals are scarce and seem to know they're guests. A single kind of small bird—dust-colored, round, nearly silent—works the inner walls for whatever lives in the moss, and there cannot be more than a handful of them in the whole settlement. There is something larger that uses the lanes at night; the cushion-plant floor shows faint pressed trails by morning, gone by noon, and no one watching has ever seen what makes them. The trails always follow the lanes. Whatever it is, it respects the architecture, or was perhaps the thing the architecture was shaped around—it is hard, in a place like this, to tell which party is accommodating which.

Because that is the strangeness of the steading, the thing that becomes unignorable the longer you stay: the layout is *too good*. The rooms drain correctly. The lanes connect. The weeping walls all face inward, toward the basins, as if plumbed. There is a structure at the center of the settlement larger than the rest—a single chamber the size of a small chapel, its willow walls braided to twice the usual height, its moss roof domed, its interior holding one column of light and one black pool and nothing else—and it sits at the exact convergence of every lane, the way a well or a shrine would sit at the heart of a built village. No one built it. Nothing with tools has ever been here. Either the plants of this pass have, in their slow century of growth, blundered into the shape of a town by pure accident of wind and water and competition—

—or a town is simply one of the shapes that life takes, when it is packed close enough, in the cold, with nowhere else to go.

The settlement does not answer the question. It just keeps closing its doorways, opening new ones, weeping down its walls, drinking the cloud. By the time anyone could decide what it is, it will have grown into something else.
