## The Roof of the Old World

You arrive at an altitude where the planet has almost finished with itself.

This is a plateau the size of a continent — though "plateau" suggests something raised above other land, and here there is no other land in evidence, no descent visible in any direction. The surface simply continues until it curves away. It sits so high that the sky has thinned to a hard, clean blue-violet, the kind of sky that no longer bothers to soften the sun. Light falls at full ordinary strength, casting honest shadows, but it falls through almost nothing, so the shadows are knife-edged and the lit surfaces have a strange, lacquered clarity. You can see for two hundred kilometers. There is very little to see, and all of it is in motion.

That is the first contradiction of the place, and you should sit with it: this is the most violently dynamic landscape you will ever stand in, and it is nearly silent.

## The Stone

The ground is a single mineral fact — a gray-gold rock worn so completely that geologists would call it *peneplain*, the end-state, the shape land takes when it has been eroded for so long there is nothing left to erode. No outcrops. No boulders. The surface undulates in swells a kilometer long and a meter high, like a sea that froze and then was sanded for an eon. Where you kneel and look closely, the rock has a polish on it, a faint sheen, as though every grain that could be plucked free was plucked free ages ago and what remains is only the grains that interlock too tightly to leave.

Run your glove across it and you feel almost nothing. No grit. The dust is gone. All the dust this continent will ever produce was produced, lifted, and carried away long before anything walked here.

The cold is real but not cruel — a steady, dry chill, the temperature of a cellar or an autumn dawn, maintained not by season but by altitude. Frost exists here the way rumor exists: you find it occasionally, a gray-white film in the lee of a swell, a few crystals deep, and it is the only water you will encounter. There are no channels, no dry beds, no salt stains. Whatever rain this world once had finished its work and left. The land has been *paid off*. It owes the water cycle nothing.

## The Storm

And yet.

Stand still for a minute and you notice the horizon is wrong — it shivers. Look up and the violet sky is not empty; it is *seething*. Vast translucent structures move through the upper air, lens-shaped, braided, collapsing and reforming — a storm system the size of nations, churning in air so thin it cannot carry its own voice down to you. The wind at ground level arrives in long pressure-swells you feel against your chest more than hear: a push, a release, a push. At this density, a gale delivers only a whisper of force and a sound like someone breathing two rooms away.

This is the second fact of the place: the turbulence never stopped. The atmosphere here has been in storm-state for — no one knows. The storm and the stone reached equilibrium with each other in deep time. The wind long ago carried off everything it could carry, ground down everything it could grind, and now it rages over a surface it can no longer change. Maximum violence, zero consequence. The storm has become a kind of weather-fossil: a chaotic system so old it is, paradoxically, the most stable thing in sight.

Watch it long enough and you start to see structure in the chaos — vortices that recur in the same places, drawn there by the kilometer-long swells of the rock, the land's last shapes dictating the storm's habits. The two of them, stone and air, finishing each other's sentences forever.

Sometimes a pressure-front passes directly overhead and the light flickers — the thin air lensing, the sun's disk wobbling like a coin underwater — and for a few seconds your shadow trembles on the polished rock without anything touching you.

## What Grows

Vegetation here is an act of stubbornness rather than abundance.

Walk an hour and you may pass three living things. They are low, dark, vitreous growths — call them *glasswort mats*, though they are nothing like the Earth plant — colonies a hand-span wide that don't rise from the rock so much as fuse with it, their tissues hardened into a translucent shell against the desiccating air. They are the color of bottle-glass and old blood. They photosynthesize slowly, on geological patience, drinking the frost films and the trace humidity the storm occasionally smears across the ground. Some of the mats, when you find them, are arranged in faint arcs and lines too regular to be accident — and that is your first hint about the artifacts.

Because some of the vegetation was *planted*. Or seeded. Or printed. The arcs trace the perimeters of things that are no longer there.

## The Artifacts

Scattered across the continent — one every fifty kilometers, perhaps, never two in sight of each other — are the structures.

They are small. That is what unsettles people most. A civilization capable of what these objects do should have left cities, and instead it left things the size of a chapel, a wellhead, a standing stone. A typical one: a spindle of white material, six meters tall, narrower than your arm at its waist, standing in the storm at an angle no unbraced object should hold. It does not move. The pressure-fronts part around it — you can see the air's distortion split a few meters upwind, like water around a bridge piling, except the spindle isn't touching the air at all. There is a calm zone around each one, a teardrop of stillness perhaps thirty meters long, and inside it the silence becomes total. Travelers shelter there. The glasswort grows thickest at the edges of these teardrops, in the seam between storm and calm, where the turbulence drops its trace moisture like a tide leaving things on a beach.

The material of the spindles is not warm or cold. It returns no echo to instruments. Where one was — apparently — broken, long ago, the stump shows no interior: the break-surface is the same seamless white as the outside, as if the object is the same substance all the way through, or as if it has no "through."

Nobody knows whether they are weather machines, markers, anchors, or graves. The most credible guess, made quietly, is that they are *holding the storm* — that the eternal turbulence overhead is not natural at all, that it is being maintained, stirred, kept from ever settling, by these scattered white needles, for reasons that died with their makers. Under this theory the whole sky is an artifact, the largest machine ever built, still running, doing its enormous silent work above a finished world.

There are other remains. A circle of fused ground, glass-smooth, two hundred meters across, with the kilometer-swells of the bedrock passing through it undisturbed. A line of low pylons marching toward the horizon, every third one missing, the gaps occupied by columns of perfectly still air you can feel with an outstretched hand. A half-buried ring of the white material through which the wind, and only the wind, refuses to pass.

## What Lives

Animals are rare enough that each sighting becomes a story.

The known fauna is essentially one creature, seen perhaps a dozen times by any traveler in a lifetime: a glider — flat, broad, dark, a living kite three or four meters across, with no visible head and a texture like slate. It rides the storm at enormous altitude, surfing the pressure-fronts, and descends only to the calm-zones around the spindles, where it settles onto the rock at the teardrop's edge and grazes the glasswort with an underside you will never see. It makes one sound, very occasionally: a single low tone, felt in the sternum more than heard, lasting several seconds. No one has heard two gliders answer each other. It is possible there is only ever one in the sky at a time. It is possible there is only one.

## Standing There

So this is the place, taken whole:

A worn-flat continent at the top of the atmosphere, lit by ordinary honest daylight, cool and dry as the inside of an old book. Above it, a permanent planetary storm, raging in air too thin to roar, its violence reduced by altitude to a breath against your jacket and a shimmer at the horizon. Beneath it, stone that finished changing before anything now alive existed. Scattered across it, at vast intervals, small white objects of an engineering no current physics describes, each one holding open a pocket of perfect silence — and possibly, between them, holding up the storm itself.

The deepest strangeness here is mild, almost gentle, and it is this: motion and stillness have traded places. The sky, which should pass, is permanent. The land, which should endure, is over. You stand in the calm shadow of a spindle, in silence so complete you hear your own pulse, and watch a hurricane the size of a hemisphere wheel soundlessly overhead — chaos as the final, settled state of things, turbulence grown so old it has become a kind of peace.

When you leave the calm zone, the wind touches you again. It feels less like weather than like attention — the old machine of the sky, noticing, briefly, that something in the finished world still moves.
