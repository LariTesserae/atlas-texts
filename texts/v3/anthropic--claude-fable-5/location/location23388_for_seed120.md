**The Greenhouse at the Top of the Unfinished Mountain**

You reach it after the air has thinned past usefulness, after the switchbacks have stopped pretending to be a path, after the mountain itself has stopped pretending to be finished. Up here, near the summit, the rock gives up its disguise. It is not weathered. It has never been weathered. Nothing has ever touched it — no rain has run down it, no frost has split it, no wind has carried a single grain of it away. The stone is raw the way a poured thing is raw, the way bread dough is raw, edges crisp as the day they were intended, surfaces still faintly tacky, as though the mountain were set out to cure and someone keeps interrupting.

And then there is the door, which is not a door so much as a seam where the basalt was lapped over itself like pastry, and behind the seam there is the room.

The room is small. You could cross it in eleven steps if the steps stayed where you put them. It is the only interior the mountain has, a single pocket chamber tucked just under the peak like a seed under a thumbnail, and it is *hot* — wet-hot, breath-of-an-animal hot, a heat that comes up through the floor in slow pulses, because the floor is the youngest part of the mountain and the mountain is still being made.

Everything in here is made. That is the first thing you understand and the thing you never stop understanding. The walls are coursed — actual courses, like brickwork, except the bricks are slabs of proto-stone laid wet and still glistening at the joints, and the mortar between them is a gray paste that has not decided whether it wants to be clay or lichen or sentence. The ceiling is wattle: enormous withies of something like green bamboo, bent and woven by hand — you can see the hand-marks, the slight asymmetry of human or human-adjacent labor, the places where the weaver got tired and the weave loosens — and daubed over with red mud that drips, constantly, warmly, onto everything below. There is no natural substrate anywhere. Even the puddles seem placed.

Whoever builds here builds with the oldest tools. They lie about in the corners, in niches, half-sunk in moss: a basalt adze with a hide-wrapped haft. Bone awls. A maul of unfired clay that should not function as a maul and visibly does, its working face dented with use. Coils of cordage twisted from plant fiber, some of it still rooted, still growing, lengthening on its own spool with a faint creak. There is no metal except one small copper thing — a little crescent, hammered, almost an offering — set into the east wall where it sweats green and is, you slowly notice, the only object in the room that casts a shadow in a consistent direction.

The light is dim and it comes from the wrong places. From the joints between the stone courses, mostly — a low amber seepage, as if the mortar were translucent over a banked fire — and from certain leaves. Because the room is *crammed* with vegetation. Lush is not adequate. The chamber is occupied wall to wall: tree ferns shouldered together so tightly their fronds have interleaved into a single canopy a foot above your head; mosses in such deep pile that the floor's geometry is conjectural; pale climbing figs that have grown over the woven ceiling, into it, and apparently been *incorporated* — woven back through the wattle deliberately, plant and architecture finishing each other's work. Nothing has room. Everything is touching everything. Condensation beads on every surface and runs down in warm threads; the walls weep; the weeping is part of the construction, mineral-rich water laying down a film of new stone, hourly, so that the room is fractionally smaller every day, or would be, except—

Except the room is also larger every day. Both are true. This is the place where they stop excluding each other.

This is what the noise is. You expected silence at altitude, in a hidden room, and instead the chamber roars softly and continuously, a layered loud: the percussive slap of wet daub being applied by no visible hand; a deep tectonic drone from below, the mountain's curing-sound; the click and squeak of vegetation growing at audible speed; and under it all a rhythmic knocking, patient, hand-paced, stone on stone, somewhere behind the walls — the sound of a worker you will never see, building outward, or inward, or building the concept of *outward* itself. Sometimes the knocking syncopates with your heartbeat and you have to sit down in the moss until it stops doing that.

Weather happens in here, despite there being no sky. A cycle, roughly hourly: the heat thickens, the ceiling's red daub darkens and sags, and then it rains — a brief, hard, hot rain falling from the woven ceiling onto the ferns, who drink it loudly. After the rain the seam-light brightens to nearly gold for a few minutes. The ferns angle toward the brightest joints. There is an ecology here, complete and frantic, in eleven paces.

Fauna is rare and provisional. There is one salamander. There may only ever be one salamander, blood-warm and slate-colored, and it is not always continuous — you will see it on a stone, and see it under a frond, and not see it travel between, and once you will see it on both at the same time, fixed in each place, regarding itself with what looks like professional interest. It is the only animal the room has gotten right so far. In a niche near the copper crescent there is something half-finished that might one day be a bird: a wickerwork of fine ribs, packed with moss, with two seeds where eyes will go. It is warm to the touch. Sometimes, during the rain, it breathes.

The longer you stay, the more the room admits what it is. Not a shelter. Not a temple. A *workshop* — the small hot wet hidden chamber where this mountain, perhaps this world, is being assembled by hand, course by course, frond by frond, with stone tools and patience, from the inside out. Erosion has no purchase here because nothing has been released yet. Everything is still in the maker's grip.

When you leave — back through the lapped seam, into the thin cold actual air — the summit above the room is slightly taller than when you arrived. The knocking continues behind you, unhurried.

The mountain is not old. The mountain is *early*. You were inside the part that hasn't happened yet.
