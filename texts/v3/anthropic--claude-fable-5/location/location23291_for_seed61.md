**The Drowned Borough**

You reach it by crawling — there is no other way in, and there never was. A throat of limestone, polished smooth not by tools but by ten thousand years of bodies sliding through, opens after forty feet into a chamber no larger than a granary. You can stand at its center and nearly touch both walls. And yet what fills it is a city.

The sound arrives before the sight does. It arrives, in fact, before you finish the crawl: a vast, layered roaring, far too large for the space that contains it. Somewhere behind the eastern wall a river runs through the rock — has run there, by the look of the wall's fluting, since before anyone thought to come here — and the chamber sits against it like an ear pressed to a chest. The whole room is a resonating body. Every drip from the ceiling lands inside that roar and is amplified by it, struck like a note against a drone, so that the chamber is never quiet and never the same loudness twice in a second, though across years it is always exactly this: thunder, held still.

The city was carved to live inside the thunder. Whoever made it — and the tool marks say flint, antler, patience, generations — cut it directly from the living floor of the chamber, a borough of buildings none taller than your knee. Streets the width of two fingers run between them. There are stairways with steps the size of fingernails, descending into doorways that go nowhere, or that go somewhere only darkness has visited. There are towers, granaries, what might be a market square with a basin at its center, walls within walls, districts crowded against districts with the density of a real city that grew rather than was planned — except it did not grow. It was carved this way: already old, already crowded, already settled into itself. A city made in the image of long habitation.

And then the water got it.

The ceiling weeps. Not dramatically — a slow, ceaseless condensation, the breath of the river coming through stone — and every drop that has fallen for however many thousand years has fallen on the same rooftops, traced the same gutters, pooled in the same fingernail courtyards. The city is sheathed in flowstone now. Calcite has rounded every corner the carvers cut sharp. The towers wear caps of sinter like candle wax; the streets are glazed; the market basin has become a perfect circular pool, lipped in white stone the water itself deposited, so that the city has, in a sense, finished building itself — the makers began it and the mountain has been completing it ever since, at a rate of perhaps a thumbnail's thickness per century. It is hard to say where craft ends and accretion begins. That is the chamber's central fact: it is no longer possible to take the city apart from the cave. They have annealed.

Nothing here changes. The temperature is the temperature of deep rock, cool as a cellar in any season, in any century. The humidity is total and constant; your breath does not fog because the air is already at its limit. The drips fall where they fell. The river roars at the pitch it roared. If you came back in a hundred years you would find the flowstone imperceptibly thicker and everything else identical, down to the position of the small standing pools in the streets — pools the size of coins, black and absolutely still despite the noise, because sound here lives in the air and the rock but not, somehow, in the water.

The light is the strangest mercy. There is no shaft, no opening. Instead, threads of something — not moss, not fungus exactly, something simpler, a film — grow in the seams where water seeps fastest, and they hold a faint grey-green luminescence, dim as the last minute of dusk. It is just enough. Just enough to see the city as a city and not as texture under your hands. Whether the film was here before the carvers, or whether they brought it, cultivated it, smeared it into the seams deliberately as a kind of permanent lamp, no one can say. It runs along the streets, though. It pools in the doorways. It outlines the borough the way windows outline a town at night, and it is difficult to believe that is an accident.

Life is rare and pale. A species of blind, colorless isopod — flat, segmented, the size of a grain of barley — grazes the luminous film, and so the only animals in the city walk its streets, slowly, between its buildings, like citizens. You might watch for an hour and see two of them. Once in a great while something longer and threadlike crosses the market square and disappears into a doorway, and does not come out while you watch, and you find yourself thinking about that doorway afterward.

There is one true strangeness, beyond the strangeness of the place existing at all. At the city's heart, where a real city would have its temple, the carvers cut nothing. They left a column of raw, unworked stone — the only unworked surface in the borough — and the dripwater, falling around it but never on it, has built the city's flowstone up to its base on every side like a tide that refuses to touch it. After all these millennia it stands matte and rough amid the glaze, a fist of original mountain. Whether it was left out of reverence, or whether the city was carved as a frame for it, an offering arranged around something that was already here and already listened to — the roar of the river gives no answer. It only continues, enormous and unchanging, the voice of a place that finished becoming itself long ago and now simply, loudly, is.
