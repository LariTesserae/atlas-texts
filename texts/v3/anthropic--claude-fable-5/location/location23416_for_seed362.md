# The Dry Green

There is no horizon here in the usual sense. The land does not curve away — it *recedes*, in the manner of a hallway, and at the far end of that recession the green simply becomes brightness and the brightness becomes green again, and somewhere in that exchange the eye gives up keeping accounts.

This is a manufactured flora the size of a continent, possibly the size of a world; no survey has closed the loop. Everything underfoot, everything overhead, everything that stands in rows to the limit of vision was *made* — but made by growing, which is the only way anyone has ever made anything here. There is no bedrock. Dig down and you find root-lattice, and below that older root-lattice gone glassy and amber with age, and below that, in the deep cores, root-lattice arranged in patterns too regular to be accident and too strange to be design, as if the place had been founded on its own blueprints.

## The Substrate

The ground is a deck of pressed leaf-matter, dead growth compiled into something like parquet, like cork, like the floor of an enormous greenhouse that has digested its own architecture. It is dry — profoundly, mineral dry. Touch it and it has the temperature of a cellar and the texture of old book covers. Nothing here has ever been wet. There is no smell of soil, no rot, because rot requires water; instead the dead material is *resorbed*, drawn back down through capillary channels as a fine pale dust, and the air carries a scent like crushed fennel and warm electronics and the inside of a seed.

The plants do not drink. Whatever chemistry runs them runs on light and on a thick, slow, oil-dark medium that moves through their stems with the patience of grease in a cold machine. Where a stalk has been broken — and breaks are rare, the place maintains itself — the cut surface beads with this fluid, which hardens in minutes to a green-black resin, smooth as solder.

It is cool. Not cold — *cool*, the specific coolness of shade in autumn, persistent and total, despite the light. The light gives no heat. This is the first thing visitors notice and the first thing that begins to bother them.

## The Light

The light is everywhere and comes from nowhere, which is to say it comes from the vegetation itself. Every leaf is a lamp. The undersides of the canopy glow with a white-green radiance like sun through young birch leaves, but multiplied, layered, reflected between surfaces until the whole interior of the land is lit like an operating theater, like noon inside a cathedral of frosted glass. There are no shadows of the ordinary kind. Objects here cast *dimmer light* rather than darkness — a person standing on the leaf-deck is surrounded by a faint penumbra of reduced brilliance, a soft grey aura on the floor, and this is the closest thing to a shadow the place permits.

There is no sky visible, or rather: sometimes there is. Occasionally the canopy thins and one can see upward through ten, forty, a hundred strata of luminous growth, and at the top of that column there is a pale region that might be sky or might simply be more of the same, farther away. The light up there is not noticeably brighter. The light down here is not noticeably dimmer. Illumination is not conserved in this place. It is *issued*.

## The Architecture of the Growth

Imagine an industrial district designed by topiary. The dominant structures are stalks the girth of grain silos, ribbed and fluted, rising in disciplined ranks, spaced with the regularity of pylons. Their surfaces are a dense, dry chlorophyll-felt, deep viridian, soft to the touch and warm with light though cool with temperature. Between the great stalks runs the *machinery* — and it is machinery, unmistakably, though every component is plant tissue:

Conveyor vines, broad as roads, that creep along at the pace of a minute hand, carrying ranked cargoes of seed-cases the size of footlockers. Flywheel blossoms, enormous radial flowers that turn — slowly, perpetually, frictionlessly — on woody axles, doing work whose purpose has not been determined. Bellows-pods that inhale and exhale on a cycle of about eleven minutes, ventilating the deep interior with that fennel-and-circuitry air. Banks of leaf-vanes arranged like louvers, like cooling fins, like the gills of an engine, all of them slightly in motion, all of them silent.

And it *is* silent — that is the second thing that bothers visitors. A factory this size should roar. Instead there is a hush like deep snowfall. The conveyor vines move without sound. The flywheel blossoms turn without sound. What sound exists is small and dry and intermittent: the papery tick of a leaf adjusting its angle, a faint settling creak from somewhere in the substrate, and beneath everything, at the threshold of hearing, a sustained low tone like a transformer two rooms away — the sound, perhaps, of the light being made.

## Density

Nothing here is far from anything else. The growth is layered to the point of near-contact: stalk against vane against vine against pod, leaving only the corridors — and the corridors are everywhere, a circulatory maze of green-walled lanes wide enough for two people abreast, floored with that cool parquet of compiled leaves, lit to brilliance, branching and rejoining without apparent plan. Walk one for an hour and you will pass perhaps ten thousand distinct organs of the machine, each one within arm's reach, each one minutely busy.

The density is not oppressive. It is *occupied*, the way a beehive is occupied, except that the bees are absent and the comb has learned to keep itself.

## Where It Breaks

This is the part that the maps refuse.

The place is old and settled — weathered into its own equilibrium, every edge softened, every surface bearing the patina of long function — and somewhere in that long settling, its relationship with ordinary space came loose at the joints. The breakdown is not violent. It is *administrative*, gentle, woven into the order of things like a clerical error repeated so many times it has become policy.

Distances do not always agree with themselves. A corridor walked north for an hour may be walked back south in four minutes, and both journeys will feel correct. Certain ranks of stalks repeat — not similar stalks, but the *same* stalk, identifiable by the same resin scar, the same nesting hollow, recurring every few hundred meters down a row like a single frame of film printed many times. Whether there is one stalk or thousands is a question the place declines to answer; cut a sample from one and all of them bear the wound.

In some districts the light arrives before its source. A flywheel blossom will brighten a half-second before it turns; a leaf will cast its glow onto the floor a moment before unfurling. Travelers learn to read these *forelights* the way sailors read weather — a corridor that brightens ahead of you is about to grow.

And there are the **misfolds**: regions, usually small, a few meters across, where the geometry of the growth has been compiled wrong. Inside a misfold, a vine may pass through a stalk without intersection, occupying the same space in polite disagreement. Leaves there show their front and back faces simultaneously. The ambient tone deepens near a misfold, the only place it ever changes, dropping to something felt in the sternum rather than heard. The vegetation does not avoid these regions; it grows through them, accepting their grammar, and the growth that emerges on the far side is subtly *translated* — its symmetry handed, its green shifted a degree toward a color that has no name and photographs as grey.

The locals — there are no locals; the few who study the place — believe the breakdown is not damage but *function*. That the factory's product is not the seed-cases or the resin or the light, but space itself: that somewhere in its silent depths the place is manufacturing geometry, and the misfolds are its workshops, and the repeating stalks are its inventory, and a continent of corridors is the warehouse where finished distance is stored before shipment to wherever distance goes.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, in the animal sense. The machine needs no pollinators; it pollinates by conveyor.

But there are the **lamplickers** — that's the field name — small creatures, palm-sized, somewhere between a moth and a gecko, the dusty grey-green of old copper roofs. One might be seen every few days. They cling to the brightest leaf-surfaces and appear to graze on light directly, leaving behind brief patches of true shadow, the only honest darkness the place contains, which heal over in minutes like frost melting in reverse. They make no sound. They are not afraid of anything, having apparently never needed to be. When a lamplicker enters a misfold it comes out the other side mirror-imaged, and seems untroubled by this, and possibly this is how they reproduce: one animal, folded into two.

There is also, by persistent report, something large. It is never seen, only inferred: corridors found freshly widened, their walls regrown smooth; a section of the deep tone gone briefly *interrupted*, like a hum with a body passing through it; the leaf-parquet bearing, very rarely, a long shallow depression, warm to the touch in a land where nothing is warm. The studies call it the Resident and have stopped looking for it, on the theory that the place is hiding it deliberately, the way it hides the sky.

## The Cycles

The land has seasons, though no sun drives them. Every few weeks the light shifts a half-tone toward gold, and the whole continental machine enters **shed**: leaves release in their billions, falling without wind in dead-straight lines, a silent dry snowfall of luminous flakes that keep glowing on the floor for a day before the substrate draws them down. During shed, the corridors fill ankle-deep with cool light, and walking through it is like wading in the idea of water — the only water-like thing the place will ever offer.

After shed comes **the press**: the deck audibly tightens, a continent-wide creaking that lasts an hour, the loudest the place ever becomes — still no louder than a house settling at night — and then the new growth begins, pale jade, unbearably bright, and the cycle resumes.

And once, perhaps twice a year, the breakdown itself has a season. The forelights run long — light arriving whole minutes before its sources — and the repeating stalks fall briefly out of sync with themselves, each copy doing something slightly different, like a choir losing unison. For a few hours the place is *plural*. Then, with no transition anyone has managed to witness, it is singular again, settled, silent, in balance: a vast cool green machine in a state it arrived at long ago and has no reason to leave, lit from within, dry as a kept secret, growing more of itself, or more of somewhere else.
