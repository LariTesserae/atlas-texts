**The Green Anvil**

The plateau is young the way a scab is young. It came up out of the earth within living memory of the rock itself — a great table of basalt shoved two kilometers into the sky, its edges still sharp as broken china, uneroded, unargued-with. No river has had time to saw a canyon here. No glacier has had time to grind a cirque. The land is all first draft: blunt scarps, raw fracture planes, columns of stone standing in the hexagonal patterns they cooled into, never yet softened by a single winter's worth of patience. Geologists would call it embryonic. Standing on it, the word that comes instead is *unapologetic*.

And every square meter of it is alive.

The moss arrived before anything else and never ceded ground. It is not a covering here so much as a second crust — a continuous pelt of green half a meter deep in places, sodden as a wrung sponge, that has sheathed the entire upland in one organism-like mass. There are no trees. There is no soil in the ordinary sense; the moss *is* the soil, the surface, the architecture. It rounds every column of basalt into a knuckle. It hangs off the scarp edges in dripping curtains, lime-green going gold where the light hits hardest. Walking on it is walking on a saturated mattress laid over knives: each step sinks to the ankle, exhales cold water around your boot, and slowly, over the next hour, the print fills back in. The plateau erases you at the pace of plant growth, which is the only pace anything here respects.

Because the place is so high and the air so thin, the light is violent. There is almost no haze; the sun comes down undiluted, and the wet moss throws it back from a billion droplet-lenses, so that the whole tableland glitters — not gently, but with a hard, mineral brilliance that makes you squint even with your back to the sun. Cloud sits far below, pooled around the plateau's base like a white sea, and the green country floats above it in permanent glare. It is cold despite the light. The brightness has no warmth in it; it is the cold brightness of altitude, light that illuminates without comforting, and your breath shows in it all day.

The water never stops. That is the first thing, the last thing, the thing underneath all other things: the *sound*. The plateau wrings moisture from every cloud that drags across it, the moss holds it, and the young rock — too unfissured, too unweathered to absorb anything — sheds it off every edge at once. The rim of the tableland is a near-continuous cataract, dozens of falls dropping clean two-kilometer drops into the cloud sea, and the roar of them rolls back up over the lip and across the whole upland as a single sustained pressure. It is not loud the way a storm is loud. It is loud the way the inside of an engine is loud: constant, structural, without rhythm. After an hour you stop hearing it as sound. You hear it as a property of the air, like its temperature. People who work here learn a hand-language, because voices are useless beyond arm's reach, and they say that descending below the cloud line afterward feels like going deaf — the silence down there has a wrongness, a missing organ.

The work is what the scattered structures are for. Spaced far apart across the green — one to a horizon, sometimes — stand the turbine houses: squat buildings of riveted iron and poured concrete, streaked with rust-orange where the iron weeps and furred green where the moss has begun its slow climb up their north walls. Each one sits at the head of a fall, swallowing a portion of the water into penstocks that plunge down the scarp face, fat black pipes bolted to the raw columnar basalt, vanishing into cloud. Inside, the turbines spin day and night, adding their own deep mechanical hum under the cataract roar — felt in the sternum more than heard. From each station, power lines march off across the moss on tarred wooden pylons, single-file, dwindling, carrying the plateau's one export — current — down to whatever country lives under the clouds. The lines sing faintly in the wind, though you only know this from touching them.

Between the stations: distance. The plateau is generous with emptiness. You can walk half a day across the green and pass nothing but one pylon line, a rain gauge on a leaning pole, a cairn of basalt chunks someone stacked to mark a route, its stones already mossing over, already being claimed. A few maintenance cabins exist, corrugated-roofed, anchored with cables against the wind, their interiors smelling of paraffin and wet wool. The crews rotate up and down by a cog-rail cut into one of the gentler scarps; you see its smoke sometimes, a thin grey thread climbing out of the cloud sea, the only motion on the horizon for hours.

Animals are nearly absent. The moss feeds almost nothing that walks. There is one bird — a dark, heavy-bodied petrel-like thing that nests in the spray zones at the lip of the falls, where the thunder is worst, perhaps because nothing else will tolerate it. The linemen call them *anvil birds*. They are seen singly, riding the updrafts off the scarps, silent as far as anyone can tell, though in this place everything is silent as far as anyone can tell. Once in a great while a fox appears at a cabin, lured up the rail line by garbage, looking baffled and overexposed in all that light, and is gone within the week.

Nothing here hurries. The plateau is still rising — a few millimeters a year, the surveyors say, enough that the benchmark bolts driven into the rock decades ago no longer agree with the maps. The moss advances over the concrete at the pace of moss. The falls cut into the rim so slowly that no one has lived long enough to see a notch form. Even the industry has settled into the landscape's tempo: the turbines were built by grandparents of the people who oil them now, and the plan, such as it is, extends generations forward. The only fast thing in the entire country is the water itself, falling — and even that, watched from the rim, seems to descend in slow motion, white ropes unraveling endlessly into the cloud, deafening, dazzling, going nowhere new.

It is a landscape with two voices and one of them is unbearable and the other is too slow to hear: the roar of the water, and underneath it, the green, growing.
