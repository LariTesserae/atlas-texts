**The Curing City**

The basin is shallow and wide—an old floodplain, maybe, though nothing about it is old anymore. Whatever river made this lowland has been absorbed into the project. You can still trace its course: a soft trench of darker green winding between the towers, where the moisture gathers and the moss grows thickest.

The city is approximately eleven weeks old.

You can tell by the edges. Everything here is sharp the way only new things are sharp—corners that haven't been touched, thresholds without wear, surfaces so freshly formed they still carry the faint stretch-marks of their own emergence. The buildings did not go up; they *came out*, extruded or condensed or precipitated from below according to a process the air still remembers. Some of them are not finished. You can find towers that taper into rough, mineral-looking stubs at their summits, like broken stalks, except they are not broken—they are paused. Now and then, in the warm dark, one of them adds a few centimeters with a sound like a knuckle cracking very far away.

**Light.** The dimness is not weather and not time of day. The sky overhead holds a deep, even, slate-violet glow, the kind of light a place gets twenty minutes after sundown, and it stays that way. The leading theory—if there were anyone here to hold theories—would be that the city is metabolizing the light. Photons go in; very few come back out. Illumination, where it exists, is local and pooled: a soft pearl sheen along the underside of a bridge, a slow gold seep at the base of a wall, light lying in the gutters like rainwater that forgot to drain. If you cup your hands in one of these pools, it clings to your palms for a few seconds, then sinks back through your skin and is gone. It doesn't hurt. It feels like being remembered.

**Materials.** The buildings are made of something between ceramic and bone and overcast sky. Pale grey-green, matte, faintly warm to the touch—everything here is faintly warm to the touch, the whole basin runs a couple of degrees above blood heat in the structures themselves, as if the city has a pulse it keeps low. The material sweats. This is the source of most of the dampness: condensation beading on every fresh surface, gathering in the sharp new gutters, dripping in slow irregular rhythms from cornices that have never seen a full year of rain. The city is *curing*, the way concrete cures, exhaling moisture as it sets. Walk down any avenue and you walk through its breath—humid, mineral, with an undertone like crushed fern and warm slate.

Rain does come, but rarely and strangely: brief, vertical, hyper-local showers that fall on a single block while the next street stays dry, as if the sky were watering specific addresses.

**The green.** The vegetation arrived with the city, or just behind it, and it is winning everywhere the architecture permits. Broadleaf growth, glossy and dark in the low light—it shouldn't be possible, this lushness under this dimness, which means the plants are eating something other than sun. Their leaves angle toward the light-pools, toward warm walls, toward the seams between buildings where the curing-sweat runs richest. Vines climb the towers in deliberate-looking spirals. Moss upholsters the lower meter of nearly everything. In the old river-trench, tree-sized ferns stand in loose congregations, fronds beaded with condensation, and the air there is so green-smelling it's almost a taste.

The strangest of the plants are the pale ones: stalks the color of the buildings themselves, growing in courtyards and along certain boulevards in tidy intervals too regular to be wild. It is not clear whether they are plants imitating architecture or architecture practicing being plants. Cut one—you shouldn't—and it bleeds the same slow light that pools in the gutters.

**Space and silence.** The scale is generous and the density is low. This is a city built at the size of a real city—you can stand on an overpass and see districts receding into the violet dimness, kilometer after kilometer of towers and terraces and the dark stitching of the green—but it is sparsely furnished with itself. Wide avenues between far-apart structures. Plazas the size of fields, empty except for moss and one inexplicable bench. Whole blocks left as bare, sharp-edged foundation pads where something has not yet decided to grow. The emptiness doesn't feel abandoned. It feels *reserved*. The city was made with room for a future that hasn't checked in yet.

So it is mostly quiet. The quiet of held breath, of a theater before the house lights drop. What sound there is, is small and specific: the tick and settle of curing material, water finding its way down a wall, a single drip in a vast colonnade keeping irregular time. Occasionally, from deep underfoot, a long subsonic note—felt in the sternum more than heard—as some buried system shifts its weight. The vegetation soaks up echoes; your footsteps land soft and stay where you put them.

**Fauna.** Animals are rare, and the ones that exist seem to be auditioning. A heron-like bird, slate-colored, stands in the light-pools the way herons stand in shallows, and now and then strikes downward and comes up with a beakful of glow, which it swallows. Small things move in the moss—quick, damp, never quite seen. There is something that calls twice a night from the fern-trench, a low double note, and nothing ever answers it, and it has not stopped trying. The city tolerates them all with what feels like patience. Possibly they are tests. Possibly they are tenants who arrived early.

**Rules.** The strangeness here is structural, not theatrical. The city obeys most physics most of the time, but it was engineered past the edge of what physics fully describes, and the seams show in quiet ways:

Distances are advisory. A boulevard walked at dusk-pace takes twenty minutes; hurried, it takes longer. The city seems to prefer that you not rush, and enforces this gently, geometrically.

Streets revise. Not while watched—never while watched—but a route taken twice is rarely identical twice. A stairway gains a landing. A canal that was on your left is now, reasonably, plausibly, on your right, and the moss on its banks is exactly as thick as it would be if it had always been there. The city is not finished deciding what it is, and its drafts overlap.

Reflections lag. In the standing water and the wet black windows, your image arrives a half-second late and leaves a half-second after you do, as if the city's record-keeping runs slightly behind its present.

And once in a while, on the long empty avenues under the slate-violet sky, with the warm walls sweating and the ferns dripping and the light lying in the gutters, you get the distinct sensation of being *expected*. Not watched—expected. A door standing open on a lit interior with one chair. A table in a courtyard, beaded with condensation, set for a number of people that is not zero.

The city is eleven weeks old. It is curing, breathing, growing its pale stalks and its dark ferns, ticking and settling, keeping its light banked low like a fire husbanded through a long night. It is, in the patient way of very new and very advanced things, waiting for whoever it was made for—and it has all the room, and all the warmth, and very nearly all the time.
