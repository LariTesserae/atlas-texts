**The Roost of Old Drum**

The mountain has finished. That is the first thing to understand about it. Whatever violence raised it happened so long ago that no edge remains anywhere on its body — the summit is a worn knuckle of grey-gold stone, polished by a hundred million years of wind into the shape of a closed fist, and on the flat top of that fist, in an area you could cross in two hundred paces, sits the entire settlement.

There is nothing above it. There is very little beside it. The peak stands so high that it has passed out of weather entirely — the clouds live far below, a dim pewter floor that the inhabitants call *the lake*, though no one living has ever touched water that fell from a sky. Rain does not reach here. Rivers are a rumor from the trading songs. The air is thin, dry as old paper, and strangely warm: the stone itself holds heat, a deep mineral warmth left over, the elders say, from when the mountain was young and angry, seeping up through every floor and wall so that the whole village is heated from beneath like bread on a hearthstone.

The light is the second thing. The sun up here does not behave as it does in the lowland stories. It crosses low and oblique, filtered through a permanent high haze of fine pale dust — rock flour, the last breath of the mountain's erosion, suspended so high it never settles. The result is an endless amber dusk. Noon is the color of lamplight through horn. Shadows are long all day and blue-grey, and the people navigate as much by sound and touch as by sight, running their hands along walls worn glassy by ten thousand other hands.

---

**The vegetation should not be possible, and the villagers know it, and tend it anyway.**

Without rain, the green here is a green of *patience*. The summit is sleeved in waxglove — a thick-leaved, resinous shrub whose roots descend impossibly far into the warm fractures of the rock, drawing up moisture that the mountain has hoarded in its deep body since the age of storms. The plants do not drip; they *hold*. Cut a waxglove leaf and it bleeds a slow clear sap that the villagers collect in stone cups, a teaspoon a day per plant, never more. There are cushion-mosses the color of old bronze growing wherever the warm air vents from cracks, and ancient dwarf trees — eleven of them, each named, each older than the village's memory — whose trunks have grown so slowly and been wind-burnished so long that their bark is smooth as furniture. The trees grow leaning, all in the same direction, recording a wind that has not changed its mind in geologic time.

Nothing here grows fast. A hedge planted at a birth might be waist-high at that child's death. The villagers do not garden so much as *negotiate across generations*; a planting is a letter to one's grandchildren.

---

**The village itself is dense as a wasp nest.**

There is no room to spread, so the settlement has folded in on itself. Every structure is dry-stone — there is no mortar, no metal worth speaking of, only the mountain's own flakes and slabs fitted by hand with a precision born of having nothing else to do for a thousand years. Houses share walls. Houses share *roofs* — the roof of one is the courtyard of the one above, and the whole village climbs itself in four or five interlocking tiers, connected by stairways so narrow that two people meeting must turn sideways and press together, which is why the village greeting involves touching shoulders. Doorways are low and rounded, worn at the lintel by ten thousand ducking heads into a shallow smooth scallop, like a stone licked by the sea no one here has seen.

The tools are stone, bone, and a little soft cold-hammered copper carried up generations ago and now heirloom-rare. Lamps burn waxglove resin with a low orange flame and a smell like pine and pepper. Everything is hand-made and everything is *old* — a basket might be a great-great-grandmother's, repaired so many times it is a genealogy of weaving styles.

---

**And the noise. The noise is the soul of the place.**

For all its stillness — and almost nothing here moves quickly, not the plants, not the light, not the people, who walk with the deliberate economy of those who live where falling is forever — the summit is *deafening*, because the summit does not belong to the people. It belongs to the birds.

The cliffs that drop away on every side, those sheer worn faces falling toward the cloud-lake, are riddled with old fracture-hollows, and every hollow holds nests. Sicklewings, sooty criers, the small bronze swifts that the children catch and release for luck — tens of thousands of bodies, wheeling in slow permanent gyres around the peak, riding the warm air that bleeds off the stone. Their sound never stops. It rises at dawn to a roar like surf, settles by night to a vast murmuring rustle, the whole mountain breathing feathers. Villagers learn to pitch their voices through it. The drum that gives the village its name — Old Drum, a hollowed section of the largest dead tree — exists because no shout can cross the summit, but the deep stone-floor resonance of the drum can.

The birds are also the economy. They bring up, from the unseen world below the clouds, everything the summit cannot make: seeds in their crops, fish-bones, bright lowland feathers, once a brass button that hangs now in the meeting house as an object of theology. The villagers harvest eggs by careful ancient quota, gather guano for the terrace-pots, and read the spirals of the flocks the way lowlanders are said to read rivers. Beneath the birds live the smaller nations: warm-crack lizards fat as a thumb, dusty moths that swarm the resin-lamps, the slow grey summit beetles that the children race, unsuccessfully, on feast days.

---

Change comes here, but it comes the way the mountain itself changed — by grains. A wall leans a finger's width per century. A name shifts pronunciation. The eleventh tree puts out, perhaps once a decade, a single new leaf, and when it does the drum is sounded and everyone comes to look at it in the long amber dusk, the birds roaring around them, the warm stone underfoot, the clouds far below going slowly dark — a small finished place at the top of a finished world, loud with life, in no hurry at all.
