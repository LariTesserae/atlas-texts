**The Standing Chamber**

You reach it by descending through a fracture that opened — geologically speaking — yesterday. The rock has not had time to soften. Every surface in the descent is a fresh break: conchoidal scallops of black glass, edges keen enough to part a glove, dust that has not yet learned to settle into corners because the corners themselves are new. There is no water anywhere. There never has been, here. The stone smells of struck flint and hot ozone, the dry mineral smell of a place that chemistry built without ever consulting rain.

The chamber itself is not large. Cathedral is the word people reach for, but it's a modest cathedral — a single nave of fractured obsidianite perhaps forty meters end to end, the ceiling close enough that you feel it as a presence on the back of your neck. The walls have not been eroded into curves. They are planes and prisms, cleavage faces meeting at angles a crystallographer could name, every join sharp as the day the pressure let go. In the dim light they don't glitter so much as *suggest* — long faint gleams that slide along the fracture planes when you move your lamp, like the chamber is tracking you with closed eyes.

The light is not yours alone. It comes, mostly, from the engine.

Someone built here. Not a settlement — there isn't room, and there's no record of who. What remains is scattered: a toppled gantry frame fused at its base into the floor; a row of ceramic insulators bolted along one wall like vertebrae; a cable conduit, armored and humming, that emerges from solid rock on one side of the chamber and disappears into solid rock on the other, as though the cave grew around an infrastructure that predates it. And at the chamber's heart, half-sunk into a fresh fracture as if it fell into the stone while the stone was still deciding to exist: the engine.

It is industrial in the honest, heavy sense — flywheel, stator housing, riveted plates gone matte with mineral bloom. It runs. It has, by every measurement anyone has made, always run, at exactly the same speed, drawing power from the through-cable and producing nothing anyone has identified except heat, a dim sodium-colored glow from a vent grille, and the sound.

The sound is the chamber's true architecture.

It is loud — genuinely loud, a sustained chord around ninety decibels that you feel in your sternum before you finish the descent. But it is loud the way a waterfall in a painting is loud: it never varies. Not a flutter, not a beat-frequency wobble, not the breathing irregularity of any real machine. The chamber's sharp geometry has caught the engine's drone and folded it into a perfect standing wave. Walk the floor and you cross nodes and antinodes like invisible furniture — here the sound crushes in on you, three steps later it nearly vanishes, and these zones of pressure and silence are *fixed*, mapped, the same today as in every survey ever taken. The chamber is a frozen shape made of noise, and the rock is merely its container.

The life arranges itself accordingly.

For a place with no water, it is shockingly crowded. The fauna are dense, layered, near-touching — but they do not move the way animals move. The dominant species is a finger-length thing the early surveyors called a *hummer*: chitinous, eyeless, six-anchored, the color of cooling slag. They cluster in the antinodes — the loud places — in stacked colonies, hundreds deep, each one trembling at precisely the engine's frequency. They are not enduring the sound. They are eating it. Their bodies are piezoelectric; the standing wave flexes their shells and the flexing drives their slow dry metabolism, and so they crowd into the pressure zones the way other creatures crowd around hot springs. They do not migrate. The wave does not move, the food does not move, and so the herd has held the same positions, as far as anyone can tell, since the chamber broke open. Individual hummers presumably die and are replaced, but the *shape* of the colony — its drifts and banks and stacked terraces along the loudest wall — is constant, like a flame that never flickers.

In the nodes, the quiet zones, live the things the hummers will not enter: pale flattened crawlers that scavenge shed chitin, slow as continental drift; thread-thin worms of metallic sheen that braid themselves through the cable conduit's heat-fins; and in one node near the gantry, a single large presence — a thing like a folded leather bellows the size of a dog — that no one has ever seen move, but which is warm to the touch and was in a measurably different position in two surveys taken eleven years apart. It is the most dynamic thing in the chamber. This is not saying much.

Vegetation, such as it is, is sparse and confined to the engine's glow: a few patches of rigid bronze-green growth, more lichen than plant, photosynthesizing — if that's even the word — on the dim sodium light and the engine's exhaust chemistry. They grow in the shape of the light, hard-edged shadows printed in living tissue. They do not spread. The light does not change, so neither do they.

The warmth is everywhere and even — engine heat distributed through a closed volume until every gradient flattened out. Thirty-one degrees at the floor, thirty-one at the ceiling, thirty-one inside the hummer drifts. There is no weather, no day, no convection worth the name. The air itself barely circulates; you can watch your own dust hang.

What unsettles visitors is not the noise, in the end. The noise becomes a kind of silence within an hour — perfectly steady sound and no sound are difficult for a nervous system to tell apart. What unsettles them is the realization, somewhere on the climb back up the sharp young fracture, that they were the only changing thing in the room. The engine runs. The wave stands. The herd trembles in place, feeding on a chord that has no beginning anyone has found and shows no sign of an end. The chamber is not waiting for anything. It is not becoming anything. It arrived, fresh-broken and fully formed, at its single eternal moment — and everything that lives there has agreed, in chitin and bronze lichen and riveted steel, never to leave it.
