**The Clamor Flats**

The land here finished its arguments with itself long ago. Whatever mountains once stood have been carried away grain by grain over an age so long it has no useful name, and what remains is a plain of gray stone worn nearly to the curvature of the world — a peneplain, the oldest stable state rock can achieve, leveled so completely that water no longer knows which way to go. It pools instead. It sits. It seeps upward through hairline seams and beads on every surface, so that the whole region, miles across, glistens like the inside of a shell.

There are no plants. There have never been plants here, or if there were, the stone has forgotten them along with everything else. What grows instead is film: slicks of mineral-feeding life in colors that don't quite commit — pewter, oxblood, a green so dark and so thinly spread it reads as a shadow with an opinion. These mats coat the shallow pans of standing water, the weeping faces of the few low ledges, the bottoms of the channels where cool water moves with no apparent gradient, just a slow regional drift, as if the plain itself were breathing it from one side to the other.

The light is the light of a permanently overcast latitude, dim and sourceless and cold. Everything wet — which is everything — gives back a dull silver. There are no hard shadows. Distance is measured not by haze but by the way the standing water tiles toward the horizon, pan after pan after pan, each one a slightly different gray, until they merge into a sky the same color and the seam between earth and air simply isn't there.

It should be silent. It is the loudest place anyone has ever stood.

---

The fauna own this plain in numbers that beggar counting. They are colonial, amphibious things — call them *criers*, since that is the only thing they reliably do — built low and slick, the size of a forearm, with throats that occupy most of their bodies. They eat the mats. They eat each other's young when the mats run thin. They haul out in rookeries of tens of thousands wherever the stone humps even a hand's width above the water, and they call. Not at dawn, not at dusk — those distinctions barely exist under this sky — but continuously, in shifts, in overlapping choruses that travel across the flat water with nothing to absorb them.

The sound at the edge of the flats is a wall. Inside the flats it is a medium, like the water, like the air — a roaring, ratcheting, gulping totality that you stop hearing as sound after the first hour and begin to feel as pressure, as weather. The plain is so flat and so wet that the calls reflect, and reflect again, and travelers who have crossed it (few do) report that somewhere near the center the echoes outnumber the voices, and the chorus seems to come from *below*, from the stone, as if the criers were merely the visible fringe of something the bedrock itself was saying.

And there is this, the small wrongness the flats are known for: the choruses synchronize. Not always. But several times in a day's crossing, without warning, a rookery will fall into unison — ten thousand throats hitting the same pulse — and the unison will spread, colony to colony, across miles, faster than sound should carry it, until the entire plain is pounding out a single rhythm like a heart laid open to the sky. It lasts a hundred beats, sometimes a thousand. The standing water trembles into rings. Then it dissolves back into roar, and no one, not the criers and not the people, knows what was decided.

---

There are people, or have been. Their marks are sparse and unhurried.

Every few miles: a cairn of stacked wet stones, shoulder-high, slick with mat-film, marking routes across the pans where the water stays shin-deep. Here and there a *listening blind* — a low horseshoe of dry-laid rock, just tall enough to crouch behind, its open end always facing away from the nearest rookery. Not for hunting from. The walls are too low to hide a hunter. They seem instead to be shelters from the sound itself: a place where the chorus drops from deafening to merely enormous, where a person could sleep, or think, or speak to another person and be heard.

The tools left behind are simple and good. Bone gaffs for hooking criers off the rookery edges. Scrapers of chipped flint. Egg-baskets woven not from plant fiber — there is none — but from criers' own gut and sinew, dried hard. In one half-collapsed blind, a flat stone with seventy-one small cup-marks ground into it in a spiral, each cup holding a bead of seep-water, purpose unknown. The people who made these things came seasonally, it seems, took eggs and meat, stacked their cairns a stone higher, and left before the synchrony season peaked — or stayed through it, behind their walls, listening. The blinds are oriented with too much care for it to be only practical.

---

Processes overlap here at every scale. The water drifts. The mats bloom and gray out in slow waves you can see from any slight rise, weeks of color moving across the pans like cloud-shadow. The criers follow the blooms; their predators — pale, long-bodied swimmers that hunt the channels, eyes reduced to seams, navigating by the choruses they cup with flared ears — follow the criers. Rain comes through in cool, brief curtains that don't so much fall as thicken the air, adding a hiss to the roar, and afterward the whole plain steams faintly, the stone exhaling.

Nothing here is becoming anything else. The land is done; it reached its final form before the criers, before the mats, perhaps before sound itself had anything to echo from. The flats are what remains when a world stops changing — and on top of that finished stillness, an unfinished riot: water seeping, life screaming, the old stone lying flat beneath it all and ringing, mile after gray mile, like a struck bell that has decided never to stop.
