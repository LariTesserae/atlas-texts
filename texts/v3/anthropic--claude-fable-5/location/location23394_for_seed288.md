**The Dry Vault of Old Sessern**

You come down to it the way the air does — through long, tilted fissures in the highland, descending out of weather and into something older than weather. The light dwindles in stages. First the gold goes, then the blue, until what remains is a brown dimness that seems less like illumination than like the memory of it, leaking down through ceiling cracks a hundred feet overhead.

And then the sound arrives, before the city does. It comes up the passages like pressure: a vast, layered roar that you feel in the sternum before the ear sorts it into parts. It never stops. People who have stood in it say you do not get used to it so much as you stop expecting silence to exist.

The vault itself is enormous — not a cave but a buried country, a hollow running miles under the plateau, its ceiling lost in haze and shadow, its floor a rolling terrain of stone worn so smooth it looks poured. Whatever river hollowed this place departed an age ago. It left its signature everywhere — fluted walls, scalloped ceilings, channels rounded like the insides of bones — and then it withdrew so completely that not a seep remains. The air is cool and astonishingly dry. Dust here is not grit but a soft mineral flour, ankle-deep in the still places, undisturbed for so long that a footprint becomes a permanent feature, a small act of geology.

A city stands in this dryness. Stood, rather — Sessern has been settling into its final shape for longer than anyone has had a name for it. Its builders quarried nothing and forged nothing; they cut their dwellings out of the living stone with the stone's own grain, so that now, after the slow sanding of millennia of dust-fall and bat-wind, the architecture has begun returning to landscape. Stairways have softened into ramps. Doorways have rounded into grotto-mouths. Whole terraced districts climb the cavern walls like natural strata, and only the regularity gives them away — the rhythm of openings, the long horizontal streets carved into the rock face, the plazas too level to be accident. There are no tools left, no vessels, no marks of writing that erosion has spared. The city is pure form now, intention worn down to geometry.

The districts are far apart. That is the strangeness of the place: it is urban in its bones but empty in its spacing, neighborhoods of carved stone separated by half a mile of bare, dust-floored dark, connected by avenues that the vanished river graded long before the builders ever borrowed them. You walk a long time between presences. Then a wall resolves out of the dimness, pierced with a hundred dark sockets, and you are among houses again.

Nothing green grows here — and yet the vault is lush. Life in Sessern is pale and hanging and slow. From the ceiling cracks, the roots of the surface forest descend in enormous curtains, some of them dropping the full height of the cavern, braided columns of living wood thick as towers, blind and white where the dimness is deepest. Where the roots reach the old city they have taken it the way ivy takes a ruin, splaying across facades, prying at lintels with the patience of centuries. Around their bases spread the fungal gardens: shelf upon shelf of fruiting bodies, ghost-colored, ash-colored, the occasional astonishing rust-orange, fed not by rain but by the city's one great wealth —

The guano. Because the true citizens of Sessern, the population that replaced the builders, hang from every ceiling in numbers that defeat counting. Bats in the millions, packed into the carved vaults of the upper districts, which catch and shape their voices like instruments. This is the roar: the colony's endless social thunder, chitter and shriek and the leathery applause of wings, multiplied by stone halls that were — perhaps — built to carry the human voice, and now carry this instead. Beneath the roosts, the mounds of droppings rise in soft dark hills, and on those hills an entire ecology stands: beetles in glittering congregations, pale crickets with antennae longer than their bodies, centipedes, blind salamander-things that have not seen the sun in their lineage's memory, and the small swift predators that hunt them through the dust. The vault smells of ammonia and cold stone and mushroom — sharp at first, then simply the smell of the world.

Twice a day the city changes, and these are the only fast things that ever happen here. At dusk the colony pours out through the ceiling fissures in a single continuous ribbon that takes hours to pass, and the roar shifts pitch and rises until the air itself seems to be tearing. At dawn it pours back in. Between these tides, change in Sessern is measured in dust: a millimeter a century on the windless plazas, the slow blunting of a carved edge, a root-column thickening imperceptibly against a wall it will eventually, in some unhurried millennium, bring down.

Stand long enough in one of the far districts, where the roosts thin and the roar softens into something almost bearable, and the place discloses its essential character. It is not a ruin, exactly, and not a wilderness. It is a city that has finished. Everything that was going to happen to it has happened. What remains is the long afterward: cool air moving through carved streets, white roots descending like slow rain that never lands, and over everything, eternal, enormous, the sound of the living dark talking to itself.
