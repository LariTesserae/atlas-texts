**The Steeped Hamlet**

There is a notch high in the mountains where a village once sat beside a small cold lake, and where the lake, over some patient century, decided to sit on the village instead. No catastrophe is recorded in the stonework — no toppled walls, no scorch, no scatter. The water simply rose, a finger's width a year, the way water does when an outlet silts shut and nobody is left who remembers how to clear it. Now the whole hamlet stands drowned beneath four or five fathoms of alpine water, and the surface above it is just a bright lens through which the sun arrives, wavering, generous, almost ordinary.

The light here is the first surprise. At this altitude the water is glacially clear and the sun is close, so the drowned lanes are lit the way a room is lit through a thick window: a cool daylight, slightly green at the edges, with bands of caustic brightness sweeping across the streets like slow searchlights when the surface ripples. You can read the chisel marks on a doorpost by it. You can watch your own shadow walk ahead of you down a submerged stair.

The hamlet itself is small — a person could swim its full length in two minutes — but it is built with the seriousness of a town. Whoever made it had only hand tools and the local granite, and they spent generations on it anyway: terraced lanes stepped into the slope, retaining walls of fitted dry stone, a covered cistern, water channels cut into the street edges (a fine irony now), a compact square with a stone trough, and rising over everything a squat bell tower, four stories of corbelled masonry, its roof slabs still mostly seated. Everything is mortarless, interlocked, weathered round at the corners. The flood did not have to defeat this architecture. It only had to be patient, and the architecture, being patient itself, simply continued.

What has changed is the substance of the place. The plants have taken the town not as ruins but as *armature*. Long cold-water weeds — something between eelgrass and a freshwater kelp, ribbon-leaved, olive-dark, faintly translucent where the light comes through — have rooted in every joint and seam, and over the long years they have grown in the shapes the stone offered them. Walls are now walls of weed two hands thick, the masonry inside them more rumor than fact. Lintels are upholstered in moss so dense it has its own topography. Roof slopes carry meadows of fine green filaments that comb back and forth in unison. In places the original stone has actually slumped or dissolved out from under its green coat, and the plant mass holds the form alone — there is one archway on the upper lane that is, if you part the leaves, entirely hollow, an arch of pure vegetation remembering a doorway that no longer exists. The town is slowly becoming a plant in the shape of a town. Nobody built this second hamlet. It grew on the instructions of the first.

For all that growth, the place is not choked. The flora keeps to the structures, as if honoring property lines, and between the green buildings the spaces stay open — the square is a clear floor of pale silt and flagstone, the lanes are navigable corridors, the lake bottom beyond the last wall is a sparse plain of scattered stones and the occasional solitary weed-column standing like a planted flagpole. The hamlet reads as it always did: discrete masses with generous air between them, except the air is water and the masses breathe.

And it is *loud*. This is the thing no one expects of a drowned place. Water carries sound four times faster than air and forgets none of it. The hamlet roars, mutters, knocks, and rings, all day:

— The bell still hangs. It is bronze, hand-cast, rough as a pot, and the slow circulation of the lake — meltwater entering cold along the bottom in spring, the whole water column turning over in autumn — swings it through a few degrees, perhaps once an hour. When it touches its clapper the tone goes through the entire lake and through your chest, a long brown note that takes most of a minute to die, and every fish in the basin flinches at once like a single shaken curtain.

— The weed itself is noisy. Millions of leaves stroking each other in the current make a continuous hush, like rain on a roof heard from inside, rising and falling with the lake's slow internal tides. Gas — oxygen pearled off the leaves on bright days — gathers under eaves and ledges and escapes in irregular gulps and chimes.

— And the fauna, which is everywhere. Char hang in the lanes in loose ranks, drifting through doorways the way cats use doorways, with proprietary calm. Drumming minnows keep up a granular crackle around the cistern. Crayfish the color of old slate have colonized the stone water-channels and tick and scrape along them all night. Newts, black-backed and ember-bellied, patrol the mossed rooftops; great freshwater snails graze the walls in slow herds, leaving clean stripes of stone behind them like mowing. In the bell tower, which is the warmest still water in the hamlet, a shifting school of small silver fish occupies the belfry, and when the bell sounds they pour out of the windows in four directions at once, a slow firework, then sift back in over the following hour.

Cool is the constant. Not bitter — the high sun warms the upper water through the long summer days — but a cleanly cold, the cold of stone that has been wet for a hundred years. It is the cold that keeps the place legible: nothing rots quickly here, nothing blooms into murk. Decay happens, but at the speed of erosion rather than the speed of appetite.

Nothing here is in a hurry, including the rules. The hamlet runs on cycles too slow to watch: the centimeter of new weed a year, the millimeter of silt, the autumn turnover that swings the bell more often for a few loud weeks, the winter ice lid that seals the lake into a hush so total the snails' grazing becomes audible. Whoever cut these stones is long gone and left no successor but the lake itself, which administers the place competently — feeding it light, ringing its bell, keeping its lanes swept by current, and raising, leaf by patient leaf, the green second town inside the bones of the first.
