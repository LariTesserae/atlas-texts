# The Undercroft of the Sinks

You reach it by going down — there is no other way. The land above is unremarkable: a cold plateau of thin grass and gray stone, pocked with holes. Some of the holes are the size of a fist. Some are the size of a town. The big ones are called sinks, and beneath them, connected by galleries that the water cut and the collapses keep widening, lies a country.

It is young, geologically — almost embarrassingly young. The ceilings came down within living memory of the longest-lived things here. You can tell because nothing is softened yet. The breakdown slopes are jumbles of fresh-fractured limestone, white at the broken faces, sharp enough to cut a palm, not yet rounded by the patient grinding that ages stone. Pillars stand where the roof hasn't finished deciding to fall. Crack lines run overhead like dark lightning, and every few seasons one of them gives, and somewhere in the system there's a single enormous exhale of dust and a new shaft of daylight, and then the moss begins its work on the rubble within the year.

Daylight is the strange wealth of this place. The collapse windows are large and numerous enough that the great chambers are lit the way a forest floor is lit — generously, indirectly, the light arriving pre-filtered through hanging gardens that crowd every opening. It is a green-tinted, shifting illumination, bright at midday under the larger sinks, dimming to a long blue dusk in the connecting galleries. There is no true dark here except in the deepest sumps, and almost no direct sun. Things grow in light without ever being warmed by it.

Because it is cold. The plateau's chill pools downward and stays. The air sits at the temperature of well water year-round, and it is wet to the point of being a presence — you breathe it more than feel it. Every surface weeps. The walls run with a constant slow film of water; condensation beads on every leaf and falls in fat, irregular drops; mist forms at the cold floor of the larger chambers in the morning and unbraids itself slowly through the day, drifting in slow rivers toward the lower galleries. Water moves everywhere, and almost everywhere it moves silently — seeping, filming, pooling. The only loud water in the whole country is one cataract at the eastern sink, where a surface stream pours over the rim and falls eighty feet into a plunge pool, and even that arrives at the floor as a steady hush rather than a roar, muffled by the moss that has eaten the chamber whole.

The moss is the foundation of everything. It is the first settler of every new collapse and the final upholstery of every old one. It grows in pillows a forearm deep, in hanging curtains, in pelts that sheath the fallen blocks until the rubble fields look like the backs of sleeping animals. Above it, in ascending order of ambition: liverworts in shining dark shingles along the wettest seams; ferns in extravagant variety, some delicate as breath, some with fronds long as a person, unrolling from the talus in crowds; sedges and cold-water cresses choking the pool margins; and in the brightest chambers, beneath the widest skylights, actual trees — slender, pale-barked, moisture-loving things that grow straight up toward the openings with single-minded vertical hunger, their crowns reaching into the shaft of light like hands into a window. Nothing here is sparse. Every cubic foot that can hold life holds it. Epiphytes ride the trees, moss rides the epiphytes, and small pale snails ride the moss in numbers that become apparent only when you look closely and then cannot stop seeing.

And then there is the city.

It is not built by hands. Nothing here has hands in any tool-bearing sense, and nothing here has ever struck flint or woven fiber on purpose. But construction is everywhere, organized, layered, dense as any human quarter, and it has been accumulating since the first roofs fell.

The lower galleries belong to the dam-builders — heavy, sleek, beaver-like rodents that have taken the seep-water and the snowmelt and the cataract's outflow and engineered it into a system. Their dams are everywhere: mud and stripped saplings and stone chips packed into weirs that step the water down through the chambers in a sequence of dozens of terraced pools, each pool feeding the next through deliberate spillways. The terraces climb the breakdown slopes like paddies. The pools are clear and cold and absolutely full — of cress, of translucent shrimp, of blind white crayfish in the darker margins and sighted dark ones in the lit margins, of small fish that hang motionless in the shadows of the weirs. The dam-builders maintain it all constantly, quietly, swimming their canals at dusk with mouthfuls of mud, and the whole system has the unmistakable character of infrastructure: arteries, reservoirs, overflow channels, repairs upon repairs.

The walls belong to the daubers — colonies of cliff-nesting birds, swift-bodied and nearly silent, who build with mud and saliva and moss fiber. Their constructions cover the chamber walls in tiers: thousands of cupped nests, fused together, generations of them, the abandoned lower stories overgrown with moss and reoccupied by other tenants, the active upper stories pale and fresh. Whole walls have the look of a hillside town — terraces, clusters, alleys of bare stone between districts. Beneath the dauber tiers, salamanders the color of wet ash occupy the seepage cracks at densities that seem impossible; lift any stone near water and you will find two or three folded together, patient as monks. Burrows riddle the soil banks. Funnel webs silk over every crevice the salamanders haven't claimed. There is, genuinely, no room. Every niche has a resident; every resident has neighbors pressed close on all sides. The country is full the way a tide pool is full.

What it is not, is loud. This is the first thing that unsettles a visitor and the last thing they stop noticing. A place this crowded should rattle with noise, and instead the loudest constant sound is dripping — irregular, omnidirectional, soft, the great chambers keeping time with themselves. The daubers fly without calling, navigating their cliff-towns with faint dry clicks you feel more than hear. The dam-builders slap no warnings; they slip underwater without a ripple. The moss absorbs footfall, wingbeat, the small collisions of living. Sound carries terribly well in stone chambers, and everything here seems to have learned that long ago, because the things that hunt here hunt by ear: pale long-winged owls that drift down through the collapse windows at dusk and float through the galleries like ash, and a low-slung mustelid the color of limestone that moves along the weir-tops listening to the pools. The whole city has agreed, without agreeing, to whisper. When something does cry out — a shrew taken at a pool margin, a rockfall, the single sharp note a dauber makes when an owl enters the chamber — the silence afterward is total and lasts a long time, every terrace and tier holding still, and then the dripping resumes, and then, gradually, everything else.

The country runs on cycles you can watch. Morning mist forming and dispersing. The daylight columns swinging slowly across the chamber floors, and the salamanders and snails migrating a few feet through the day to stay in the wet shadow. The dam-builders' dusk shift, the owls' descent, the daubers' dawn exodus up through the skylights to feed on the surface and their evening return — thousands of small bodies funneling down the sinks in near-silence, like the chambers inhaling. Twice a year the snowmelt comes down the eastern cataract in force and tests every weir in the system, and the dam-builders work for weeks afterward, and the terraced pools come out of it slightly rearranged, the city revised but never abandoned.

And underneath all of it, slower than any of it, the place is still being made. Water is still hollowing the stone below the lowest pools. The crack lines overhead are still lengthening. Somewhere down-gallery there are chambers no light has reached yet, fully formed, weeping, dark, furnished with breakdown sharp as the day it fell — vacant districts waiting for their roofs to open, for the light to come in, for the moss, for everyone.
