**The Roost in the Unfinished Mountain**

You climb most of a day to reach it, and the mountain does not want you to know it is there. From outside, the peak is nothing but raw stone — a heap of dark, young rock that has not yet learned to be a landscape. No soil to speak of. No streams have had time to carve their arguments into it. The slopes are all angles and edges, basalt still sharp enough to open a palm, scree that rings underfoot like broken pottery. This is geology in its first draft. The mountain came up hot within living memory of stone — a few thousand years, maybe less — and erosion has barely begun its long editorial work. Everything is still where the eruption left it.

Then the ground exhales.

You feel it before you see anything: a breath of warm, wet air rising through a fissure, smelling of minerals and something animal. Follow it, and the fissure widens into a slot, and the slot into a throat, and you descend maybe twenty feet through rock the color of an old bruise, and the mountain opens into a room.

---

**The chamber** is not large. You could cross it in forty paces if the floor allowed, which it doesn't. It is a gas pocket — a colossal bubble frozen in the lava as it cooled, its ceiling a smooth, ropy dome where molten stone once sagged and set. In places the walls still carry the texture of flow: pleats and drips and long-tongued curtains of rock, glassy where you'd expect roughness, as if the whole room were poured rather than built. Which it was. The mountain has not had time to disguise its origins. Break any surface here and it is fresh underneath, black glass with a rainbow skin, mineral crystals no bigger than salt.

High in the dome, three cracks admit the sky. The light that falls through them is thin and grudging — the pale grey-gold of high altitude filtered through steam — and it lands in three soft columns on the floor, moving slowly across the chamber over the course of a day like the beams of an impossibly patient lighthouse. Everything else lives in a warm dusk. Your eyes adjust and never fully arrive.

**The warmth** comes up from below. Somewhere under the floor, the mountain is still digesting its own heat, and it releases it here in slow sighs. Hairline vents along the eastern wall breathe steam — not violently, nothing that hisses or scalds, just a constant gentle exhalation, the temperature of blood. The steam rises, meets the cooler air near the ceiling cracks, and condenses. The whole dome weeps. Slow beads gather on the ropy stone and fall, and have been falling for centuries, and the floor beneath the drip-lines has begun — just begun — to soften into something like the first idea of a cave formation: pale nubs of mineral no longer than a fingernail, the embryos of stalagmites that will take ten thousand years to amount to anything. The mountain is in no hurry. Nothing here is.

The water collects in perhaps a dozen small basins — flow-formed hollows in the black floor, none bigger than a washtub, most the size of cupped hands. The pools are warm, faintly cloudy, ringed with a crust of white and orange mineral. Between them the floor is dry and dark. It is a damp room in a dry mountain: moisture in pockets, never in abundance, every drop accounted for.

---

**And the room is full of voices.**

This is the thing you cannot prepare for. The chamber is *loud* — continuously, structurally loud, the way a marketplace or a waterfall is loud — and every bit of the sound is alive.

The upper walls, everywhere the rock frothed into small cavities as it cooled, are riddled with holes, and nearly every hole is occupied. Swiftlets, mostly — small, soot-colored, quick as thrown stones — thousands of them, nesting in the vesicles of the lava, navigating the dusk with sharp clicking calls that ricochet off the glassy walls and return to them shaped like the room. Their clicking never stops. It rises and falls in tides — densest at dawn and dusk when the colony pours in and out through the ceiling cracks in braided ropes of wings — but even at midnight the dome ticks and rustles and whirs like a vast dry rain.

Below them, in the deeper shadow, hang bats — a different shift, a different pitch, a leathery shuffling and a squabbling chitter that occupies the register beneath the birds. Where a beam of daylight touches the floor, black crickets the size of thumbs assemble in loose congregations and saw at the warm air. Pale salamanders, blunt-faced and slow, patrol the rims of the warm pools eating whatever falls; the pools themselves wink with small translucent shrimp that have never seen full daylight and are the color of the steam. A kind of vole or pika — round, tawny, unafraid — has worn faint paths between the guano slopes and the pool edges, and their whistled alarms punctuate the general roar whenever a shadow crosses a ceiling crack.

The whole chamber is an economy running on two currencies: heat from below and bodies from above. The birds and bats bring the outside world in — insects, seeds, the nitrogen of the wider mountain — and everything else lives off the traffic. It is one of the richest rooms for a hundred miles, embedded in one of the barrenest slopes, and the barrenness is the point: out there, nothing; in here, everything, concentrated, shouting.

**Vegetation** is scarce and precise. It exists only where the light-columns intersect the damp: three ragged circles of green on the black floor, mosses and a stiff dark fern and one twisted shrub with leaves like leather coins, growing directly out of a crack, older than it looks, fed by drip-water and centuries of guano. Along the weeping walls, in the perpetual dusk, films of something between algae and moss make faint green-gold shadows on the stone — visible only when a light column swings near, like writing that appears in certain angles of sun. That is all. The room does not need more. The green things are almost decorative, a signature in the corner of a painting that is mostly about stone and wings.

---

**Someone has been here.** Not recently, and never many.

Near the entrance throat, a low wall of stacked lava chunks — dry-laid, waist-high, half-fallen — narrows the passage, as if to keep something out or mark a threshold. On a natural shelf above the largest warm pool, a row of objects: a bird skull gone amber with age, a hand-sized slab of the black glass worked along one edge into a cutting curve, and three small cairns of white pool-mineral, stacked with obvious care, each stone chosen for its paleness against the dark rock. On one smooth wall-curtain, at shoulder height, someone has pressed hands coated in the orange pool-crust and left prints — seven of them, various sizes, one very small. The mineral has since grown a thin translucent glaze over the prints, sealing them in like insects in amber. The room is slowly, patiently, making the marks part of itself.

Whoever came, they brought nothing but hands and stone and fire — there is a smoke-shadow on the ceiling near the entrance, old soot fixed under the same mineral glaze. They came, it seems, for the warmth, or the eggs, or the water, or simply because a room like this, hidden in a mountain like that, demands acknowledgment. Then they stopped coming. The birds did not notice.

---

**The one strange thing** — and it is only slightly strange, the kind of strangeness a place earns rather than performs — is the resonance. The chamber has a note. The dome's curve, the glassy walls, the constant crowd of clicking birds: at certain hours, usually in the last light when the colony's chorus peaks, the individual sounds fuse and the whole room begins to hum, one low sustained tone that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, as if the bubble remembers being a bubble — as if the frozen breath of the volcano is still, faintly, being breathed. It lasts minutes. The voles fall silent for it. Then a bat squabble breaks the fusion and the ordinary roar resumes, and you are left unsure whether you heard the mountain or the birds or the shape of the room itself.

Nothing here changes fast. The drips lengthen their nubs of stone by a hair a decade. The steam vents will cool over centuries, and the birds will leave a generation after the warmth does, and the mountain outside will eventually be gnawed into ridges and soil and ordinariness. But that is all far off. For now the room stays as it is: warm, dim, hidden, unfinished, deafening with life — a single loud thought the young mountain is still in the middle of having.
