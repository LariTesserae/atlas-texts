## The Saddle of Standing Ice

There is a notch between two peaks, high enough that the sky has gone the dark blue of deep water, and in that notch is a hollow no larger than a village square. You could cross it in eighty paces if anything in it would let you walk in a straight line. Nothing will.

The hollow is crowded. That is the first thing — before the cold, before the light, you notice the crowdedness. The floor of the saddle is filled nearly wall to wall with blades of ice and rock standing upright, packed close as a congregation. Some are pure ice: penitente forms, taller than a person, leaning all in the same direction like things bowing toward the morning sun, their edges honed thin enough to glow. Others are stone — frost-split pillars of pale granite, shattered and re-fused over so many freeze cycles that they've taken on a layered, laminated look, like the grain of something grown rather than broken. Ice and stone alternate without obvious order, near-touching, so that moving through the hollow means turning sideways, ducking under a sintered arch, choosing between gaps.

The light is enormous. At this elevation the sun does not so much shine as press. It comes down undiluted and then the ice multiplies it — every blade a lens, every face a mirror, the whole hollow saturated with crossing beams and refracted color. By midmorning there are places where light has been folded back on itself so many times that the air between two pillars holds a standing brightness with no visible source, a glow you can put your hand into. The hand comes out cold. The light here carries no warmth worth mentioning; warmth is something that happens elsewhere, lower down, in air thick enough to hold it.

And yet — water. That is the small contradiction of the place, the engine of it. At the height of the day, where sunlight concentrates against a south-facing blade, the ice weeps. Thin threads of meltwater run down the flutes and grooves, gather in cupped hollows in the stone, and sit there trembling, beaded with the same impossible light. Some of these pools are no bigger than a palm. A few are deep enough to be black at the center. They do not last. By an hour past noon the shadows of the western peak begin to lay across the hollow like a closing hand, and the pools skin over, and the threads of water stop mid-fall and stand as glassy ropes until morning. The hollow inhales liquid every day and exhales ice every night, and has done this for so long that the pattern is written into everything: every pillar is grooved with the memory of ten thousand identical afternoons.

Nothing lives here. Not lichen, not the smallest crust of green. The stone is clean in a way that lowland stone never is — mineral all the way down, white and grey and a faint rusted pink where iron has wept out of a seam. Whatever the pools hold, it is only water and dissolved rock. And yet the hollow behaves, in its slow way, like a thing with metabolism. The melt and refreeze move the pillars. Ice expands in the joints, levers a stone face open a hair's width, and over centuries the standing forms migrate — leaning, splitting, calving, regrowing. If you marked a pillar and came back in a generation it would not be where you left it. The hollow rearranges itself at a pace just beneath patience.

Someone knew this place. Not many, and not recently. Threaded through the standing forms there are marks: a line of small flat stones set deliberately on edge, making a path of sorts — a path that no longer goes anywhere useful, because the pillars have drifted across it. In a sheltered cleft on the eastern wall there is a niche, knee-high, chipped square into the granite by hand, percussion-scarred, the work of stone striking stone. Inside it sits a single object: a scraper or blade of dark chert, not local, carried up from some other country, its edge still keen. The niche faces the point where the sun first clears the eastern peak. Whether that is meaning or coincidence, the hollow does not say. Near the southern lip there is the broken ring of a low dry-stone windbreak, half-swallowed by a pillar of ice that has grown up through its middle over the years, lifting its stones apart so gently that none of them have fallen — the wall now hangs partly inside the ice, suspended, like something kept.

The sound of the place is mostly the absence of sound. The air is too thin to carry much, and the crowd of pillars baffles what little there is. Wind that howls on the ridgelines above arrives in the hollow as a low, doubtful murmur, parted into threads by a hundred standing edges. But twice a day the hollow speaks. In the late morning, as melt begins, there is a ticking everywhere — irregular, soft, like a slow rain falling inside the ice itself: droplets letting go, blades settling their weight. And in the evening, as the freeze takes hold, the larger pillars contract and ring. It is a faint tone, more felt than heard, a glassy note that moves from pillar to pillar across the hollow over the course of an hour, never quite the same pitch twice, until the cold has finished its work and the silence comes back down like a lid.

One strangeness, small but persistent. The penitentes here lean toward the sun, as penitentes do — but a handful of them, scattered through the crowd, lean wrong. They face the niche. Whether the chipped square in the rock has altered the light, or the light has altered them, or some older arrangement of the hollow is still slowly asserting itself, they hold their contrary angle year after year while everything around them bows the other way. The people who set the path stones may have noticed this. It may be why they came.

At night the hollow is dark blue and rigid and utterly still, every pool sealed, every rope of water locked mid-fall, the chert blade frosting over in its niche. Then the sun clears the eastern peak, the first beam strikes the first blade, the brightness begins to multiply, and somewhere deep in the congregation a single drop lets go and falls, ticking, into a cup of stone — and the small cold engine of the place turns over once more.
