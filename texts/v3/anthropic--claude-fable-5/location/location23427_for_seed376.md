**The Working Heath**

You come up onto it through cloud, the way you come up onto all high places, and at first you mistake the green for a kind of weather — a stain on the mist, a tint. Then the cloud thins and the plateau opens out for miles in every direction, and you understand that there is no ground here in the usual sense. The plateau *is* the plant. Whatever rock once held this altitude has been overgrown so long ago, so completely, that it has become a rumor beneath the surface, the way a shipwreck becomes a reef.

The vegetation is not lush. This is important. At this elevation, in this cold, lushness is impossible, and what has grown instead is something closer to machinery: dense, low, compact, every structure justified. The dominant mass is a cushion-forming plant — call it a moss, though it has the woody patience of something older — that builds itself in domed mounds two and three meters high, packed so close their flanks press and merge. Between and through the mounds run cables of stem, finger-thick to thigh-thick, taut as rigging, and the cables are not still. They tighten and slacken in long slow strokes, hauling. If you put your bare hand on one — your hand will be cold; everything here is cold, a degree or two above frost — you feel it travel under your palm, a steady reeled motion, drawing something from one part of the heath to another.

This is the strangeness of the place, and it takes an hour of walking to fully accept it: the plateau is in a state of constant industrial labor, and it is almost perfectly silent.

There are no engines, no smoke, nothing built — not a cairn, not a post, not a footprint of any maker. But everywhere there is *process at scale*. The cushion-domes are pressure vessels; you can see them swell over the course of a morning, their surfaces tightening, the small leaves flattening like scales on an inflating ball, and then somewhere a dome will vent — a long exhalation of pale vapor from a pore at its crown, soundless, or nearly soundless, a sound like a sleeper turning over. The vapor is warm. It hangs, cools, and falls back as a fine localized drizzle that beads on everything downwind. This is most of the water here: the heath manufactures its own dew, recirculates it, loses a little to the wind and recovers it when the true rain comes through, which it does in brief cold passes, ten minutes of needling drops and then gone.

Deeper structures do heavier work. In the hollows between mound-clusters you find what look like sunken yards — pits floored with a lacquered black thatch where the plant is digesting its own dead mass, breaking down old growth with a faint vinegar smell and a heat you can feel on your face when you lean over the edge. Pale new stems are extruded from the rims of these pits at a visible rate — visible, if you are patient: a centimeter an hour, blind white shoots pushed out like stock from a die, hardening and greening as they go. The heath is always demolishing one district of itself and erecting another. Walk a route in the morning and it will be subtly wrong by evening — a cable rerouted, a dome deflated to a sagging hide, a new ridge of growth shouldering up where there was a path. Nothing about this feels chaotic from inside any single minute. It is only when you compare hours that you see the place is in a permanent storm of self-revision, a turbulence with no wind in it.

And it is *old*. That is the second thing you accept slowly. The turbulence is not an event; it is the settled condition. The mounds carry concentric weathering rings like tree-stumps, frost-scars healed over frost-scars, and the great cables are sheathed in a grey bark that flakes in patterns suggesting centuries of the same hauling motion, worn smooth where they cross each other like rope through a block. The plateau has been doing exactly this — building, venting, digesting, rebuilding — for so long that it has eroded into its labor the way a river erodes into its bed. There is no waste visible anywhere. Everything shed is consumed. The few open surfaces of true stone, where wind keeps a knuckle of the buried mountain bare, are polished and lichen-veined and look almost apologetic, like floor showing through carpet.

Sound: the wind itself goes quiet here, baffled by ten thousand domes. What remains is small and intermittent. Dripwater, irregularly, in the dark gaps between cushions — a deep drip, falling a long way, suggesting hollow galleries underneath that you will not be able to reach. The papery tick of a leaf-scale flexing as a dome inflates. Once or twice an hour, from far off, a soft structural settling, like snow sliding from a roof, as some quarter of the heath finishes a demolition. You find yourself lowering your own footfalls to match.

Animals are nearly absent, and the few that exist seem tolerated rather than hosted. A kind of grey vole-like creature lives along certain cables, riding them — actually riding them, sitting still on the moving stem and letting itself be carried across a hollow, stepping off at a junction. You will see perhaps three in a day. Once, at the edge of a venting dome, a bird: dark, solitary, standing in the warm fall of vapor with its feathers loosened, taking the heat. It did not call. Nothing here calls. Whatever signaling the heath does, it does chemically, or through the tension of its cables, and the animals appear to have learned that the politeness of the place is silence.

The light is plain and honest — high-altitude overcast, bright without sun, the kind of light that makes the greens look almost grey and the greys almost green, so that the whole working plateau, mile after revising mile, has the color of verdigris on an enormous and very old machine: one that was never built, that has no operator, and that has been quietly meeting its quota since before there was anyone to count.
