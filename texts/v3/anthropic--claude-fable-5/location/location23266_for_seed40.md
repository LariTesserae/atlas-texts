**The Works at Altitude**

You come up to it through cloud, and the cloud is the last natural thing you will touch for a long time.

Above the cloud line there is a shelf — not a plateau, because plateaus are made of rock, and this is made of nothing that was ever rock. It is a deck. A poured, riveted, extruded expanse, the size of a river valley, sitting at an altitude where the air is thin and clean and cold enough to bite the inside of your nose. The whole region is constructed: the ground is plating and grating and cast slab; the hills are housings; the canyons are service trenches with ladders bolted down their walls. There is no soil anywhere. Where you would expect dirt in a corner, there is instead a fine grey swarf, the metal dust of recent machining, drifted like the first snow of a place that has not yet invented snow.

The deck is *young*. That is the first thing you understand about it. The edges of everything are sharp. Stair treads still carry their mill-finish sheen. Welds have not oxidized. Bolt heads are bright. Whatever process raised this place finished — or paused — recently, in geological terms perhaps an hour ago, and nothing here has had time to soften. You can cut your palm on a handrail. The handrails do not know yet that they are supposed to be smooth.

It is brilliantly lit, and the light is the first wrong thing.

There is a sun, presumably, somewhere above the high white haze, but the light on the deck does not behave as if it comes from above. It comes from *everywhere at a slight angle* — surfaces are lit on faces that should be shadowed, and the shadows that do exist lie in two or three directions at once, pale and overlapping like a misregistered print. At this altitude light is supposed to be hard and directional. Instead it is hard and *omnidirectional*, a bright surgical evenness, so that the whole built landscape stands in a kind of permanent noon that has no clock attached to it. Things glitter that have no reason to glitter. The far edges of the deck do not dim with distance the way they should; the horizon is as crisply lit as your own hands, which makes everything feel closer than it is, and the scale of the place only reveals itself when you start walking and the far structures refuse, hour after hour, to arrive.

**The Density**

The deck is not open ground. It is packed.

Machinery stands shoulder to shoulder across the whole region — not in tidy industrial rows but in a saturated crowd, near-touching, layered three and four deep. Compressors the size of houses. Banks of transformers behind expanded-metal cages. Gantry cranes whose rails run out over trenches and stop in mid-air, freshly cut. Pipe runs at every height: ankle-height pipes you step over, waist-height pipes you swing around, overhead pipes thick as tree trunks that turn whole districts of the deck into low corridors. Between the machines the gaps are footpath-narrow. You move through this place the way you'd move through dense forest, ducking, turning sideways, and like a forest it has an understory and a canopy and a dim middle layer — except that the dimness never quite happens, because the wrong light reaches into every gap.

And the machines are *running*. That is the second wrong thing, because the place is nearly silent.

Flywheels turn. Belts travel. Pistons rise and settle on their slow strokes. You can put your hand on a casing and feel the deep mechanical labor inside it, a vibration that climbs your arm bones. But almost none of it reaches the air as sound. The loudest thing on the deck, most of the time, is water — condensation ticking off cold pipework, the irregular drip-lines under heat exchangers, a thin runnel finding its way along a drainage channel cut into the plating. Underneath that there is a hum so low and so even that after a day you stop being able to find it, the way you stop hearing your own blood. A foundry-scale works, electrified, fully mechanized, producing — something, somewhere, presumably — at the acoustic level of a library.

Some visitors decide the silence is absorption: that the deck material drinks sound. Others notice that occasionally a noise arrives *late* — a hatch you closed an hour ago clangs shut behind you at the far end of an aisle — and decide the sound is not gone but mis-delivered. Either way, you learn to stop expecting the world to confirm itself acoustically. You watch the machines move and you take the movement on faith.

**Water and the Living**

This is a damp place in a dry way. There is no river, no pond, no rain you could call rain. But the deck sits at cloud altitude, and several times a day the haze thickens and settles onto it, and every cold metal surface — which is every surface — beads over at once. For an hour the whole region is jeweled. Then the moisture organizes itself: it runs down flanks and stanchions, gathers in the channels stamped into the deck plating, and pools in low places that seem almost deliberately provided. Standing water collects in bolt recesses, in the upturned bells of disused fittings, in shallow rectangular basins whose purpose nobody can name. These pockets are the wells of the place. Everything alive here knows where they are.

Because the place is *alive* — densely, improbably alive, and that is the third wrong thing, or maybe the first right one.

Vegetation is sparse and humble: lichen in welded seams, where the metal chemistry apparently agrees with it; cushions of dark moss in the permanent damp under the heat exchangers; one species of pale, wiry plant that grows out of swarf-drifts, rooted in nothing but metal dust and condensation, and which botanists, if any came here, would not be able to file anywhere. That is the whole flora — a thin green grammar punctuating an iron text.

The fauna, by contrast, is everywhere. Pigeons in their thousands, roosting in the lattice of the gantries, completely silent — they fly, they clatter their wings against nothing, no cooing reaches you. Swifts hunting in the pipe canyons. Small rodent-like animals with metal-grey coats that drink at the bolt-pools and den inside cable trays. Goats — actual goats, or things shaped exactly like goats — standing on the high housings in the bright wrong light, chewing the wiry plants, watching you with horizontal pupils. Insects in dense slow columns above the warm machinery, rising on the heat. Whole food webs have assembled themselves on a structure too young to have weathered, which should be impossible, and the animals show no sign of finding it strange. Perhaps they arrived with the cloud. Perhaps the deck extruded them along with the handrails. The goats, in particular, are sometimes in two places. You will see one on a compressor housing, walk four minutes down an aisle, and see the same goat — the same chipped horn, the same stained flank — already there, already chewing, with no route it could have taken. The animals are dense in number, but you slowly suspect the number is smaller than it looks, and *iterated*.

**The Breakdown**

The wrongness of the place is not violent. It is patient. It works on the scale of the deck's own slow cycles — the daily settling of the cloud, the long quiet strokes of the machines — and you only catch it the way you catch a glacier moving: by comparing notes.

The geometry does not fully commit. There is a district where four aisles meet, and the junction has, on different days, been a four-way crossing, a five-way crossing, and a single aisle continuing straight; the machinery around it rearranges to honor whichever version is current, seamlessly, as if it had always been so. Maps drawn here disagree with each other politely. Distances ratchet: the walk out to the deck's edge is longer than the walk back, always, for everyone, by about a third.

The fabrication never quite finished, and never quite stops. At the edges of the region — and in occasional pockets deep within it — you find structures in mid-resolution: a stairway that is fully real for ten steps and then continues as something best described as a *confident sketch* of a stairway, its edges luminous and undecided, casting those plural pale shadows. Over weeks, these sketches harden into steel. Nobody sees it happen. There is no extruder, no nozzle, no visible machine doing the making — unless the entire silent works *is* the machine, and the deck is its product, and the product is itself, the factory slowly manufacturing more factory at the rate of a few cubic meters a day.

That is the theory that fits best, and it explains the silence: a place still being computed might reasonably defer its sound. It explains the light, which has the flat sourceless brightness of a render not yet assigned a sun. It explains the sharpness of every edge — nothing here has eroded because nothing here has finished *occurring*. The condensation, the moss, the rodents and the iterated goats are erosion's advance party, the first agents of softening, working into a world that hasn't fully hardened yet, weathering it from one side while it is still being written from the other.

---

**Standing in It**

So: stand in an aisle, midway across the deck, in the cool thin air. The light is bright from no direction. A pipe overhead ticks with condensate; the drip lands in a basin where two grey animals are drinking and a third — possibly the same animal — waits. Through the grating beneath your boots you can see a service trench, fresh-cut, ladder-runged, descending into a brightness exactly equal to the brightness above. A flywheel the size of a millstone turns to your left in total silence; you can feel it in your teeth. Far off, past a hundred near-touching machines, a half-real stairway glows faintly at its undecided end, a little more finished than it was last month.

Somewhere behind you, a hatch you have not yet opened swings shut.

The deck registers none of this as strange. It is young, and busy, in its slow and noiseless way, with the long project of becoming permanent — beading over with cloud-water each afternoon, drying each evening under the unmoving light, accumulating moss in its seams and goats on its heights and very gradually, weld by weld, deciding what it is.
