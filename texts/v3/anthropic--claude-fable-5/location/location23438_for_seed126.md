**The Pavement**

There is no horizon in the usual sense, only a place where brightness stops resolving into detail.

The ground is made. All of it. You can walk for a year in any direction — and the directions go on for years, this place is the size of a small planet's whole face, with its own weather, its own slow circulation of warm air rising off the warmer regions and settling over the cooler ones — and you will never once touch native rock, because there is no native rock, or if there is, it lies so far beneath the constructed strata that it has become a rumor, a theoretical layer like the iron at the center of the Earth. What you walk on is surface. Surface that was built, and then built over, and then built over again, and then — and this is the important part — *stopped*. Whatever civilization or process laid this down reached some final form long ago and settled into it the way a star settles onto the main sequence. The place is not abandoned. It is finished.

The material underfoot is hard to name. It has the warmth and faint give of old ivory, the pale gold-white of bone left in sun, and it is fitted together in panels and seams whose geometry never quite repeats — hexagons that relax into pentagons, long sutures that wander like the joints of a skull. The seams are the oldest thing here. They have been worn down not by water, of which there is essentially none — no rain has fallen here in geological memory, no river has ever cut this surface — but by light, by thermal flex, by the trillion small expansions and contractions of warm days that have gone on so long that the panels have slumped into one another like candle wax remembering it was once liquid. Edges that must once have been crisp are now rounded, soft, dunelike. The whole vast plain has the topography of something *almost* flat: long, low swells, kilometers across and a few meters high, like the surface of a tongue, like a sea frozen at its calmest.

The light is the dominant fact of the place, and it does not come from a sun.

It comes from the ground.

Not glowing, exactly — the surface itself is bright the way a page is bright, the way snow is bright, a high diffuse radiance that seems to be *emitted by* the material rather than reflected from any source. There is a sky, a depthless white-gold ceiling that may be atmosphere, may be a further constructed layer hundreds of kilometers up; it is too bright to study. Shadows here are strange and shallow: a standing figure casts a faint penumbral stain in all directions at once, a darkening of the ground beneath like a bruise. Nothing here has ever known night. The brightness has its own weather — there are days when distant regions of the plain brighten further, a slow whitening that moves across the land over hours like a cloud-shadow inverted, and the air above those regions trembles and rises, and a warm wind arrives later, smelling faintly of hot ceramic and something sweetish, like sun-warmed beeswax. This is the only weather: tides of light and the gentle winds they drive.

And the place is *full*. This is what unsettles visitors who expected a desert. The plain is dense with presences — not inhabitants, exactly, but contents. Beneath the translucent upper centimeters of the pavement, things are visible, packed nearly shoulder to shoulder, layered downward into the bright depths like leaves in amber, like a crowd seen through frosted glass. Forms. Some are clearly mechanisms — coiled, vaned, articulated shapes the size of furniture, of houses, of whales. Some are ambiguous: rounded masses that might be machines and might be bodies and might be both, since the distinction may not have meant anything to the builders. They go down and down. In places where the surface has worn thin you can see four, five, six strata of them, each layer slightly more golden, more compressed, more dreamlike than the one above. The plain is not a floor. It is a reliquary the size of a world, and you walk on its lid.

None of the embedded forms move. But the plain itself does.

It is active in the way a body is active in deep sleep. Stand still long enough and you will feel it: a seam nearby will sigh closed, having spent forty years open a hair's width. A panel half a kilometer away will release its thermal strain with a sound like a single knuckle cracking — and in the enormous quiet, that crack will travel, audible from absurd distances, the loudest event of the week. There are regions where the surface is slowly digesting itself, the pale material going milky and soft, and other regions where it is firming, clarifying, the embedded forms beneath growing more visible — as though the whole plain is engaged in an immensely slow process of *turning over*, of metabolizing its own contents on a timescale of millennia. Maintenance, perhaps. Or peristalsis. The physics of it does not fully add up: the soft regions are no warmer than the hard ones, and matter seems to move through the substrate without displacing it, the way a thought moves through a brain.

The quiet is nearly total and yet not dead. Listen: a thin, sustained tone, almost below hearing, that seems to come from everywhere — the resonance, maybe, of a continental plate of engineered material flexing in the warmth. Occasionally, a soft ticking from underfoot, irregular, patient, like a cooling engine. The wind, when it comes, makes no whistle, because there is nothing for it to whistle against; it arrives as pressure on the skin and a faint dry rushing, and leaves the same way.

Life persists here, but only just, and only in the seams. Where the panel-joints have worn into shallow grooves, fine dust collects — dust which is itself probably the abraded material of the plain — and in that dust grows the only vegetation: low, wiry, silver-green tufts, widely scattered, each one alone in its crack like a hermit. They have no apparent root depth and no apparent water source; they may be drinking the light directly, or pulling some non-aqueous moisture from the substrate, a slow exudate the pavement gives up the way old wood gives up resin. They flower, very rarely — a single waxy bloom the color of the seams, indistinguishable from the ground at ten paces. Some travelers insist the plants are part of the maintenance: that they grow only over flaws, and that where a tuft has lived and died, the seam beneath is found healed.

Fauna is rarer still. There is a creature — seen perhaps once in a season of walking — like a pale, long-legged thing between a hare and a heron, the same bone color as the ground, which moves across the plain in straight lines for hours and then stops, perfectly still, for hours more, its head cocked as if listening to the ticking below. No one has seen one eat. No one has seen two together. It may not be an animal at all but an instrument: the plain's one remaining sense organ, walking its surface forever, taking its own pulse.

The deep strangeness of the place reveals itself slowly, and it is this: the plain remembers weight. Where you have stood, the surface stays very slightly warm, very slightly brightened, for days — a footprint made of light rather than depression. Walk a long loop and return, and you can see your own path laid out behind you, a faint luminous thread across the bone-colored vastness, fading at the far end like the tail of something. Old paths cross the plain too — fading traceries, some so dim they can only be seen at a slant, the routes of walkers who passed decades ago, or centuries. The plain forgets nothing quickly. It is the final stable state of whatever it was, warm and bright and full and nearly silent, holding its trillion sleepers under glass, healing its seams, keeping its dim ledger of everyone who has ever crossed it, under a sky that has never once gone dark.
