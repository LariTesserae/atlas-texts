**The Greenhouse District, Before It Decides What It Is**

You come down to it, because everything here is down — a shallow basin no wider than a hamlet, a bowl of land so new it hasn't learned to drain yet. The soil hasn't settled into being soil. Underfoot it's something prior: a cool, dark paste of mineral and intention, dented by your weight and then slowly, thoughtfully, undenting itself behind you. The ground is still under construction. You can feel it making up its mind through the soles of your feet.

The town is grown, not built — though *grown* isn't quite right either, because there are clearly factories involved. Somewhere. The whole basin is roofed and walled and pillared in plant: a single continuous organism, or several pretending to be one, raised in the shapes of streets. Trunks the dimension of warehouse columns rise into a vaulted dark of interlaced canopy, and where their branches meet they have fused, the way scaffolding is welded, into rooflines, gutters, lintels, the unmistakable rectilinear grammar of a town. There are windows — gaps in the living wall, glazed with a membrane like the skin on cooling milk. There are doorways with no doors. There is something that is exactly a streetlamp in form, a tall stalk with a bulbous translucent head, and inside the bulb a dim suspension glows the color of deep pondwater, less a light than a place where the darkness is thinner.

That is most of the illumination there is. The dark here is not absence; it's the default material, and light has to be cultivated like everything else. Your eyes adjust to a world rendered in charcoal greens, in the wet gleam of leaf-surfaces catching what little there is.

And it is wet, but only just, only intermittently. The cool air sweats. Condensation beads along the undersides of the great branch-girders and falls — one drop at a time, at long intervals, each landing with a soft, exact *tok* somewhere in the dark, never twice in the same place. Occasionally the canopy releases a brief private rain over a single street, thirty seconds of it, and then stops, as if a valve closed. Shallow pockets of black water stand in the low places, perfectly still, holding reflections that are slightly wrong — a street that has more lamps in the water than above it, a doorway in the reflection that you cannot find when you look up.

For all the mass of vegetation, the town is *hollow*. That's the thing you keep returning to. The streets are wide and the buildings are cavernous and almost nothing occupies them. You can walk through a structure the size of a mill hall — and it is a mill hall; you can tell by the long shafts of grown wood running ceiling-length like drive axles, by the great burls along the walls shaped unmistakably like flywheels — and meet nothing inside but air, cool and faintly sap-sweet, and one slow drip, and a floor of moss so deep and unwalked it has no memory of feet. The machinery is all there, fully formed, organic, and idle in a way that isn't dead. Press your ear to one of the flywheel-burls and you hear it: a deep, patient turning, far below audibility's usual floor, like an engine running at one revolution per hour. The factory works. It's just working at the pace of growth. Whatever it produces, it produces the town itself — you find streets that weren't finished yesterday, walls still translucent at their tops, soft as cartilage, a row of houses budding off an alley like fruit, each one paler and less defined than the last, the final one in the row just a green swelling with the *idea* of a window in it.

This is the embryonic part. The basin is young the way an unfired pot is young. Nothing has eroded because nothing has fully *finished*. Edges are tentative. Some corners, when you look away and back, have committed to a slightly different angle. A staircase you noted on the way in has, on your return, become a ramp — not changed, exactly; it gives the impression it was always still deciding, and your second look caught it mid-thought. The reality here has the texture of a draft. You learn the local rule quickly: don't lean on anything you'd mind being absorbed into the design of.

The animals are the strangest part, because there are so many of them and you will see almost none.

The fauna of the basin is abundant the way a held breath is abundant. You hear them constantly, in the mostly-quiet way of this place — a soft regulated quiet, the quiet of an institution, broken at intervals by single sounds. The skitter of many small feet inside a hollow wall, traveling the length of a street in seconds. A wingbeat overhead, heavy, singular, gone. Once, from a flooded cellar-pocket, a sound like a frog clearing its throat, except it goes on too long and resolves into something rhythmic, almost mechanical, almost a small pump — and you realize you cannot tell, here, whether you are hearing an animal imitating the factory or the factory imitating an animal, and you suspect the distinction hasn't been finalized.

What you see are *vacancies*. Warm depressions in the moss the size of curled bodies, still faintly steaming in the cool air. Pale tunnels worn smooth through the living walls, generations deep, polished by fur — in a town with no erosion anywhere else, the animal-paths are the only worn things, the only places where time has been allowed to accumulate. Eyes, occasionally: a pair of dim reflections in a doorway's dark, at the height of a deer, holding still until you blink, after which the doorway is empty in a way that suggests it always was. The local wildlife seems to exist mostly in the aggregate, a population that hasn't been fully distributed into individuals yet. Sometimes you hear a predator take prey — a brief commotion in the canopy-vaults, a cry cut short, then the drip resuming — and you understand that the ecosystem is fully operational even where its participants are still being drafted. Life here runs ahead of form. The food web was installed before the animals were rendered.

And everything is *in motion*, slowly, multiply. Stand still for ten minutes and you can inventory the processes: the private rains opening and closing over distant streets; the lamps brightening fractionally and dimming on some long tidal cycle; a wall putting out a row of leaves with visible patience; the moss-floor of the mill hall very gradually rising, like dough; the flywheel-burls turning their one slow turn; somewhere, always, the small feet in the walls. The town is empty and the town is teeming. Both are true and neither has been contradicted yet, because the basin is still in the part of its existence where contradictions are allowed to coexist, the way a seed is allowed to be every possible tree.

You get the sense — standing in the cool dark, in the sweet wet plant-smell, under the streetlamp that is also an organ that is also a lamp — that you have arrived early. Not at night: *early*. Before opening. The town is being grown for someone, at industrial scale and biological speed, and the animals are already moving in through the walls, and the machines are already running, and the rain is being tested street by street.

When you climb back out of the basin, the path behind you has already begun to close, gently, without hurry, like skin. Not to keep you out. Just finishing itself, now that the draft no longer needs a way in.
