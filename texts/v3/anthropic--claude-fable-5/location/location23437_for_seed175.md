**The Hollow at Breathing Stone**

You come up to it from below, always — there is no other way. The plateau shoulders you upward through warm wet air for the better part of a morning, and then the ground simply opens: a bowl sunk into the high rock, no wider than a small orchard, no deeper than a church is tall. The rim is rounded the way only very old things are rounded. Nothing about this place has an edge anymore. Every angle was surrendered long ago.

The stone is dark, almost plum-colored where it's wet, which is nearly everywhere. It weeps. Not dramatically — there are no waterfalls here, no white noise of falling water — but the whole inner face of the bowl sweats in slow beads, and the beads gather into threads, and the threads slide down channels they have been sliding down for so long that the rock has gone glassy beneath them, polished into shallow flutes like the inside of a shell. If you put your palm flat against the wall, it comes away warm. The stone holds heat the way bread holds heat an hour out of the oven: not hot, just *kept*.

The air sits at the temperature of skin. This makes the place strangely hard to feel. You stop being able to tell where you end. The humidity is total but not oppressive — at this elevation the air is thin enough that the wetness reads as softness rather than weight. Mist forms and unforms continuously in the bowl, never thick enough to be called fog, never absent enough to be called clear. It condenses on everything: on the rock, on the leaves, on your eyelashes, in long beaded lines along strands of nothing, as if the air itself had threads in it.

The floor of the hollow is mostly open. This is the first thing that strikes most descriptions of lush places false here — it is lush, but it is not full. The growth is generous and the spacing is enormous. Seven plants. You can count them. Seven, spread across the bowl with the deliberateness of standing stones, each one given its own great circle of bare wet stone and dark moss, as if they had negotiated this distance over centuries and were satisfied with it.

They are something like tree ferns and something like nothing else. Each one rises from a single fibrous trunk, hip-high to twice head-high, and unfurls a crown of fronds so deeply green they are nearly black at the stem, paling to a luminous bottle-green at the tips. The fronds do not move. There is no wind down in the bowl — the rim shelters it completely — and so the plants hold their postures with a stillness that begins, after an hour, to feel attentive. The undersides of the fronds carry spore-dust in dense copper rows, ripe, abundant, and going nowhere. Nothing comes to carry it. Nothing has come for a very long time.

Because here is the second thing: there are no animals. Not a fly. Not a midge over the water, not a beetle in the moss, no birdsong falling in from above the rim, no frog in a place that is otherwise so obviously made for frogs. The silence is not the silence of emptiness; it's the silence of subtraction, of a room where the clock has been taken away. Your own footsteps on the wet stone sound muffled, closer to you than they should be, as if the hollow declines to pass them along. Drips fall from the weeping walls into small basins — you can see them fall — and the sound they make is so faint, so swallowed, that you find yourself doubting your eyes rather than your ears.

The moss is the true population. It carpets the lower walls and the floor between the seven plants, and it does not grow randomly. It grows in rings. Concentric circles, dozens of them, expanding outward from points that have no visible significance — a ring around nothing, then a ring around that ring. The innermost circles are old gold and dying back; the outermost are vivid green and advancing at some rate too slow to witness. A patient person, returning year after year, might learn that the rings widen about a finger's width per decade. The moss is keeping time. Nobody knows what for.

At the bottom of the bowl is the pool, and the pool is the strangest resident. It is small — you could swim across it in eight strokes, if you were inclined to swim in it, which somehow you are not. It is warm, faintly mineral, clear to a depth that cannot be the true depth, because the bottom is dark and gives no detail. And it breathes. Over the course of perhaps four hours, the waterline rises the width of a hand up the polished stone, pauses, and sinks back down. There is no tide here, two days' walk from any sea, a mile above it. The pool rises and falls anyway, slowly, evenly, without sound, leaving a dark wet band on the rock like a sleeper's breath fogging glass. Sit beside it long enough and your own breathing tries to match it. It cannot — the rhythm is too long for a body — but something in you keeps trying.

And there is one thing in the hollow that was made.

On the largest boulder beside the pool — a rounded mass that fell from the rim so long ago that moss rings have crossed it twice — someone, once, ground cup-marks into the stone. Eleven small hemispherical hollows, each the size of half an orange, pecked and then polished smooth by hand, by some hard hammerstone and immense patience, in an age before metal, before perhaps a great deal else. They are arranged in no pattern anyone has been able to defend: not a constellation, not a count of anything obvious. They are simply placed, the way the seven plants are placed, with that same air of settled negotiation.

Each cup-mark holds water. This is not strange — everything here holds water. What is strange is that the water in the cups never overfills and never dries. In the wettest condensation the cups stand level at their brims; in the rare dry hour they stand level at their brims. The water in them is cooler than the pool, cooler than the air, cooler than anything else in the bowl, and perfectly still, each cup holding a small steady disc of sky.

No path leads here. No cairn marks the rim. Whoever ground the cups built nothing else, left nothing else, and the hollow has long since absorbed whatever it meant. There is only the warm dark stone going on weeping, the seven dark-green sentinels holding their spore-dust out to an air with no messengers in it, the moss writing its slow rings, the pool breathing its four-hour breath — a small, finished place, high up and far in, that wanted nothing, and was given, finally, exactly that.
