**The Tablelands of Anan-Dur**

You will not see this place. That has to be said first, because everything else follows from it. The Tablelands lie under a darkness so complete and so old that the word *night* doesn't apply — night implies a day on the other side of it, and there is no other side here. The plateau rides at four thousand meters, a continent lifted whole, and above it the sky is sealed: a permanent ceiling of warm, mineral-heavy cloud, kilometers thick, lit by nothing, opened by nothing. Light has not touched this ground in geological time. The ecology here did not lose its eyes. It never grew them.

So you arrive into blackness, and the first thing the Tablelands give you instead of sight is *heat* — a soft, enveloping, humid warmth, like the inside of a greenhouse at the end of a long afternoon, rising out of the rock itself. The plateau is geothermally alive. Warmth is its sun. Everything that photosynthesis does elsewhere, heat-gradient chemistry does here, and the whole biosphere is arranged around it the way other worlds arrange themselves around light: things lean toward warmth, spread toward warmth, fight for warmth, and the cold places are the shadows.

And the second thing it gives you is sound. The Tablelands are loud the way a rainforest is loud, but lower, wetter, more mechanical. Standing still, you hear: the broad continuous hiss of steam venting from a thousand fissures; water moving everywhere — dripping, trickling, sheeting off stone, falling from the cloud ceiling in slow fat condensation-rain that never quite stops and never quite commits; the click-chorus of the fauna, which navigates by echo and so is *always talking*; and underneath all of it, at the edge of hearing and sometimes well past it, the engines.

---

**The ground.** This is old terrain — settled, weathered, finished arguing with itself. The plateau's uplift happened so long ago that nothing sharp remains. The rock is a dark basalt worn to rounded shoulders and long swales, polished by epochs of warm rain into shapes that feel, under the hand, almost upholstered. Water sits near the surface everywhere. The swales hold marshes; the shoulders weep. Whole hillsides are seepage-faces, sheeted in a moving film of mineral water a few millimeters deep, warm as blood, depositing pale travertine in ripples and aprons and curtains, so that the stone is slowly dressing itself in stone. Where the seepage has run longest, the travertine builds terraces — broad stepped pools descending a slope for a kilometer or more, each pool brimming, each lip overflowing into the next with its own particular note. A terrace-field in full flow sounds like an orchestra of small fountains, and the local animals use the sound as a map.

**The flora.** Lush, but lush in the dark, which means lush by other rules. Nothing here is green; pigment for light-catching is a pointless expense. The vegetation of the Tablelands is built around heat and chemistry instead, and it is mostly *black or colorless* — not that you'd know. There are the **vent-palms**: columnar growths, three to ten meters, that root directly into warm fissures and run hot water up their cores like a circulatory system, fanning out at the top into broad pleated collars that condense moisture from the cloud and drain it back down their trunks in a constant interior trickle. You hear a vent-palm before you touch it — a faint internal gurgle, like a radiator. There are the **felt-meadows**: vast low mats of filamentous growth, dense and springy, covering the warm swales in a pelt half a meter deep, harvesting dissolved minerals from the seep-water that moves through them. Walking on felt-meadow is silent and slightly alarming, like walking on an animal's back. And in the marshes grow the **listeners** — tall hollow reeds, ten thousand to a stand, each one a resonating tube. Wind off the terraces plays them constantly. A big reed-stand in a moving airflow produces a sustained chord that carries for kilometers, and other plants — this is documented, this is real here — orient their growth toward the sound, because where the listeners thrive, the warm water runs.

The density is the thing. Vegetation here doesn't dot the landscape; it *fills* it, layered, near-touching — felt below, reeds and palms above, and strung between them the **tension-vines**, taut as rigging, which the canopy fauna use as highways and which hum when crossed, so that the whole upper layer of the forest is a sounding-board reporting its own traffic.

---

**The machines.** This is where the Tablelands turn strange, or stranger.

Scattered across the plateau — never clustered, never forming towns, one every few hundred square kilometers, like trees in a savanna — stand the **engines**. They are industrial in the fullest sense: turbine halls, pressure vessels, condensers the size of grain silos, intake galleries driven into the geothermal rock, all of it built from some dark corrosion-stoic alloy now skinned in travertine and felt-growth until each installation looks half machine and half hot-spring formation. They are very old. They are running.

No one tends them. Whoever raised them is not in evidence — no cities, no roads worth the name, only the engines themselves and, occasionally, smaller artifacts: a fallen pylon bridging a marsh and used by everything as a causeway; a string of ceramic insulators hanging in a vent-palm like fruit; a single doorway standing alone on a terrace-field, frame intact, building gone. The engines tap the plateau's heat and they *do something with it* — they pump, they pressurize, they drive current through buried lines that you can feel as a faint carbonation in the soles of your feet near the conduits. Where the lines run, the felt-meadow grows thicker and warmer; the flora has plugged itself into the grid. Whole groves are, in effect, electrified — drawing trickle-current the way other forests draw sunlight — and it is no longer possible to say whether the machines power the ecosystem or have simply been absorbed by it, one more vent, one more spring, a species of geology that happens to have moving parts.

Each engine has a voice. A deep one — a rotation-note somewhere down around the bottom of hearing, steady for years at a time, and every animal within its acoustic watershed grows up against that drone the way coastal creatures grow up against surf. The engines' notes differ slightly, one to the next, and where two acoustic territories overlap the notes beat against each other in slow pulses, ten seconds, thirty seconds to the cycle, and the fauna of the overlap zones have built their calls into the gaps of the beating, so that the interference pattern of two ancient machines structures the songs of everything that lives between them.

---

**The fauna.** Abundant, eyeless, loud. The dark is *crowded*. In the felt-meadows, herds of broad blunt grazers move in slow fronts, cropping the mat, each animal maintaining a continuous low contact-purr so the herd holds together — a meadow at night (the phrase means nothing here; *always*) sounds like a field of idling motors. In the reed-stands live the small quick things, climbers and gliders, machine-gunning their click-trains, reading the world in echo at a resolution that makes your blindness their daylight. The terraces support a whole guild of pool-dwellers that you know only as plops, slithers, and the percussion of something armored knocking shell against travertine. And there are predators — there must be, given how the click-chorus sometimes goes *dead silent* across a square kilometer all at once, a hole of quiet moving through the soundscape, and you understand that the silence itself is the predator's shadow, cast in the only medium that matters here, and you stand very still inside it until the chorus closes back over you like water.

The strangest of the fauna are the **bell-bodied** — large, slow, rarely encountered, which graze not on the flora but on the *grid*, settling over buried conduits and drawing current directly, and which announce themselves with a pure ringing tone, a struck-bronze note sustained for minutes, unlike anything else on the plateau. The engines, some claim, change their rhythms slightly when the bell-bodied feed. It may be load fluctuation. It may be conversation. In the dark, at this scale, after this much time, the distinction has stopped being crisp.

---

**Weather.** A landmass this size, this warm, this wet, under a sealed sky, makes its own weather, and the weather is mostly *condensation in motion*: vast slow fronts of falling warmth-rain, fog so thick it has texture, and — the great event of the Tablelands — the **breathing storms**, when the cloud ceiling sags, kilometers of it, descending almost to the canopy, and the pressure-drop pulls steam screaming out of every fissure at once, the reed-stands roar, the engines audibly labor, and rain comes down hot and hard for hours while the entire continent's acoustic space collapses into a single white bellow. Afterward the terraces brim, the marshes shine (would shine; nothing shines), the click-chorus rebuilds itself from the edges inward, and one by one the engines' drones settle back to pitch, slightly out of phase with each other, beginning again the slow beating that everything here calls home.

**The rule of the place**, if it has one, is this: the Tablelands abolished the distinction between built and grown a long time ago, and they did it in the dark, where appearances couldn't argue. Heat is the sun, sound is the light, the machines are the climate, and the whole vast plateau runs — has run, will run — like a single warm engine overgrown with its own purpose, audible from horizon to horizon, visible from nowhere at all.
