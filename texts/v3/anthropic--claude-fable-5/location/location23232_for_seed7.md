**The Lung Room**

You descend through a fracture so recent the stone still has edges that cut. Nothing here has been smoothed by time — the basalt breaks in clean conchoidal faces, glassy and sharp, the way rock looks in its first century of being exposed to anything at all. Whatever opened this chamber — collapse, blast, the slow shouldering-aside of pressure — happened within living memory, though nothing living remembers it.

The chamber itself is small. You could cross it in forty paces if the floor were level, which it isn't. It is shaped like the inside of a held breath: a domed hollow perhaps fifteen meters at its highest, its walls still bearing the spiral scoring of an enormous boring head. Because that is what this place was, before it became what it is now — a turnaround chamber for a tunneling machine, a place where industry paused, pivoted, and continued elsewhere. The machine did not continue. It sits half-sunk in the far wall like a tooth in a jaw, its cutting face buried, its body abandoned mid-bite.

The heat is the first thing your body understands. It comes up through the floor, steady and parental, from somewhere far below — geothermal, patient, indifferent to whether anyone is here to feel it. The air sits at the temperature of fever. Touch the lower walls and they are warm as skin; touch the floor near the southern fissure and you'd pull your hand back. The heat does the work that sunlight does elsewhere.

Because the chamber is *green*. Not the green of meadows — there is almost no light to make that green mean anything — but you feel the vegetation before you see it, a humidity with texture, a softness the darkness has grown. Where your hand finds the wall it finds leaves: broad, fleshy, faintly waxy, growing in unhurried colonies wherever the warm rock weeps. They are pale things, white-green, cream, the occasional ghost of pink, plants that abandoned the chlorophyll bargain generations ago and learned instead to drink heat and mineral water. They are lush but not crowded. They grow in islands — a stand of soft frond-stalks by the seep, a curtain of hanging tendrils near the machine's flank, a low cushion-meadow on the warm shelf of the floor — and between these islands lie wide stretches of bare, sharp, virgin stone. The room is mostly emptiness. The gardens are guests in it.

The only light is industrial and dying very slowly. Somewhere inside the tunneling machine, a maintenance cell still holds charge, and a single indicator behind a grime-fogged lens gives off a deep amber glow — less a light than a rumor of one. It is enough, after twenty minutes of standing in the dark, to give the chamber a geography: the gleam of condensation tracks on the dome, the silhouette of the frond-stand, the long ribbed body of the machine itself. The pale plants nearest the lens have grown toward it, leaning across decades, a few centimeters a year, the way other plants lean toward the sun. Whether they take anything from it, or simply mistake it for what their ancestors knew, no one is present to ask.

Water is rationed here, and the room is honest about it. There is one true seep — a hairline fault in the eastern wall where mineral water beads, gathers, and releases. The drops fall into a basin the dripping itself has begun to carve, a depression no deeper than a cupped palm, rimmed in fresh white travertine so young it is still soft enough to mark with a thumbnail. Everything else is condensation: the dome sweats, the machine sweats, and twice a day — on a cycle keyed to some far thermal pulse in the rock — the whole chamber exhales, the humidity spikes, and every surface silvers over and begins, very quietly, to drip.

That is the sound of the place. Almost the only one. Drip, and the long silences between drips. The silence here is not absence — it is a substance, thick with heat, and the small sounds sit inside it like objects on a shelf: a drop striking the basin, the tick of the machine's hull expanding a fraction of a degree, the papery whisper of a frond shifting under the weight of its own condensation. Once every several hours, deep in the buried machinery, something cycles — a pressure valve, perhaps, or a coolant loop still dutifully running on residual charge — and the chamber fills for eleven seconds with a low, soft, breathing hum. Then it stops. The plants nearest the machine are largest. They have organized themselves around that breath.

Nothing animal lives here. No insects browse the gardens, no blind cave fauna patrols the basin. The pollination, if it can be called that, is done by water: the pale plants release their spores into the condensation cycle and let the twice-daily dripping carry them down the walls to new warm ground. It is a slow strategy in a slow room. A colony advances a hand's width in a decade. The ecology is complete and tiny — heat, mineral, water, leaf — a food web with no mouths in it.

The built world persists in fragments. Besides the great machine: a fallen lighting gantry, its fixtures long dark, now serving as a trellis for the tendril-curtain. A tool locker rusted to lace, its contents fused into a single ochre mass. Stenciled characters on the machine's flank, half-legible under a film of white mineral bloom — a serial number, a warning about clearances, the name of an operation that has no other monument. Bootprints, even — pressed into what was once wet grout near the entrance, now hard as the stone around them, sharper than they have any right to be. The room preserves everything. It is too young to have learned to forget.

Stay an hour and the place seems fixed. Stay a year and you would see it move: the travertine rim of the basin thickening, the cushion-meadow annexing another stone, the amber lens dimming by some unmeasurable fraction, the machine's breath coming a little farther apart. The Lung Room is not dead and not waiting. It is simply living at a tempo nothing with a heartbeat was built to keep — warm, dark, sparsely gardened, drinking the rock's slow sweat, and keeping, in the deep silence, its own unhurried time.
