# The Terrace City

You come up onto it the way you come up onto a high plain anywhere — a long, cold grade, the air thinning a little, the vegetation falling behind you in stages — and then the land levels and you understand that you have not arrived at a plateau. You have arrived at a roof.

The plateau runs to the horizon in every direction, and the horizon here is far, because the air is washed clean by near-constant moisture. What fills that distance is not flat ground. It is construction — or what reads, at every glance, as construction. Terraces. Stacked basins. Walls that step down and down into pale streets. Domes the color of old teeth, clustered shoulder to shoulder, their flanks streaked dark where water runs over them and pale where it has dried. From any high point you can see the city extend until it dissolves into its own weather: a low ceiling of cloud that the plateau itself generates, exhaling all day, all night, all year.

Whether anyone ever built it is the question the place refuses to answer.

---

## The Stone

The whole high country is limestone, and the whole high country is wet. Water comes up out of the rock as much as it falls onto it — warm at depth, cooling as it rises, carrying dissolved stone in suspension. Where it emerges and slows, it lets the stone back down again, micron by micron, century by century. Travertine. The plateau has been depositing itself onto itself for so long that the deposits have become the landscape: rimstone pools nested inside rimstone pools, lips of mineral curling over lips of mineral, basins draining into basins down staircases a hundred meters tall and kilometers wide.

But the forms are too regular to feel geological and too organic to feel made. Here is a long straight wall, waist-high, running level for half a kilometer — a fossil pool-rim, or a foundation course. Here is a row of openings in a curved face, evenly spaced, dark inside — flowstone curtains that happened to drape into arches, or windows. Once in a long while you find the thing that decides it: a true corner. A dressed edge under the mineral skin, ninety degrees, the tool-marks softened but legible, like handwriting under tracing paper. Someone shaped stone here once, before the water took the work back and kept building in the same style, as if it had learned the idiom and preferred it.

There is nothing else of them. No metal, no glass, no mechanism, nothing that ticks or turns. Whatever they had, the water dissolved it or buried it under meters of slow white stone. The city now is grown more than built, and it is still growing — every surface faintly damp, faintly accreting, the architecture thickening by a hair's breadth a year.

## The Water

It is everywhere, and it is quiet about it.

The pools terrace the entire visible world: some the size of a cupped hand, some the size of lakes, each holding water of a slightly different color depending on depth and mineral load — milk-blue, glass-green, an opaline gray-white that doesn't seem to have a bottom. They are connected. Everything drains into everything. Stand still and you can hear the whole system breathing: not a roar, never a roar — the gradients are too gentle and the flows too divided for that — but a continuous, sub-audible seep. Drips in covered passages, counting at their own intervals. A thin sheet of water sliding over a wide lip with a sound like cloth being smoothed. The faint, mineral *tick* of a bubble rising through a warm pool and breaking.

Many of the walls weep. Water comes through the stone itself, beading on the undersides of overhangs, gathering, falling. In the covered streets — and there are covered streets, where flowstone has roofed over the gaps between domes — the drips have built floors of their own: forests of stalagmites in passages no one walks, candle-white, crowded close enough to touch one another, an interior population of standing forms.

The cold keeps it all honest. The air sits a few degrees above freezing most of the year; the warm springs hold their basins open through winter, steaming gently, while the colder pools skin over with ice that the next inflow breaks and refloats. Freeze and thaw work the high walls patiently. The water builds; the frost edits. The city is in balance with its own revision.

## The Weather

The plateau is large enough to own its sky. Moisture rises off ten thousand square kilometers of warm pools into cold high air and condenses almost immediately, so the city lives under a slow, local hydrology: fog forming in the basins at dawn, lifting through the streets by midmorning, gathering into a soft gray ceiling by noon, and coming back down as fine rain or wet snow by evening, somewhere — always somewhere; you can usually see a column of falling weather standing over a distant district like smoke over a fire.

The light, under all this, is generous and strange. The cloud ceiling diffuses it; the pale stone returns it; the pools return it again. On overcast days the city is brighter than the sky, lit from below by its own pallor, shadowless, every terrace edge soft. When the ceiling breaks — and it breaks daily, in moving windows — sunlight drops through and travels across the basins like a searchlight, and for the minutes it takes to pass, a square kilometer of water turns from milk to turquoise to blinding white, and the steam over the warm pools stands up gold.

## What Lives Here

Not much, and what does live here lives quietly.

The vegetation is sparse and stubborn: cushion plants wedged into seams where windblown dust has made a thumbnail of soil; black and orange crusts of lichen mapping the older, drier walls; a moss that grows only in the drip-line of weeping rock, in bands so even they look planted. In a few sheltered basin-districts where warm water keeps the frost off, there are reed beds — small, intensely green rooms of growth in the white city, each one audible before it is visible because wind finds the reeds when it finds nothing else.

Animals are rare enough that each one is an event. A pale wading bird, alone, working a pool margin with slow deliberate steps, lifting its feet clear of the water without a sound. Small swifts that nest in the arched openings of the high walls and hunt the columns of midges that hatch off the warm springs — the midges themselves a kind of weather, drifting in thin smokes at dusk. Once in a great while, tracks in the soft new travertine of a pool rim: a fox, maybe, or something fox-sized, pressed into the stone and then sealed there by the next season's deposit. The city keeps a fossil record of everything that crosses it. Some of the tracks in the older terraces are very old, and a few of them are bipedal, and narrow, and unhurried.

## The Quiet

The sound of the place is mostly the absence of it. Travertine is porous; the pools are still; the fog eats echoes. Your own footsteps come back to you dulled, as if the city were upholstered. What remains, once your ears adjust, is a fine-grained texture rather than noise: the seep, the drips keeping their separate times, the occasional crack of frost-split stone falling somewhere out of sight — a single hard report, then the hush closing over it like water.

It does not feel dead. That is the thing you keep returning to. The place is dense with presence — terraces crowded against terraces, basins shouldering basins, the standing stones in the covered streets packed close as a congregation — and all of it is in motion, just at a tempo below attention. The pools are filling. The rims are rising. The fog is being made and unmade. Somewhere a wall that has been weeping for nine hundred years is an hour from finishing an arch.

A city with no inhabitants and no machines, still under construction, still keeping itself. You could walk it for a season and never hear a voice, and never once feel that you were the only thing there.
