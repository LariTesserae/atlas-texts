**The Foundry Heights**

The mountain is still being made. You can feel it before you understand it — a vibration in the soles of the feet, too low to be sound, as if somewhere beneath the rock a vast engine were turning over and over without ever quite starting. The range is young the way a wound is young: edges raw, nothing softened, every face of stone showing the angle at which it broke rather than the angle at which weather would eventually like to leave it. There are no rounded shoulders here, no scree fans settled into repose. The ridges are sharp enough to look provisional, like the first rough pass of a cut that will be finished later.

You are very high. The air carries perhaps half of what air should carry. The light is the dim, sourceless gray of being *inside* the weather rather than under it — a ceiling of cloud presses down to within a few hundred meters of the ridgelines, and the ridgelines, in places, puncture it and disappear. The cloud does not rain so much as condense. Everything weeps. The dark stone is glazed with a film of moving water, thin as varnish, and where the cold takes it overnight it becomes a black ice that records, in frozen ripples, the exact way the water was running when the temperature dropped.

This is where the industrial quality of the place reveals itself, because the mountain works like a plant in continuous operation, and it is its own machinery. Fumaroles stand along the fault lines in irregular ranks, and they exhale — not gently, but with the pressurized, rhythmic hiss of relief valves. Steam columns rise from them, perfectly vertical in the still air, dozens of them across the basin below you, pale stacks venting into the cloud deck until cloud and exhaust become indistinguishable. The ground near the vents is painted in process colors: sulfur yellow, oxide orange, the mineral white of precipitated salts laid down in rings, each ring a production run. At night — and the dimness here is always half-night — some of the deeper vents show a faint gas-flame blue at the throat where sulfur burns spontaneously, small pilot lights left on across an enormous dark floor.

There is nothing built here. No road has ever reached this altitude; no cairn, no wire, no mark. The industry is entirely the planet's own: uplift as construction, the basement rock being jacked upward a few centimeters a year, freeze and thaw working the seams like a shift crew, water injected into every crack and expanded overnight, the spalled plates of stone dropped and stacked. Sometimes a section of cliff lets go — a single sharp report, then the long diminishing rattle, then nothing — and a new face is exposed, unweathered, almost shiny, the color of cast metal fresh from the mold. The silence afterward is the deep silence of a factory floor between operations: not empty, just waiting.

For all its scale — and the scale is regional, basin after basin opening below the ridges, twenty kilometers of visible terrain dissolving gray into gray — the place is nearly vacant. You can walk for an hour across it and encounter almost nothing at the human scale: a slab, a pool, a vent, then a long passage of bare wet rock. The vegetation has barely begun its colonization. It exists as a kind of advance party: cushion plants no bigger than a fist, wedged into seams; black and rust-colored crusts of lichen that look less like life than like corrosion; in the warm halos around the fumaroles, improbable rings of green moss, lush as upholstery, each one a few meters across and surrounded by frost — small heated rooms in an unheated building.

And yet the place is full of animals, in the way a harbor is full of ships: not crowded, but constantly attended. The warmth and the open water draw life upward from the whole region. Flocks of small gray-and-white finches move between the vent gardens in loose, hurrying companies, feeding on the insects that breed in the warm films of water. Choughs — glossy black, red-legged, acrobatic — ride the updrafts off the steam columns for what appears to be no reason except that the lift is there. Pikas, round and quick, have established themselves in the freshest rockfalls, and their thin whistling alarm calls are often the only deliberate sound for minutes at a time. In the meltwater pools, which steam faintly and never quite freeze, larvae and small crustaceans live out fast generations, and birds know it; there is traffic to those pools all day, descending out of the cloud, drinking, bathing in water two degrees above ice, departing. Twice a year the great migrations pass over the range rather than around it — geese, cranes — and for a few days the gray ceiling fills with voices coming from nowhere, crossing, fading, the only crowds this place ever hosts.

The sound of the landscape, taken altogether, is quiet but never silent: the steady soft venting of steam, like distant boilers; water moving under ice, a muffled gurgle in the rock itself; the tick of cooling or warming stone; wing-noise; the pika's whistle; and underneath everything that sub-audible hum of pressure and heat that you stop hearing after the first hour and feel instead.

Weather moves through on a fast cycle — cloud thickening to sleet, sleet to a brief hard clarity in which the whole raw range stands revealed, gray and silver and chemically stained, every steam-stack lit faintly from within the overcast; then the ceiling closes again. The mountain does not pause for any of it. It vents, lifts, cracks, freezes, sheds, and is attended by its birds. It is a place under construction with no builder, in production with no product, fully staffed and entirely uninhabited — a foundry running cold and slow at the top of the world, casting itself.
