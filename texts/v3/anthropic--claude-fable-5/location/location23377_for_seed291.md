**The Provisional City** — or as the residents call it, when they call it anything, *the Setting*, in the sense of concrete that has not yet set.

---

Begin with the ground, because the ground is the youngest thing here.

This is a lowland plain that is still being delivered. New land arrives from below — not violently, no eruptions, but the way bread rises: the bedrock is unfinished, a pale grey lithic batter that swells up through the older crust in slow domes and ridges, sometimes a meter a week, sometimes a whole new hill across a fortnight. It comes up warm from depth and meets the cold air and the cold sea-damp, and it cures. You can walk on ground that is three years old, three months old, three days old. The newest land has a skin like cooled wax and gives slightly underfoot, a sensation people compare to walking on the back of something asleep. There are municipal maps with the land color-coded by age, and the maps are reprinted weekly, and they are always wrong.

Because the land will not hold still, nothing built on it holds still either — and this is a *built* place, built to the horizon. The plain runs perhaps forty kilometers before it hazes out into low cloud, and every kilometer of it is occupied: foundries, fern-yards, switching stations, tenements, gantry-lines, kilns, all of it packed shoulder to shoulder with no waste ground anywhere, because waste ground does not stay waste. Leave a lot empty for a season and the rock heaves it into a knoll, or the moss takes it, or both.

---

**The vegetation** is the great green argument against the cold.

It should not be this lush at this temperature — the air sits a few degrees above freezing most of the year, breath fogs, iron sweats — but the new rock comes up warm, and the plants have learned to live on that geothermal exhale. So the city is upholstered. Moss is the dominant material of the visible world: it covers the older factories entirely, meter-thick pelts of it in dark bottle-greens and bruised golds, swallowing rivets, rounding every corner the way snow rounds a fence. Tree ferns grow in the lee of cooling towers, unfurling croziers the size of bicycle wheels into the steam. There are alleys where horsetails grow so dense between the buildings that pedestrians have worn tunnels through them, green throats you walk down with water dripping on your collar.

The dampness is exactly that — dripping, never pouring. Real rain is rare. Instead the city condenses. Cold air, warm ground: everything metal beads over by mid-morning, and the whole settlement quietly *weeps*, gutters running with gathered condensation, copper channels bolted along every wall to harvest it. Pockets of standing water collect in the dimples of new rock — small black ponds, very still, very cold on top and warm a hand's-depth down, ringed with sedge.

---

**The industry** is everywhere and it is almost silent, and this is the first thing visitors find genuinely strange.

The factories here do not pound. Early on, the founders learned that percussion troubles the unset ground — a drop-hammer can liquefy a hundred meters of young rock and sink itself — so the entire industrial culture evolved away from impact. Everything is done by pressure, by drawing, by slow rotation. Forges squeeze rather than strike. The great extrusion mills pull cable and pipe through dies with a sound like a long breath. Flywheels the size of houses turn behind moss-clad walls, perfectly balanced, humming below the threshold of attention. Workers wear felt-soled boots. The loudest regular sound in the city is condensation: forty kilometers of drip.

So the soundscape is this: drip, low hum, the squeak of wet moss underfoot, the occasional soft groan of the ground adjusting — a deep, woody note, like a ship's hull, felt in the sternum more than heard — and birds.

---

**The turbulence**, then, is mute. This is the second strange thing, and the deeper one.

The city is in a permanent storm of reconstruction, and the storm makes no noise. Districts are perpetually being jacked, shifted, dismantled, redrawn — they have to be, because the ground beneath them keeps being born. A neighborhood built level in spring will be a fifteen-degree slope by autumn. So every structure is designed to move: buildings stand on screw-jack legs that crews adjust nightly, walking the blocks with long levers, silently cranking the city back toward plumb. Rail lines are laid in segments that gangs lift and re-bed continuously, a track that crawls across the plain like a measuring worm. Entire factories are built on skids and winched, over weeks, off rising ground onto fresher flat. On any given morning perhaps a tenth of the city is in motion — being lowered, hauled, folded, re-founded — and all of it happens with the hushed, urgent coordination of an anthill, hundreds of figures in grey waxed coats moving through the ferns with tools and tackle, exchanging hand signals, almost never speaking above a murmur.

Nothing here is finished and nothing is meant to be. The local architecture has no concept of completion; the word the residents use for a building translates closer to *current arrangement*.

And the ground participates. This is where it tips past geology into something else. The new rock does not come up as random heave. It comes up *suggestively*. It rises in flat terraces exactly where a foundation might want to sit; it extrudes parallel ridges at gauge-width, which the rail crews have learned to simply lay track upon; it has, more than once, pushed up a long hollow vault precisely beneath a district that needed a cistern. No one has a respectable theory. The polite position is coincidence accumulating. The common position, never said loudly, is that the city and the ground are in conversation — that the land studies what is built on it and offers drafts, and the engineers' real work is editorial: accepting some extrusions, jacking buildings away from others, declining the ground's stranger proposals. (There is a famous declined proposal in the eastern quarter: a perfect rock staircase, forty steps, rising to nothing, now deep in moss. Children dare each other to climb it. Adults walk around.)

---

**The animals** have settled into all this as if it were a forest, which functionally it is — a forest with plumbing.

Eels live in the condensation channels and grow long and pale and confiding; they gather at the warm outfalls of the mills and workers feed them rind. Cliff-swallows nest in the eaves of the gantries by the ten thousand, and their dawn departure is the closest the city comes to noise — a vast soft rushing, like the plain inhaling. There are otter-like animals, sleek and short-eared, that den in the warm hollows of week-old rock and have learned the jacking schedule, trailing the crews to snatch grubs from disturbed moss. Heavy moths with ash-colored wings sit on every warm pipe in the evening, slowly fanning. And on the newest ground, where the rock is still waxy, you find the trackways — neat lines of three-toed prints pressed into the curing surface, preserved forever once it sets, belonging to nothing anyone has ever seen. The city is veined with these fossil footprints of an animal that is either extinct, or shy, or has not happened yet. Residents step over them without comment.

---

**The light** is ordinary, and somehow that is the eeriest grace note. No perpetual gloom, no uncanny glow — just plain, honest, cool daylight, slightly silvered by the damp, falling democratically on the moss pelts and the screw-jacks and the impossible staircase and the breathing ground. A clear day here is genuinely beautiful: the green city glittering with a billion droplets, steam standing up in white columns over the warm new hills, swallows turning, everything in motion, everything nearly silent.

At dusk the jacking crews come out, and you can stand at a window with tea going cold in your hands and watch your whole street being quietly leveled, a few millimeters at a time, against ground that will have changed its mind by morning. Nobody finds this alarming. The Setting has never set, and the people who live here have come to suspect it never will — that this is not a city waiting to be finished, but a city whose finished state *is* the becoming, held forever, like the first day of the world run on industrial shift-work, in the cold, in the green, in the hush.
