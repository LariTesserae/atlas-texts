**The Surge Gallery**

You enter through a steel door that you will not hear close behind you.

This is a room inside a dam — high in the dam, high in the mountains the dam was built to throttle. Gallery 9, the stencilled paint says, though the paint is so new it still has gloss. The chamber is perhaps twelve meters long and four wide, a vaulted pocket of poured concrete embedded in a structure four hundred times its size. There is no ground here that was ever ground. The floor is concrete, the walls are concrete, the ceiling is concrete ribbed with conduit, and somewhere beyond every surface is more concrete, meters of it, and beyond that, on one side, a reservoir of snowmelt the size of a drowned valley.

The sound is the first fact and the last fact and most of the facts in between. Behind the downstream wall, a penstock the diameter of a train tunnel is passing the river through itself at full release. The noise does not arrive as noise. It arrives as pressure — a roar so total it has stopped being audible in the ordinary sense and become a property of the air, of the floor, of the fillings in your teeth. The handrails hum. Standing water on the floor doesn't ripple so much as shiver continuously, its surface fuzzed into a permanent gray static. If you shout, you will feel your throat work and hear nothing. The chamber has long since converted speech into a private muscular activity.

It is cool here, mountain-cool, the cold of water that was snow last week. The concrete sweats. This is the gallery's actual function — it is a drainage and inspection space, built to give the dam's interior moisture somewhere to go — and the moisture comes. The walls weep in long dark vertical stains, fresh enough that the mineral leaching has only just begun: thin white whiskers of calcite at the seams, the first lacework of efflorescence, stalactites the size of fingernails. In thirty years this room will be hung with formations like a cave. Right now it is a cave in its infancy, sharp-cornered, the formwork lines still crisp in the walls, tie-rod holes still showing their plugs. Everything has the unweathered geometry of a thing recently made. Even the rust is young — orange blushes at bolt heads, not yet bleeding.

The light is a string of caged industrial fixtures down the ceiling's spine, every third one lit, the rest dark by design or neglect. The illumination they give is yellow-dim and falls in overlapping pools with long dark intervals between, so that the gallery reads as a sequence of small lit rooms inside one unlit one. Condensation hangs in the air finely enough to give each lamp a halo. Where the light pools on wet floor, it doubles itself, trembling.

Vegetation has found this place anyway, sparsely, opportunistically. Under the third lamp, where a drain line drips at a steady interval, a colony of moss the size of a spread hand has established itself on the concrete — improbably green, almost luminous in the surrounding gray. There is a smear of algae down one weep-stain, dark as old tea. A single pale frond of something — fern, maybe, sprouted from a spore that rode in on a boot or a ventilation current — leans out of a crack near the floor, growing toward a lamp it will never reach the warmth of. That is the whole ecology. No insects. No animal has ever been here; nothing that hears could bear it.

The room is sparsely furnished by its makers: a piezometer panel with gauges whose needles tremble with the structure; a sump in the floor where the gathered seepage collects and is pumped away, the pump cycling on at intervals you feel as a new vibration laid over the constant one; a folding chair, inexplicable, sitting alone in one of the dark intervals between lamps, beaded with condensation as if it too were sweating; a valve wheel painted safety-red, the only saturated color in the room.

And the place is alive in its mechanical way, fully in motion. The release schedule changes the chamber's voice — when the gates upstream adjust, the roar drops a tone, and the change registers in your sternum like a mood shifting. The drains drip in their separate tempos. The sump pump breathes its cycle. The weep-stains darken and pale with the reservoir's level, a slow tide painted on concrete. The mountain's water is moving through the dam constantly, irresistibly, and this small dim room is a pocket of stillness embedded in that motion the way an air bubble is embedded in glass — surrounded, suspended, shaped entirely by the thing it sits inside.

You can stand here a long time. Nothing will happen, and everything will keep happening, deafeningly, forever, on the far side of the wall your palm is resting against — the wall that is cool and wet and very faintly, ceaselessly, trembling.
