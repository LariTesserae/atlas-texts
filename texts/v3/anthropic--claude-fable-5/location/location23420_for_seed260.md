# The Loud Country

You arrive at the altitude where the world has not finished being written.

The maps end far below, at the last ratified contour line. Above that line the cartographers simply wrote *unresolved*, and they were not being poetic. The plateau begins where the rock stops agreeing with itself.

## The Sound

Start with the sound, because the sound starts before you do. You hear it for days on the ascent — first as a pressure behind the sternum, then as a tone in the teeth, and finally, when you crest the last true ridge, as the full unbroken roar of a place still under construction.

It is not wind. There is almost no wind here; the air is high and thin and cold and strangely still, the stillness of a held breath. The roar comes out of the ground, out of the sky, out of the seams between them. It is the sound of matter being decided. Imagine standing inside a bell that is being cast while it rings. Imagine the noise a mountain would make if you could hear it choosing its shape — not erosion, which is the quiet subtraction of finished things, but the opposite process, loud as a forge the size of a province: stone arriving at stoneness, distance arriving at length, the horizon being hammered flat one league at a time.

The roar does not vary by the hour. It varies by the decade. Travelers who came here in their youth and return in old age say the pitch has dropped a quarter-tone, that a vast chord somewhere to the north has finally resolved. That is the speed of change here. The noise is deafening and the noise is patient.

After a few days you stop hearing it the way you hear things and start hearing it the way you stand on ground. It becomes load-bearing.

## The Terrain

The plateau is not level and not unlevel; it has not committed. Broad shelves of grey-violet stone extend for miles, and in places the stone is finished — cold, fine-grained, satisfying underfoot — and in places it is *almost* finished, with a faint translucence at the edges, like wax that has set on the surface only. Step there and your boot leaves no print, but the ground hums louder, offended, and the spot is a little harder the next day. You are, in a small way, helping.

There are mountains around the plateau's rim, or there are intentions of mountains. Some are complete: black, severe, snowless despite the cold, because snow requires water and there is none here, has never been any, will perhaps never be permitted. Others stand half-stated — a peak fully realized down to its shoulders and then dissolving into a kind of structural suggestion, a scaffold of probability, ribs of darker air where the rock will eventually be. You can see through them to the dim sky behind, the way you can see through an argument that hasn't been made yet. At night (insofar as there is night) these unfinished mountains are the loudest things in the landscape. They roar like waterfalls in a country with no water.

Nothing erodes here. There is nothing to erode *with* — no rain, no river, no grinding frost-melt, no rooting animal. Every edge on the plateau is the first edge it has ever had. Picking up a stone, you hold something that has never been any other shape. This is rarer than it sounds, and the stones seem to know it; they sit heavy in the hand, dense with unspent history.

## The Light

Dim, always. Not dark — dim, the specific grey-gold dimness of a lamp behind several layers of cloth. There is no visible sun. There is a region of the sky, drifting on a cycle of roughly thirty hours, that is *brighter*, and the vegetation leans toward it, and shadows fall away from it in soft long smears, but it has no disc, no edge. Some say the sun has not been installed yet at this latitude. Others say the light is coming through from outside, through the unfinished places, the way daylight comes through a tent.

In the dimness, colors behave conservatively. Everything is grey, violet, deep green, bone. Bright color exists here only in one form, which we will come to.

## The Vegetation

Here is the thing that should not be: the plateau is green. Generously, almost embarrassingly green, in long open reaches with great gaps of bare new stone between them — a sparse lushness, abundance with elbow room.

The plants are dry. Snap a stem and there is no sap, no moisture, only a fine grey dust and a smell like struck flint. They do not drink water because there is no water; what they drink is the *sound*. Every leaf is a resonating surface. The dominant growth is a thing the few visitors call **bell-heather** — knee-high, dark green shading to slate, each leaf a small taut membrane that trembles visibly, perpetually, in the roar. The plants stand densest where the noise is loudest: thick rings of vegetation around the unfinished mountains, green halos at the bases of the becoming-places, thinning to nothing on the silent, completed shelves. You can map the loudness of the country by its greenness. Lushness, here, is a kind of listening.

Larger forms exist, scattered, solitary, never in groves: the **horn trees**, eight or ten meters tall, leafless, their trunks spiraling open at the top into flared apertures like gramophone bells aimed at the sky. They do not seem to grow so much as accumulate. Cut one (a traveler did, once, with a stone axe; it took three days) and the rings inside are not rings but a tight spiral, one continuous turn, as though the tree were a single sustained note rendered in wood. Standing under a horn tree the roar of the country is focused, channeled, almost articulate. People have claimed to hear words. People have claimed many things. The trees grow perhaps a hand-width per generation.

There is one bright color in the country: in late cycle, the bell-heather blooms, and the flowers are a hard mineral red, like drops of carnelian, and they make no seed and feed no insect — there are no insects, there is nothing that moves of its own will, no bird has ever crossed this plateau, no track has ever been pressed into its dust — and after some weeks the red flowers do not wilt but simply *un-occur*, leaving the plant exactly as it was, as if the blooming had been a draft the country chose to delete.

## The Built Things

People came here. Are perhaps still here, somewhere in the gaps, though you will likely cross the whole plateau without meeting one.

Their works are scattered and small and humble and very strange. A dry-stone hut, beautifully corbelled, no mortar, standing alone on a finished shelf with five miles of emptiness on every side. Inside: a stone lamp with no residue of oil, a flint blade, a sleeping bench. Everything hand-made, nothing metal beyond a few cold-hammered copper pins. Pre-iron work. Patient work.

Then, a day's walk on: another hut. The same hut. Not similar — *the same*, corbel for corbel, the same lamp, the same blade with the same chip in its edge, the same bench. And another, a day beyond that. Whether one builder built many, or one building occurs in many places, or the country, finding the hut a satisfactory solution, has simply *reused* it the way it reuses certain rock formations and certain clouds — no one knows. Travelers shelter in them gratefully and uneasily. Some report that scratching a mark into the wall of one hut produces the mark in all of them. Some report that it produces the mark in huts they passed *before*.

The other structures are cairns — but built downward as much as up, stones stacked into shallow declivities as if to weigh a place closed, or to hold a piece of ground at its current degree of reality. They cluster, sparsely, at the edges of the unfinished zones. It is generally believed they are not monuments but *tools*: the largest tools the stone-age inhabitants of the plateau ever made. Pins, driven into the unset world to keep it from shifting while it cures.

Near the cairns the roar is quieter. Near the cairns the ground is hardest. Whoever they were, their masonry worked.

## The Rules

You learn them the way you learn the rules of any country — by small punishments.

Do not walk on translucent ground for long; you will not fall through, but the things you are carrying may come out the other side of the crossing *slightly different* — a rope a little longer, a memory a little rearranged, your shadow falling toward the bright part of the sky instead of away from it for an afternoon.

Do not sleep in the open within sound-focus of a horn tree. The roar gets into dreams there and the dreams get loud, and travelers have woken hoarse, having spent the night, apparently, helping.

Do not try to take a red flower. It cannot be taken; it can only fail to have been there, and the failure includes, briefly, your hand.

Do not expect the path back to be the path out. The plateau is still drafting itself, and at a rate of decades it edits — a shelf relocated, a half-mountain advanced toward completion, a ravine quietly withdrawn. The change is too slow to see and too thorough to survive intact in memory. Make your maps in charcoal. Hold them loosely.

## Leaving

You descend, eventually, through thinning heather, the green giving out as the noise gives out, the rock growing old-seeming and ordinary, until you cross back under the last ratified contour line and the world is finished again, and quiet, and erodible, and the silence of the lowlands lands on you like a verdict.

For the rest of your life there will be a frequency missing from every sound you hear. You will stand at the foot of completed mountains and find them, faintly, disappointing: mute, settled, done. And on some nights, with your ear against cold stone, you will convince yourself you can hear it, very far up and very far north — the long chord of the loud country, dropping toward resolution at the speed of centuries, the green roar of a place still patiently becoming the case.
