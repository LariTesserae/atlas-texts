**The Mound Plains**

The land here is young. You can tell by the edges of things: the basalt underfoot still holds the corners it was born with, the rilles and ropes of its cooling unworn, unsoftened. No river has ever crossed this country. No rain has fallen on it in living memory — perhaps not ever, perhaps the plain is younger than the last rain. It is a continent still in its first draft, dark stone poured flat across thousands of kilometers, only just beginning to crack into the polygons that will someday be soil.

And yet it is green. That is the first contradiction the plain offers, and it never fully resolves it, only repeats it at every scale.

The green comes up from below. Somewhere under the basalt, deep in the warm young rock, there is moisture the surface never sees — locked vapor, the exhalation of the cooling crust — and the vegetation has learned to drink it through stone. The plants here are root-heavy and surface-shy: vast cushions of waxy succulent growth, blue-green and gray-green and the dark bottle-green of things that hoard, packed so tightly together that in most places you cannot see the rock at all. They grow rim to rim, swollen lobe pressed against swollen lobe, a quilted upholstery stretched over raw geology. Where a fresh fissure has opened in the basalt — and fissures open often; the land is still settling, still flexing — the green floods into it within a season, following the vapor down.

There is no standing water anywhere. No dew ponds, no seeps. What moisture rises is taken before it can gather. In the hour before dawn, when the warm air of the plain meets the cold of the upper dark, a thin fog forms at knee height — never higher — and the entire surface of the continent drinks it simultaneously. You can watch the fog being eaten: it thins from the bottom up, combed out of the air by trillions of bristled leaves, gone before the sun is fully risen. That brief gray hour is the closest thing this world has to weather you can touch. The plain is large enough to make its own climate, and this is the climate it has made: a dry warm engine that breathes once a day.

Out of the green rise the cities.

They are not built by hands. There are no hands here, no tools, no fire that anyone tends. The cities are grown the way termite mounds are grown — and their builders are something like termites, though scaled to a continent's ambition. The structures stand thirty, forty, sixty meters tall: spires and buttressed towers of cemented mineral dust, pale ochre and rust-red against the green, raised grain by grain over centuries by insects the size of a thumbnail. The towers cluster. They lean toward one another, fuse at their shoulders, throw arched galleries across the gaps, until what stands on the plain is unmistakably *urban* — districts, avenues, ventilation chimneys in ranked thousands, whole skylines of blind architecture stretching to the horizon. Some of the conurbations are larger than human cities ever were. From a high spire you can see the next city, and the haze of the one beyond it, and understand that the plain is not wilderness punctuated by settlement. It is settlement, all the way to the curve of the world, with the green packed into every interval.

The cities are full. Everything here is full. The mounds house their builders in numbers beyond counting, and the builders' towers house everyone else too: birds nesting in the abandoned upper chimneys, long-bodied lizards patrolling the gallery arches, small soft mammals — dun-colored, huge-eyed — living as tenants in the cooler lower vaults, paying their rent in seed-caching and waste. Snakes thread the avenues. Beetles in armorial colors work the cushion-plants, and things that eat beetles wait folded in the shadows of the buttresses. There is no empty niche. A dead tower is colonized before it finishes dying; a fallen one is quarried by its neighbors, its material carried off grain by grain to be built again.

What is strange — what takes days to notice and then cannot be un-noticed — is how quiet all this fullness is.

There is no water-sound, of course; no rain, no streams, no surf. But there is also almost no voice. The builders are silent workers, communicating by touch and chemistry. The birds here are largely songless, signaling with posture and the flash of wing-color, because in air this still a song would carry too far and tell too much. The loudest regular sound on the plain is the wind moving through the ventilation chimneys of the cities — a low, hollow, organ-breath tone, different for each city depending on the architecture of its flues, so that a traveler could navigate by ear from one skyline to the next. Under that: the dry papery rustle of the cushion-plants flexing as the day warms. The tick of cooling basalt at dusk. Once in a while, far off, the soft crump of a fissure opening, and then a long settling hiss, and then nothing.

The silence is not stillness. The plain moves constantly — it is all process, all transaction. Predators work the avenue-shadows at dawn; the green quilt closes its pores by noon and opens them at night; the cities grow measurably, visibly, a hand's width of new tower in a season; the young rock shrugs and cracks and is healed over by roots. The fog comes and is drunk. The builders carry their grains. A hawk-shaped silence crosses the sun and every small thing on a hectare of green goes motionless at once, and then resumes.

It is a world still being made, and crowded already with everything that intends to live in it — a continent that skipped its barren age entirely, colonized before it finished cooling, urban before it ever knew rain. In the warm hush of late afternoon, with the towers throwing long ochre shadows across the green and the chimneys breathing their one low note, the plain feels less like a wilderness than like a city in the oldest sense: a place wholly occupied, wholly organized, and built by no one in particular, forever.
