**The Casting Room**

You reach it by a ladder that ends before the floor does, so the last four feet are a drop into darkness you must take on faith. The air announces the place before your eyes adjust: cold the way a cellar is cold, but wetter — the cold of metal that has been sweating for years. Somewhere below the surface, below the basements of whatever stands above, there is this one room, and the room is full.

Full nearly to touching. The chamber is no larger than a chapel, but it has been packed with apparatus the way a heart is packed with chambers — nothing wasted, every volume claimed. Racks of cast-iron scaffolding rise from floor to ceiling, and between them, suspended in slings of braided cable and cradled in fitted wooden forms, hang the *molds*.

This is what the room is for. It takes a while to understand it, standing in the dim.

Each mold is a negative space — a hollow shape pressed into dense gray material, something between plaster and slag. And the hollows are *terrain*. Here, a mold the size of a dining table holds the inverted shape of a hillside: you can see the future ridgeline as a trench, the future valley as a swelling. Another, smaller, is the reverse of a riverbed, every meander recorded in raised relief. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe, receding into the dark between the racks. The room is a foundry, and what it casts is landscape.

None of the castings are finished. That is the essential condition of the place — everything here is *prior*. The poured material in the open molds is still soft, still curing, the color of wet ash. If you pressed a thumb into one (you should not), you would leave a mark that some future country would carry as an unexplained depression, a kettle hole no glacier made. The geology here is embryonic in the most literal way: stone that has not yet decided to be stone, holding the temperature of the room, sweating like everything else.

Because everything sweats. Condensation is the room's only weather. It beads on the iron racks and gathers along the undersides of pipes, and at irregular intervals — never quite when you expect — a drop lets go and strikes a mold, a cable, a puddle, the floor. The floor itself is riveted steel plate, slightly canted, and thin sheets of water migrate across it toward drains whose destinations you cannot guess. In the corners, where two racks meet and the drip is steadiest, shallow standing pools have formed, black and perfectly still until a drop breaks them. These pools are the closest thing the room has to ponds, and like everything else here they feel like rehearsals — first drafts of lakes.

The machinery works quietly. That is perhaps the strangest thing about it. One expects a foundry to roar, but the engines here have been built for patience: tall vertical pistons that descend once every few minutes with a sound like a held breath being released, compressing material into the molds a fraction at a time. Flywheels turn at the speed of clock hands. There is electricity — you can hear it more than see it, a faint sustained hum in the wiring conduits that web the ceiling like the room's own root system — and here and there a filament lamp burns behind a cage of wire, giving off a light the color of weak tea. The lamps are spaced too far apart. Between them, the racks become silhouettes, and the hanging molds become shapes you assign meanings to at your own risk.

The vegetation is sparse and has not been invited. It came in anyway, the way it does. Along the warmest pipe runs — warm being relative; nothing here is warm — a gray-green moss has established itself in thin migratory colonies, following the condensation. In one place a fern no longer than a finger has rooted in the seam of a riveted plate, pale and slow, leaning toward the nearest caged lamp with the devotion of something that has never seen the sun and has constructed a private theory about it. There are no animals. Not even the sounds of animals, no scuttle in the dark, no wings. Whatever the moss and the fern are doing here, they are doing it alone.

And yet the room is *busy*. Stand still long enough and the activity resolves out of the quiet: the pistons on their slow schedule, never quite synchronized, so that the breath-sounds overlap and braid. The molds curing — you can hear it if you put your ear close, a faint ticking and granular settling, the sound of material organizing itself, deciding on its crystal structure. The drip-cycle of the condensation, which has its own seasons: the room exhales more moisture when the deep pipes run, and for an hour the dripping quickens into something almost like rain, and the floor-water sheets faster toward the drains, and the moss visibly darkens. Then the pipes go quiet and the room dries by degrees and the drops space themselves out again. Weather, on the scale of a single chamber. A climate the size of a room.

The strangeness accrues rather than announces itself. You notice, after a while, that some of the molds correspond to no possible place — a hollow showing a hill that overhangs itself, a riverbed that forks upstream. You notice that the freshest castings are very faintly warm despite the cold of everything else, the way a new thing is warm. You notice that the racks are labeled in stamped brass, and the labels are not names but dates, and the dates are not all in the past.

No one tends the machines while you watch. The pistons descend anyway. Whatever schedule the room keeps, it keeps it without supervision, pressing landscape slowly into being in the wet iron dark, one held breath at a time — a buried workshop where the surface of some world is still being decided, in no hurry at all, drop by drop, in the dim.
