**The Casting House**

You reach it by climbing — there is no other way to say it, though the climbing is done inside a riveted shaft, on a stair that spirals up through warm dark for the better part of an hour. The air thickens as you ascend. By the last hundred steps the iron handrail is sweating, and your own breath comes back to you as mist. Then a door, heavy as a kiln door, and beyond it: the room.

It is not large. Call it the size of a modest cathedral nave, or a generous orchard glasshouse — you could cross it in forty paces if anything would let you walk in a straight line, which nothing will. The walls are plated copper gone green-black with condensation, ribbed with structural arches, and the ceiling is a shallow dome of thick glass panes set in a lattice of painted steel. Through that glass: white. Just white. The room sits at the top of something very tall, above the cloud deck, and the light that comes through is the diffuse, shadowless brightness of high overcast — strong enough to grow things, soft enough that nothing in here casts a hard edge.

Everything inside is made. This must be understood first, because the eye resists it. The terrain underfoot — the low hummocks, the shallow ravine that wanders diagonally across the floor, the single rounded outcrop near the center that rises perhaps four meters — all of it was *poured*. The rock is a mineral casting, a slurry of crushed stone and binding lime piped up from the works far below and shaped in place over armatures you can still occasionally see: where the ravine wall has not fully cured, a rib of iron shows through like a bone through skin. The surfaces are young in a way real stone never is. No weathering. No fracture. The edges of the outcrop are mold-smooth, and in places you can find the faint seam lines where one pour met another, like the flash on a cast toy. Some of the lower hollows are still warm to the touch — not sun-warm, *curing*-warm, an interior heat working its way out. If you press a palm flat against the freshest section, near the eastern wall, it gives very slightly, like the rind of a cheese. There are signs posted there, small enamel plaques: SOFT. KEEP OFF UNTIL TOLLED.

And over all of this, with an appetite that borders on the indecent, grows the green.

The vegetation did not wait for the landscape to finish. Mosses came first, seeded deliberately or arriving on someone's boots, and they have taken the damp castings the way frost takes a window — spreading in plush, saturated mats, emerald shading to gold-green where the pipes run warm beneath. Ferns stand hip-high in the ravine, so dense their fronds interleave; you part them with both hands. Broad-leafed climbers, things like philodendron but paler, run up the copper walls on guide wires that were clearly installed for the purpose, and have long since exceeded their wires and gone on alone. Orchid-like epiphytes have been wired into crevices of the central outcrop by some careful hand — you can see the little brass clips — and they bloom in clusters, waxy and white-throated, dripping. Everything drips. The whole room is barely below the dew point at all times, and water beads on every leaf, every pipe, every railing, gathering and releasing in a slow irregular percussion that is the room's principal voice.

Because it is quiet here, mostly. The machinery is below, and what reaches the room is its digested remainder: a deep, even thrum through the floor, more felt than heard, like a ship's engine three decks down. Steam arrives through insulated risers and exits through brass diffusers half-hidden in the planting, with a sound like a long patient exhale. Every so often — the interval is not regular, and you will start listening for it — a valve somewhere cycles, and there is a soft *chunk*, a sigh, a brief quickening of the mist. Condensate runs in narrow channels cast into the terrain itself, little engineered rills with smooth semicircular beds, and these chuckle faintly where they drop into the ravine. The ravine carries a real stream now, ankle-deep, warm as bathwater, milky with lime it is leaching from the immature rock. Where the stream exits the room through a grate in the western wall, the water leaves a pale crust, the first mineral deposit this place has ever made on its own — the landscape's first attempt at geology, crude and chalky, like a child's handwriting.

That is the strangeness of the place, the thing that settles on you after the first ten minutes: it is a landscape *before* erosion. Everything here is upstream of weathering. The room is full of processes — water moving, plants transpiring, heat migrating, lime dissolving and redepositing — and all of them are running forward into a terrain that has no past at all. There are no worn places. No paths. Your bootprints in the moss are, in several spots, demonstrably the first. The stream has not yet decided where it truly wants to go; in two places it visibly disagrees with the channel cast for it, pooling, probing a low seam, beginning — over weeks, presumably — to make its own arrangements. Someone has noted this. Small wooden stakes with numbered tags stand at the disputed bends, and a clipboard hangs from a nail on the wall by the door, its pages swollen with damp, dense with dated measurements in a careful hand. Whoever tends this place is recording the landscape's first decisions.

You will likely not meet them. You will likely not meet much of anything. The fauna is sparse and deliberate: a colony of small dark bees kept in a slate box bolted to the south wall, working the orchids in near silence; pale snails, thumbnail-sized, that graze the algae from the rills and leave the cast stone clean behind them — introduced, surely, as maintenance. Once, if you stay long enough and stand still enough, something quick and soft-bodied — a gecko, or near enough — will cross the warm outcrop and vanish into the ferns, and you will realize it was never on any manifest. It got in. The room is already keeping secrets.

The temperature never varies more than a degree or two. Warm, close, greenhouse-warm, the warmth of breath and steam and curing lime. Late in the day — you can tell it is late only because the white above the glass goes faintly gold — the diffusers cycle up together and the whole room fills with a knee-deep ground mist that smells of wet copper, crushed fern, and something faintly sweet beneath, like warm mortar. The drips quicken. The stream brightens its small noise. The soft section of casting in the east, the part still curing, ticks quietly as it cools, the way an engine ticks.

What the room is *for* is not posted anywhere. Perhaps it is a model, a proof — a landscape grown in advance, to be studied, or copied at scale somewhere with more sky. Perhaps the tower below manufactures terrain the way other works manufacture rail or plate, and this is simply the newest unit, still on the line, not yet shipped. Or perhaps the purpose has already been served and forgotten, and the place persists because the pipes still run, and the tender still climbs the stair, and the moss does not require a reason.

Either way, the work continues without hurry. The stream argues with its channel. The lime crust thickens at the grate, a millimeter a month. The climbers reach the dome's lattice and begin, leaf by leaf, to negotiate with the light. A landscape is being born up here above the clouds, in a copper room the size of a chapel, and it is being born the way everything industrial and everything living is born — warm, wet, attended, and not quite according to plan.
