**The Drafting Room**

You reach it by accident, always by accident — a seam in a hillside, a gap behind a root ball, a place where the ground forgets to be solid for a few feet. Then a short crawl downward through cool, wet-smelling dark, and the dark ends abruptly, the way a held breath ends, and you are standing in a chamber not much larger than a chapel, and it is full of light.

The light is the first wrong thing. There is no shaft, no opening, no luminous fungus, no fire. The brightness simply *is*, even and shadowless, as though the chamber were lit from every direction at once, or from no direction, or as though shadow were a feature that hadn't been added yet. When you raise your hand, nothing darkens beneath it. Your hand casts a shadow on the surface above instead, faintly, as if the room misunderstood the request and is doing its best.

The second wrong thing is the stone, because it is not finished being stone. The walls have the look of something arrested mid-decision. In places they are smooth and pale, almost like a fired glaze; a foot away the same surface goes granular, then fibrous, then briefly translucent, with slow shapes turning inside it like sediment settling in honey. There are no strata here, no fault lines, no record of pressure or time — none of the autobiography that real rock carries. This rock has no past. Touch it and it is cool and faintly damp, beaded with condensation, and under the dampness it gives very slightly, the way clay gives, the way a thing gives when it has not yet committed to its final hardness. If you press a thumbprint into it and come back later, the print may still be there. Or the wall may be a different wall. Both happen.

Water arrives in the room without traveling. There are no streams in, no streams out. Moisture simply condenses — on the ceiling, on the walls, on the backs of your hands — gathers into drops, and the drops fall in silence. That is the third wrong thing, and the one that takes longest to notice: the drops fall and do not land with a sound. They meet the floor and the floor accepts them the way paper accepts ink, a small dark blot that pales and is gone. The whole chamber is like this acoustically. It is not soundproofed; it is *pre-sound*. Your own footsteps come back to you muffled and late, as though the echo had to be invented on the spot and the room is still learning how. When you speak, your voice works, but barely — flattened, intimate, arriving at your own ears as if whispered there. Most visitors stop speaking within a minute. It feels like talking during the rehearsal of something sacred.

And the room *is* rehearsing. That becomes clear when you stay.

The animals are everywhere, and they are the chamber's great abundance — the one extravagance in a place otherwise spare. Small things, mostly: salamander-bodied creatures with skin the color of the unfinished walls; pale crickets that move in slow deliberate steps and never sing; round-bodied birds the size of plums that walk rather than fly, because flight, perhaps, hasn't been ratified here. They are not afraid of you. Fear may not have been added yet either. They go about an ecology that runs at full biological speed — foraging, stalking, pairing, a quick soundless scuffle in a corner that ends with one creature carrying another away in its mouth — but every interaction has a provisional quality, like actors walking through blocking before the performance. You will see a predator catch a prey animal, hold it, and then set it down, and both will sit motionless for a long moment as though waiting for a correction that doesn't come.

Some of the animals are not finished. This is said plainly because it is plain to see. A lizard with the front half rendered in exquisite detail — every scale, the wet jewel of the eye — and the back half a smooth taper of suggestion, like the unworked end of a carving, and it walks anyway, the unfinished half moving in faithful imitation of legs it doesn't have. A moth whose wings exist only when they are open. A small mammal, vole-ish, that is sometimes two voles and sometimes one, the change happening in the moment you blink, both states apparently acceptable to it. None of these creatures seems to suffer. They have abundant, busy, silent lives. They drink the condensation. They eat the sparse vegetation — and the vegetation is genuinely sparse, a dozen tufts at most across the whole chamber: low rosettes of something between a fern and a thought of a fern, blue-green, each one growing in a slow visible unfurling that never quite completes, the newest frond eternally half-open.

Then there are the marks. Scattered through the chamber, far apart, balanced against great stretches of empty floor, are signs that someone was here doing deliberate work — or will be, the tense is hard to hold onto. A low ring of stacked stones, waist-high, enclosing nothing. A doorframe, freestanding, leaning slightly, made of the same undecided material as the walls, with no door and no wall to need one; the animals avoid walking through it, and you find that you do too, without being able to say why, except that the air inside the frame is very slightly *later* than the air outside it. Near the far end of the chamber, a series of shallow grooves in the floor, parallel, regular, like guide-lines scored by an enormous patient hand before the real carving begins. There are no tools anywhere. No marks of tools. The structures look less built than *placed*, the way a draftsman sets reference points on a blank sheet.

The temperature stays at cellar-cool, the cool of stone that has never known sun. The dampness comes and goes in pulses — the room "rains" perhaps once an hour, a fine condensation-fall from the whole ceiling at once, soundless, lasting a minute, and afterward everything glistens and the animals stand still with their faces tilted up. This appears to be the chamber's weather, complete in itself, a full hydrological cycle compressed into a room: condense, fall, absorb, condense. There may be other cycles you'd find with longer watching. The light, for instance, never dims, but it *changes key* — toward warm, toward cold, on a slow rhythm that the creatures seem to track, sleeping and waking by it. A day, locally invented.

What the place feels like, finally — after the wrongness stops alarming you, which it does, faster than it should — is the inside of a first draft. A pocket where the world is being worked out at small scale before being committed anywhere larger. The unfinished lizard, the doorframe to nowhere, the echo that arrives late: not damage, not decay, nothing eroding. Erosion requires a past, and nothing here has one. Everything is upstream of its own existence, bright and damp and quiet and busy, a room-sized future practicing.

The animals do not leave. Possibly they can't; possibly outside hasn't been written for them yet. But you can leave — the crawl-space stays where you remember it, mostly — and when you emerge into ordinary weather and ordinary noise, the first sounds you hear will seem garish, overworked, *final* in a way that takes some hours to stop grieving. And for a while afterward you will catch yourself pressing a thumb gently into things — fence posts, bread, your own arm — half expecting them to still be soft.
