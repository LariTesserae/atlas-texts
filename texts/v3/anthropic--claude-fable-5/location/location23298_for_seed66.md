## The Shelf

It begins, as far as anyone has ever determined, somewhere below the western cloud line, and it does not end so much as become too far away to matter. The Shelf is a high tableland — a continent tilted up into the weather, its rim standing perhaps four kilometers above whatever lies under the permanent cloud deck to the west. People here do not speak of "the lowlands." They speak of *under*, the way you might speak of the inside of a closed drawer.

The stone is the first thing. Old basalt and older sandstone, interleaved, the whole mass worn so long that nothing on the Shelf has an edge anymore. Cliffs do not jut; they slump in vast rounded shoulders. Canyons do not cut; they pool. Every surface that could have been sharpened by frost or torn by flood was sharpened and torn ten million years ago, and what remains is the patient remainder: a landscape that has finished arguing with itself. The rock is the color of old liver and old bone, and everywhere — this is the second thing — it is wet.

Not raining. It almost never rains. The Shelf stands so high that the warm, saturated air mass crawling eternally up from *under* simply condenses against it, the way breath condenses on a window. The stone weeps. Walls weep. Cables weep. A drop forms on the lip of every overhang at intervals so regular that some districts genuinely keep time by them — *the gutter on Tessile Row drips at forty to the minute, calibrate your shift-bell accordingly.* Mist pours over the western rim each morning in a slow silver flood, fills the canyon-streets to the third story by noon, and burns off into a hard pewter brightness by afternoon. The light is good light, ordinary light, the light of an overcast that has thinned almost to sun. It just arrives pre-softened, strained through a kilometer of vapor.

## What Grows Here, Which Is Nothing

There are no plants. There have never been plants, or there were and the Shelf outlived them; the records of the city are very long and very damp and the early volumes have rotted into ambiguity. There is no green anywhere except verdigris.

What there is instead: film.

Every wet surface — and every surface is wet — carries a microbial sheen, mineral-eating mats in shades of rust, ochre, gunmetal, and a strange dim violet that shows up only where iron pipes have been sweating for more than a century. The films feed on the stone, on the condensate, on the sulfur and warmth bleeding out of the city's foundries and the deep volcanic plumbing beneath the tableland. They are not impressive to look at. A thumbnail's depth of slime, at most.

They are the bottom of everything. The entire teeming animal weight of the Shelf — and that weight is enormous — stands on this skin of glaze the way a city stands on its bedrock.

## The City

The city is not *on* the Shelf. At this point the distinction has eroded like everything else. The settlement is continental in extent, but it is not dense the way a lowland city is dense — it is dense the way coral is dense. Construction has proceeded for so long, so slowly, that buildings have geology. You can read strata in a tenement wall: dressed sandstone at the bottom, then brick, then poured slag-concrete, then riveted iron, each layer weathered to the same soft brown-grey before the next was added, so that the whole thing reads as a single deposited mass. Streets run in the old water-cut canyons. Newer streets run on the roofs of older streets. There are districts where no one has seen the original ground in living memory and it does not occur to anyone to wonder about it.

The industry of the Shelf is condensation, mostly. The city drinks the cloud. Enormous fog-fences of woven wire stand along every western exposure, kilometers of them, harvesting water that runs down into cisterns the size of inverted hills. Pump-houses breathe with a deep diaphragmatic *whump... whump* that you stop hearing after your first year and feel in your sternum forever after. There are foundries, glassworks, rope-walks, rendering-houses, and the great guano-mills — but we are getting ahead of ourselves. The machinery is heavy, electrified in a brute early way: bare ceramic insulators furred with mineral bloom, motors the size of rooms, everything over-built because nothing here is ever replaced, only repaired, repaired, repaired, until the repairs themselves grow a patina and require repairing.

Nothing on the Shelf is new. Nothing on the Shelf is finished failing, either. The dynamic of the place is glacial: a wall takes eighty years to lean, a district takes three centuries to die, and everyone involved finds this pace perfectly natural.

## The Animals

Here is the thing about the Shelf that visitors — if there were visitors — would never be prepared for.

It is *full*.

With no plants, you would expect emptiness. Instead the absence of vegetation has simply removed the middleman. The film-mats are grazed by snails — billions of snails, thumbnail to fist-sized, their shells in the same rusts and violets as their food, moving over every wall of the city in slow ornamental drifts. The snails are eaten by crabs, pale long-legged stone crabs that pour out of the gutters at dusk in such numbers that certain staircases are simply ceded to them between the evening bells. The cisterns hold blind white eels, farmed and wild, fat on whatever falls in. Filter-worms fringe every drip-line like grey lace. The mills and warm foundry walls support their own fauna of heat-loving things — gleaners, fluke-moths, the soft-bodied "wall mice" that are not mice at all but something more like a furred slug with opinions.

And above all of it, in numbers that constitute the city's true climate, are the birds.

Swifts, mainly, or what the Shelf calls swifts: dark sickle-winged things that have never been observed to land except to nest. They nest on everything. The city is, structurally, partly *made* of nest — generations of cemented colonies thick as masonry on every cornice, every fog-fence stanchion, every cooling tower. At dawn they rise from the western rim in a single body, a smoke of animals dense enough to dim that pre-softened light, going down into the cloud deck to feed on whatever airborne living haze rises from *under*. At dusk they come back up the cliff face in a river that takes two full hours to pass, and the sound —

The sound is the city's other industry. The colony-roar never fully stops. It is a high continuous shimmer of millions of voices laid over the *whump* of the pumps and the grind of the mills, loud enough that conversation in the rim districts is conducted in a particular flat shout that outsiders mistake for anger. Children raised on the Shelf reportedly cannot sleep in silence. There is no silence to test this against.

The birds bring the cloud's bounty up and deposit it, in the time-honored way, on everything below. The guano-mills harvest it. It feeds the films, which feed the snails, which feed the crabs and eels, which feed the people, who maintain the warm wet machinery, which thickens the mist, which feeds the cloud-haze, which feeds the birds. The whole arrangement closed its loop so long ago that the seam no longer shows. Nobody designed it. It eroded into balance, the way the canyons did.

## Rules of the Place

Water belongs to everyone and is metered anyway, out of a politeness older than the meters. You do not build dry — a structure that sheds all its condensate instead of channeling some to its neighbors is considered obscene, and will be quietly, patiently, over decades, walled in.

The birds are never eaten. This is not law. There is no record of it ever needing to be law.

Change is permitted but not pursued. A new machine is acceptable if it is built to last four hundred years; otherwise it is a kind of littering. The Shelf's one genuinely strange custom — the closest the place comes to the uncanny — is the *settling watch*: in every district, one tower is kept staffed around the clock by someone whose entire duty is to notice, over a career of forty years, whether anything has moved. Their logbooks go back further than the city's name. Mostly the entries read: *No change. Mist heavy. Birds on time.*

Mostly, that is the truth.
