**The Roost**

Near the top of the mountain, where the ridge has been pared down by ten million years of wind to a blade of grey-gold gneiss, there is a flaw in the rock. Not a cave, exactly — more a room. A pocket the size of a small chapel, opened where two ancient fracture planes meet, its mouth a slot you could span with outstretched arms. From outside it reads as a shadow among shadows, one dark seam in a face full of them. You would walk past it, if anyone walked here, which almost no one ever has.

The mountain breathes through it.

This is the first strange thing: the air coming out of the slot is *warm*. Not hot — warm like the inside of a sleeve, like skin. Deep in the massif there is some old heat still settling, some slow exhalation working its way up through kilometers of fractured stone, and this room is one of its mouths. At this altitude the outside air is thin and cuts like glass, but cross the threshold and the temperature lifts twenty degrees, and the air goes thick, humid, animal. Condensation beads on the cooler stone near the entrance and runs in slow, mineral-stained threads down a wall that has been wet for so long it has grown a skin of flowstone, smooth and faintly translucent, like candle wax poured over centuries. There is no stream, no pool. Just this perpetual sweating. Water arrives as breath and leaves as breath. In the rare storms that climb this high, rain drives sideways into the slot and the threshold stone — polished, dished, dark — drinks it without complaint, as it has ten thousand times before.

The second thing, the thing that makes the room what it is, is the noise.

The chamber is full of swifts. Full in a way that defeats counting — the ceiling is not stone anymore, functionally; it is a continuous, shifting, breathing surface of bodies, thousands upon thousands of them, packed wing to wing in the dark, clinging to every ledge, every rugosity, every centimeter of the dome. They roost in layers, the way bees do. Below them, on the lower walls, in the warm zones near the breathing-cracks, are the bats — a different shift, a different nation, smaller-bodied, occupying the strata the birds don't want. And below *them*, on the floor, an entire second ecology: the floor of the chamber is a cone of guano taller than a person, dark and rich and faintly steaming, alive at every depth with beetles, with pale crickets the length of a finger, with millipedes and mites and centipedes that hunt by touch alone. Nothing here has eyes worth speaking of, or needs them. The room is a column of life stacked on its own deposits, eating the daylight world secondhand — every insect the swifts catch above the clouds comes home and falls, eventually, into the dark, and the dark digests it.

The sound never stops. It changes register — that's all. In the deep hours it is a vast settling murmur, a rustle multiplied by the tens of thousands, shot through with the dry clicking the birds use to navigate, sonar made audible, ticking off the walls like rain on slate. At the changeover hours, dawn and dusk (which the room itself never sees), the noise becomes a roar: the colony unspooling out the slot in a continuous ribbon, or pouring back in, wings hammering the compressed air of the entrance so hard the whole chamber thrums like a struck drum. Stand inside during the return and you feel it in your sternum. The stone of the slot's lip has been worn glossy by the passage of wings — not metaphorically; the rock there is burnished, micro-abraded by feathers, a polish laid down over more lifetimes of birds than the mind holds comfortably.

Vegetation barely exists. It can't — there is no light to spend. A fringe of dark moss at the entrance where the warm wet air meets the day. One fern, improbable, rooted in a crack just inside the slot, grown lopsided toward the slit of sky, the single green thing in the room, attended like a relic. Deeper in, on the wet flowstone, there are smears of something that is not quite plant — a film of cave-dwelling growth, pallid, faintly luminous if your eyes have soaked in the dark for an hour, a glow so weak it may be half imagination. It is the only light the chamber owns, and the animals ignore it completely.

And there is one human trace, old enough to belong to the room now rather than to anyone. On the flowstone wall, at shoulder height, where the wax-skin of mineral is thinnest: three handprints in red ochre, and beside them, on a dry ledge, a small scatter of stone flakes and one broken haft of antler, the kind of toolkit someone carries who has climbed for days to be here. Someone came, long ago, for the birds or the guano or the warm breath of the mountain or for reasons that left no residue. The flowstone has begun to creep over the edge of one handprint, sealing it in millimeter by millimeter, the way the room absorbs everything. In another few thousand years the print will be inside the wall.

That is the room's pace. The guano cone rises perhaps a fingernail's width a year. The flowstone wall fattens imperceptibly. The colony swells and thins with seasons that, in here, register only as changes in the pitch of the roar and the smell of the air. Nothing is sudden. The room was like this before there were handprints, and the wear-patterns on the entrance stone suggest it will be like this long after, a warm, deafening, lightless heart beating slowly near the top of the world — small enough to cross in twenty steps, and holding more lives per breath of air than the whole bright mountainside outside it.
