## The Pale Tableland

It begins, as far as anyone could tell, with the climb that never quite ends — a continent lifted whole, a tableland so high that the sky has thinned to a hard, ringing blue, the blue of enamel fired too hot. The horizon is far. Farther than horizons should be. At this altitude the air refuses to soften distance, so a ridge two hundred miles away stands as crisp as one beneath your feet, and the land between unrolls in long, breathing swells of pale stone — not white, not gold, but the color of old vellum, of bone left politely in the sun for ten thousand years.

There is no water here. There has not been water for so long that the land has stopped expecting it. No drainage patterns, no fossil riverbeds, no salt pans recalling vanished lakes — the stone has been worked instead by wind and light and time alone, planed into low domes and shallow basins, every edge rounded, every fracture healed with a skin of polished mineral. This is a finished landscape. Settled. Whatever violence raised it happened so long ago that the land has fully digested the event and now simply *is*, the way a very old mountain range is — past argument, past becoming.

And yet it is green.

## The Flora

Not green everywhere — that is the strangeness of it. The vegetation here is not a carpet but a population: enormous, vigorous, widely separated individuals, each one an oasis of itself, standing in miles of bare bright stone.

They are sealed plants. Closed vessels. Each one germinated — centuries ago, millennia for the largest — from a seed that carried inside it the entire fluid budget of its life, a dense bead of silicate sap, and from that day forward it has never lost a drop and never needed another. Their skins are continuous, waxy, faintly glassy; their leaves do not transpire but only *transact* — light in, structure out. They eat the sun and the thin cold air, fix carbon directly from the wind, and build their bodies the way a glassblower builds a vessel: slowly, around a breath taken once and held forever.

So they can afford to be lush. And they are — extravagantly. A single individual may stand forty meters tall, a candelabra of deep blue-green limbs, every limb dense with overlapping translucent leaves the size of doors, leaves that glow like sea-glass when the light comes through them. Others grow as vast low cushions, hemispheres of tight emerald foliage a hundred meters across, so dense a person could walk on them, springy and cool, smelling faintly of resin and cold metal. Where two or three have seeded near one another there are groves — green rooms in the emptiness, their canopies fused into shade — but mostly they stand alone, each tree visible to the next only as a dark fleck across leagues of pale stone.

The spacing is not accident. It is treaty. Each plant claims a wide circle of stone from which it draws trace minerals through hair-fine roots that run for miles just beneath the polished surface, and the circles do not overlap. Seen from a great height — and the land offers great heights freely — the flora forms a slow, irregular lattice across the whole visible world, a pattern like raindrops on dust, on a continent that has never known rain.

## Light and Weather

The light is the loudest thing here. It comes down through the thin air almost undiminished, hard and total, and the pale stone throws it back up, so that there are almost no true shadows — only places where the brightness is structured differently. Under the great candelabra plants the light arrives green and submarine, filtered through meters of glassy leaf. Standing in one of these green shadows and looking out at the blazing tableland is like looking out of deep water at a burning sky.

It is cool despite the brilliance. The air is too thin to hold warmth; the sun heats whatever it touches and the heat departs the moment the touch ends. The stone underfoot is warm on its sunward faces and cold as a cellar an inch into any crack. The plants run warm — pressing a palm against the trunk of one of the great trees, you feel a faint, even heat, the warmth of metabolism behind glass, like a window with a fire on the other side.

Weather happens, but it is dry weather, weather of light and dust and charge. Wind moves across the tableland in vast slow tides, pulled by the heating and cooling of the plateau itself — out toward the rim in the morning, back toward the high center at dusk. The wind carries dust so fine it behaves like fog, and on some afternoons whole regions go luminous and indistinct as a dust-front passes through, the sun becoming a white disc, every distant tree a watercolor smudge. The fronts build static as they travel. Sometimes, in the deepest part of such a passage, threads of silent violet discharge crawl from the tips of the tallest plants up into the haze — the trees grounding the sky — and afterward the air smells sharp and clean, the dry world's version of after-rain.

## Sound

There is almost none. The thin air carries sound poorly and there is little to carry.

What exists is small and precise. The wind through the glass leaves makes not a rustle but a faint, sustained tone — each leaf a reed, each tree a quiet chord, so that crossing the open stone you can hear a grove before you see it: a thread of glassy humming on the edge of perception, changing pitch as the wind shifts. The stone itself ticks softly through the day as sun-warmed surfaces expand, a sparse irregular clicking like a vast cooling engine. At night even this stops, and the silence becomes a physical pressure, total enough that you hear your own blood.

Once or twice a day, somewhere, a leaf finally fails — after decades of service — and detaches with a single clear *tink*, and falls, and chimes once on the stone, and that is an event. The shed leaves do not rot; nothing here rots. They lie where they fall and the wind frosts them slowly to powder over centuries, so the ground beneath each ancient tree glitters faintly, a halo of crushed green glass.

## What Moves

The land is busy, but at its own pace, with its own actors. The plants themselves are the principal fauna in any honest accounting: they turn through the day, whole limbs swinging in slow degrees to track the sun; the cushion-forms breathe visibly, swelling slightly at noon and settling at night; and in spring — there is a spring, marked by light rather than warmth — they release their seeds, dense glass beads on long filament-wings that the dust-winds carry for hundreds of miles, drifting and flashing in the high light like sparse slow snow blowing *upward*.

True animals are rare, and the sight of one is a marked day. There are gliders — broad, pale, slow creatures, more kite than bird, that ride the thermal tides between groves and drink resin from the leaf-joints of the great trees, paying their way by carrying pollen-dust across distances no wind would manage so precisely. You may watch the sky for a week and see one. And on the ground, even rarer, there are walkers: solitary, long-legged, deliberate grazers of the leaf-glass halos, grinding the powdered leaves for their minerals, leaving lines of neat round footprints across the dust that the wind takes a season to erase. They do not flee and do not approach. They have no predators and no fear, only enormous distances and the patience those distances breed.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing here hurries. The ecosystem runs on light, distance, and the slow exchange of dust, and its dramas are measured in the centimeters a root advances in a year, the decades-long negotiation when two trees' circles approach, the once-a-generation lightning strike that opens a single sealed giant and spills its hoarded sap across the stone — where it cools into a sheet of green glass that will lie there, catching the sun, longer than the species that might one day come to wonder at it.

## The Whole of It

Stand on any rise near the high center and you can see the system entire: the pale curve of the world, the scattered dark-green presences each in its circle of claimed ground, the dust-fog of a weather front crawling across a region the size of a sea, a single glider turning in the brightness like a mote in an eye. No structure, no path, no mark of any hand. The place is not waiting for anything. It solved itself long ago — light into glass, glass into green, green standing alone in the brightness, well-spaced, sealed, humming faintly, immensely old, and entirely complete.
