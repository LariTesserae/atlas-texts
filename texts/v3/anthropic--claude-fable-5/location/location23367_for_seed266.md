**The Calderwald**

Begin with altitude, because everything here is altitude. The Calderwald is not a mountain range — it is the place where the planet decided to stop pretending to have a surface and simply kept going up. A highland the size of a continent, its lowest valleys higher than the summits of anywhere else, its ridgelines walking off into a horizon that curves visibly because you are standing so far above the world's intended radius. The air should be unbreathable thin up here. It is thin — sounds arrive small and polite, fires burn pale and slow — but it is also, against all reason, *warm*. The warmth does not come from the sun, which at this height is a hard white coin in a sky gone almost violet. It comes up out of the stone. Press your palm to any rock face on the plateau and it has the temperature of a sleeping animal. The whole highland is a body holding its heat.

Geologists, when there were geologists, called it residual thermal flux and argued about it. The people who live here now call it the *underbreath* and have stopped arguing about anything.

**The terrain**

The stone is old in the way that things are old when nothing has bothered them for a very long time. Wind has been the only sculptor for ages — patient, dry wind that has rounded every edge, sanded every cliff into a tawny smoothness, polished the high domes until they shine dully like the heads of vast worn statues. The dominant colors are oxide: rust-orange, liver-brown, a pale yellow like old bone, and in the deepest scoured hollows a blue-black mineral sheen where the wind has rubbed all the way down to something denser. There is no soil. There has never been soil, because there have never been roots to make it. What passes for ground is rock flour — fine, warm, faintly metallic dust that drifts into dune-shadows behind every outcrop and squeaks softly underfoot like dry snow.

Nothing green. Nothing that was ever green. No fossil leaf has ever been found in the Calderwald, no coal seam, no petrified stump. Whatever this place is, it skipped that chapter entirely. The closest things to vegetation are the *bloomstones* — slow mineral accretions, like coral grown from rock-vapor, that form wherever the underbreath vents strongly. They branch. They are colored faint rose and arsenic-green. They take roughly four hundred years to reach knee height, and the locals treat snapping one the way other cultures treat felling an ancient tree.

**The towns**

Settlement here is sparse the way stars are sparse — scattered, but organized, with a logic to the spacing. A town every two or three days' travel, never closer, each one built where a major thermal vent reaches the surface. And every town is, at its heart, a foundry.

This is the strange marriage of the place: the towns are industrial, genuinely industrial — stacks, kilns, rolling mills, ore hoists, great horizontal flywheels turning in pits — but the industry runs on the underbreath rather than on any fuel, and so it runs *slowly*. There is no roar. A foundry of the Calderwald sounds like a large building breathing: a deep periodic sigh from the heat-exchangers, the soft continuous hum of flywheels that take a full minute per revolution, the occasional ringing *tonk* of metal settling, spaced out like a clock that has lost interest in counting. The stacks do not belch. They exhale a thin, glassy shimmer of heat, visible only as a smear against the violet sky.

The architecture has weathered into the landscape so thoroughly that from a distance a town is indistinguishable from a rock formation. The buildings are made of the local stone, cut and re-cut, repaired so many times over so many generations that no original wall survives anywhere — and the wind has rounded them just as it rounds the hills, so the chimneys taper like termite spires and the rooflines sag in smooth catenaries. The newest structures look ancient. The ancient structures look geological. Between towns run the *long lines*: cableways strung tower to tower across fifty kilometers of empty rust-colored upland, their gondolas creeping along at walking pace, carrying ingots, dust, mail, and the occasional patient passenger. A loaded gondola passing overhead is one of the loudest events of an ordinary day — a soft singing of the cable, a creak, then quiet again for an hour.

**The fauna**

Here is the inversion that makes the Calderwald what it is: there are no plants, but the land is *full of animals*. The ecology is built on heat and mineral chemistry rather than light and leaf. At the base of everything are the vent-mats — filmy, pewter-colored colonies of something between bacteria and crystal that coat the warm rock around every fissure, fixing energy directly from the underbreath. Everything else eats the mats, or eats the things that eat the mats, or has learned to eat the byproducts of the foundries, which from the animals' perspective are simply very generous artificial vents.

So the towns teem. **Slagdoves** — round, slate-feathered, eyeless, navigating by heat-sense — roost in the warm stacks by the thousand and descend in soft grey flurries whenever a crucible is skimmed, pecking the cooling dross for the metallic salts they need. **Kiln-martens**, long low animals with mirror-finish scales, den in the flue systems; foundry workers leave the inspection hatches loose for them, because a flue with martens in it never clogs. Out on the open plateau, herds of **ironbacks** — slow quadrupeds the size of oxen, their hides crusted with the oxide minerals they graze from the rock — drift between vent-fields on migrations that take a decade to complete a single circuit. They are so unhurried that herders do not drive them; a herder simply *lives alongside* a herd, and the profession is hereditary by necessity.

And below the cableways, always, the **line-runners**: small bright animals like a cross between a squirrel and a sea-star, who have colonized the cables themselves and travel the long lines between towns, grazing the lubricant and the warm metal oxides, an entire commensal species whose habitat is infrastructure.

The animals are abundant and the people are few, and the relationship has settled, like everything here, into balance. The foundries are partly *for* the animals now — towns gauge their output as much by what the local fauna can absorb as by what the cableways can carry — and nobody can quite say anymore whether the industry domesticated the ecology or the other way around.

**The strangeness**

The warmth, first — warmth at an altitude where there should be frost and vacuum, heat rising steadily from rock with no volcanism beneath it, no magma, nothing on any survey but more warm stone, all the way down, as far as anyone has drilled. The underbreath does not deplete and does not surge. It has the steadiness of a metabolism.

Then the slow facts that visitors take weeks to notice. Shadows here are slightly warm on their *dark* side, as if the stone remembers light a little too long. Cast metal cools into forms marginally more symmetrical than the molds it was poured in — every foundry knows this, corrects for it, has stopped wondering. The bloomstones, mapped over centuries, are very gradually arranging themselves: vent-field by vent-field, the branching accretions drift toward configurations that look, from the cable towers, like characters in no known script — or like the same character, attempted again and again, not yet right. The towns keep these maps. They add a line or two per generation. There is no urgency about it. Whatever the highland is spelling, it has been at it longer than there have been people to watch, and it shows no sign of finishing soon.

**Evening**

Light here is ordinary, almost reassuringly so — clear, mild, honest light that gives the rust hills their full color twice a day, at the long slow ends of the long slow days. In the evening the foundry towns glow faintly from within, like banked coals seen through stone. The slagdoves settle into the stacks in a final grey sigh. A gondola crosses the darkening upland, singing its one soft note. Somewhere out on the plateau an ironback herd stands where it stood yesterday and will stand tomorrow, warm rock under warm hide under cooling violet sky.

Underfoot, patient as ever, the highland breathes out.
