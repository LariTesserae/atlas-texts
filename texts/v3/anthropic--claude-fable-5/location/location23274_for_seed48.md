**The Pans at Erebh-Saline**

The town sits eleven meters below sea level, in a depression where the air arrives already used — breathed by the salt flats to the south, exhaled over the brine fields, thick and mineral and warm as bathwater even in the hour before dawn. There is no dawn to speak of, really. The sun comes up behind a permanent gauze of evaporation haze and sulfur-dust, and the light that reaches the ground is the color of weak tea, casting shadows so soft they seem optional.

It rains here more than anyone expects. Not storms — the depression is too hot for storms to survive their descent into it — but a constant low-grade weeping. Moisture condenses out of the saturated air onto anything cooler than itself: the iron flanks of the evaporator halls, the underside of pipe galleries, the rungs of ladders, your own forearms. By midmorning everything metal is streaked dark and dripping. The drips fall into brine channels and gutters cut a century ago, and the gutters carry them back out to the pans, where the heat takes the water back and leaves the salt. The whole town is a slow respiratory loop of water that never quite escapes.

Nothing green grows. Nothing has, in living memory — the soil is salt to a depth no one has bothered to measure, crusted in places into pavements of halite that crunch and ring underfoot like crockery. Where groundwater seeps up through the crust it builds low terraces, white shading to ochre shading to a bruised orange where the iron comes through, smooth-lipped pools stacked like dinnerware, each one breathing a thread of steam. The terraces are the oldest things here and the works were built around them, not over them; even the original engineers understood that the springs were the reason for everything and would outlast everything.

The town itself is large — far larger than its population, which by now is a few hundred souls scattered through housing rows built for ten thousand. The extraction company laid it out in the confident grid of its era: pump houses, crystallizer sheds the size of cathedrals, a rail spur, water towers on riveted legs, six brick chimneys of which two still run. The works never closed. They simply concentrated, the way the brine does. Most of the operation runs on a skeleton of automated pumps and a night shift of eleven people, and so the streets have the particular emptiness of a place still maintained — gutters clear, valves greased, windows intact and dark. You can walk for twenty minutes down a service avenue between blind warehouse walls, your footsteps the loudest thing in the world, and pass no one, and know that someone passed an hour ago because the condensate on the handrail is wiped in one long swipe.

It is quiet the way machinery rooms are quiet between cycles. The base note is a pump house two streets over, a deep slow thudding more felt than heard, like a heartbeat in a wall. Above that: dripping, everywhere, irregular, from a thousand pipe joints. Sometimes a pressure-relief valve sighs. Sometimes, far off across the pans, a sheet of salt crust settles with a sound like a door closing in another building.

And then there is the life, which is the thing no visitor is prepared for.

The brine kills plants and welcomes almost everything else. The pans at the right concentration are soup — brine shrimp in densities that tint whole hectares the dull red of rust, copepods, the larvae of flies. The flies themselves rise off the water in the warm hours in columns you can see from the chimney tops: smoke-gray pillars, drifting, dissolving, reforming, utterly silent until you walk into one and the air goes granular against your skin. They do not bite. They do not need to. They eat the pans.

The birds eat the flies and the shrimp, and the birds are beyond counting. Stilts and avocets work the shallow pans in loose thousands, stepping with that fastidious high-kneed delicacy through water hot enough to redden a hand. Flamingos hold the far southern lagoons in a pale band along the horizon, a smear of pink so wide it reads at first as a mineral stain. Swallows have colonized the cold ends of the cooling galleries, ten thousand mud nests under the pipe racks, and at dusk they come off the structures all at once and hunt the fly columns in shoals. Owls live in the dead chimneys. A fox population, salt-stained to a grubby cream, has run the warehouse district for generations, growing fat on eggs and the heat-killed birds that wash up against the dike walls. On the iron itself: geckos, wherever the metal stays warm into the night, which is everywhere.

The few residents have arrangements with all of this. Doorways are netted against swallows. The night-shift workers carry brooms not for dust but for moving roosting birds off the catwalks, gently, the way one moves a sleeping cat. There is a man whose entire job, three days a week, is clearing fly drift from the intake screens, raking it into windrows that the foxes and the ravens take down by morning.

The cycles are visible if you stay long enough. The pumps lift brine, the pans concentrate, the shrimp bloom, the flies rise, the birds arrive in their seasons and strip the pans and leave guano that feeds the next bloom. The condensate drips and returns. Salt creeps up the brick of the older buildings in a white tideline, a few millimeters a year, and a man with a wire brush walks the company rows knocking it back down, and has been losing slowly, and seems untroubled by this.

Nothing here is strange. Everything here is only what hot brine, old iron, and a hundred and twenty years will do, given each other and time. The town does not feel abandoned and does not feel inhabited. It feels *tended* — by its eleven night workers, its foxes, its million birds — the way a body is tended by things too small to thank.
