**The Terraces of Loud Water**

You arrive at altitude — real altitude, the kind where the air thins and the sun stops negotiating. The plateau sits somewhere above four thousand meters, and yet it is hot, wet-hot, a greenhouse heat that should not survive up here. The sky overhead is a hard cobalt, almost violet at the zenith, and the light comes down with a high-elevation clarity that makes every leaf-edge look cut from glass. But the ground breathes steam. The whole plateau is geothermal — riddled with hot springs, weeping fissures, fumaroles dressed in moss — and so the cold of the height and the heat of the deep earth meet at the surface and produce this: a tropical city in the sky, sweating under a near-black blue.

It *is* a city, though no one built it. That has to be said early, because the eye refuses to believe it. The plateau is carved — or grown, or settled, the verb keeps slipping — into streets, terraces, plazas, colonnades. The travertine that the hot springs deposit has been laying itself down for so long, in such patient, mineral-witted layers, that it has produced architecture: stacked white-and-rust balconies, fluted columns where water once dripped from overhangs, broad shallow stairs descending from pool to pool. There are structures here four and five stories tall, with window-shaped voids and door-shaped passages, arranged along avenues that run kilometer after kilometer in both directions, dense as any old town's heart — and not one chisel mark anywhere. Not one tool. The stone has the slumped, melted-candle smoothness of pure precipitation. The city is a secretion. It looks designed because design, here, is simply what water does when it is given long enough and never interrupted.

And it has never been interrupted. That is the deepest fact of the place. The terraces are weathered — softened at every edge, stained in long mineral banners of orange and pale jade — but they are weathered the way a river stone is weathered: into a final form. Equilibrium. Every pool deposits exactly as much stone as it dissolves. Every channel carries exactly the flow it has always carried. You could come back in a thousand years and find the same drop falling from the same lip of the same balcony.

The vegetation is everywhere and obscene with health. Tree ferns crowd the avenues, their fiddleheads frozen mid-uncurl. Orchids stack themselves up the travertine columns three and four plants deep, roots interlacing, blooms shouldering each other aside — the density is jungle-density, things growing on things growing on things, hanging moss thick enough to part like curtains. Steam-fed, spring-warmed, sun-hammered: every condition for riot. And yet — look closely — no petal on the ground. No browning frond. No seed, no fruit, no rot. The lushness is at its absolute peak and *holds there*. Nothing here is in the act of growing. Everything is in the state of having grown.

No animals. Not one. No insect hum in all that flowering, no bird in all that canopy, no fish in the steaming pools. The flowers gape open for pollinators that do not exist and never seem to have existed. The absence is total enough that you begin to feel it as a presence — the city is *for* something, shaped around an inhabitant-shaped hole.

Then there is the sound.

It hits you before the city does, while you're still climbing the last ridge: a roar so large it stops being a noise and becomes a medium, like submersion. The plateau's far edge is a falls — not one falls, an entire rim of them, the hot rivers of the terraces pouring off the mountain's shoulder into cloud, a curtain of white water several kilometers long. The roar of it fills the city completely. It is deafening in the literal sense: stand in the open plazas and you cannot hear your own voice, cannot hear your footsteps, can barely hear your thoughts. The sound has texture and architecture of its own — deep continental bass under the streets, a hissing treble in the steam vents, mid-range thunder rolling along the avenues like traffic. The whole city is a resonating body and the falls play it constantly, identically, forever. After an hour you stop hearing it as loud. You start hearing it as *silence with the volume turned all the way up* — because it never varies, not by a decibel, not by a tremor, and a sound that never changes is the closest thing to no sound at all.

It is in the roar that you first notice the breakage.

Small things at first. An echo that arrives before you make the noise. A staircase of eleven steps that takes thirteen footfalls. Steam that rises from a vent, hangs, and rises *again* — the same coil of vapor playing itself over, like a held breath the air keeps re-taking. The falls at the rim, watched long enough, can be seen to fall in loops: a particular comb of white water descending, and descending, and being the same descent.

Then larger things. The city repeats. Walk an avenue west and you will pass a plaza with a leaning column wrapped in a single scarlet orchid — and twenty minutes later pass it again, the same column, the same flower, the same drop of water falling from the same lip, though you never turned. The streets are not a maze; a maze implies you could be elsewhere. Here there is increasingly a sense that the city is one neighborhood, perfectly rendered, tiled across the plateau like a reflection between two mirrors, and that the open scale of the place — the avenues running to the steam-blurred horizon — is the same few hundred meters extended by repetition rather than by distance. Look down certain colonnades and the perspective fails to converge: the far end is no smaller than the near end. Look into certain pools and the reflection shows the terraces from an angle you do not occupy.

At the rim itself, where the roar becomes a physical pressure on the chest and the spray rises hot as breath, the breakdown is frankest. The water goes over the edge and into a white nothing — not mist, exactly, more like the place where the renderer of the world stopped — and some of it can be seen, far out in the blankness, falling *upward*, returning along impossible diagonals to the springheads, completing a circuit that no geography could host. The falls are loud because they are the engine of the whole stasis: the same water, going around, at the same temperature, forever, and the roar is the sound of that perpetuity straining at its own seams.

Stand long enough in one of the steaming plazas, under the violet-blue sky, hemmed in by tree ferns and orchids and the white melted-candle architecture, deafened into a kind of peace, and the place resolves into what it is: a moment, not a location. Somewhere, once, a hot wet morning on a high green mountain achieved such perfection that it refused to end — and the refusal has consequences. Time pools here like the water does, deposits its layers, builds its terraces. The city is what a single instant looks like after it has been left running for ten thousand years.

Nothing will change while you are there. Nothing changed before you came. The roar holds every note. The steam holds every coil. The one scarlet orchid holds its bloom on the leaning column — in this plaza, and the next, and the next.
