**The Hollow in the Weft**

There is a pocket of open water, roughly the size of a small chapel, and everything around it — above, below, on every side, for distances no one has measured — is plant.

You would not find it by looking. The green goes on in all directions: a drowned continent of moss, or something that has been moss for so long it has become the thing moss aspires to. The plant-mass is the substrate here. There is no rock beneath it that anyone has verified. There is no surface above it that the light insists on. There is only the weft — dense, cold, slow, ancient — and here and there, rarely, cavities like this one, held open the way a lung holds a breath it has decided to keep.

The hollow is cold. Not killing cold, but the deliberate cold of deep still water, the kind that finds the joints first. The water is utterly clear in the strange way water gets when nothing animal has ever stirred it — no silt kicked up by fins, no cloud of larvae, no drifting flake of anything that once had eyes. Nothing has ever swum here. Nothing has ever needed to.

The walls of the hollow are the plant itself, and they are old in the way riverbeds are old: every surface settled, every filament lying in the direction the centuries pushed it. The green is not one green. Near the floor of the cavity it deepens toward a black-viridian, dense as felt. Higher up it pales through jade into a color that has no dry-land name — a luminous celadon that seems to sit slightly in front of the surface producing it. The walls do not drip or wave. They have finished moving, mostly. When they do move, it is over hours: a slow clenching, a settling, like a sleeper adjusting a shoulder.

The light is the first strange thing, if you are counting. It is ordinary in quality — clear, faintly cool, about the brightness of an overcast morning — but there is nowhere for it to come from. The weft is hundreds of meters thick in every direction, maybe thousands, maybe more. The light arrives anyway. It arrives *through* the plant, or from it, delivered without glow or source, as if illumination were simply one of the nutrients the mass circulates, piped along the filaments the way other things pipe sugar. If you press your hand into the wall (the wall permits this, closing softly around your fingers like cold wet velvet, releasing them just as softly), the light inside the hollow dims very slightly, in a place unrelated to where you're touching. Something is accounting for you.

Because the plant is not merely alive. It is *engineered*, or it engineered itself, so long ago that the distinction has eroded like everything else here. There is no artifact in the hollow, no strut, no seam, nothing made — and yet the whole place is made, at a scale below sight. The filaments of the weft are threaded with structures finer than cells, doing work that ordinary botany does not describe: they hold this cavity open against the pressure of the surrounding mass without any visible architecture of support; they keep the water in the hollow a fraction of a degree warmer than the water in the walls, for reasons of their own; they bend gravity here very slightly, so that drifting things — a loosed strand of fiber, a bubble of gas the plant exhales once a day — fall not downward but *inward*, toward the center of the room, where they hang in a sparse, slow constellation before the walls quietly reabsorb them. The center of the hollow is always gathering a thin population of these suspended fragments: five, six, a dozen pale motes turning in place, spaced far apart, like furniture in a house that owns almost nothing.

The sound is the second strange thing, and it is not subtle. The hollow is loud. The weft hums — a deep, layered, resonant thrum, felt in the sternum as much as heard, the sound of an unimaginable volume of living machinery running at an unimaginable patience. Water carries it perfectly. There is a base drone somewhere below hearing, and above it, tones that shift on cycles of hours: a fifth resolving over an afternoon, a slow beat-frequency between two nearly identical pitches that takes half a day to complete one throb. It is not song and it is not noise. It is the sound of computation, possibly, or circulation, or thought — the plant conducting whatever business justifies its own physics. Inside the hollow the sound gathers and folds on itself; the cavity may exist partly *as an ear*, or a resonating chamber, a place the weft keeps open so it can listen to itself.

Nothing here hurries. The gas bubble is exhaled from the same fold in the western wall — call it western; the hollow has no compass — once per long cycle, rises inward, hangs, is taken back. The pale motes accumulate and are absorbed. Over a span of years the whole cavity is believed to migrate slowly through the mass, the leading wall growing forward filament by filament while the trailing wall closes like water behind a very slow ship, so that the room is always the same room and never in the same place.

There are no bones in the walls. No shells, no burrows, no fossil of anything that ever moved on its own. Whatever bargain the weft struck with the deep, animals were not party to it. The hollow is not lonely, exactly — loneliness needs an expectation of company. It is simply complete. A cold green room, humming with its own vast interior life, holding a chapel's worth of clear water open in the middle of a world made entirely of itself, and keeping it, patiently, the way one keeps a thought one is not yet finished thinking.
