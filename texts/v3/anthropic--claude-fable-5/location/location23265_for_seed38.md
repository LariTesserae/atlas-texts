# The Verdancy

There was rock here once. You can take that on faith, the way you take the existence of the sea floor on faith — somewhere down beneath everything, under perhaps a kilometer of accumulated living matter, there is a continent of stone, ground nearly flat by a length of time that has no honest name. The plateau it became stands high enough that the air thins and cools toward something merely warm, high enough that clouds do not pass over it so much as graze against it, dragging their bellies through the upper growth and leaving themselves behind as water.

But you will never see the rock. Nothing here has seen it for an age. The land is plant, all the way down to where the question stops mattering.

## The Substrate

What passes for ground is a mat — though *mat* is too thin a word — of interwoven root, rhizome, compacted moss, and the soft dark flesh of older growth that died in place and was simply grown through rather than removed. Nothing here is removed. The Verdancy does not discard. It builds downward in layers the way coral does, the living film riding on the dead, except that the boundary between living and dead is unclear at any depth, and there are slow pale threads running through even the deepest strata, doing work no one watches.

The surface gives underfoot. Not dangerously — it has had so long to settle that it has found its final density, a firm wet sponge that takes a footprint and returns it over the course of a day, water welling up grey-green in the heel mark and then receding. After rain, and it is nearly always after rain, the whole surface weeps. Press a hand flat anywhere and water rises around your fingers, body-warm, smelling faintly of crushed stems and something older underneath, a mineral darkness, the breath of the buried continent coming up through a kilometer of filter.

There are no hills in the ordinary sense. The land has been worn and grown and re-settled so many times that it has arrived at long, continental swells — rises you walk up for half a day without noticing you are climbing, hollows wide enough to hold their own weather. In the hollows the mist pools and never fully lifts. On the swells the growth changes character, becomes tighter, more silvered, combed flat by winds that have been blowing the same direction for so long the plants have made the wind part of their architecture.

## The Architecture

Because the vegetation is the architecture, here. It does not merely cover the land; it *constructs*. In the broad hollows the growth has raised itself into vaults — trunks, if you can call them trunks, that left singularity behind long ago and exist now as fused colonnades, dozens of stems grown together and braided and buttressed, holding up ceilings of leaf so dense and so old that they have become structural, a roof of layered green flesh through which the daylight arrives strained, doubled, dimmed to a permanent submarine gloaming.

This is the quality of light everywhere under the canopy regions: dim, green-gold, directionless. Noon is a brightening of the ceiling, a sense of glow rather than a source of it. There are places where a vault has thinned — never torn; nothing tears here — and a column of true light stands down through the interior space like something solid, full of drifting spores, and the growth around it leans in across decades, closing it with the patience of a wound healing.

Between the vaulted hollows, on the high open swells, the canopy gives way to meadow — but a meadow as deep as a house, a tangled three-dimensional pasture of vine and cushion-plant and great soft ferns the color of old bronze, layered so thickly that walking through it is closer to wading, or to moving through a crowd. Density without menace. Everything touching everything. You cannot extend an arm without your hand coming to rest on something alive, and the something alive does not react, because it has been touched by things for ten thousand years and has incorporated being touched into its idea of weather.

## The Water

Water does not flow here so much as *circulate*. There are no rivers — a river requires a channel, and the Verdancy permits no channel to stay open. Instead the whole landscape conducts water through itself the way tissue does. Rain falls, or mist condenses on the high silvered swells, and the water travels down through the mat, body-warm by the time it has gone a meter deep, and emerges days later and kilometers away, seeping from the green walls of a hollow, beading on the underside of vaulted roots, dripping in slow irregular rhythm into the pools.

The pools are the only open water, and they are strange — perfectly still, perfectly dark, rimmed not with mud but with a tight cuff of moss like the fitting of a lens. The water in them is not quite water. It is faintly thickened, faintly sweet, infused with whatever the mat exudes, and things live in it that live nowhere else: pale translucent swimmers, slow as drifting leaves, that surface once an hour to take air with a sound like a soft kiss, the loudest noise that pool will make all day.

Everything weeps. The colonnades weep. The leaf-ceilings collect condensation along their veins and release it in long deliberate drops. Walk for an hour and you are soaked, not from rain but from the air itself, the warm exhalation of a continent-sized organism that breathes moisture in and out on a cycle measured in seasons.

## The Inhabitants

The fauna is abundant in the way that fauna in old, stable places is abundant: not in spectacle, but in saturation. Nothing flees here, because nothing has needed to flee for a very long time. The predators that remain are slow, ambush-patient things that have themselves half-merged with the architecture — there is a creature like a flattened mantled sloth, moss-furred, that occupies the junctions of the colonnades and may not move for a week, and whose hunting consists of being in the right place across geological patience.

Mostly the animals are grazers, browsers, burrowers in the living mat. Soft-bodied things the size of dogs that move through the deep meadow like swimmers, leaving trails that close behind them. Flocks of small flightless climbers, round and dark-eyed, that travel the vault-ceilings upside down in loose congregations, feeding on the leaf-flesh in patterns that, seen from below, leave pale spirals the canopy slowly re-greens. Their calls, when they call, are quiet — clicks, low whirring purrs, brief contact notes. Loudness has been bred out of this place. In an environment with no open distances and no sudden dangers, there is nothing to shout across, nothing to shout about.

So the soundscape is dripping. Dripping, and the muffled creak of the mat adjusting its weight, and at long intervals a deep soft groan from far below — the buried strata settling another fraction, the continent of dead growth compressing under the living one. Travelers, if there were travelers, would learn to sleep through the groans and wake at true silences, because a true silence means the dripping has stopped, and the dripping stops only when the whole canopy holds its water at once, just before the mist comes down.

## The Marks

Someone was here. Not recently — *recently* is not a scale this place offers — but the evidence persists precisely because the Verdancy does not discard.

You find them on the high swells mostly, where the growth is shallowest: standing stones, except they are not stone. Whatever they were made of has been replaced, the way fossil replaces bone — the mat grew over each structure, digested it across centuries, and rebuilt it in its own materials, a faithful copy in dense woody tissue, soft-edged, green at the seams. A doorway with no walls, rendered in living grain. A ring of low pillars holding nothing. A flight of seven steps rising out of the meadow and ending in air, the whole staircase quietly flowering along its risers in the wet season.

There is no tool-mark anywhere, because there are no longer any surfaces that held tool-marks. There is only the *shape* of intention, preserved the way a wave is preserved in sandstone — the substance entirely changed, the gesture intact. Whether the Verdancy keeps these forms out of something like memory, or whether it simply has no mechanism for forgetting, is the kind of question the place declines to answer.

Once in a great while, in the dim hollows, you come on something more intimate: a hollow in the mat the size and shape of a sleeping body, moss-lined, faintly warm, which the surface has maintained, open and unhealed, against all its habits of closure. These are the strangest artifacts here. Everything else, the land copied. These it *kept*.

## The Tempo

Nothing here happens quickly, and so nothing here, exactly, happens. Change arrives the way age arrives in a face — invisible day to day, undeniable across the long view. The vaults migrate, whole architectural districts of canopy drifting downslope at the pace of centimeters a year. The pools open and close like very slow eyes. The mist comes down each evening as the high swells cool, fills the hollows, beads on every surface, and burns off into the green-gold dimness by midmorning: the closest thing the Verdancy has to a heartbeat, one beat per day, and even that more a sigh than a pulse.

It is warm, and wet, and dim, and crowded with quiet life, and it is finished. That is the deepest strangeness of the place, deeper than the copied doorways or the kept beds: the sense, standing in the colonnaded gloom with warm water rising around your boots, that you are not in a landscape that is becoming anything. The becoming is over. This is the settled state — what a world looks like after every faster process has run out, when only growth and rain and patience are left, repeating themselves with the calm of something that has already won.
