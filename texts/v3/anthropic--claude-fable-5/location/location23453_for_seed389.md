# The Annealing City

The mountain is not finished. That is the first thing anyone learns here, and the last thing anyone forgets: the rock underfoot is younger than the buildings that stand on it.

You arrive — if arrival is the word for the long crawl up the funicular ramps — at something close to seven kilometers of altitude, where the air has gone thin and glassy and the sky overhead is a deep mineral blue, almost violet at the zenith. Below, far below, cloud decks lie like a sea floor. The city occupies the upper slopes and shoulders of a massif that is still rising out of the earth at a rate the surveyors measure in finger-widths per year. The stone is dark, almost iridescent in places — fresh basalt and something stranger, a feldspar that hasn't yet decided on its final crystal habit. In road cuts you can see it mid-thought: bands of rock that are still faintly translucent, like wax beginning to set. The geologists call it *green stone*, not for its color but in the sense that lumber is green. Uncured. Whole districts are built on foundations of rock that has not yet finished becoming rock.

The heat comes up from below. Not violently — there are no lava fields here, no fountaining vents — but as a constant, patient pressure, the warmth of an enormous body that has not yet cooled from its making. Pavements are warm through boot soles. Cellar walls sweat. In winter, which at this altitude should be lethal, the city is merely hot, the streets steaming gently where snow falling out of the thin air touches stone and vanishes before it lands.

## The Water

This is the damp paradox of the place: the air is desert-dry, the slopes are bare mineral for a hundred kilometers in any direction, and yet the city drips.

Every cold surface condenses. The mountain exhales steam from a thousand hairline fissures — water cooked out of the deep rock as it crystallizes — and the city has organized itself around catching that breath. Above the rooflines stand the *condensary towers*: tall fluted columns of dark cast iron, finned like radiators, their upper reaches deliberately exposed to the freezing high-altitude air. Steam rises into them, beads, and runs down internal channels into cisterns. At dawn you can hear them, just barely — a fine ticking and trickling, thousands of droplets finding their way down, the closest thing the city has to birdsong.

Water collects in unexpected pockets. There are streets where a single fissure breathes more heavily than its neighbors and the cobbles are always slick, ferns of mineral scale blooming white and orange across the stone — the only flowering this city knows. There are interior courtyards kept deliberately cool, where the walls weep and shallow black pools stand mirror-still, doubling the violet sky. Children — there are children, the city is old enough for that — are taught which pools are drinking pools and which are *charged*: water so saturated with dissolved silica that a hand left in it too long comes out wearing a faint glass glove.

Rain comes rarely, and when it comes it falls as the mountain's own exhalation returned to it — clouds that the massif breathes out in the afternoon condense against its cold upper pinnacles at night and slide back down as a brief, hot, mineral-tasting drizzle. The whole hydrology is closed, intimate. The city drinks the mountain's sweat.

## The City Itself

It is dense in the way that things built on a slope are dense: not sprawling but *stacked*. Streets are staircases. Districts are terraces. Buildings lean into the gradient and into each other, sharing walls, sharing heat, their foundations keyed deep into the green stone with iron pilings that the masons drive in while the rock is still soft enough to take them — a brief window, a few decades, before the stone cures hard as anything on earth and holds those pilings forever.

The architecture is iron and stone and almost nothing else. No timber — there are no trees, there have never been trees, the mountain is too young and too high and too hot for anything green to have found it. So the city is mineral through and through: walls of fitted basalt block, roofs of riveted iron plate gone deep brown-red with patient oxidation, window frames of cast bronze, the glass itself made on-site from the mountain's own silica in the great rolling mills of the lower terraces. The effect is somber and beautiful: a city in the colors of rust, charcoal, verdigris, and the occasional startling white of mineral bloom, all of it under that hard violet sky.

And everything is built to *move* — slowly, slowly. Because the mountain is still rising, and not evenly. The west shoulder lifts faster than the east. Districts drift apart at the pace of a centimeter a year. So the streets are jointed like the plates of a skull: expansion seams run down the centers of the avenues, bridged with sliding iron plates that grind apart almost imperceptibly over decades and are periodically re-shimmed by the city's most respected guild, the *Adjusters*, who walk the seams with gauges and long pry bars and keep ledgers going back three hundred years recording the growth of every block, the way one might mark a child's height on a doorframe. Buildings have turnbuckle tendons running through their walls, tightened a quarter-turn each solstice. The cathedral — there is a cathedral, or something shaped like one — has been under tension for two centuries and hums very faintly if you press your ear to its piers, a single deep note that drops in pitch by a fraction of a semitone per generation as the structure relaxes into its own weight.

## The Industry

The city exists because of the heat. That is its whole economy, its founding logic.

Drilled into the slopes are the *heatworks*: shafts sunk into the warm young rock, lined with iron, drawing up the mountain's thermal breath to drive the city's machines. There are no furnaces here in the ordinary sense — no one burns anything, there is nothing to burn — the mountain *is* the furnace. The foundries of the lower terraces cast iron and bronze and glass using bore-heat alone, taps driven deep enough to reach rock that still glows. Steam from the deep fissures turns turbines in vaulted halls; the turbines turn slowly, enormous, their flywheels the size of houses, geared down and down and down because the mountain's pressure is patient rather than fierce. Power here is not explosive. It is *gradual and immense*, and the machinery is built to match: everything oversized, everything slow, drive shafts thick as columns rotating at speeds you can watch, count, almost converse with.

This is why the city is quiet. One expects industrial racket and instead finds something closer to a monastery with machinery: the deep, sub-audible rotation of flywheels felt in the sternum more than heard; the soft continuous hiss of regulated steam; the tick of the condensary towers; the occasional long groan, like a ship's hull, of a building taking up slack. The thin air carries sound poorly anyway — voices don't travel, footsteps fall dead. Workers in the turbine halls communicate by hand-sign and by tapping on the iron, codes rapped out on railings that travel through the metal further than any shout would through the air. The loudest regular event in the city is the noon release, when excess steam pressure is vented from the high stacks all at once: a single vast white exhalation, a sigh you hear as a soft roar for perhaps a minute, watched by everyone, and then silence settling back down like dust.

The export — what goes down the funicular ramps in iron hoppers — is glass and crystal and *annealed things*. The city's specialty, the craft no lowland works can match: objects buried in chambers cut into the warm rock and left there for years, decades, while the mountain's heat works through them with a patience no kiln can imitate. Glass annealed this way is said to be unbreakable. Bells cured in the rock for a generation ring, they say, with a tone that settles arguments. There are chambers in the deep galleries holding objects whose commissioners died long ago, tended by the Annealers' Guild, whose ledgers are wills as much as work orders.

## The Strangeness

Live here long enough and you learn the things visitors are not told outright.

The green stone *grows*. Not metaphorically. Pilings driven into it are found, decades later, sheathed in new mineral, the rock having crept up around the iron like slow water closing over a thrown stone. Tunnels narrow. The deep galleries must be re-broached every century or so, their walls shaved back. The Adjusters' ledgers record more than expansion seams; they record the places where the mountain has begun, very gently, to *take things back* — a boundary marker swallowed to its waist, a forgotten cellar found seamlessly filled, the iron door still in place, fused into a wall of flawless new stone.

And the stone remembers heat in patterns. Press a hand to certain walls in the old districts and the warmth is not uniform — it moves, over hours, in slow waves, like circulation. The masons say young rock is just like that, convection in the cooling deep, nothing more. The Annealers say less and charge more for chambers cut in the "circulatory" zones, where their long curing works fastest. There is a third opinion, held quietly, mostly by the old: that a mountain still forming is a mountain still, in some pre-living mineral sense, *deciding*, and that the city was wise or lucky to have built itself flexible, jointed, deferential — a city that asks permission of its ground every solstice, quarter-turn by quarter-turn.

The water carries it too. The charged pools, the silica-heavy springs — things left in them are not merely coated but *completed*. A cracked bowl submerged for a season comes out whole, the crack filled with clear glass finer than any repair. The hospital keeps a charged bath; what it is used for is not discussed with outsiders, but the city's old workers have hands webbed with thin glassy seams where injuries healed shut, and they flex them in cold weather, and the seams catch the light.

## The Living

Almost nothing lives here that did not arrive on the funicular. Almost.

There are the *vent swifts* — that's the city's name for them, though no naturalist has agreed they are birds. Small dark shapes, perhaps a few hundred in all, that ride the thermal columns above the fissures and the noon release. They are seen and not caught. They do not land where anyone can watch. Their droppings — found on the high iron, white and crystalline — are nearly pure silica, which has led to a long, unresolved municipal argument about what, exactly, they eat, and whether "eat" is the word. They wheel up in the noon steam, scattering through the white plume like punctuation, and that brief silent spiraling is the city's one daily spectacle of animal life, watched from a thousand windows.

In the deep galleries, the Annealers speak of *seam mice*: warm spots that move along the walls at a walking pace, that pause when you pause. Heat-shadows. Nothing has ever been seen. The guild leaves small dishes of charged water in the curing chambers anyway, and the dishes are sometimes found grown over with crystal in shapes the chemistry does not quite explain — branching, recursive, the closest thing to a fern this world will ever produce. Pre-life, perhaps. Or the mountain practicing.

There are no insects. No moss. No lichen even — the founders' generation kept watch for it, the first colonist of any bare rock anywhere, and it never came; the stone is too hot, or too busy. The city's only green is verdigris.

## At Evening

Light here is severe and exact: thin air, no haze, shadows like cut paper. At evening the sun drops below the cloud sea and for a long hour everything is lit from beneath, the undersides of the condensary towers glowing amber, the iron roofs burning red-gold, the steam from the fissures turned to slow columns of fire. The expansion seams in the avenues show as dark lines of shadow, the city's skeleton briefly visible. The temperature, which never really falls, holds steady; only the light goes.

Then the violet deepens to black, and the stars come out in numbers that frighten newcomers, and the city does not light itself much — a few sodium lamps at the funicular heads, dim warmth-colored windows — because everyone is mostly indoors, and the streets stand empty and faintly steaming, and the only sounds are the tick of the towers drinking the mountain's breath, the bone-deep rotation of the flywheels, and now and then, from somewhere under everything, a long, slow, settling groan: the sound of the ground continuing, unhurried, to become itself.

The Adjusters say the mountain will finish rising in perhaps ten thousand years. Then it will cool, the heatworks will fail, the condensaries will run dry, and the city will have to leave or learn another way to live. No one is troubled by this. It is recorded in the ledgers, between the seam measurements, in the same patient hand: *the mountain is not finished. Neither are we. Adjust accordingly.*
