**The Summit Sea**

There is a mountain whose peak you will never see, because the peak is a basin, and the basin is full.

You must imagine first the climb that no longer matters: miles of stone rising out of whatever world lies below, up past where weather thins, up past where the maps would end — and then, at the top, instead of a summit, a rim. Inside the rim: water. A sea perched at the roof of everything, cold and dark and so old it has stopped having moods. It does not storm. It does not tide. The water at this altitude pressed itself into stillness long ago and stayed.

Beneath the surface, everything is made.

Not made by hands — there were never hands here. The walls were grown. Long before the basin flooded, a kind of vegetation lived on this height that built the way coral builds, the way trees build, but with intent spread across centuries: vast architect-plants that laid down cellulose the way other things lay down bone. They raised terraces. They wove vaulted halls from their own braided trunks, spiraled stairways that climb nothing, colonnades of root grown straight and fluted as if to mock the idea of pillars. There is no native rock exposed anywhere. Dive to the floor of the summit sea and your fingers find only plant-matter, dense as ironwood, smoothed and silted, a substrate of slow green construction going down farther than anyone has measured. The mountain's top is a building, and the building is a plant, and the plant is — mostly — asleep, or finished, which down here may be the same thing.

The flood came so long ago it reads as geology now. Whatever the architects were building toward, the water ended it gently, and erosion did the rest. Edges that were once ornamental have rounded into suggestion. Doorways have slumped into ovals. Whole districts of the grown city have settled, compressed, half-buried themselves in their own shed fibers, so that the landscape underwater rolls like drowned dunes with the occasional intact spire breaking upward — a tower of woven heartwood, leaning, holding, having leaned and held for ten thousand years and showing no intention of changing its mind. This is the final form. The place is not decaying. It has *arrived*.

It is sparse, in the way of finished things. Swim out from any structure and you cross long volumes of open black water — gaps the size of valleys between one grown ruin and the next, the floor below you a dim plain of compacted plant-mass, featureless for a quarter mile, and then: another shape resolving out of the dark. A dome. A row of arches. A single colossal trunk, hollow, wide as a street, standing alone with nothing around it, its interior a vertical cave rising two hundred feet toward the faraway surface. The emptiness between structures is not absence. It is spacing. Something about the architecture insists on it, the way old trees in old forests insist on their distances.

The light, what exists of it, is a rumor. The surface is far overhead and the sky above the summit is itself thin and pale, so what filters down is a gray-violet dimness that gives up entirely past the first hundred feet. Below that, the only illumination is incidental and faint: a slow blue-green smolder along certain seams of the living walls, where the architect-plant still metabolizes — not glowing for any reason, just leaking light the way a fire leaks heat. The seams trace the structures like dim circuitry, just bright enough to show you an edge, never bright enough to show you a room. Most of what you would know of this place, you would know by touch and by sound.

And it is *loud*. This is the surprise. Stillness above; chorus below.

The cold water carries sound for miles and the fauna never stops producing it. The summit sea is thick with life that has nowhere else to be — pale eels braided into the colonnades by the hundreds, their bodies making a low communal rasp against the fluted root; blind heavy-bodied fish that drum, each colony in its own hall, each hall its own pitch, so that swimming the length of a drowned terrace is like passing a row of struck bells; vast loose shoals of small silver things that turn all at once with a sound like an exhaled breath, filling the dark above the plains. Crustaceans the size of dogs graze the wall-fibers and their mouthparts click in overlapping rhythms that never resolve and never stop. Add to this the hollow trunks, which breathe — water moving through their flooded chambers with each faint thermal stirring, producing a continuous organ-note, deep, structural, the same note it has held for millennia. The sound is dense and layered and absolutely constant. It does not build to anything. There is no quiet hour. The drumming, the rasp, the clicking, the standing note of the towers — it is the sound of an equilibrium, life and architecture vibrating at the frequency they settled into long ago and will not leave.

The cold is steady too — not bitter, just permanent, the temperature of deep stone, the temperature of a thing that has stopped exchanging heat with the world. The water tastes faintly green. Fine pale silt — the shed dust of the architecture itself — hangs motionless at every depth, undisturbed for so long that a swimmer's wake would hang behind them like a scar in the dark, slow to close.

The one strangeness, the thing that keeps the place from being merely a drowned ruin: in a few of the deepest halls, the building is not quite over. The walls there are smoother. The arches are unworn. The blue seam-light is stronger, and the fiber underfoot has the faint resilience of new growth. The architects are not dead, only slowed past any rhythm an animal could perceive — still adding, a layer a century, to a city at the top of the world that flooded before it was finished and will be finished anyway, on its own terms, in the dark, in the cold, to the constant ringing of its tenants.

Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here is ending. The summit sea simply continues — full, loud, black, complete — the oldest room in the world, still under construction by a hand too slow to see.
