**The Kettle Ravine**

High on the shoulder of a mountain that is still being made, there is a slot in the rock perhaps forty meters long — a flaw, a split, a place where the new stone failed to knit. You cannot see it from any distance. You hear it first.

The mountain here is young in the way that only volcanic rock is young: black, glassy in places, edged like broken crockery. Nothing has had time to round it. The walls of the ravine rise close on either side, ten or twelve meters, and they are sharp enough to cut a careless hand — basalt that cooled fast, fractured along clean conchoidal curves, never weathered, never softened. Frost has only begun its slow work of prying. Here and there a flake the size of a door leans away from the wall at an angle that suggests it will not lean much longer.

It is night, or it might as well be. At this altitude the cloud deck often sits *below* you, but tonight it has climbed, and the ravine is filled with a darkness so complete that the eye invents textures. The only light — and it is barely light — comes from the floor of the slot: a dull, deep orange seam, visible only where the steam thins, the color of an ember seen through a closed fist. The mountain's heat is down there, not far down, and it announces itself constantly.

This is the sound. The ravine is loud the way a boiler room is loud. Steam forces itself through dozens of fissures under pressure, and each vent has its own voice — a sustained industrial hiss, a rhythmic chuff like a piston with a bad seal, a low resonant hooting where a chamber in the rock has the right dimensions to act as an organ pipe. The vents do not hold steady. One will choke, go silent for thirty seconds, and then clear its throat with a percussive *bang* of released pressure that slaps back and forth between the walls. Gravel rattles down after each report. The whole place runs like an unattended factory floor: valves opening and closing on no schedule, pressures rising and venting, the floor itself occasionally shuddering with a deep settling thump as the rock adjusts to its own newness. Nothing here was built. It only sounds built.

And in all this violence — the moss.

The walls of the ravine, wherever the steam breathes warmth onto cold stone, are upholstered in vegetation of an almost embarrassing lushness. The air outside the slot is thin and bitterly cool, a few degrees above freezing, scoured by wind; nothing grows on the open mountain for a thousand meters in any direction. But inside, each vent makes a microclimate, a sphere of warm humidity, and life has crowded into these spheres with desperate density. Cushion mosses a hand-span deep, swollen with condensed steam, layered shingle on shingle. Liverworts glistening black-green in the dark, lying flat against the heated rock like wet leather. Filmy ferns — one cell thick, translucent, trembling continuously in the vent-wind — packed so close their fronds overlap into a single quivering surface. Where two vents' warmth overlaps, the growth is thickest, walls of green meeting and merging until you could push your arm into the wall to the elbow and touch nothing but plant.

The dampness is not rain, though rain comes sometimes, briefly, sideways. The dampness is manufactured on site: steam rises, meets the cold air pouring over the lip of the ravine, and collapses into water against every cool surface. The upper walls weep constantly. Droplets fatten on the tips of fern fronds and fall, and the dripping is the quiet undertone beneath the roar — thousands of small impacts on moss, a sound like soft static, audible only in the lulls between vents.

There are no animals. Not an insect, not a bird, nothing. The gases that ride up with the steam see to that — the air near the floor carries a faint mineral sourness, sulfur and something sharper, survivable in passing but not livable. The plants tolerate it; nothing with lungs or spiracles has tried. So the ravine has the strange completeness of a world with one kingdom: green things and rock and water and heat, and no eyes in it at all.

The place is changing as fast as a place can change. A vent that hooted for a season goes cold, and its sphere of moss browns and slumps within weeks, while two meters away a new crack begins to whisper and the first green film appears around it like rust around a leak. Slabs fall. Steam finds new routes. The orange seam at the bottom brightens some nights and dims others, and after the brighter nights the whole floor of the ravine smells scorched and the lowest ferns are cooked pale.

Stand at the lip in the dark and the slot below you is pure turbulence rendered as sound: hissing, knocking, hooting, banging, dripping, the occasional grinding shrug of the rock itself. A new mountain, talking to itself through its only mouth, in a room it has not finished, lined floor to ceiling with the only audience that can bear to listen.
