**The Unfinished Terraces**

You come up onto it through cloud, and the first thing you understand — before you understand anything else — is that there is no ground here. Not in the mineral sense. The plateau you are standing on, a shelf of land the size of a small valley, hanging high enough that the weather forms below you as often as above, is floored entirely in a single woven surface: green-bronze, slightly sprung underfoot, made of flattened stems plaited tight as basketry and grown together at every crossing. It runs to the horizon in all directions. Rainwater stands on it in broad warm sheets an inch deep, and when you walk, you walk through your own reflection, and the floor flexes under each step like the lid of an enormous drum.

There is no stone. There is no soil. Dig your fingers into the weave and you find more weave beneath it, layer under layer, descending into a green darkness that is faintly warm, like the flank of something sleeping. The whole terrace is plant. The whole terrace is *made*.

Made — and not finished. That is the second thing you understand.

Scattered across the open distance, far enough apart that each one stands alone in its own pool of sky, are the structures. A buttressed hall, perhaps, here: walls of living trunk grafted side to side, rising forty feet and then simply stopping, the upper edge raw, the would-be vault sketched in by a few enormous tendrils that arc out into open air and end in pale, blunt, embryonic tips — the soft nub a fern has before it unrolls. A half-mile off, a staircase of stacked root climbs in wide spirals toward a tower that was never grown above its third turn. Farther still, what might have become a bridge reaches from one raised platform toward another and hangs incomplete over the gap, its end frayed into a hundred fine green threads feeling toward a far side they will never touch.

And here is the strangeness, the thing that takes longest to accept: nothing is growing. The tendril-tips are soft, new, infant — and they have been soft, new, and infant for longer than anything alive should be permitted to remain so. There is no deadwood, no rot, no autumn color, no fallen leaf anywhere on the wet floor. No seedling volunteers in the open spans. The terraces are caught at the exact moment of becoming and held there, embryonic forever, a place where construction stopped not by abandonment but by some deeper decision — as if the world here agreed, mid-gesture, that *this* degree of completion was the true and final form, and froze the unfinishedness itself into permanence.

It is warm. The air is heavy and sweet, faintly resinous, like the inside of a greenhouse crossed with the inside of a violin. Mist condenses on every surface and the surfaces weep continually: water beads along the underside of the great arcing tendrils and falls in slow fat drops into the standing sheets below, each drop landing with a round, resonant *tok* on the drum-tight floor.

This is why the place is loud.

Not loud the way a storm is loud — loud the way a cathedral is loud, structurally, inescapably. The hollow trunks of the unfinished halls are open at their raw tops, and the wind that crosses the plateau pours into them and sounds them like organ pipes, each structure holding a different chord depending on how far its growing was allowed to go. Beneath that, constant, is the floor itself: miles of taut woven membrane drummed by condensation, a vast irregular percussion with no downbeat. And beneath *that*, if you press your ear to the warm weave, a third sound — a slow internal surge, tidal, enormous, sap or water or something less nameable moving through the body of the terrace. The whole landscape is an instrument that plays itself at the same volume forever. After an hour you stop hearing it. After two hours you realize you have started breathing in time with it.

What built this had no tools. You can see that in every joint. Nothing is cut, nothing is fastened, nothing is shaped from outside. Every union is a graft, every angle is a trained curve, every wall is a negotiation between trunks persuaded to fuse. The builders worked the way a gardener works, except the gardener was also the garden. Whether they left, or died, or simply *became* the terraces and are now the warm floor under your feet, the place does not say.

Life, otherwise, is scarce and deliberate. Once in a long while a single pale bird — colorless, almost translucent, like something grown rather than hatched — crosses the open sky between two structures, lands on a soft tendril-tip without bending it, and sits motionless so long you begin to doubt it. There are slow, glossy snails, few and far between, that travel the standing water in straight lines as if assigned to routes. Nothing hunts. Nothing calls. The fauna here behaves less like animals than like punctuation.

Toward the edges of the plateau the floor thins and the weave loosens, and you can look down through gaps into a falling green understructure — buttress below buttress, root below root, the made world descending into cloud with no bottom in evidence. The light up here is plain and honest, ordinary daylight on an extraordinary place: no glow, no shimmer, just sun and mist and the bronze-green shine of wet living architecture.

You could stay a year and the soft tips would still be soft. The bridge would still be reaching. The water would still drum the floor with the same patient, sourceless music. The Unfinished Terraces are not waiting for completion. They have arrived. This — the arc half-flung, the stair half-climbed, the hand of the world stopped open in mid-offer — is what they are, and what, warmly, loudly, motionlessly, they will go on being.
