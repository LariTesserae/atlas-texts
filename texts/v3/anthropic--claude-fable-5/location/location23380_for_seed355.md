# The Basin of Settled Stone

You know you have arrived because the heat changes character. Out on the approach it was a dry heat, a furnace-breath; here, at the bottom of the long shallow descent, it becomes intimate. The air is at blood temperature and saturated, so that you cannot tell where your skin ends and the atmosphere begins. Everything sweats. The darkness itself seems to perspire.

There is almost no light. What little exists is not light in any honest sense — it is more that the darkness here is unevenly thick. Standing water holds a faint pallor, a gray luminance with no source, as if the water remembers a sky it once reflected and is still, very slowly, giving that memory back. By this non-light you can make out the city.

## The City

It fills the basin from rim to rim, a settlement the size of a river valley, and it is old in the way riverbeds are old. Streets, plazas, stairways, long colonnades half-sunk in warm shallow water — all of it built, unmistakably built, organized around axes and intersections and a logic of habitation. And yet there is not a tool mark anywhere. No chisel line, no mortar seam, no joint. Run your hand along a wall (it will be wet; everything is wet; the stone weeps continuously, beading and running like the flank of something alive) and you will find the surface smooth and continuous, as though the city were not constructed but *deposited* — secreted the way a reef is secreted, accumulated the way travertine accumulates, layer on layer over a span of time that makes the word "ancient" seem flippant.

It is finished settling. That is the feeling of the place. Whatever process raised these walls completed its work long ago and withdrew, and erosion has since brought everything into agreement with itself: corners gentled, thresholds dished by water rather than feet, every edge rounded to the exact softness the climate demands. Nothing here is crumbling. Nothing is being destroyed. The city has simply arrived at its own equilibrium and rests in it, hot and dark and breathing steam.

The buildings stand far apart. This is not a crowded city and never was; between structures lie wide flats of dark stone sheeted in an inch of warm water, expanses you cross for long minutes with nothing near you but your own reflection — and the reflection is one of the things you learn not to look at directly.

## The Water

Water is the city's true citizen. It stands in the streets, ankle-deep and body-warm. It hangs in the air as a fine perpetual mist that condenses on every surface and runs back down. It fills cisterns that were never dug, pools in courtyards, slides in thin silent films down stairways — and not always downward. There are stairs in this city where the water climbs, unhurried, step by step, pooling briefly on each tread before lifting itself to the next, ascending toward some upper darkness with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else it needs to be. No one built a pump. There are no pumps. There is nothing mechanical in the entire basin, not a hinge, not a wheel, not a worked piece of metal. The water simply has its own opinions about gravity, and gravity here has learned to negotiate.

There is a tide, though there is no moon and no sea. Twice in some long interval — you can feel it more than measure it — the standing water in the streets deepens by a hand's width, pauses, and recedes, leaving rings of pale mineral on the stone like the growth rings of a tree. The walls are striped with thousands of these rings. The city has been keeping time this way for longer than anything else has been keeping time at all.

## What Grows

Almost nothing. In the joints where wall meets water you will sometimes find a colorless reed, single, vertical, translucent as boiled glass, swaying though the air does not move. Pale mats of something between lichen and mineral bloom on the north faces of certain buildings — though "north" is a courtesy here, since direction does not entirely cooperate. These growths are scattered, one to a courtyard, one to a street, as though the city has rationed life to itself and is satisfied with the allotment. They are warm to the touch. Everything is warm to the touch.

There are no animals. No insects on the water, no calls in the dark, nothing that swims in the cisterns. The water is sterile and warm and absolutely clear, and when you stand still in it, nothing investigates you. The absence is total enough to feel deliberate — as though fauna is a category this place declined.

## The Breakdown

It begins gently and gets less gentle the longer you stay.

Distances do not commit. A colonnade that took two hundred steps to walk takes forty to walk back, and the difference is not yours to keep — somewhere, the city absorbs it. Certain intersections, approached from different streets, are revealed to be the same intersection, and you understand with a slow vertigo that the city may be much smaller than it appears, folded over on itself many times in the dark, a modest plaza wearing the geometry of a metropolis. Or it may be much larger. The basin's far rim, when you look for it, is sometimes a low black line and sometimes not there.

Doorways are the worst of it, or the best. Most open onto the rooms behind them, dark and wet and empty. Some open onto the room you are already standing in, so that you may look through a doorway and see your own back, beaded with condensation, three paces ahead of you. The convention among the doorways seems stable — the same doors misbehave in the same ways, with the reliability of long-settled habit. Even the impossibilities here are eroded smooth. This is reality not in the act of breaking but long after the break, healed crooked, weathered into a new and self-consistent shape.

Reflections run a few seconds ahead. You will see yourself in the street-water turn down an alley, and feel — not compulsion, exactly, but a kind of social pressure — to follow yourself. The reflections are never wrong about where you will go. It is unclear whether they predict you or instruct you, and the city, characteristically, declines to clarify.

## Sound

Near silence, but a furnished silence. The drip of condensate from a thousand eaves, irregular, never quite resolving into rhythm. The faint mineral tick of stone equalizing its temperature with the air. Occasionally — perhaps four times in a long stay — a single deep sound from far across the basin: a tone like a vast jar being set down gently on a vast table. It does not echo. It arrives, sometimes, a moment before it occurs, so that you find yourself already turning toward it when it sounds.

Your own footsteps come back to you wrong: sometimes doubled, sometimes from the wrong direction, sometimes not at all, the splash swallowed entire. The city handles sound the way it handles distance, with discretion and creative accounting.

## The Processes

Do not mistake the stillness for stasis. The basin is busy in the manner of a body asleep. The tide breathes in and out. The mist rises off the warm water, films the walls, and runs down again — a complete hydrological cycle conducted within a hundred vertical feet, with no sky required. The mineral rings creep upward by a hair's width an age. And the buildings themselves trade places: slowly, over the span of many tides, a tower noted in the southern quarter will be found in the west, its rings of tide-scale intact, its single glass reed swaying in its courtyard, having moved without ever, at any observable moment, moving. The city rearranges itself the way a sleeper rearranges limbs — for comfort, unconsciously, settling deeper into a rest it has been perfecting since before there were words for any of this.

It is hot. It is dark. The water stands in the streets and climbs the stairs. Somewhere ahead of you, your reflection has already turned the corner, and the doorway it passed through is the doorway behind you, and the city — patient, finished, sweating in the warm black air — folds itself one notch smaller, or one notch larger, and goes on keeping its slow, wet, immaculate time.
