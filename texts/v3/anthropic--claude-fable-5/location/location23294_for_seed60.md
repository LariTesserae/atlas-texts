# The Green Works

You come down into it, because everything here is down — a hollow the size of a large barn, maybe a small chapel, with walls of vegetation so thick they have stopped being walls and started being the world. There is no horizon. There is no sky to speak of, only a ceiling of interlaced canopy maybe forty feet up, through which daylight arrives pre-filtered, the color of weak tea, even and shadowless and warm.

The first thing you understand is that the hollow is *occupied*. Not crowded with things in a space — the plants are not in the space, they *are* the space. The floor is root-mat, sprung and damp, exhaling when you step on it. The walls are columnar trunks fused at their flanks like organ pipes, packed so close that the gaps between them have been sealed by a secondary growth of broad waxy leaves the size of doors. Lianas as thick as a forearm do not hang so much as *span*, horizontal, taut, structural — they cross the hollow at every height like the catwalks of a mill. Some are smooth with age and use. The place is old. Nothing here is colonizing; everything has long since settled into its station and stayed.

The second thing you understand is the sound, and the sound is the secret of the place.

It is loud — genuinely loud, a sound you raise your voice over — and it is mechanical in rhythm if not in origin. The dominant note is a deep, periodic *thump-and-sigh*, perhaps every four seconds, that you feel through the root-floor in your knees. It comes from the largest trunks, the ones forming the eastern wall: hollow boles, swollen at intervals like the chambers of a heart, through which the grove pumps water up from the saturated ground. Each thump is a valve of living tissue slamming shut somewhere inside the wood; each sigh is the column of sap rising. Stand with your palm on the bark and you can feel the pulse travel past your hand, floor to canopy, a freight of water on a fixed schedule.

Layered over the pumping: a percussive clatter from above, irregular but constant, like a loom. Seed-pods — woody, fist-sized, hinged — mature in racks along the high lianas, and when they dry to a certain point they snap open with a *crack* and a flat ratcheting sound, hurling seed against the leaf-walls, where most of it sticks in the wet and is reabsorbed. The grove fires its production back into itself. Very little leaves. Underneath everything is a hiss: condensation. The air is warm and so wet it is nearly weather, and every cool surface — and there are a few — weeps continuously, the water gathering into channels in the root-floor, narrow dark veins of it running always toward the low center of the hollow, where there is a pool the size of a kitchen table, black, faintly steaming, ringed with a pale mineral crust.

Because here is the strangeness, settling on you third: people built something here once, an industrial something, and the grove did not destroy it. The grove *adopted its method*.

The artifacts are scattered, partial, half-digested. A brick chimney stands at the western edge, twenty feet of it, sound and plumb, its mortar long replaced by a cement of root-tissue; the grove uses it. Warm moist air rises through it day and night — you can hear the draw, a low flute-note under the pumping — and the chimney's mouth above the canopy is the hollow's only deliberate opening, its regulated breath. A cast-iron boiler lies on its side near the pool, shell rusted to lace, and through the lace grows a single enormous root that has taken on, unmistakably, the boiler's shape: riveted-looking ridges in the bark, a cylindrical swelling, even a bole-knot where the pressure gauge sat. Whether the root grew through the iron and was molded by it, or whether it is — the word arrives uninvited — *copying* it, you cannot tell, and the distinction stops feeling important the longer you stand there. A run of pipe, six inches of it visible, emerges from one trunk and disappears into another, and you cannot find either end, and when you put your ear to it you hear sap moving at pressure.

Half-buried in the root-mat near the south wall is a brass plate, polished — not by anyone; by the constant traffic of water — bearing a maker's name and a date you can mostly read. The date is old enough to mean that everything here has had a long time to come to terms with everything else. It has. The place is finished. Balanced. The works have been running so long that running is simply what the hollow is.

Production: the grove makes something. Along the lower walls, at chest height, rows of growths protrude from the fused trunks — regular, evenly spaced, like taps or teats or tapped-in spigots, each one slowly extruding a translucent amber resin. The resin descends at the speed of glacier candy, accumulating in stalactites, and beneath each one the root-floor has formed a shallow cupped depression, a catch-basin, and in the basins the resin pools and clouds and sets. Some basins hold a season's worth; some hold what looks like a century's, hard as glass, with leaves and pod-hinges and the occasional small bone suspended inside like inventory. Nothing collects it. The grove manufactures, warehouses, and forgets, and manufactures more. Whatever the original works produced, the hollow has decided that production itself is the point, and has continued the practice with vegetable patience.

Animal life is nearly absent, and its absence is part of the order of the place. Once, you may see a single moth the size of a hand, pale green, resting on the chimney brick where the warm air rises — the only animal warmth in the room, motionless so long it might be ornament. In the black pool, something turns over, once, without surfacing. Otherwise the fauna of the hollow is the hollow: the heartbeat in the boles, the loom-clatter overhead, the flute of the chimney. It does not need animals. It barely tolerates them. You get the sense — never threatening, simply procedural — that you have been logged, the way a mill notes a visitor on the floor: not interfered with, not welcomed, accounted for.

Change here happens at the pace of the resin. The pulse never quickens. The pool neither rises nor falls. Across years, a basin overflows and a new one cups itself open downstream; a pod-rack exhausts itself and the lianas, over a decade, shift their crossing by a foot. The brick chimney is, by a fraction of a degree a generation, being lifted — straightened, actually, corrected — by the roots beneath it, as if the grove maintains its one piece of inherited equipment to spec.

Leaving is a matter of climbing the slope you came down, pushing through the door-sized leaves, which part and reseal behind you with a wet, deliberate sound. From outside, from even thirty feet away, the hollow is invisible — just dense low forest, low wet country, unremarkable. But you can still hear it if you stand still: *thump and sigh, thump and sigh*, the patient industry under the leaves, the works that no one runs and that have never once stopped.
