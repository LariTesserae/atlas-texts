# The Firing Floor

Begin with the heat, because the heat is the first thing the place insists on. It comes up from below — not the sun, which is somewhere behind a sky the color of unfired slip, a low grey-ochre overcast that never resolves into cloud shapes — but from underneath, through the floor, as if the whole continent were resting on a banked kiln. And it is. That is the simplest true thing you can say about this place: everything here is clay, and the clay is still firing.

The ground is brick. Not bricks scattered on ground — brick *as* ground, brick all the way down, as far as anyone has dug, and people have not dug far because the deeper courses are still hot enough to glow. The surface courses are cooler, walkable in thick soles, laid in a bond pattern that changes as you cross it: running bond for a few hundred meters, then herringbone, then a basketweave that drifts gradually out of true, the joints widening, until the pattern gives up and becomes a kind of brick scree, half-formed units pressed together while soft. There is no soil. There is no stone that was not first shaped. Every horizontal surface carries tool marks — the long parallel drags of a wooden screed, the cupped depressions of thumbs smoothing mortar — at every scale. You will find a thumbprint the size of your own thumb. You will also find one, walking its full length over the better part of a morning, the size of a valley.

No one has ever seen the hands.

## Water

Rain fell recently, or is always about to have fallen. The distinction does not hold up well here. The bricks are dark with wet in patches that move — not drying, exactly, but *migrating*, a stain of moisture that slides across a plaza over the course of an hour like the shadow of a cloud that isn't there. Standing water collects in the low joints and the slumped places, warm as bathwater, faintly milky with dissolved clay. Where the floor is hottest, the puddles do not evaporate so much as stand at a perpetual near-simmer, hazing the air above them, each one wearing a small private column of steam.

The mortar weeps. This is the dominant water of the place: not rain, not river, but seepage from the joints themselves, as if the binding paste between every brick were still curing and would always be still curing. Run a finger along a fresh-looking wall and it comes away slick and grey. The weep gathers into threads, the threads into trickles, the trickles into shallow channels that the floor — accommodatingly, suspiciously — already has built into it, gutters and runnels laid in courses that pre-date the water they carry, or that the water carries because the gutters demanded it. The channels all run somewhere lower. Lower is a direction here the way north is a direction elsewhere: reliable, omnipresent, and never quite where you left it.

The air itself condenses. In the dim hours — and most hours are dim, the light a sourceless russet murk, brighter where the floor-glow leaks up through cracked courses, the cracks running like dull orange lightning frozen into the ground — the humidity reaches the point where the atmosphere starts giving its water back. Droplets bead on every surface that faces up, and on some that face down, and occasionally, in the strange pockets, on surfaces that face no consistent direction at all. You can stand still for ten minutes and find yourself glazed.

## What Stands

The land is mostly empty. This must be said clearly, because the structures, when they occur, are so insistent that memory crowds them together; but in truth you can walk for half a day across open firing floor and meet nothing taller than your knee.

What you meet, when you meet it, is architecture in the act of becoming architecture.

A wall, freestanding, two hundred courses high, terminating in raw toothing at both ends — the stepped, waiting profile of brickwork that expects to be continued. It will be. Come back in a day and it has gained thirty courses and a doorway, the doorway looking onto nothing, framing the same fog it stands in. Come back in three days and it is gone — not ruined, not collapsed, but *unbuilt*, the bricks reabsorbed, the floor where it stood showing only a faint scar of mortar like the ghost of a healed wound.

A flight of stairs rising from the plain, well-made, with a hand-burnished rail of fired clay polished by use no one has supplied, ascending forty steps and stopping in the air. The topmost step is always wet, as though just troweled.

Kilns — or things shaped like kilns, beehive domes the size of hills, scattered ten or fifteen kilometers apart across the flats, each one exhaling a slow rope of steam from its eye. These are the warmest places and the most active. Around their skirts the floor is soft, plastic, taking footprints, and it is here that the new units form: you can watch, if you are patient and do not mind the heat, a brick *arrive* — the clay of the ground swelling into a low blister, the blister squaring itself, edges firming, a seam appearing around its base, until it sits proud and separate and slowly dries to the dark terracotta of everything else. Sometimes a tool mark appears on it as it forms. The drag of a wire, the kiss of a wooden float. The mark appears in real time, deepening into the surface, made by nothing.

The technology, if you study the joints and the bonds and the simple corbeled arches — there is not a true arch with a keystone anywhere; everything spans by corbeling, by patience, by stacked offsets — is old. Pre-metal. Mud and fire and the straightedge and the line. Whatever builds here builds the way the first builders built. It simply does so across the whole face of a world, ceaselessly, and without ever finishing.

## The Turbulence

Do not let the quiet mislead you. The sound of the place is mostly a hush — the tick of cooling brick, the patient drip of mortar-weep into the runnels, the distant respiratory sigh of a kiln — but the place is not still. It is in a state that resembles storm, if a storm could happen at the speed of masonry.

Whole districts rise overnight: courtyards, cisterns, blind arcades, towers of stacked drum-rooms with no doors between them. They stand a while in the steam, settling, weeping, the freshest mortar still grey against the fired red. Then the unbuilding moves through like weather. You can sometimes see its front approaching — a line across the plain beyond which the structures are subsiding, courses sinking back into the floor in reverse order of their laying, last brick first, the whole edifice swallowed with a low wet sound like a sigh through teeth. The front passes. The floor smooths. Within hours, blisters rise from the smoothed places and the squaring begins again, differently.

Nothing is ever rebuilt the same. The place does not have memory; it has appetite. Or it has memory and is dissatisfied with it. The kilns are the only constants, and even they shift — slowly, a few meters a season, leaving drag-scars in the floor like the trails of immense snails.

There is weather above as well, of a kind. The slip-colored overcast thickens and thins. Sometimes it lowers until it touches the tallest unfinished towers and the two materials — cloud and clay — seem briefly confusable, the tower tops going soft, the cloud bottoms taking on courses.

## The Breakdown

Here is what cannot be smoothed over, the thing that the heat and the water and the brick all sit on top of like a lid on a pot:

The geometry does not hold.

It holds locally. Any wall you stand beside is plumb. Any course you sight along is level. The craft, joint by joint, is impeccable — better than human, in its primitive way, every brick bedded full. But the *aggregate* is wrong. Walk the four sides of a courtyard and you will count your paces: forty, forty, forty, and then the fourth side runs sixty and delivers you to a corner that was not in the courtyard you entered. Two parallel walls, sighted carefully, converge behind you when you turn around. A doorway passed through left-to-right opens onto a different room than the same doorway passed right-to-left, and the rooms know nothing of each other.

The unbuilding fronts are blamed for this — that the world rearranges itself behind your back — but it is worse than rearrangement. There are places, mercifully sparse, scattered like the structures are scattered, where the masonry simply stops agreeing with itself. A wall that occupies two positions, a hand's-breadth apart, flickering between them not visibly but *conceptually*, so that your eye reports one wall and your reaching hand finds the other. Joints that run in a bond pattern with no name, that hurts to trace, where a brick is somehow above and beside its neighbor in the same gesture. A stair, in one of the larger ruins-in-progress, that descends seven steps and arrives one step higher than it began, every individual step honest, the dishonesty distributed somewhere among them and never findable.

The water knows. This is the most useful thing a traveler learns. The mortar-weep avoids the broken places; the runnels route around them with the long-suffering practicality of plumbing. Where you see the trickles bend without reason, bend with them.

And in the worst pockets — the deep breakdown, the places even the steam will not enter, where the air goes still and dry in a world that is nowhere else either — the bricks are blank. No tool marks. No thumbprints. No evidence of making at all, in a world that is nothing but evidence of making. They look poured, or grown, or *asserted*. They are warm like everything else but the warmth has no direction. Travelers who have stood at the edge of such a pocket describe the same small horror: that the blank bricks feel more real than the marked ones. As if the thumbprints and the screed-drags everywhere else are reassurances. A story the world tells itself about having been made by someone, in a place where the someone has never once been seen, and the story wearing thin in patches, and underneath the story: this.

## Ecology of an Empty Place

Nothing lives here. No moss softens the north faces of the kilns; nothing has a north face for long enough. No insect tracks the soft clay around the brick-blisters. The warm puddles, which on any other world would be soup-thick with thriving, are sterile and faintly mineral on the tongue, tasting of iron and rain and the inside of a flowerpot.

And yet the place behaves like an ecology. The kilns exhale; the structures bloom around them like growth around warm springs, densest near, sparser far; the unbuilding fronts move through like grazing; the water cycles from joint to runnel to puddle to steam to overcast to the migrating stains of wet. There is metabolism without organism. Generations of architecture, none surviving, none mourned, the floor patient underneath. If you wanted to argue that the world itself is the single living thing — a creature whose body is brick, whose blood is grey water, whose dreaming is courtyards — the world would not contradict you. It would build something overnight, off in the dim, in the sparse middle distance where the floor-glow seams the dark like banked embers, and unbuild it before you arrived, and leave you the scar to read.

The quiet is the quiet of a workshop between strokes. Somewhere — you become sure of this, crossing the great hot flats under the slip-colored sky, droplets beading on your shoulders, the soles of your boots gone soft — somewhere a tool has just been set down, or is just about to be picked up. The whole vast floor waits inside that pause. It has been waiting since before there was anything here to wait, and the bricks keep rising, and the marks of thumbs keep appearing on them, patient, ancient, ten thousand kilometers of fresh wet evidence for hands that the world cannot stop describing and has never once produced.
