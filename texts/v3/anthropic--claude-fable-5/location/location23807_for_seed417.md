High on the shoulder of the mountain, above the last of the true forest, there is a place you would walk past a hundred times without finding. From outside it looks like nothing: a dense green swelling on the ridge, one more mound of wind-stunted vegetation among many, its surface combed flat by decades of weather. But this one is hollow.

The way in is a gap you have to crouch for — a low arch where two ancient trunks diverged centuries ago and everything since has grown around the absence. You push through a curtain of hanging moss beaded with cold water, and then you are inside, and you can stand.

The chamber is perhaps forty feet across. It is made entirely of living plant. Not walls with plants on them — the plant *is* the wall, is the ceiling, is the floor. Generations of high-altitude trees, dwarfed and braided by wind, have grown into and over one another until their crowns fused into a single continuous vault. The inner surfaces are upholstered in moss so deep your fingers disappear into it, moss over lichen over bark over older moss, layered like sediment. Roots the width of your leg cross the floor in slow arcs and vanish back into the green. There is no soil visible anywhere. There is no stone visible anywhere, though the mountain must be just underneath, holding all of this up. The room has forgotten it.

What strikes you first, after the greenness, is the light. It should be dim in here and it is not. The vault overhead has thinned in one broad region — the leaves there grown pale and sparse, a natural oculus — and the high-altitude sun comes through it almost undiminished, a wide column of brilliance standing in the middle of the empty air. The light is the cold, exact light of two miles up: it does not warm anything, but it makes every water droplet on every moss-tip burn white, thousands of them, so the walls seem faintly on fire at their edges. Where the beam touches the floor, the moss glows a green so saturated it looks lit from within.

And the air itself is nearly empty. That is the strangeness of the place — a huge held breath of open space inside a solid mass of vegetation. Cool, damp, still. Your exhalation shows faintly. Condensation gathers on the underside of the vault and lets go at long intervals: a single drop falls twenty feet through the sunbeam, flashing once, and lands in the moss without a sound. That soft interception is the room's whole acoustic principle. The moss eats every noise. Your footsteps make nothing. Your voice, if you tried it, would die within arm's reach.

Then your eyes adjust, and you see that you are not remotely alone.

The walls are moving. What you took for the texture of the moss — a rusty, scaled, shingled quality in the shadowed regions — is wings. Butterflies, tens of thousands of them, maybe hundreds of thousands, folded and clinging in overlapping masses, roosting so thickly on the vault's cool underside that in places the green is entirely hidden beneath layers of grey-brown wing-backs. They are packed along every root, crowded into every fold and pocket of the walls, hanging in clusters that sag under their own collective weight like strange fruit. The whole population breathes: a slow, uneven shiver runs through the masses continuously, wings opening a few degrees and closing, opening and closing, so the walls ripple as if the room were dreaming.

They are almost perfectly silent. Almost. If you hold still long enough, you begin to hear it under the silence — a papery susurrus, dry and faint, like someone turning pages in another room.

And then, without warning or apparent cause, the room detonates.

A cluster lets go — a hundred of them at once — and the disturbance propagates in a wave across the vault, and within two seconds the empty center of the chamber is a blizzard. They pour off the walls and fill the air, thousands upon thousands, tumbling through the column of light where their undersides catch fire in orange and amber, wheeling in currents and counter-currents that form and shear and dissolve. There is no pattern, or the pattern changes too fast to hold: for a moment they are a rising spiral, then the spiral tears itself apart into three eddies, then the eddies collapse toward the oculus and rebound off the bright ceiling and rain back down. The turbulence is total and it is very nearly soundless — the loudest storm you will ever stand inside, delivered at the volume of a whisper. You feel it more than hear it: the air itself moves against your face in small chaotic gusts, stirred by a million wingbeats.

Then, as arbitrarily as it began, it ends. They resettle — never in the same arrangement, the masses redrawing themselves on the walls in new shapes, new densities, whole regions abandoned and new regions colonized — and the room returns to its shivering near-stillness, and the single drops of condensation resume their long fall through the light. Ten minutes might pass, or two, before it happens again. It always happens again. The room spends its entire existence oscillating between these two states, silence and storm, and has apparently done so for a very long time; the moss directly beneath the densest roosts is stained a faint ochre by generations of settled wing-dust.

People knew about this place once. The evidence is slight and easy to miss. Set into the wall of the chamber near the entrance, half-swallowed by moss, there is a flat stone about the size of a book — the only stone in the room — with a shallow depression worn or carved into its face, as if it once held offerings, or water, or a lamp. Below the oculus, mounded in the moss, lie the soft remains of what might have been a wooden platform or a low bench: a few beams gone punky and green, kept in their rough rectangle only by the roots that have grown through them and now hold the shape out of something like habit. Outside, on the approach, a careful eye can find two or three stacked stones under the vegetation of the ridge — the knuckles of an old cairn line, marking a path nobody has renewed.

Whoever they were, they brought nothing else and took nothing away. There is no metal here, no glass, no mark of any tool more complicated than hands and patience. Perhaps they came for the storms. It is easy to imagine someone sitting on that platform in the cold bright column of light, waiting through the silence for the walls to erupt — coming a long way up the mountain to be inside the one room in the world where a hundred thousand living things can lose their minds all at once and make no more noise than the rain.

The butterflies do not mind you. If you stand still through an eruption, they will treat you as terrain: they land on your shoulders, your hair, the backs of your hands, weightless and cool, opening and closing their wings in that same slow breathing rhythm, waiting — like the room, like the mountain under the moss — for whatever it is that tells them all, in the same instant, to let go.
