## The Tableland of the Standing Herds

The plateau is so old it has stopped arguing with the sky. Whatever mountain it once was has been ground down to a single vast surface, higher than weather, higher than the memory of rivers — a stone plain at the top of the world, level enough that the horizon runs in a clean grey line in every direction, broken only by the low whaleback rises of summits that wore away in place rather than falling. There is no water here and there has been none for so long that even the absence has eroded. No dry channels, no salt pans, no ghost-deltas. The stone simply never learned the shape of water. It is the color of old bone and weak tea, frost-fractured into a pavement of flat hexagonal plates that fit together like a thing that was tiled rather than broken.

The air is thin and cool — not cold, never cold, never anything other than this: a steady chill like the inside of an unlit room. The light is ordinary, which is its own kind of strangeness at this altitude. It should be savage up here, ultraviolet and glaring. Instead it is the mild even light of an overcast afternoon, sourceless, casting shadows so faint and so fixed that you can use them as landmarks. The shadows do not move. You learn this slowly, then all at once.

Vegetation survives in the seams between the stone plates: cushion plants the size of a fist, grey-green, hard as coral, some of them — when you press them — warm, or humming faintly, or simply older than the rock they grow from. A scattered lichen the color of dried blood maps itself across the southern faces of certain boulders and never the northern ones, even on boulders that, if you walk around them, turn out to have two southern faces.

And then there are the animals.

They are everywhere. That is the first thing, the overwhelming thing, the fact of this place that makes every other fact a footnote. The plateau is *full*. Herds stand flank to flank across the tableland in their thousands of thousands — heavy-shouldered grazers like wild yaks but taller, with horns that spiral the wrong way; long-legged things like deer drawn from a description of deer; flocks of grey birds standing on the stone in formations of hundreds, all facing the same direction; hares packed beneath the larger animals' bellies; serpentine creatures lying in the seams of the pavement like the mortar between tiles. In places they stand so densely that to cross the land you must turn sideways and slide between warm bodies. They breathe. Their eyes are wet and attentive. Their nostrils flare slowly, like sleepers.

They do not move.

Or — they move the way a mountain moves. A head that was lowered last year is fractionally less lowered now. A bird's wing, half-extended, has been half-extended for as long as the lichen has been red. The herds are not frozen; frozen implies an interruption, a before. The herds have *settled*, the way the plateau settled, into the final stable arrangement of themselves. There is no migration left to make. No grass worth the lowering of a head. They have arrived at the end of behavior and they stand in it, warm, breathing, dense as a forest, silent as one.

The silence is nearly total, and what sound exists is small and intimate: the collective breath of a million animals, which registers not as noise but as a texture in the air, a faint tidal softness you feel on your face more than hear. Occasionally, somewhere out across the plain, a single hoof scrapes stone — one note, never followed by another — and the sound travels for miles in the thin air, and every standing creature within earshot does not turn to look.

It is in the density of the herds that the world's seams begin to show. Walk far enough into a standing flock and you will find two birds that are the same bird — not similar, the *same*, down to the chipped beak and the asymmetric eye, occupying two places a hand's-breadth apart. The herds repeat. Stand on one of the worn whaleback rises and look out, and the pattern of animals on the plain resolves, in places, into tiling: the same group of eleven grazers recurring across the land like a motif in a carpet, here, and here, and here, each instance breathing in unison with the others. Deep in the densest gatherings, animals stand partially inside one another, or inside the stone, a flank emerging from a boulder as smoothly as if the boulder had grown it, the boundary between hide and rock not violated but *unresolved*, like a question the world declined to answer. None of this disturbs the animals. They have stood inside the contradiction long enough that it has eroded too. The breakdown is not an event here. It is a feature of the geology.

People came up, once. The evidence is scattered thin across the immensity: a cairn every few miles, hand-stacked, the stones fitted dry and well; a ring of upright slabs around an old hearth that holds ash with no charcoal in it, ash like grey flour, ash that may never have been wood. There are windbreak walls built in arcs against winds that no longer blow, and against several walls, leaned with care, hand tools — bone scrapers, a hammerstone, a long staff of dark wood polished by gripping. The staffs are the most common artifact. They are everywhere among the herds, planted upright between the animals or leaning against their flanks, as if a great many herders walked out into the standing multitude one cool afternoon and simply set their staffs down. Some of the cairns, when you circle them, have a face on the far side — not carved, exactly, but worn into expression, a long patient face the same way the lichen is the same lichen, the same way the eleven grazers are the same eleven grazers.

In one place — only one that I have found, though the plateau extends past every horizon and the repeating land makes counting unreliable — there is a shelter still roofed with slabs, low enough that you must crawl in. Inside, the air is the same cool as outside, the light the same mild grey though no light should reach there, and on the floor, arranged with obvious intention, are forty small carvings of animals. Each carving matches, exactly, one of the creatures standing within sight of the door. The carvings are warm. The animals outside, when you press a palm to them, are the temperature of stone.

Nothing changes here. The plateau has no seasons, no weather, no night that anyone has stayed to verify. The faint shadows hold their angles. The breath of the herds neither quickens nor fades. A footprint you make in the thin grey dust will be there when you return — *will already be there*, sometimes, on paths you have not yet walked, your own stride recognizable, leading out into the standing animals toward some point too dense with warm bodies to see.

It is not a dead place. It may be the least dead place there is — life packed to saturation, flank against flank to the edge of the world. It is simply a *finished* place. Everything that was going to happen here has happened, and the happening proved so complete, so total, that the world keeps all of it at once: every animal, every herder's resting staff, every repetition, held in the cool even light at the top of the worn-down earth, breathing, breathing, going nowhere, missing nothing.
