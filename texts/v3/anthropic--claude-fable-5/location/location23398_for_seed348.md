**The Sump Works**

You come down into it, always — there is no approaching the Works on the level. The land falls away in long shallow terraces of grey siltstone, each one polished by ages of seep-water until it shines like old pewter, and at the bottom of the last terrace sits the town. Town is a generous word. It is perhaps nine buildings, a canal, a stack, and the space between them, and you could walk its full circuit in twenty minutes if the birds let you.

Nothing grows here. Not a lichen, not a smear of algae on the wet brick, not a weed in the seams of the cobbles — and the cobbles have seams, deep ones, worn by water and footless centuries into channels that would cradle a root gratefully if a root ever came. None ever has. The dampness of the place is mineral dampness: condensation beading on cast iron, a fine cold sweat on the limestone footings, the canal sliding through the middle of everything with water the color of unpolished tin. It rains here the way a cellar rains — not from weather but from itself. The big stack breathes out a warm white vapor day and night, the vapor climbs to the cool air at the basin's rim, gives up, and comes back down as a drizzle so fine and so local that you can stand at the edge of it with one shoulder wet and one dry. The Works waters itself. It has watered itself for so long that the drip-lines off every eave have carved grooves in the stone below, smooth as the inside of a shell.

The buildings are brick gone the color of liver, iron gone the color of brick. They were built by someone with an engineer's contempt for ornament and then weathered into accidental beauty: rounded corners, fluted downpipes, rivets worn to soft buttons. There is a pump hall with a roof of glass panes so old they have sagged into lenses. There is a winding house whose great wheel turns at a speed you can only confirm by watching it for a full minute. There is a row of low arched vaults along the canal, half-flooded, exhaling cool air. The machinery inside all of it runs. This is the first strange thing you accept about the Works: it runs, it has always run, the beam engine in the pump hall rising and sinking with the patience of a sleeper's chest, and it neither consumes anything you can find nor produces anything you can point to. No coal comes in. No goods go out. The flywheels turn because they turn. The Works is not making something. The Works is being something, and has settled into being it the way a river settles into its bed.

The space between the buildings is wide and bare and would feel abandoned — would feel like a photograph — if it were not for the animals.

The animals are the population, the weather, the traffic, the noise. They are everywhere and they are uncountable. Starlings sheath the winding-house wheel so thickly that the wheel appears feathered, lifting and resettling with each slow revolution in a black iridescent wave. Eels pack the canal — you see them when the tin-colored surface wrinkles, a braid of muscle yards deep, moving upstream through the cooling vaults where the water runs warm off the engine jackets and back down the spillway, around and around, a current of bodies inside the current of water. The brick walls wear a living render of snails, their shells the same liver color as the brick, so that the buildings seem to be very slowly boiling. Bats hang in the flues in such numbers that the flues whisper. Pale moths orbit the lamps — and the lamps burn always, a steady industrial amber that holds the basin at a permanent overcast-afternoon brightness no matter the hour — orbit them in disks so dense the light comes through stroboscopic, flickering on the wet cobbles like film.

And rats, and pipistrelles, and a kind of slate-grey heron that stands one to a rooftop, evenly spaced, like finials. And things harder to name: a smooth blind salamander the length of an arm that lives in the eave-grooves and drinks the condensation; something in the deepest vault that is only ever a sound, a vast contented bubbling.

Here is the second strange thing, the one that takes longer: nothing here is born and nothing here dies. The starling flock is the same starling flock. The eels do not arrive from any sea and depart for none. There are no nests, no eggs, no small skeletons in the corners, no smell of rot — only the smells of wet iron, warm oil, guano gone chalky and inert, and the cold-stone smell of the drizzle. The fauna of the Works is not an ecology. It is a mechanism that happens to be made of animals, each species describing its circuit — eels through the warm vaults, bats through the flues, moths around the lamps, starlings around the wheel — with the same untiring regularity as the beam engine. Feed one of the herons (people have tried; people have come down the terraces with bread and worse) and it will take the food politely and stand exactly where it stood, and the next day weigh what it weighed. The animals are not hungry. They are running.

The sound of all this is enormous and absolutely constant, which is its own kind of silence. The engine's deep four-second sigh; the wheel's groan; the canal's braided hiss; and over it all the fauna in full voice — ten thousand starlings, the flue-whisper of the bats risen to a hoarse roar at the chimney mouths, the wet percussion of eels rolling, the moth-wings around each lamp making a papery applause that never stops. It is the loudness of a waterfall: after an hour you stop hearing it, and then a single new sound — your own boot scraping a cobble — rings out like a shot, and every heron's head turns toward you at once, and then turns back.

Because that is the last thing, the thing that sends visitors back up the terraces walking a little faster than they came down. The Works tolerates you. You may walk its lanes, warm your hands on the engine housing, watch the moths flicker the lamplight, stay a night or a year. Nothing will harm you. But nothing will change for you either. The drizzle will fall in its same fine ring; the wheel will turn at its same patient rate; the starlings will lift and settle, lift and settle. You will begin to notice that you are the only thing in the basin with a heartbeat that varies — the only gradient in a place that has spent everything it had on arriving at this one wet, roaring, lamplit moment and holding it. The Works is finished. It was perhaps finished before it was built. And the animals look at you, when they look at all, with the mild incuriosity of clockwork regarding something that still, poor thing, keeps inaccurate time.
