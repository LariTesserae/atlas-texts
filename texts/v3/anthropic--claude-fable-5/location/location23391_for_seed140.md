**The Bowl at the Top of the Mountain**

You climb for a long time through ordinary daylight to get here — switchbacks of pale, exhausted stone, a mountain so old it has given up being a mountain. No peaks remain. The range has been ground down to a high, rolling tableland, its bones rounded like river cobbles the size of hills. The rock is the color of weathered bone and the grain of it has gone soft, every edge dissolved by a hundred million years of rain that fell on it patiently and asked nothing.

And then, near the summit plateau, the trail bends around a shoulder of stone and the light simply *stops*.

Not shade. Not dusk. A boundary, faintly curved, hanging in the air like the meniscus on the top of a glass of water. On your side: afternoon. On the other side: nothing. Absolute, mineral, total darkness, filling a sunken bowl in the mountaintop perhaps two hundred paces across — a natural amphitheater, an old collapsed caldera or a karst hollow worn into the summit, no one is sure, because no one has ever seen it. You can stand at the rim with the sun on the back of your neck and hold your hand across the boundary and watch your fingers cease to exist.

The hand is still warm. Warmer, in fact. The air inside the bowl is blood-temperature and dense with moisture, the breath of a greenhouse, and it spills over the rim in slow exhalations you can feel against your face — wet, vegetal, faintly sweet, like crushed stems and warm stone after rain.

Somewhere down in the bowl, half-buried, there is a made thing. This much is known from the few instruments that have been lowered inside and hauled back up. It is small — the size of a seed, or a bell, or a heart, the readings disagree — and it drinks light. Not blocks it: *drinks* it. Every photon that crosses the boundary is gathered, gently and completely, the way a drain gathers water, and what the thing does with all that light no physics yet written can say. It has been doing this, by the depth of the soil that has formed in the darkness, for a very long time. Whoever set it there left almost nothing else. A few low courses of fitted stone around the rim, worn to nubs, marking where some structure once stood watch. A threshold slab. A channel cut into the rock that still carries a trickle of springwater down into the dark, as though the bowl were a garden someone meant to keep watered. They did their work and went away, and the mountain kept aging around their one small permanent gesture.

Here is the strange part, the part that takes a moment to believe even standing at the rim:

The darkness is *full of life*. Loud with it.

The sound comes up out of the bowl in waves — a layered, continuous chorus, surprisingly loud, amplified by the curved stone walls into something with the resonance of a cathedral nave. There is a deep, slow throbbing call, frog-like but lower, that repeats on a cycle of several seconds and never hurries. Over it, a shimmer of stridulation — thousands of small clicking, rasping voices, insects or things that have become like insects. Wingbeats, leathery and unhurried, crossing the open air of the bowl. The constant punctuation of water: drips falling from a great many heights onto a great many surfaces — leaf, stone, pool — so that the whole hollow ticks and plinks like an enormous slow instrument. And beneath everything, almost below hearing, the seep and weep of the saturated rock itself, the walls of the bowl sweating warm groundwater out of every pore, beading and running, feeding the dark.

Because things grow down there. Lushly. The probe samples come back tangled in vegetation — pale is the wrong word; the growth has no color at all in the sense of *reflected* color, since nothing is ever reflected, but in daylight at the rim the recovered cuttings are ghost-white, ivory, faint translucent green at the stems. Broad soft leaves with no need to be leaves anymore, kept out of old habit. Mosses deep as mattresses. The plants do not eat light — there is none — they eat *warmth* and the mineral-rich water bleeding from the stone, and possibly, at the root, whatever the buried artifact gives back. Something must come back. An ecosystem this rich does not run on darkness alone. The leading guess is that the device returns its harvest as heat, or as some slower, stranger currency fed directly into the soil — that the bowl is, in effect, a closed garden powered by all the daylight of all the days it has ever swallowed, paid out gradually, patiently, on a schedule of centuries.

For all the noise, the interior is mostly *empty*. That's the unsettling thing the sound maps reveal: the vegetation clings to the walls and floor in a thick living rind, but the volume of the bowl — that great dark dome of warm air — is hollow, open, vast in proportion to its size. Echoes travel a long way in there. The flying things cross it singly, far apart. A call goes out and the answer comes back from clear across the darkness, with whole seconds of humid silence in between, and you understand that the loudness is not crowding — it is distance. Many voices, widely spaced, in a black room with perfect acoustics.

Nothing in the bowl has eyes anymore, presumably, or has eyes the way cave things do — vestigial, sealed, ornamental. They navigate by the chorus. The whole soundscape may *be* navigation: a permanent, communal map made of voices, every creature both singing the room and listening to it, the way fish feel a current. Stand at the rim long enough and you start to hear structure in it — the deep throbbing call always answered from the same three bearings, the click-chorus thickening near the water channels, the wingbeats following lanes.

Change comes to the bowl, but slowly, on the mountain's schedule rather than any animal's. The seep lines migrate across the walls over decades. The white forest creeps a few finger-widths a year. Twice in recorded memory the entire chorus has fallen silent for several days — no one knows why — and then resumed, identically, as if a long-held breath had been let out and taken again. The boundary of darkness itself has not moved in living memory, or in the memory of the worn stones at the rim, or, by the look of the soil cores, in the memory of the soil.

At the threshold slab, where the old builders cut their water channel, there is a smooth depression in the rock the size of two cupped hands, polished by something — water, or use. Visitors who climb this far tend to sit beside it for a while without quite deciding to. The sun on one side of you. The warm exhale of the dark on the other. The chorus rising and falling on its slow cycle, the drips counting out a time that has nothing to do with yours.

It is very hard, sitting there, not to feel that the bowl is finished — not dying, not abandoned, but *complete*. A garden that reached its final form long ago and is now simply being itself, loudly, in the dark, with the patience of something that drank the sun once and has never since needed to see it.
