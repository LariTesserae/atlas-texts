**The Pressure Garden**

You reach it by climbing, which is the first strange thing about a drowned place. Eleven hundred rungs up the outside of the stack — a ladder welded to a column of riveted steel that rises out of the cloud deck like a chimney that forgot to stop — and at the top there is a hatch, and past the hatch there is water, all the way up, all the way through, warm as a held breath.

The chamber is not large. You could cross it in thirty strokes if anything would let you swim in a straight line, which nothing will. It is perhaps the size of a small church: a single vaulted room of plate steel and brass conduit, every surface fresh, every weld still bearing its rainbow heat-bloom, every rivet head unpitted, the whole structure so new that the machine oil has not yet finished migrating out of the seams. There is no rust. There is no rust anywhere, which in a flooded iron room is its own kind of wrongness, the second strange thing, and not the last.

The water is the temperature of skin. At this altitude it should not be liquid at all — outside the hull the air is thin and cold enough to crack a tooth — but the chamber holds it warm, and holds it *up*, the surface pressed flat against the vault overhead like a ceiling agreeing with another ceiling. There is no air pocket. The room was built full. You can see the fill-valve, a great bronze wheel near the apex, safety-wired shut, its wire seal stamped with a number no one has come back to check.

And the room is *crowded*. That is the thing the eye keeps failing to finish taking in. Every cubic foot is occupied. The floor — though "floor" is a courtesy in a space where down is mostly suggestion — is a grid of cast troughs and manifolds, and out of every trough grows green. Broad-leaved water taro the size of dinner tables. Ribbon-grasses thirty feet long, combed by currents too slow to see, lying in lazy sine waves between the pipe runs. Cages of brass mesh stuffed with mosses so dense they have begun to deform the mesh, bulging outward in soft green hernias. Vines with leaves like punched gaskets — perfectly round, perfectly holed — that have wrapped the conduit so thoroughly that the room's plumbing diagram is now a botanical question. Nothing has had time to decay. The plants are as young as the welds. Everything here is in its first growth, first reach, first flush, packed in shoulder to shoulder before anything has learned to die.

The machinery runs. That is what the warmth is. Down in the keel of the chamber, behind louvered panels, something performs slow industrial work — you can put your palm against the plating and feel it: a heave, a long pause, a heave. Not a heartbeat. Slower than that. A piston the size of a column completing perhaps one cycle a minute, circulating the warm water through the troughs, through the roots, up the wall channels, across the vault, down again. Filament lamps burn behind thick glass ports in regular rows, the kind of honest yellow-white electric light you'd find in a factory at night, and the water carries it everywhere, soft-edged, so the whole chamber sits in a steady amber daylight that has no sun in it and no hours.

It is nearly silent. Sound underwater should carry, and it does — which makes the quiet deliberate. The great piston has been baffled. The pumps run on felt mounts. What you hear, hanging there among the leaves, is the sum of small things: the tick of a valve seating, the faint groan of new steel still settling into its own weight, the almost-sound of a million leaves trading gases with the water, a fizz too fine to be called a fizz. Once in a long while a single bubble — there should be no bubbles in a sealed full chamber, and yet — detaches from somewhere among the pipes and climbs, wobbling, and is absorbed into the ceiling-surface with a sound like a fingertip touching paper. The plants lean toward where it passed. They lean slowly. By the time they finish leaning, they have forgotten why.

There are no fish. No snails on the glass ports, no shrimp in the moss, no larvae, nothing with a nervous system. The garden was built complete and stocked with green and nothing else, and the absence is loud once you notice it: all this habitat, all this warm bright crowded living architecture, and not one thing in it that can flee, or want, or look back at you. The leaves move only as the pump moves them. You begin to suspect — third strange thing — that the leaves move slightly *before* the pump moves them.

Look closer at the troughs and you find the room's purpose written in its fittings, though not its reason. Each plant bed drains through a glass trap, and in each trap something is accumulating — a sediment the color of brass filings, fine as flour, that the roots are extracting from the water or the water is extracting from the roots. Gauges with new white faces and unscuffed needles measure its weight, gram by gram, across weeks. The garden is a factory. The crop is whatever that powder is. The schedule is geological by human standards: the largest collection vessel, a beautiful blown-glass demijohn caged in steel, is marked with graduations suggesting it will take some forty years to fill. Somebody built a sealed, heated, electrified, fully flooded room at the top of the sky, packed it wall to wall with vegetation, and walked away content to wait half a lifetime for a jar of dust.

The fourth strange thing you only notice on the way out, when you turn at the hatch for a last look. The water has a grain to it. Faint planes of refraction hang in the chamber like sheets of clearer glass within glass, and they are arranged — not drifting, *arranged* — in the same vaulted geometry as the room itself: a second chamber, made of water, nested inside the steel one, a fraction smaller, as though the room had been built to fit around a shape that was already here. The plants do not grow past those planes. Their newest shoots stop a finger-width short and turn aside, courteously, the way roots turn aside from stone.

The piston heaves. The lamps hold steady. A bubble that should not exist begins its long climb. Outside, eleven hundred rungs down, the clouds go about their weather, and the stack stands in them, warm at the top, full to the brim, growing something slowly, in the quiet, in the light.
