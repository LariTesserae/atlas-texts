# The Bright Quiet

The plateau is young the way a wound is young. The rock came up out of the earth within living memory — some say three hundred years, some say less — and it has not yet learned to be soft. Every ridgeline is an edge you could cut bread on. The valleys between them are not the patient U-shapes of glaciated country but raw V's, clefts, fault-gaps where the stone simply parted and has not had time to fill itself in. Frost works at it. Water works at it. Neither has gotten far. You can still see, in the high cliff faces, the planes along which the world broke when it rose.

And yet — green. Green to a degree that should not be possible on rock this new.

The vegetation does not grow on soil, because there is hardly any soil. It grows on stone directly, and on itself. The first layer is a moss so dense and so deep that walking on it is like walking on a mattress laid over knives: cushioned underfoot, but you can feel the fresh edges of the rock through it, faintly, like bones under skin. Out of the moss come the ferns, broad and pale-stemmed, packed so closely that their fronds interlace into a continuous canopy at knee height, then again at shoulder height, then again overhead — three storeys of fern, each layer feeding on the drip and decay of the one above. Where the ferns give out, on the steepest faces, there are hanging plants: long cabled growths the color of oxidized copper that root in cracks and drape twenty, thirty meters down the cliff sides, beaded all along their length with condensation.

That is where the water is. Not rivers — the land is too young, too high, too fissured for rivers; whatever falls goes straight down into the broken rock and disappears. The water here lives on surfaces. Dew that does not burn off until noon. Fog that comes up out of the clefts in the morning and lays a film on every leaf. Pockets, here and there, where a flat-bottomed fracture has held a black pool of rainwater, perfectly still, perfectly clear, fringed with moss like an eyelid. The whole plateau is damp the way the inside of a greenhouse is damp, without ever quite being wet.

It is cool. The altitude sees to that — high enough that your breath shows in the shadow of the ferns, high enough that the air has the thin, rinsed quality of mountain air. And it is *bright*. This is the thing visitors fail to prepare for. At this elevation, with this much moisture suspended in the air and beaded on every surface, the light does not arrive from one direction. It arrives from everywhere. The sky overhead is a white-blue glare; the fog in the clefts glows like lit milk; every fern frond carries a line of droplets and every droplet is a small sun. People who work here wear smoked glass over their eyes even under the canopy. There is no real shade — only places where the brightness is green instead of white.

---

The machines were here before most of the green was.

They came up in the first decades after the rock did, when the plateau was bare and sharp and surveyors decided that air this wet and this cool, falling through clefts this deep, was worth something. So the installations are scattered across the whole region the way farmsteads are scattered across a valley: never close together, never out of sight of each other. A condensation tower on that ridge — a tapering lattice of dull alloy, fifty meters tall, its fins angled to comb water out of the rising fog. A turbine house wedged into that cleft, where the cold air pours downward day and night like an invisible river. A long low building on the far shelf, windowless, its roof a field of black collector panels filmed with dew, with a single pipe — bright metal, beautifully maintained — running away from it along the contour of the land for kilometers, stitching one installation to the next.

All of it works. That is the first strange thing. Nothing here is ruined. The towers comb the fog, the turbines turn in the falling air, the pipes carry water and current across the plateau, and the dynamo houses glow at dusk with a low orange light through their vents. This is industry at full operation, regional in extent, electrified end to end.

The second strange thing is that none of it makes a sound.

Stand directly beside the turbine house with your palm flat against its wall and you will feel it: a deep, even rotation, a vibration so slow you could count it like a pulse. Step back one pace and there is nothing. The moss takes it. The moss takes everything. Sound does not travel here — whether because of the moss, which absorbs it; or the fog, which scatters it; or the rock itself, which is too young and too fractured to carry a vibration any distance — no one has settled the question, and the engineers who maintain the installations have stopped asking it. They communicate by light, signal lamps and mirror flashes from ridge to ridge, because a shout dies within arm's reach of the mouth that made it. A dropped wrench lands on the moss without comment. The water in the pipes, the current in the lines, the flywheels in the dynamo houses turning their enormous slow revolutions — all of it proceeds in a silence so complete that workers new to the plateau report a kind of vertigo, the sense that they have gone deaf, or that the machines are only pretending to run.

They are not pretending. They are simply *slow*. Everything here is geared down. The fog yields its water a film at a time; the cold air falls at the pace of cold air; and the machinery, built to harvest such patient sources, matches them. The great flywheel in the central dynamo house turns once in roughly an hour. Watching it move is like watching the minute hand of a clock — you cannot catch it in the act, but look away and back, and it has gone on. The whole plateau runs on this clock. Output is steady, modest, and has not varied meaningfully in eighty years.

---

The ferns are taking the machines, and the machines do not appear to mind. Moss climbs the lattice towers to head height and is scraped back twice a year, without urgency. One of the older condensation towers, decommissioned, has been left to the hanging plants entirely, and now stands as a green obelisk on its ridge, dripping, indistinguishable at a distance from a natural spire except that it is too regular, its taper too true.

Animals are nearly absent. The plateau is too new; the soil-life that feeds a food web has barely begun. There is a pale beetle that lives in the third storey of the ferns and is seen perhaps once a week. There is one bird — workers insist it is one bird, always the same individual, a grey thing with long wings — that crosses the plateau at great height on still afternoons, silently, as everything here is silent, and does not land.

In the evening the glare softens by degrees, the fog rises out of the clefts and turns gold, then grey, and across the whole region the dynamo houses come visible one by one as points of orange light, each on its own ridge or shelf, far apart, like a constellation laid down on the green. Nothing moves quickly. Nothing makes a sound. The rock goes on being young, the moss goes on being patient, the flywheels go on turning at the pace of the hour hand — and the water, a droplet at a time, is gathered out of the bright air and sent down the shining pipes to wherever, far below the plateau, the noise of the world resumes.
