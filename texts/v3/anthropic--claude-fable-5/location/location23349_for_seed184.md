**The Works at Lowmost**

You arrive from above, because there is no other way to arrive. The light goes from green to olive to a kind of brown-gold dusk, and then the chimneys come up to meet you — eleven of them, brick, barnacled, still standing in a cluster so tight you could step from one rim to the next. They do not smoke. They breathe. Once every few minutes a single fat bubble of warm gas detaches from somewhere far down inside and wobbles up past your face like a slow silver animal, and that is the closest thing this place has to weather.

Below the chimneys, the town. It is small — you could swim its length in two minutes if anything here encouraged hurrying — but it is *full*. The Works was packed even before the water came: a mill town built in a hollow, every yard roofed over, every alley narrowed by pipework, lean-tos grown against boiler houses grown against the foundry like coral on coral. Now the water has filled in the last of the gaps. There is no open square, no green, no breathing room. There are only passages: between the gasometer and the rope-walk, under the conveyor gantry, through the cooling hall where the great fans hang furred in algae. You move through Lowmost the way blood moves through a body, because that is the only geometry on offer.

**Warmth**

The water is warm, and the warmth is the first strange thing, because it is not even. It comes in plumes. The foundry's deep hearths still hold heat — or hold something that behaves like heat — and the water above them shimmers visibly, a column of distortion rising through the dim like a glass pillar. Cross one of these plumes and it's like passing through a doorway into summer; the warmth presses on your chest, smelling faintly (yes, smelling — taste and smell blur down here) of iron and yeast. The vegetation knows the plumes intimately. Where the warm water rises, the growth is riotous.

**Green**

And it is riotous. The town is dressed. Long ribbon-weeds, broad as belts and the dark green of bottle glass, stream from every gutter and sill, all of them combed in the same direction by a current too slow to feel. Cushions of moss-like growth, dense and springy, upholster the steam engines in the main hall so completely that the machinery has become topiary: you can read a flywheel under the green the way you read a body under a blanket. Pale ferny things, almost luminous, crowd the window frames of the workers' terraces, growing inward toward the dark rooms as if the houses were pots. Nothing is bare. The brick has a velvet of red-brown algae; the iron has its own slower garden of mineral bloom, orange and white, where rust and lime have negotiated their long peace.

This is the thing the place wants you to understand: it is not ruined. Ruin implies an interruption. Lowmost has simply changed administrations. The plants run it now, and they run it well, on the heat the old owners left in the ground.

**The dim**

Light arrives used. It has come down through a great deal of water and through the canopy of weed on the gantries, and what reaches the streets is a low amber-grey, the light of a room with the curtains drawn at four in the afternoon. Your eyes adjust and then keep adjusting; there is always a little more to see than you thought, never quite enough. In a few places the town makes its own light, and this is the second strange thing: certain gauges still glow. Round brass-rimmed dial faces, set into walls and engine casings, hold a faint phosphor green — needle positions frozen, or not quite frozen. Watch one long enough and the needle creeps. Pressure is still being measured down here. Something is still being kept track of.

**Quiet**

Sound has the texture of felt. Water carries sound well, which makes the silence of Lowmost a deliberate-seeming thing, an achievement. What there is: a deep, irregular *tonk* from inside the foundry, like a vast pot cooling, at intervals of minutes; the whisper of weed against weed when a warm plume stirs the streets; your own breath; and, if you hold very still near the engine hall, a sound you will at first refuse to credit — the slowest possible rhythm of machinery. The great horizontal engine under its moss is turning. Not at speed. One revolution takes most of an hour. The piston eases out along its slide like a sleeper turning over, and the moss flexes with it, and the green-glowing gauges acknowledge it, and then everything rests again. No one knows — there is no one to know — what it drives, or whether driving was ever the point, or whether the engine has simply found, after all the urgency was drowned out of it, the speed it always wanted.

**Inhabitants**

Animals are scarce, and the few there are behave like staff. A single conger-bodied thing, pale as a peeled root, keeps the pipe runs along Foundry Lane; you see its tail withdrawing more often than you see its head. Small translucent shrimp tend the gauge glass, grazing it clean — which may be why the dials are legible at all. Once in a long while a school of slim grey fish passes overhead between the chimneys, high up where the light is better, and does not come down. They cross the town the way birds cross a graveyard.

Of people there is the usual furniture of absence: a kettle on a hob, furred green; rows of clogs in a terrace hallway, each grown its own soft anemone of weed; a time-clock by the works gate with its rack of cards still in place, the ink long since dissolved into the general water, so that every shift recorded here has been returned, letter by letter, to the sea.

**Rules**

The place has rules, learned slowly. Stay out of the warm plumes when the engine completes a stroke; the water there twitches, briefly, with something that is not quite current. Do not clean a gauge yourself; the shrimp will do it, and they keep their own ledger. Take nothing that is still being measured.

And do not hurry. Lowmost extends a deep, settled, brick-and-iron patience to its visitors, the patience of a town that did one thing for a hundred years and is now doing a slower thing, possibly forever. The chimneys breathe their single bubbles. The needle creeps. The green light holds. Somewhere under the moss, the flywheel commits to another hour-long turn — and the whole drowned, crowded, warm little town leans into it together, like a workforce that never received the order to stop, and decided, in the absence of one, to continue gently.
