# The Quiet Tablelands

You come up onto it through a notch in the rimrock, and the first thing the land does is take away your sound.

It is a high country — a plateau broken into broad terraces, each one stepping down toward a haze-filled distance that might be lower desert or might be cloud. The air is thin and warm at once, which the body finds confusing: sun-warmth on the skin, but every breath has that high, mineral cleanness, slightly insufficient, so that you breathe a little deeper than you mean to. The light is enormous. Not harsh, exactly — the altitude does something to it, scatters it less, so that shadows have hard edges and the colors of the rock are saturated past what seems reasonable. Rust, ochre, a pale rose-gray where the older stone shows through. Everything looks freshly lit, like the first hour after rain, though the ground underfoot is dry.

Mostly dry. That's the second thing you learn about this place: the water hides.

## Water

There are no rivers up here, no standing lakes. But the plateau sweats. In the lee of certain boulders, in the deep folds of the terraces, you find dark stains on the stone where moisture has wept out and dried and wept again for so long that the rock has grown a skin of black varnish. Some hollows hold actual water — small still pools, never larger than a sleeping animal, rimmed with a fine green margin of moss so dense and so confined it looks painted on. The pools do not seem to evaporate at the rate they should. Stand near one in the late afternoon and you can feel a faint coolness coming off it, a column of chilled air rising in defiance of the warm wind, and if you look closely the surface of the water carries a slow internal circulation, turning over like something breathing in its sleep.

The locals of the air know where every pool is. You can map the water by mapping the flight paths.

Rain comes maybe a handful of times a season — brief, vertical, oddly polite. A gray curtain walks across one terrace while the next terrace over stays in full sun. The rain rarely makes it to the ground in volume; much of it seems to be *taken* on the way down, and the air after a shower has a metallic sweetness, faintly like a struck bell smells, if a bell could smell.

## The Standing Things

Scattered across the tablelands — one every few kilometers, never closer, never in any pattern you can hold in your head — are the structures.

Most are small. A column the height of a door, made of something that resembles ceramic until you touch it and find it has the warmth and faint give of skin. A low ring of fused material, half-buried, weathered down until it could be mistaken for an outcrop except that the wind has eroded *around* it and never *into* it, leaving each one standing on a slight pedestal of protected stone, the way a coin protects the sand beneath it on a beach. Whatever they are made of does not abrade. The plateau has been wearing down around them for a very long time — you can read tens of meters of vanished ground in those pedestals — and the things themselves are unmarked, though they don't look new. They look *settled*. They have the posture of things that have finished happening.

A few are larger. On the third terrace down there is a shape like a folded wing, forty meters tip to tip, half-sunk in talus, and the talus has stabilized against it so completely that junipers — the only real trees up here, scattered, wind-bent, ancient — grow from the seam where artifact meets scree. The wing casts a shadow that is measurably cooler than other shadows. Animals shelter there at midday in numbers, and they have worn a smooth amphitheater of trails into the slope around it, generations deep.

The structures do things, but quietly, and at the edges of perception. Compass needles drift near them and recover a few paces away. The largest pools of water are always within sight of one. At night — though you will mostly know this place by day, because the days here feel endless — some of them carry a dim grain of light along their surfaces, like dust illuminated inside the material, slowly falling sideways.

Nobody built them recently. Nothing maintains them visibly. They have simply become part of the erosional bargain of the place: the rock wears, the water hides, the standing things stand.

## Vegetation

Sparse, but not impoverished — *edited*, you might say. The plant life of the plateau seems to have agreed on spacing. Bunchgrasses in pale gold clumps, each clump alone in its circle of bare ground. The junipers, one to a ridge, sometimes one to a whole terrace, each so old and wind-carved it has more dead wood than living, the live foliage held out in a single green banner on the lee side. Around the seep-pools, that intense miniature lushness: moss, a fine-leaved creeping plant with flowers the size of pinheads, the whole garden small enough to cover with your two hands.

And here and there, a strangeness: a plant that grows only on the pedestals of the standing things, a low silver rosette with leaves that track not the sun but — as far as anyone has determined — nothing visible at all. They all face the same direction at any given hour, all of them, across the whole plateau, and the direction slowly changes through the day, and it is not the direction of the sun.

## Fauna

This is the surprise of the place. The land looks empty and is not.

The tablelands are *full* of animals, and almost all of them are quiet. Hares the color of the rose-gray stone, abundant enough that any patch of ground, watched for a few minutes, resolves two or three of them sitting motionless. Lizards sunning on the varnished seep-stains, sometimes a dozen to a boulder. Flocks of small dove-like birds that travel low between the pools — and their wings make no whistle, no clap; they have somehow, over whatever stretch of time this place has had to work on them, evolved or been granted the silent flight of owls. Larger things, too: a slow, heavy-bodied grazing animal something like a marmot scaled up to the size of a sheep, moving in loose herds along the terrace edges, cropping the bunchgrass with a sound you can only hear from a few feet away. And the predator of the country, glimpsed mostly as a long tan shape pouring between rocks — built like a cat, marked like the stone, utterly soundless.

The whole ecology runs at full speed in near silence. Chases happen — you will see one, a hare flashing across open ground, the tan shape behind it, dust kicked into the bright air — and they happen like films with the sound removed. Whether the quiet is the altitude, the thin air, the animals themselves, or something the standing things do to the medium of sound, no one has settled. Probably it is all of these. The effect is that the plateau's abundance never announces itself. You have to *look*, and when you look, the place is teeming.

What sound there is, you come to treasure. The dry tick of grass in wind. Water, somewhere under a rock, a drip every several seconds, patient as a clock. Once or twice a day, from no clear direction, a single low tone — felt in the sternum more than heard — that the herds answer by lifting their heads all at once, holding still, and then going back to grazing. It is the loudest thing the plateau ever says, and it is barely a sound at all.

## Weather, Time, Motion

The plateau is not still; it only moves quietly. Cloud shadows the size of villages slide across the terraces. The polite rains walk their curtains around. Dust devils form in the warm afternoons — but here they last too long and travel too straight, and more than one observer has noted that they detour around the standing things by a margin wider than seems necessary. The hares ignore them. The hares ignore most things. The hares have been here a long time.

In the evenings the warmth holds — the rock gives back the day's heat well into the dark — and the seep-pools exhale their cool columns into the dimming air, and you can see them then: faint pillars of mist, one to a pool, standing across the terraces like a second, softer set of monuments, dispersing by full dark.

## The Sense of It

What the place finally communicates, if you stay long enough, is *resolution*. Something happened here once — the standing things are the residue of an intelligence working at a level the rock and the rain don't argue with — and whatever it was, it is over, and the land has spent an enormity of time folding the remainder into itself. The artifacts shelter the animals. The animals wear trails to the water the artifacts keep cold. The silver plants face their invisible direction. The wind erodes everything it is permitted to erode.

It is a warm, bright, high, quiet country at peace with its own strangeness, and it does not need you to understand it. It only asks, by its silence, that you lower your voice.
