**The Plateau of Standing Engines**

You arrive at altitude, though there is no way to see it. The darkness here is not the darkness of night — night implies a day that left. This is older. The air is thin, dry, and faintly warm, the way the inside of a long-unplugged machine stays warm if it ran for ten thousand years first. Warmth without source. Warmth as a *property*, the way stone has hardness.

The ground tells you where you are before your eyes adjust to the fact that they never will. It is mineral, planed nearly flat — not by water, which has not existed here in any era worth naming, but by time alone, by the slow settling of a highland that has finished moving. Once there were ranges here. You can read them in the floor: long, low swellings, the roots of mountains worn down to their gums, polished by aeons of thin wind carrying nothing but its own dust back and forth, back and forth, until even the dust gave up and lay down. Underfoot the surface is smooth basalt-dark stone, slightly soapy to the touch, warm as a hand. If you kneel and press your palm flat you can feel, very far down, a hum. Not vibration exactly. More like the *memory* of vibration, held in the rock the way amber holds an insect.

The city is spread across this near-plain the way constellations are spread across a sky: structures with great distances between them, but unmistakably belonging to one another. You walk for twenty minutes between presences. Then one resolves out of the dark — first as a deeper blackness, an absence of the faint mineral sheen of the ground, and then as form.

They are engines. Or they were, or they are something for which "engine" is the closest surviving word. Each one is the size of a cathedral, and each one is different: a fluted cylinder canted at eleven degrees, its surface ribbed with cooling vanes long since fused smooth at the edges; a low broad dome pierced by chimneys that exhale nothing; a lattice tower whose interior is dense with flywheels the diameter of plazas, all stopped, or all turning so slowly that stopping and turning cannot be told apart. Between them run the casings of conduits, half-sunk in the ground like the spines of buried animals, connecting engine to engine across kilometers of emptiness. The whole plateau is one machine with a great deal of space inside it. The space is part of the design. You become sure of this the longer you walk: the emptiness between structures is not abandonment. It is clearance. It is the gap a mechanism leaves for a motion that happens once an age.

There is light, technically. Call it a tenth of light. Some of the engines carry, low on their flanks, small apertures behind thick glass — not windows, gauges — and behind the glass, a color glows that your eye keeps trying to name and failing. It is near to the orange of a banked coal, but drier, and it does not flicker. It has not flickered in so long that the glass around each gauge has developed a faint shadow-stain in the shape of its own steadiness, the way a picture left on a wall for a century prints itself onto the plaster. By gauge-light you can see perhaps three meters. You navigate the rest by warmth: the conduits underfoot are a half-degree warmer than the bare stone, and your feet learn to follow them like roads. They *are* the roads. There were never any others.

The quiet is layered, which is how you know it is not silence. At the bottom, the deep hum in the rock, felt rather than heard. Above that, occasionally, a single sound: a tick, vast and unhurried, from inside one of the engines — one tick, then nothing, sometimes for hours. The ticks do not seem to advance anything. The city is at equilibrium; every pressure has long since found its level, every gradient has flattened, every flow has slowed to the speed of geology. The ticks are not the sound of work being done. They are the sound of work being *held*. A clock that has reached its final time and now merely confirms it.

There is no life of the green kind, and there never was — no fossil seed in any stratum, no rumor of chlorophyll. Whatever built this place either was not biological or did not bring its biology with it. But there is fauna, of a rare and questionable sort. Perhaps once in a long traverse you will see one: a heat-moth, the walkers call them, though nothing walks here to call them anything, so say instead that the name is simply *true*, arrived-at independently by every mind that meets them. They are pale, soft-bodied things the size of an open hand, and they cling to the warm conduits, flattened against the metal, drinking the half-degree of warmth through their undersides. They do not fly. The wings are vestigial, or ornamental, or antennae — broad, veined panes that may be how they listen to the hum. They move perhaps a body-length per day. When you pass one it does not react, but hours later, far behind you, it will have turned — slowly, certainly — to face the direction you went. No one knows what they eat. The honest suspicion is that they eat the equilibrium itself: that they live on the absence of change, grazing on stability, and that a single sudden event — a shout, a falling stone, a new dawn — would starve them instantly.

The strangeness of the place is not in any one thing but in its grammar. Distances behave conservatively but not quite lawfully: the walk between two engines is always the same number of paces, but never the number you counted last time, as if the plateau keeps its measurements precise and private. Your shadow, when a gauge is bright enough to cast one, falls correctly — but it falls in the same direction at every gauge, regardless of where the gauge is, as if all the light here is secretly one light, parceled out. And in the largest structure — the dome at what may be the city's center, or may be its edge, the plateau declining to clarify — there is a hall of dials. Hundreds of them, each behind its steady glass, each needle at rest. Every needle points to the same mark. The mark is not zero. It is some other value, settled on long ago, and the entire city, the engines and conduits and clearances and the warm dark air, exists to keep the needles exactly there. Not to reach the value. To *remain* at it.

That is the rule of this world, as near as it can be read: this is not a ruin and not a working city but a third thing, a finished one. It achieved its purpose at some point deep in the unrecoverable past, and its purpose turned out to be a state rather than a product — a precise warmth, a precise darkness, a precise stillness on a high worn plain — and so achievement did not end its operation but perfected it. The engines do not run because something needs power. They run because the running is the thing made.

You can stay as long as you like. The place neither welcomes nor objects; you are within its tolerances. Walk the warm roads in the dark. Count the ticks. Pass the heat-moths and feel them, hours later, turn. Nothing will happen, and you will come to understand — somewhere out on the long stone between one black engine and the next — that *nothing will happen* is not a lack here. It is the monument. It is what the mountains were ground down for.
