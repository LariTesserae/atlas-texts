## The Uplands of Ler

There is no shore here, and no memory of one. The ocean did not rise to cover these mountains — the mountains rose, eon over eon, carrying the ocean with them, until the whole drowned range stood higher than the clouds of the world below. Now warm water fills the sky for a thousand miles in every direction, and what you walk through, swim through, drift through, is a sea wearing the body of a highland.

The peaks themselves gave up being peaks long ago. Whatever sharpness they had has been licked away by water that has been moving over them since before anything had eyes to watch it. They are domes now, broad and patient, their granite worn to a finish like the inside of a shell — pale grey, faintly pearled, smooth enough that touching it feels less like touching stone than touching the idea of stone after the details have been forgotten. Between the domes lie the old valleys, soft-floored basins of silt so fine and so deep that nothing thrown into them ever makes a sound when it lands. The whole topography is in its final shape. Erosion has nowhere left to go. The land has settled into the form it will keep until the end of things, and you can feel that settledness the way you feel the quiet of a room where an argument ended centuries ago.

The water is warm — not tropical-bright but blood-warm, womb-warm, the temperature of something alive that you are inside of. It carries heat up from somewhere far below in slow vertical rivers you can find with your skin before you see them: columns of warmer water rising through cooler, shimmering faintly, bending the dim light into long trembling threads.

And the light is dim everywhere, an even amber-green dusk with no clear source. It does not come from above. It seems to come from the middle distance in every direction, as though the water itself were faintly, tiredly luminous — a sea remembering daylight rather than receiving it.

The vegetation is the loudest thing here, and it makes no sound at all. The summits and saddles are forested — true forests, dense and layered — in growths that are not quite kelp and not quite trees. Their holdfasts grip the polished granite like knuckles. Their trunks, rubbery and dark bronze, rise thirty, fifty, eighty meters, and their canopies spread into broad translucent fronds the color of old wine bottles, layered so thickly that beneath them the dim light dims further into a brown-gold gloaming. Epiphytes crowd every trunk: cushions of red weed, soft pale fans, ribbon-growths that hang and sway in the slow internal weather of the sea. The forest is crowded — presence pressed against presence, frond against frond, almost nothing untouched by something else growing. It breathes. Whole hillsides of canopy lean together in unison when a current passes, then lean back, in a motion so slow and so vast it reads less like wind in trees than like a tide moving through a single enormous body.

Because this place has weather. It is large enough for that. Currents the size of nations migrate across the range in seasons of their own: months of stillness, then a long warm flow from the sunken east that combs every forest flat in the same direction and carries clouds — actual clouds, suspensions of silt and spore and milky plankton-haze — that pass over the domes and cast moving shadows through the already-dim water. Sometimes it rains upward: the warm columns from below grow strong enough to lift the silt off the valley floors in slow grey curtains that climb past you toward whatever is above, and the forests release their spores into these updrafts in pale glittering snows that fall the wrong way.

Listen, though, and there is almost nothing. The sea swallows sound the way the silt swallows stones. What remains is intimate and small: the faint creak of a trunk flexing near your ear, a tick of grit against rock, your own pulse, and beneath all of it — at the threshold of hearing, or perhaps below it, perhaps felt in the sternum rather than heard — a single sustained tone, very low, that may be a current passing through a gap in the range somewhere over the horizon, and may be something else. It never stops. After a while you stop noticing it. After a longer while you notice that it is the thing holding the silence up.

Animals are rare, and the rarity is felt. The forests are lush enough to feed nations of creatures, and almost nothing eats them. You may travel for days through the dense canopies and meet only a single translucent eel-thing threading between fronds, or a cluster of pale, slow stars grazing the stone, moving perhaps a hand-span in an hour. Once in a great while — once in a lifetime of travel, maybe — the canopy darkens overhead and something passes: immense, unhurried, its shape never fully resolving in the dim water, its wake arriving minutes after it has gone and combing the whole forest sideways. It makes no sound. The forests lean after it like grass after a departed wind, and then everything is still again, and you are left wondering whether the place has fauna at all or only one inhabitant, very large, very old, making its rounds.

There were people, or something like people, at some point too distant to name. Their traces are sparse and modest: a ring of standing stones on a summit dome, worn now to soft pillars like melted candles; a single doorway — just the doorway, lintel and jambs, no walls — standing alone in a saddle between hills, packed with silt to the knee; here and there a carved channel in the granite that gathers the warm rising water and bends it, deliberately, around the base of a hill, still working, still doing whatever it was made to do. Nothing they left behind hums or glows or thinks. It is all stone, all hand-shaped, all nearly returned to landscape. The carvings on the standing stones have eroded to the point where they could be writing or could be the marks of currents, and the honest answer is that this far into the age of the place, there may no longer be a difference.

Because here is the thing about the Uplands, the thing every traveler learns slowly and none can fully say: **the water is old enough that reality has gone soft in it.**

It begins gently. You notice that distances do not stay agreed-upon — a dome that took an hour to reach yesterday takes a few minutes today, with no sense of having moved faster. You notice that "up" is a local custom rather than a law: in some valleys the silt drifts ceiling-ward and the forests grow downward from the polished stone above you, and your body accepts the inversion without complaint, and only later, thinking about it, do you feel the vertigo. The dim light, having no source, casts shadows that do not always belong to their objects; you will see the shadow of a forest on a bare hillside, swaying, with nothing above it.

It deepens. There are regions — locals, if there were locals, would mark them, and perhaps the standing stones do — where the water holds *time* badly. Swim through a warm column there and you arrive damp with a different hour: the light a shade dimmer or brighter, the spore-snow falling that fell last season, your own wake arriving ahead of you. Some valleys repeat. You cross one, and cross it again, identical to the silt-grain, and the third time there is a fourth standing stone in the ring where three stood before, and the rock is just as worn, just as ancient, as if it had always been there — and from then on, everywhere in the Uplands, there are four.

It is not violent, this breakdown. That is what makes it bearable, even tender. The world here is not shattering; it is *relaxing* — an old fabric whose weave has loosened, threads drifting apart in the warm water, distinctions dissolving the way the mountains' edges dissolved. Surface and depth, before and after, one hill and another hill, yourself and the warm water: the boundaries are eroded too. They were always the youngest things here, those boundaries, and they are wearing away last.

The deepest expression of it sits at the heart of the range, where the oldest domes circle a basin that has no bottom — not deep, but *absent*, a floor that the eye and the hand both decline to find. The forests around its rim are the lushest in all the Uplands, dense to the point of solidity, growing in slow spirals that all lean inward. The low tone in the water is strongest there. Things lost anywhere in the range are sometimes found drifting at its edge, unaged or over-aged: a frond from a forest a thousand miles east, the lintel-stone of a doorway whose jambs still stand elsewhere, once — travelers swear it — the worn likeness of a face.

Nothing here will harm you, in all probability. The water is warm, the forests are gentle, the one great inhabitant pays you no mind. The danger of the Uplands is only this: that the place is at peace in a way that is contagious. Its work is finished. Its shape is final. Its physics are unknitting in comfort, like an old creature uncurling in the sun. Stay long enough in the dim warm water, among the leaning bronze forests and the candle-soft stones, and you begin to settle into your final form too — to feel your own edges going smooth, your own up and down becoming optional, your shadow wandering a little, sweetly, off on its own.

Travelers who leave say the same thing, all of them. Not that they escaped.

That they were *put down gently*, like something the sea had been holding, and was not finished holding, and would hold again.
