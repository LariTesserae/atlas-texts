**The Undercroft Garden**

You reach it by a stair that was cut, not found — thirty-one steps of hand-laid brick descending through what was once soil but is soil no longer, every inch of it faced, mortared, made. At the bottom the stairway opens into a single vaulted room, perhaps eight paces across, and there is nowhere further to go. This is the whole of the place. It has been the whole of the place for a very long time.

The chamber is entirely built. The floor is brick set in a spiral pattern worn shallow at the center by feet that stopped coming generations ago. The walls are brick. The ceiling is a low dome of brick, corbelled by hand, each course leaning inward a finger's width more than the last, the way people built before they had arches worked out properly — the whole dome held up by patience and weight. There is no bedrock showing anywhere, no raw earth. Whoever made this sealed the ground away completely, as if the point of the room was to be inside something human and nothing else.

Light comes down through three shafts bored up through the dome to the surface, each lined in pale plaster and angled so the sun walks through them in sequence across the day. Right now two of them are lit, and the room is bright — genuinely bright, an ordinary honest daylight, pooling on the floor in slow ellipses that migrate as the hours turn. Small flakes of dust and wing-scale drift through the beams constantly. Something is always moving up there.

Because the room is *full*. That is the first thing after the light: the density of it. Whatever this chamber was built for — grain, roots, the dead, no one remembers — the dry green things have taken it entirely. Not water-fed plants; there is no water here. No seep in the walls, no condensation on the brick, nothing. The air is warm and papery-dry, the kind of dry that makes your lips aware of themselves. What grows here is the vegetation of drought perfected: lichens in overlapping shields of gray-green and mustard and rust, so old and layered they've built up a crust two knuckles deep on the north wall. Air-plants wedged into every mortar joint, silvery rosettes that drink nothing you can see. Resurrection ferns furled tight and gray along the ceiling courses, and here and there — mysteriously — unfurled and green, as if they've struck some private bargain. Strands of a dry hanging moss, pale as flax, drape from the dome in curtains you have to part with your hands. The plaster of the light-shafts is furred green a full arm's length up.

It should not be lush and it is lush. Everything grows nearly touching everything else. The brick shows only in worn patches, like skin through old cloth.

And the room is inhabited, thickly. Small brown geckos hold still on the walls in the dozens, so well-matched to the brick that the wall seems to shiver when they move. Darkling beetles trundle the floor with the unhurried confidence of things that own a place. Silverfish. Little dry-country snails sealed to the dome, their shells bleached like small moons, some of them alive, some of them not, hard to say which. A colony of harvester ants keeps a road along one wall, immaculate, generations old — the brick along their path is polished glossy by traffic. In the darkest shaft a pair of small owls nest; their pellets on the floor below are the room's richest soil, and the densest lichen grows there. Moths sleep in the moss curtains all day and rise at the hour the last shaft goes gold, and the geckos go to work, and the owls above them, and nobody hurries, and nothing here has hurried in a hundred years.

It is quiet, but not silent. Listen: the tick of beetle legs on brick. The dry papery whisper when a draft from the stairwell moves the hanging moss. Once in a while a snail-shell drops from the dome and lands with a small, precise *tok*, and the geckos flinch, and settle. That is the loudest thing that happens.

The tools that made this place still lean where they were left — a wooden maul gone silver with age, a mason's line wound on a stick, a broken clay lamp. Simple things, hand things. They have lichen on them now too. The room does not distinguish between what built it and what grows in it anymore.

The place is in balance and knows it. Warm, bright, dry, crowded, patient. A single sealed room where an ecosystem the size of a held breath goes on turning — moth to gecko to owl, lichen eating brick a grain at a time, the sun arriving through its three appointed holes and leaving again — and nothing in it needs you, and nothing in it minds you, and when you climb back up the thirty-one steps it will simply continue, quietly, at its own pace, in its own light, forever.
