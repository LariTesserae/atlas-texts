The hollow sits low, in the kind of low that water remembers — a shallow bowl of land that was a riverbed once, maybe, ten thousand years ago, before the river gave up and went elsewhere. Nothing about it is steep. Nothing about it is sharp. Every rise has been worn to a suggestion, every stone long since buried or dissolved or carried off, so that the ground itself feels less like terrain than like the final resting posture of terrain — the shape land takes when it has stopped becoming anything and is simply, permanently, what it is.

And what it is, here, is plant. Entirely.

There is no soil visible anywhere in the hollow, no rock, no bare earth. The floor is moss over older moss over the compressed brown felt of moss that died centuries ago, layered down so deep that walking on it is like walking on the body of something patient. The walls of the bowl — and it is a bowl, small, the size of a chapel, the size of a room you could call across — are made of the trunks and limbs of trees so old they have ceased to behave like individual trees. Yew, perhaps, or some ancient laurel-dark thing; their wood has gone the color of wet iron, and their limbs have grown down into the ground and rooted and come up again, so the boundary of the hollow is a single continuous lattice of living timber, a wall the grove built around itself and then forgot it was building. The canopy closes overhead but not tightly. Light comes through in broad ordinary panes — not gloom, not radiance, just daylight strained green and laid in pale rectangles across the moss.

The strange thing is the emptiness. For all that everything here is alive — floor, walls, ceiling, all of it one circulatory system of leaf and root — the interior of the hollow is almost vacant. The grove holds its mass at the edges. Inside there is mostly air: cool, damp air, air that smells of cold bark and rain that fell weeks ago and never fully left. A single low limb crosses the open space at hip height, furred entirely in moss, beaded along its underside with condensation. Drops gather there, swell, and fall — onto the moss floor, soundlessly — at intervals so regular you could almost set time by them, if time mattered here. It doesn't. The hollow looks today exactly as it looked in your grandmother's day. The same limb. The same drops. The grove finished changing long before anyone living was born.

But it is not quiet. That is the thing a visitor never expects.

The hollow is *loud* — filled, brimmed, pressurized with sound, and the sound comes from two worlds at once. The first is just beyond the living walls, invisible and ever-present: the works. Somewhere past the lattice of black limbs — a quarter mile, less — there is industry. A mill, a pumping station, a rail siding, something with engines; you cannot see one girder of it, not one light, not one stack, because the grove permits nothing through but noise. So the noise comes alone: a deep continuous mechanical roar, the throb of something rotary, the periodic shriek of metal under load, a rhythmic concussion like an enormous slow heart. It rolls through the wood-walls and fills the empty bowl and the moss soaks up the harshest edges of it, so that inside the hollow the industrial din becomes almost geological — a roar like a waterfall that isn't there, a thunder with no storm attached.

And the second world of sound rides on top of the first, because the fauna learned long ago that the roar is just weather. Wrens detonate their songs from inside the wood-wall, pitched high and hard to clear the machine-drone — generations of birds here sing louder than birds anywhere else, and they sing constantly, defiantly, redundantly. Something woodpecker-like hammers on the iron-dark limbs and the hollow's emptiness amplifies it like a drum chamber. Wood pigeons grind out their five-note phrases. In the moss, things rustle — voles, beetles the size of thumbnails, a toad that has lived under the crossing limb for what may be a very long time. The animals are everywhere in the walls and nowhere in the open center, so the hollow has the feel of an arena perpetually full of audience and permanently empty of performance.

Nothing was ever built here. No path leads in; you arrive by pushing through a soft seam in the living wall where two great limbs almost meet, and the seam closes behind you with a sound like a held breath. No one cut anything, marked anything, left anything. The grove predates the works outside by such an absurd margin that the relationship between them isn't conflict — it's indifference. The factory roars; the grove has decided, in its slow vegetable way, that the roaring is simply the new wind, and it grew its walls a little denser, and that was the entirety of its response, and that response concluded a hundred years ago.

It is cool inside, always — the cool of a cellar, of the underside of a leaf. Moisture never quite leaves and never quite gathers; the moss is damp to the press of a palm, the air fogs faintly in the morning panes of light, and twice a day or so a drop falls from the crossing limb.

If you stand in the center of the bowl — and there is room for only a few people to stand there, it is that small — you can feel the two sounds at once: the engine-roar coming up through the moss into your feet, the birdsong coming down through the green air onto your shoulders. Between them, the hollow itself: old past aging, full past hearing, empty past filling, finished.

It will sound exactly like this tomorrow.
