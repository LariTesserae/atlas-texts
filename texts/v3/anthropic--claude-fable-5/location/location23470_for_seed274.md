## The Seep

You descend into it rather than arrive — a shallow bowl in low country, no more than a hundred paces across, where the ground gives up its heat through water. It is a hollow of warm springs that has been doing exactly this, in exactly this way, for longer than anything nearby has been alive.

The light is the first thing you adjust to. The hollow sits under a permanent ceiling of its own steam, a low grey-white vault that the sun never quite penetrates. What comes through is a soft, sourceless dimness, the kind of light that casts no shadows and tells you nothing about the hour. Morning and evening look the same here. So does noon. The steam does not drift away because the air above the hollow is always cooler than the air within it; the vapor rises, chills, folds back down. The place breathes in a closed circle.

### The Stone

Nothing green grows here. The water that wells up from below is dense with dissolved limestone, and it has spent its long tenure murdering soil and building stone in its place. Every surface is travertine — bone-white, ivory, the faint orange of old teeth — laid down drop by drop in terraces, lips, curtains, and basins until the hollow is *full* of itself. There is almost no open ground. You move through it the way you'd move through a crowded room: sideways past a flowstone pillar thick as a barrel, ducking under a stone curtain hung from an overhang, stepping along the rims of pools because the rims are the only paths.

The basins descend in steps, each one brimming, each one spilling a thin warm film over its lip into the next. The water is the temperature of blood, or a little above. Where it runs shallow over the terrace edges it leaves a sheen like melted candle wax, and indeed the whole hollow has a dripped, poured, accumulated look — as if a vast candle had been left burning here through several ages and this is the stub.

The rock weeps everywhere. Not just the springs themselves, which rise in three or four places as slow domed welling — barely a disturbance, just a lens of water standing slightly proud of its pool — but the walls of the hollow too, beaded and running, condensation and seepage indistinguishable. Touch any surface and your hand comes away wet and faintly slick with mineral.

### The Sound

It is almost quiet. Not silent — silence would be a different place. There is a continuous low texture of water: the *tick* of single drops falling from the stone curtains into pools, irregular, patient. A faint hiss where one spring rises through a constriction. The papery whisper of the thin sheets sliding over terrace lips. None of it is loud enough to mask a footstep. The steam deadens everything further, eats echoes, so each small sound arrives once, close, and is gone. You become aware of your own breathing. After a while you become aware of the hollow's.

### What Was Made Here

People found this place. Not many, and not recently in the way the springs measure time, but the marks are there if you look.

Along the rim of the third basin, someone in some former generation cut a channel — a hand's width, gouged with stone tools, the marks of the picking still visible though softened now under a glaze of new travertine, the way a scar softens. The channel diverts a trickle of the warm water into a side basin that is clearly artificial: too square at the corners, too convenient in depth. A bathing pool, or a soaking pool for hides or reeds carried in from elsewhere. The mineral has been quietly reclaiming it ever since, rounding the corners back, filing the human geometry under stone.

There are other traces, scattered: a stack of flat stones on a dry shelf, deliberate, three high, meaning something or nothing. A niche in the hollow's wall, chest height, holding two objects fully sheathed in white deposit — their shapes suggest a cup and something curved, an antler perhaps, but the spring has made them its own and will not give the answer back. Near the entrance slope, half-buried, a grinding stone with a worn dish in its face, dry, abandoned above the waterline.

Whoever they were, they used hands and stone and fire, nothing more, and they did not stay. Or they stayed and the place simply absorbed the evidence at its own unhurried rate. The hollow does not distinguish between a fallen branch and a forgotten tool. Everything left here is offered the same fate: a thin coat the first year, a thick one the tenth, anonymity by the hundredth.

### What Lives Here

Almost nothing, and that almost matters.

The water is too mineral and too warm for fish. The stone supports no moss, no algae mat thick enough to see — at most a faint orange-pink blush in the hottest channels, bacterial, the simplest possible tenants, painting the throat of one spring like the inside of a shell.

But there is a salamander. Possibly several; you only ever see one at a time. Pale, smooth, the length of a finger, it lives in the cooler outermost basins where seepage from the hollow's walls dilutes the spring water. It does not flee when you find it. It hangs in the warm shallows with its tiny hands spread on the white stone, and it watches you with the unhurried attention of something that has never had a reason to hurry. And once, perhaps, in the dim upper steam, the crossing shadow of a bird that did not land. The hollow offers fauna nothing — no seeds, no insects in quantity, no cover — and so fauna mostly declines the invitation.

### The Stillness Underneath

What settles on you, after time in the hollow, is not strangeness — there is nothing strange here, every part of it is plain mineral process — but the absolute *settledness* of it. The springs rise at the same rate they rose before the channel was cut. The terraces grow at the pace of a fingernail and dissolve at almost the same pace, so the hollow's shape, century to century, barely drifts. The steam ceiling forms and folds and reforms in a loop with no beginning. Rain comes sometimes, and the hollow swallows it without changing temperature or level by more than a hand's breadth; the surplus slides out through the lowest lip into the marsh-flats beyond and is forgotten.

It is a place at equilibrium with itself — warm, wet, dim, full, and finished. Not dead. Finished is different. The drops still tick down from the curtains. The salamander still spreads its hands on the stone. The cup in the niche still thickens, imperceptibly, toward sphere.

You could leave and come back in forty years and the only thing changed would be you.
