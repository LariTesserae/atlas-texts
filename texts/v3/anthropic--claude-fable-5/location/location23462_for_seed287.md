# The Greenhouse Under the Hill

You reach it by going down — not far, maybe forty feet, through a sloped passage where the air thickens with every step until it stops feeling like air at all and becomes something you wear. The heat arrives before the light does. It is an old heat, a settled heat, the kind that has been sitting in stone so long the stone has stopped objecting.

Then the passage opens, and you are standing at the edge of a single chamber roughly the size of a small church — and like a church it has a nave and a vaulted ceiling and a hush that is not silence, because this place is never silent. It is, in fact, *loud*. The loudness is the first thing you understand and the last thing you stop noticing.

## The Sound

Somewhere under the floor — or perhaps the floor itself — there is an engine still running. No one tends it. Nothing has tended it for a very long time. It runs at the pace of something that decided, generations ago, on a rhythm and has not reconsidered: a deep cyclic *HAAH... HAAH... HAAH...*, a breathing-bellows note that you feel in the bones of your face. Every fourth or fifth cycle a relief valve somewhere in the walls lets go with a shriek of steam that scatters condensation off the ceiling in a brief, warm rain. The plants have grown around this rhythm. You can see it in them — they lean in time, their leaves angled to catch the falling drops, an entire ecology choreographed to the schedule of a machine nobody remembers building.

Between the engine-breaths there are smaller sounds, layered and close: water ticking down through foliage, the creak of swelling stems, the faint glassy chiming of mineral crusts flaking off pipes. The chamber is so densely grown that sound doesn't travel here so much as get passed along, hand to hand, leaf to leaf.

## The Light

There is no sun and there never has been. The light is dim and comes from two competing sources, and the vegetation has had to choose its allegiance.

The first is the **furnace-glow**: low along one wall, behind a grate furred with growth, something burns or smolders at a temperature it has held for so long that the grate's iron has half-melted into organic-looking drips. The light it gives is a deep ember-orange that pools near the floor.

The second is the **plants themselves** — not all of them, but the older ones. The big leaning trunks (they are not quite trees; their bark peels in plates like rusted metal, and underneath the wood has a damp, fibrous, almost cable-like grain) carry veins of a pale chartreuse luminescence, brightest where the bark has split. It is unclear, and has been unclear for a long time, whether the glow is the plants' own invention or something they drank up out of the machinery — whether they learned to photosynthesize the furnace, or whether the engine's slow electrical seep through the soil taught their sap to shine. Likely both. Likely the distinction stopped mattering centuries ago.

The combined light is just enough to see by, never enough to see *well*. Everything beyond ten feet is suggestion.

## The Growth

The vegetation is lush in the way of a closed terrarium: not endless, but *total* within its bounds. Every surface that can hold root, holds root. Vines as thick as a wrist have entered the engine housings through their inspection hatches and emerged from their exhaust ports, looking faintly different on the way out — darker, oilier-leaved, as though they passed through something and kept some of it. Broad fan-leaves the size of doors sweat continuously; the chamber's water cycle is almost entirely internal, condensation rising off hot stone, beading on the cool iron pipework near the ceiling, falling back as that intermittent valve-triggered rain. The floor is never flooded but never dry. There are pockets — a basin formed in the bowl of a collapsed pressure tank, a seam in the rock — where black, blood-warm water sits perfectly still and reflects the chartreuse veins above like a second, drowned chamber.

There are no animals. This is the strangest thing, stranger than the glow, and you feel it before you can name it: nothing scuttles, nothing hums on the wing, nothing watches. Not even insects. Whatever pollination happens here happens by drip and by spore and by the engine's breath stirring the air on schedule. The plants have evolved past the need for visitors. Some of the flowers — waxy, mouth-shaped, opening only during the steam-shriek — appear to be vestigial, blooming out of ancestral habit for animals that either never existed down here or left so long ago the soil has forgotten them.

## The Works

The built things are scattered, partial, dignified. A row of gauge faces along the eastern wall, their glass crazed, needles frozen at readings that may still be accurate. A catwalk, intact for fifteen feet and then simply *absorbed*, its grating continuing as a lattice imprinted in the trunk of the largest glowing tree, which grew up through it and took the metal into itself. Near the furnace grate, a workbench: a wrench fused to its surface by mineral deposit, a ceramic mug filled to the brim with moss.

Nothing is ruined, exactly. Ruin implies an interruption. Everything here finished what it was doing and then kept going. The engine still breathes. The plants still lean. The valve still shrieks and the warm rain still falls, and the gauges hold their readings like a sentence held mid-word — patiently, indefinitely — in a hot green room under the hill where the loudest sound is something very old, very slow, refusing in the most peaceful possible way to stop.
