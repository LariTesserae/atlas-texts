**The Ringing Room**

You reach it through a fault no wider than a coffin set on end — a fresh crack in the deep rock, its edges still sharp enough to cut, unsmoothed by anything, because nothing flows here. No water has ever touched this place. Not a drop, not a film of damp, not even the memory of humidity locked in mineral. The stone is dry the way bone is dry after a thousand years, except this stone is young: it split open recently, in geological terms maybe yesterday, and the chamber it revealed has the raw, unworn look of something just born.

The room itself is small. You could cross it in twenty paces if the floor allowed it. It is shaped roughly like the inside of a held breath — a vaulted hollow, warm as skin, warmer in the low places, sealed deep beneath the surface where no weather has ever reached and never will.

And it is *loud*.

Not loud like a storm or a machine. Loud like a single sustained chord that has been struck once and never permitted to decay. The whole chamber rings. The rock here carries a standing resonance — some conjunction of pressure, geometry, and a deep tectonic hum from far below — and the room is tuned to it perfectly, a resonating body, a bell that is always mid-toll. The sound is physical. It presses on your sternum. It lives in your back teeth. After a few minutes you stop hearing it as sound and start experiencing it as a property of the space, like its temperature, which it never changes from, because nothing here changes. The chord you hear on arriving is the chord that was ringing a century ago and the chord that will be ringing when you are dust.

This is what the vegetation drinks.

There is no water, so the plants — call them plants; they fill that office — feed on vibration. They are lush, in their way: thickets of them, glassy-stalked, rising from the fractured floor in scattered stands with wide dark intervals between, the way oases space themselves. Each one is a tuned structure. The tall ones — *resonance ferns*, if you need a name — unfurl in fractal fronds of translucent mineral fiber, each frond a different length, each length a harmonic of the room's eternal chord. They tremble constantly, visibly, sipping energy from the standing wave, and where they tremble they glow: a dim amber-rose phosphorescence, the chamber's only light. It is not much light. It is the light of embers seen through honey. The dark spaces between the stands are genuinely dark, and warm, and full of the chord.

Because the room never changes, the plants have no seasons, no growth rings, no dead matter. They are exactly as developed as the resonance permits and no more. They are young — sharp-edged, crystalline, freshly formed like the chamber itself — and they will remain exactly this young forever. A frond broken off does not decay; it simply falls silent and dark, and lies where it fell.

The fauna is everywhere once your eyes adjust, and the fauna is the strangest part.

Small things — none larger than a hand — and abundant: dozens visible at any moment, moving through the dimness. They are eaters of sound, like the plants, but mobile, and they have evolved a terrible elegance around the room's one law. *Mutes*, you might call the commonest kind: soft-bodied, felt-skinned creatures that press themselves against a glowing fern-stalk and damp its vibration, absorbing the energy directly, leaving behind a brief dark patch in the plant's glow — a bruise of silence — that refills the moment the mute moves on. There are *chord-skaters*, hard little tripod animals that walk on the floor's resonant plates, striking them in patterns, and where they strike, the standing wave bends locally and small shimmering interference-fringes appear in the air itself, visible as faint distortions in the plants' light. And up in the vault cling the *holdfasts*: limpet-like, motionless their entire lives, each one grown into the rock at a precise antinode of the chord, fat and content, never needing to move because their food is delivered eternally to the exact spot where they were born.

Predation here is a matter of detuning. A hunter does not chase; it sings a counter-frequency, very slightly off, and its prey — suddenly starved of the resonance it lives on — goes sluggish and dark and is taken. You will see this happen and hear nothing change, because the chord absorbs everything back into itself. The room's loudness is total and totalitarian. No event leaves an acoustic trace.

Someone has been here. Not recently — though "recently" is a hard word in a place without change — but the marks remain, exactly as fresh as the day they were made. Small things, scattered: a cairn of seven flat stones stacked beside the largest fern-stand, each stone chosen, you eventually realize, because it rings at a different pitch when tapped — a hand-built scale, an instrument, or an offering. A few yards off, a length of cord woven from dead fern-fiber, strung between two rock spurs, hung with five drilled stone beads that vibrate faintly in the chord, forever, a wind-chime in a place with no wind, playing the room's note back to it. There are scratch-marks on one wall, made with a harder stone: tally lines, or notation — rows of marks at varying heights, which might be a record of pitches, a primitive score, an attempt by some patient person with stone tools and no metal to *write down the chord*. And in a low alcove, a single hand-shaped depression worn — no, not worn, nothing wears here — *carved* into the rock at chest height, polished smooth by deliberate effort, as if someone stood in that spot for a long time with their palm pressed to the stone, feeling the room ring through their bones.

Whoever they were, they took nothing and changed nothing, or the room would have noticed, and the room does not notice things, because the room is finished. It reached its equilibrium the moment it formed and has been held there ever since by the chord, the warmth, the sealed dry dark. The ferns glow at their fixed dimness. The mutes graze their circuits. The holdfasts hold fast. The cairn's seven stones sit waiting at their seven pitches.

Stand in the warm dark long enough — palm in the carved depression, if you like; it fits most hands — and you begin to understand what the scratch-marks were for. The chord is not one note. Inside its loudness there are layers, harmonics, a structure of astonishing intricacy that never varies and therefore can be learned completely, the way nothing on the changing surface ever can. Someone stood here learning it. The room is a single sustained moment, fully furnished, fully inhabited, fully lit by its own dim singing, and it does not need you, and it will not remember you, and when you squeeze back out through the coffin-crack into the silent dead rock, the loudest thing in the world will go on ringing behind you, exactly the same, forever.
