**The Cistern at Hale's Reach**

You find it by the sound first — a low, complicated thrumming coming up through the limestone, like a hive built by something that never learned to be quiet. Then the lip of the sinkhole, no wider than a barn floor, and below it: water, full to the brim, cold-clear, lit from beneath.

The whole thing is submerged. Not flooded the way a basement floods — *occupied* by water, the way a lung is occupied by breath. The pool is perhaps fifteen meters across and forty deep, and every cubic meter of it is taken. There is no open water here in any meaningful sense. There is only the city, and the thin films of water that the city permits between its parts.

It is a city. That's the only word that fits, even though it would fit inside a grain silo. Towers the size of your forearm rise in dense ranks from the floor of the shaft, packed so close they share walls, share buttresses, share long arterial conduits that thread between them like the city decided streets were a waste of volume. The architecture is unmistakably *young*: every edge is knife-fresh, unfouled, the mineral surfaces still carrying the slight iridescence of material that crystallized recently and fast. Nothing has had time to soften. Corners that would draw blood. Spires terminating in points fine enough that the water itself seems to part around them with a kind of caution.

The light is the strangest comfortable thing about it. There is no glow, no bioluminescent eeriness — the city is lit like a town at four in the afternoon. Honest, neutral daylight, even at forty meters down, even at midnight, sourced from nowhere you can identify. Shadows fall correctly. They simply fall correctly from a sun that does not exist. Researchers who lowered instruments report that the photons arrive "already in transit," with no measurable point of emission, which is the kind of sentence people write when they would rather not write anything at all.

The sound is constant and layered. Through the water it carries beautifully: a deep structural drone from the foundations, where the city is still extruding itself from the bedrock; a mid-register chatter, fast and granular, like ten thousand looms; and above that, intermittent percussive cracks as new material cures and settles — the city's knuckles popping as it grows. Stand at the rim and you feel it in your sternum. Locals say you can hear it from the road on still nights, and they are right.

Vegetation is sparse and looks tolerated rather than welcomed. A few rust-green ribbons of something like eelgrass grow in the gaps the city hasn't claimed yet — the narrow water-streets, the small plazas near the surface. Patches of pale algal felt cling to the older towers near the bottom, the ones that are now almost six weeks old. The city does not remove the plants. It builds around them with a precision that reads as either respect or indifference, leaving each blade a perfectly fitted channel, a green hair threaded through stone.

There are no animals. Not one. No fish strayed in from the aquifer, no insect larvae, no snails. The water is cool, oxygenated, chemically unremarkable — it should be teeming. It is not. Whatever signal the city broadcasts in its endless thrumming, animal life declines the invitation completely. A minnow released at the surface as a test swam one slow circle and then held position at the exact center of the pool, motionless, until it was retrieved. It lived. It never schooled with other fish again.

The city is busy. That's the word the long-term observers use: *busy*. Material rises through the conduits in visible pulses — slurries of suspended mineral, fine as smoke, traveling in directed currents that ignore convection and sometimes ignore gravity. Towers gain a few centimeters a day. Districts reorganize overnight; a cluster of structures near the east wall dissolved over three days last month, and the same volume now holds something taller and more interconnected, with deep recesses sized — this is the uncomfortable part — like doorways. Doorways for nothing, in a city of no one, packed shoulder to shoulder with itself, growing upward.

The waterline used to be two meters below the rim. It is now forty centimeters below the rim. The city is not flooding the sinkhole; the city is *raising the water with it*, carrying its medium upward like a snail carries its shell, and the topmost spires are now close enough to the surface that on bright days you can see them from above without leaning in — sharp, new, gleaming, lit by that sourceless interior afternoon, humming louder every week as they near the air.

No one has decided yet what should be done when they reach it.
