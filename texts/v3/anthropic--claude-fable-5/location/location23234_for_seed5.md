# The Bright Quiet

You would walk past it. Almost everyone does. On the north slope of a low hill, where the land never quite climbs and never quite drains, there is a split boulder furred so thickly in moss that it reads as a green mound — and in the split, if you kneel, if you wait for your eyes to adjust to the strange brilliance inside, there is a city.

---

**The light comes first**, and it should not be there. The split faces away from the sun. Above the gap, a permanent shelf of old snow has compacted into something between firn and lens-ice, and it gathers the whole grey daylight of the cold country and pours it downward, whitened, doubled, scattered until it is shadowless. Inside the crevice it is brighter than the open hillside. The light has the quality of an overcast noon concentrated into a room: every surface lit from every direction, nothing hidden, nothing dim. Moisture beads on the ice-shelf's underside and hangs there. Some of the drops are old. They have been hanging so long that dust has settled on their crowns and tiny rosettes of green have begun to grow *on the water itself*, rooted in nothing, drinking the drop they stand on. The drops do not fall. The place is past the age of falling.

**The cold is total but gentle** — a degree or two above freezing, never more, never less. The ice-shelf and the stone and the deep wet ground below have argued their way to a temperature centuries ago and have not revisited the question. Breath fogs. The fog drifts down rather than up, pooling in the lower streets, and the inhabitants walk through it without remark.

---

**The city itself** occupies every surface of the crevice — floor, both walls, the undersides of overhangs — because at this scale, *down* is a suggestion. It is perhaps two meters deep and a meter wide and it is *full*. Structures stand shoulder to shoulder with no gap wider than a finger: round-bodied houses of mudded pebble, granaries shaped like wasps' nests but built in courses, like masonry, of chewed lichen-pulp pressed and dried pale grey. Terraces of split quartz grit are stacked five and six high up the rock faces, each terrace fenced with palisades of spruce needles driven butt-first into mortar. Bridges of braided root-hair cross the crevice at a dozen heights. Stairs are everywhere — stairs cut into moss stems, stairs of stacked fish-scale (the river is forty meters away; someone carried them), stairs worn into the living stone itself in grooves so old and so smooth they shine.

That is the deepest fact of the place: **everything is worn**. The doorways have thresholds dished by passage. The bridge-ropes are glassy where hands grip them. Tool marks exist nowhere, not because tools weren't used — they are simple things, fire-hardened thorn-awls, chips of flint set in pitch — but because every mark made has since been polished away by ten thousand years of small traffic. The city is not being built. It was built. Now it is only *inhabited*, the way a riverbed is inhabited by its river.

**The vegetation does not surround the city; it interpenetrates it.** Moss grows on the roofs in tended thatches, clipped flat. Liverworts pave the lower lanes deliberately, their flat green scales fitted like flagstones. Tall stems of haircap moss serve as columns, hollowed at their bases into doorways, alive and growing and yet never growing — held at their height, in their balance, by some agreement between plant and resident that no one renegotiates. Pale coral lichens fill the alleys like topiary. Ferns at the crevice mouth form the city gate, their fronds trained into an arch generations of curl ago and never uncurled since. Everything is the saturated, almost luminous green of cold-climate moss under bright light — a green so dense it seems wet even where it is dry.

---

**The inhabitants are abundant and you will not agree with yourself about what they are.** They are roughly the size of the last joint of your thumb. They are furred, or feathered, or mossed — at this light, at this scale, the distinction loses its footing. They move through the streets in unhurried numbers: carrying water in acorn-cap pails from the seep-wall at the city's deep end, where condensation gathers in carved stone gutters and runs to a cistern the size of a teacup; tending the snail-folds, where banded snails graze in pebble-walled pens and are milked, somehow, of something; sitting in doorways working flint against bone with movements too small and too practiced to make any sound at all.

Other animals share the streets without ceremony. Springtails move through the lanes like pigeons. A vole-sized something, too large for any door, lies curled at the crevice mouth in the position of a creature that has lain there every day forever, and the citizens walk over it as terrain. Tiny pale moths rest open-winged on the bright walls in such numbers that some streets appear tiled in them; they do not startle. Nothing here startles. Startling requires the expectation of change.

---

**And the silence — listen.** A city this packed should murmur. It does not. The cold dense air seems to swallow sound at the source; the moss upholsters every surface; the inhabitants speak, if they speak, in some register below hearing, or in the touching of hands, or not at all. What you can hear, kneeling at the gate with your ear in the green: the ice-shelf settling once, a sound like a held breath shifting. A single drop, somewhere deep in the seep-wall, finally falling after its long century, striking the cistern with a *tik* so small and so clear it feels confided rather than heard. Then nothing again. The silence is not empty. It is the silence of a room where everyone has already said everything, long ago, and is content.

**Nothing here is becoming anything else.** The snow above does not melt and is not added to. The moss is grown and stays grown. The drops hang. The citizens are not young and not old. If you came back in forty years you would find the same beaded ceiling, the same moth-tiled street, possibly the same moths. The city does not remember being founded and does not anticipate ending; it has reached the state that all places are secretly trying to reach, the still point at the bottom of weathering, and it sits in its cold bright pocket of the world like a sentence that was finished long ago and has been simply, perfectly true ever since.

You will rise, eventually, because your knees ache from the cold ground. The hillside will be dim and loud by comparison — wind, birds, the rude motion of clouds. Behind you, in the split in the green mound, the bright quiet goes on not happening, fully occupied, needing nothing, missing you not at all.
