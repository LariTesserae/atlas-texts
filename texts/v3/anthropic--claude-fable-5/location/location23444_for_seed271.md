There is a hollow in the land here, no wider than a churchyard, and the land has long since finished making it.

You come down into it without quite noticing the descent — a gentle bowl pressed into low country, the rim only head-high in most places, so that standing at the bottom you can see nothing of the world beyond except a pale band of sky. The light is the light of late overcast afternoon, and it stays that way. Dim, even, sourceless. Shadows here are soft suggestions rather than statements. Nothing glares; nothing is hidden.

The floor of the hollow is stone — a single broad sheet of old grey limestone, worn so smooth in places that it has the dull sheen of a riverbed long after the river has gone. But the river has not entirely gone. That is the first thing the place tells you, if you stand still: a faint mineral smell, like wet chalk, like the inside of a well. The stone is damp. Not wet — there are no pools you could cup a hand in, except one — but the rock holds moisture the way old plaster does, darkening in patches, sweating in the warm air. Run a palm along the low walls of the bowl and it comes away cool and faintly slick.

The warmth is the second thing. It is not the warmth of sun, because there is no sun to speak of; it is the warmth of stored heat, of stone that has been mild for so long it has forgotten any other temperature. The air sits at the temperature of skin. You stop noticing where your body ends and the air begins, which is perhaps why people who find such places tend to stay longer than they meant to.

The hollow has been weathered into a settled shape. Every edge has been rounded; every crack has been widened just enough and no further. Along the eastern side the limestone has dissolved into a low run of fluted ribs, like the pipes of a small organ laid on their sides, each rib polished at its crest and dark with damp in its grooves. Water made these over a very long time, working at its own pace, and then arrived at a form it was satisfied with. There is no rubble. There is no fresh break anywhere, no raw face of stone. Whatever erosion is still happening here happens at a rate indistinguishable from rest.

In the lowest part of the bowl there is the one pool. It is the size of a washbasin, set in a natural cup of stone, and it is fed — you have to crouch and watch a long while to see it — by a seep in the rock wall above, a darkened streak where water emerges one slow bead at a time, travels down a groove it has spent centuries deepening to exactly its own width, and falls. The drop, when it comes, makes a small clear note. Then the long quiet again. Then the note. The interval never changes. It is the only clock the hollow keeps, and it does not measure anything; it simply continues. The pool neither rises nor sinks. What arrives, the warm air takes back.

Apart from the drip, the quiet is nearly complete — but it is the quiet of a small enclosed room, not of open waste. Sound does not vanish here; it settles. Your footsteps return to you slightly rounded off, softened by the damp stone. Occasionally, very high and far away, there is the faint white sound of wind crossing the rim of the hollow, but the wind never comes down. The air at the bottom does not move. A held breath of a place.

The vegetation is sparse and has chosen its positions carefully, generations ago, and not moved since. A cushion of dark moss, almost black-green, in the permanent damp beside the pool — dense, ancient, springy as felt, no larger than a folded coat. Three or four ferns of a small leathery kind growing from horizontal seams in the wall, their fronds dusty grey-green, looking less like plants than like something the stone itself put out and then thought better of extending. In one corner, where a little soil has gathered in a depression, a single low woody shrub, waist-high, many-stemmed, its bark silvered and twisted. It bears a few small thick leaves. It is impossible to say whether it is fifty years old or five hundred; it has the look of a thing that arrived at its final size long ago and now simply persists. Between these few green presences there is mostly bare stone — wide intervals of nothing, which somehow do not read as emptiness but as spacing, the way silence between notes is part of the music.

There are no animals. No birds cross the band of sky. No insects stitch the air above the pool, which is strange, given the water, and yet the strangeness fades the longer you stay — the place is simply outside the routes of living things, a room nothing has reason to enter. The pool's surface is perfectly black and perfectly still between drops, and nothing has ever skated across it.

People were here once, lightly, long ago. The signs are few and easy to miss. At the southern lip of the hollow, where the natural descent is gentlest, three steps have been cut into the stone — shallow, broad, their edges worn to soft curves, indistinguishable in texture now from the rock around them, so that they seem almost a geological accident, except for their regularity. Near the pool, set into a niche in the wall at about chest height, there is a small shelf of fitted flat stones, no wider than two spread hands, holding nothing. Whatever was kept or placed there is long gone; only the gesture of the shelf remains. And on one of the smooth fluted ribs, if the dim light falls right, you can make out a few short scored lines — tally marks, perhaps, or the ghost of them, dissolved nearly back into the surface. They count something no one remembers. The stone is patiently erasing them at the same rate it erases everything, which is to say, almost not at all.

Nothing here is tool, nothing is mechanism, nothing waits to be used. The shelf holds air. The steps lead down and that is all they do.

What the hollow has, instead of event, is condition. The damp does not gather toward rain; the warmth does not build toward heat; the dim light does not lean toward morning or evening. You could return after a year, or a lifetime, and find the moss the same darkness of green, the seep producing its bead, the pool at its level, the shrub holding its few leaves at the same angles. The place has finished becoming and is now only being. Some landscapes feel like sentences in the middle of being spoken. This one is a sentence long since ended, kept legible by the stillness — small, warm, dim, faintly wet, and entirely at rest.

If it has a mood, it is not loneliness. It is more like the mood of a room in an old house that nobody uses but somebody, once, loved enough to cut steps into — a room the house keeps anyway, swept by no one, lit by no lamp, holding its single slow drop of water like a thought it has no need to finish.
