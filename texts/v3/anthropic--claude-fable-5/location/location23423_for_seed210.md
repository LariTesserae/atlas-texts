## The Cirque That Runs

You reach it by climbing through cloud — a long, cold ascent over rock so new it still has edges, scree that hasn't learned to lie down yet, ridgelines like fresh fractures in glass. The mountain feels recently arrived. Lichen hasn't fully claimed it. And then the trail (not a trail; nothing made it; it is simply the only way the stone permits) crests a lip of basalt and you are looking down into the cirque, and the first thing you understand is that you are hearing machinery, and the second thing you understand is that there is no machinery here. There is nothing built here at all.

The cirque is small — you could walk its floor in twenty minutes, if walking here meant what it means elsewhere. A bowl of dark young stone, perhaps half a kilometer across, walls rising sheer on three sides, the fourth side open to a drop into permanent mist. The light is dim and even, a deep green-grey, as though the sky has been turned down rather than clouded over. It never brightens. It never darkens. There is no sun position to track. The light is simply *the setting this place runs at*.

And it runs.

## The Sound

It is loud in the way a working floor is loud — not painful, but total, a sound you stand inside rather than listen to. The dominant note is a deep rhythmic *thud... thud... thud* that comes up through the soles of your feet, regular as a piston, originating somewhere below the cirque floor. Geologists, if any came here, would want to call it a geyser cycle or harmonic tremor. It is too regular for either. It has the patience of a flywheel.

Layered over it: the hiss of escaping steam from a dozen fissures in the rock — except the steam is cold, a chill white vapor that vents in measured exhalations, each fissure on its own interval, the intervals interlocking like cam timing. A waterfall comes off the eastern wall in a thin hard line and strikes a basin of stone with a sound like stamping metal, *clang*-adjacent, because the basin rings. The rock here rings. Strike any boulder and it answers with a tone, and the tones are tuned to each other, and nobody tuned them.

Underneath everything, a fine electrical crackle, like distant arc welding. You will find its source soon enough.

## The Floor

The cirque floor is lush in a sparse way — that sounds wrong until you stand in it. The vegetation is enormously vigorous but widely spaced, as though planted on a grid by no one: islands of growth separated by clean expanses of wet black stone. Each island is a dense fist of life — dwarf willow grown thick as hedge, deep sphagnum, ferns of a green so saturated it looks lit from within, cushions of moss heavy with water. Between the islands, nothing. Bare young rock, slick with condensation, sharp-cornered, almost polished.

The spacing is the unsettling part. The green islands sit at regular intervals. Not perfectly regular — *machined* regular, with tolerances. As though the cirque grows vegetation the way a press stamps parts: where the process puts it, and nowhere else.

Water is everywhere and going nowhere. It weeps continuously from the walls in thin braided sheets. It stands in the moss. It beads on every leaf and never quite falls — or rather, you will watch a droplet fall from a willow leaf, strike the stone, and then watch the *same droplet* fall from the same leaf again. And again. The drop is on a cycle. Most of the water here is on a cycle. The waterfall on the east wall, if you watch it long enough, repeats: there is a particular flourish in its spray, a little leftward whip, that recurs every eleven seconds exactly, identical down to the individual flung beads. The cirque is not being rained on and drained. The cirque contains a fixed quantity of water, in motion, in a closed loop, like coolant.

## The Walls

The walls are where the place stops pretending. The basalt is columnar — and the columns *move*. Slowly, smoothly, certain columns slide upward out of the wall by half a meter and sink back, in sequence, in rotation, a row of stone pistons working at the speed of breathing. Where they slide, the rock does not grind. It hums. Veins of some bright mineral run through the walls, and along these veins travel slow pulses of pale blue light, branching and rejoining like current in a schematic — the source of the welding-crackle, and of much of the cirque's dim glow. The light in the veins is the closest thing this place has to weather.

There are no tool marks. No seams, no rivets, nothing cut. The columns were never quarried; they grew this way and they work this way. The cirque is industrial the way a heart is industrial. It was not built. It *erupted already running*, and it is so young that the rock still has its casting edges — and it will never get older, because erosion requires change, and change is the one thing the cirque does not stock.

## The Animals

The fauna is the richest thing here, and the most disquieting. The cirque teems — but with a workforce, not a wilderness.

Small dark birds, something like swifts, fly the airspace in continuous circuits. Watch one: it traces a loop around the bowl, banks at the same fissure-vent, clips the waterfall spray at the same point, returns. Watch it again: the same loop, wingbeat for wingbeat. There are perhaps two hundred of them and each flies its own fixed circuit, and the circuits interleave without collision, a three-dimensional timing diagram written in birds. They never land. They never tire. They are, you slowly realize, *moving parts*.

Pale salamanders work the weeping walls, gripping the wet stone, and where they pass, the blue mineral-light brightens behind them, as though their bodies close a circuit. Fat grey moths cluster on the sliding basalt columns and ride them up and down, up and down, and seem to be necessary — once you watch a column whose moths had drifted to a neighboring column, and it hitched, audibly, a skipped beat in the great underground thud, until the moths returned. Something large lives in the ringing basin under the waterfall; you only ever see its back, smooth and dark, surfacing on the same eleven-second cycle as the spray, like a component being rinsed.

None of the animals eat. None of them are eaten. There are no carcasses, no droppings, no nests, no young. There is reproduction nowhere in the cirque and death nowhere in the cirque. The ecology has no flows in or out. It is not a food web. It is an *assembly*, and every creature in it is original equipment.

## The Breakdown

Stay an hour and the deeper wrongness assembles itself, quietly, at the edges:

Sound arrives before its cause. You hear the droplet strike stone, and then watch it fall. You hear your own footstep a half-beat before your boot lands, so that walking the cirque floor feels like following yourself.

Distance does not conserve. The far wall is half a kilometer away and forty minutes' walk; the walk back is four minutes. The open fourth side, the drop into mist, recedes as you approach it. No one has ever reached the edge. The mist is not concealing what lies beyond. There is a real possibility that the mist *is* what lies beyond, and the cirque is a small finished thing suspended in unfinished world — a single component, complete and operational, that the rest of reality was never manufactured around.

And the frozen-ness, the terrible smoothness of it: nothing here is at rest, and nothing here changes. Every motion is cyclic. Every cycle is closed. Your watch runs; your hair grows; you get hungry — you are the only open system in the bowl, the only thing aging, and the place registers this. The swifts' circuits bend, very slightly, around the space you occupy, and re-close behind you, the way water closes behind a hull. You are being routed around. You are a contaminant in the works, tolerated, compensated for.

What does it produce? Stand long enough in the dim green light, in the thudding, in the cold steam, and the question stops making sense. A factory makes things by changing matter, and change has been engineered out of this place at the root. The cirque produces *its own continuation*. Each eleven-second cycle's output is the next eleven seconds, delivered to spec, identical, forever. It is the only factory whose product is its process, running at full capacity on a mountain too new to have a name, and it does not need you, and it was never, ever built.

When you leave — downhill, into the cloud, into ordinary erosion and ordinary dusk — the thudding stays in your bones for days, regular as a flywheel. Some nights you can still hear your footsteps arriving slightly before your feet.
