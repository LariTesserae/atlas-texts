# The Condensery

You come up to it through cloud, because there is no other way to come up to it. The road switchbacks out of the lower valleys and somewhere around the fourth turn the world goes grey-white and stays that way, and you understand that the city above does not have weather so much as it lives inside weather, permanently, the way a fish lives in a river.

The ridge it occupies was, at some point, rock. That point is no longer recoverable. Every surface for miles — the long saddle of the summit, the spurs running off it, the steep flanks dropping into fog — has been built over, built up, terraced, walled, vaulted, paved, planted, and re-planted so many times that the original mountain exists only as a rumor inside the construction, the way a shipwreck exists inside a reef. Dig anywhere and you hit masonry. Dig through the masonry and you hit older masonry. The city is its own geology now: strata of brick and slag-block and cast concrete, the deepest courses worn round as river stones, mortar lines softened until walls look poured rather than laid. Nothing here is new in the sense of raw. Even the newest work is made of reclaimed material, so that a wall finished last month already carries two centuries of soot in its grain.

And everything — everything — is green.

Not wild green. Cultivated, total, deliberate green. The city grows its surfaces the way other places paint them. Walls are faced with moss panels, thick as upholstery, kept in slotted frames so they can be lifted out and swapped when a section browns. Roofs are turf and sedge and low tough greens grown in built trays of manufactured soil — there is no natural soil here, none; every cubic foot of growing medium was composted, hauled, or milled from rock dust in the grinding houses on the north spur. Vines with leaves like wet leather climb the cooling stacks on permanent trellises of zinc wire. Terraces of kitchen crops — cold-hardy, broad-leafed, glistening — step down every south-facing wall in masonry troughs old enough that their carved drain-spouts have worn into anonymous lumps. The vegetation is packed in so tightly that from a distance, in the rare moments the cloud thins, the city reads as a single dense pelt draped over an animal whose shape you can only guess at: humps and ridges of green, with chimneys and water towers and crane masts emerging from the fur.

The cloud is the reason for all of it. The ridge stands at exactly the altitude where the moist air of the lowlands, shoved upslope by the prevailing wind, condenses — and so the city drinks the sky. Vast fog-fences of fine mesh stand in ranks along the windward crest, hundreds of them, each one beading with water that runs down into gutters, into cisterns, into the arterial system of channels and pipes that threads the entire settlement. Every wall weeps on schedule. Every stairway has a runnel down its center, a finger's width of clear water moving always. The air itself is at the point of saturation; your coat is damp within minutes, your eyelashes carry droplets, breath comes out as a brief contribution to the general fog. It is cool — not bitter, but a deep, even, cellar coolness that never really varies, the cold of high places under permanent overcast. Stone is cold to the touch. Moss is colder. Metal railings sweat.

The light is the cloud's light: dim, sourceless, shadowless. Noon is a brighter shade of grey. The city compensates with electricity — it has had electricity a long time, turbines in the channel-drops turning the water it harvests into current — but it spends the light sparingly and low. Lamps under eaves, shaded amber, glowing through the mist like things seen underwater. Workshop windows that show as soft rectangles of gold a street away and vanish two streets away. After dark the dimness simply deepens; the lamps don't fight it, they punctuate it.

Here is the strange thing — the thing that takes a visitor days to understand. The city is almost silent, and the city never, ever stops moving.

The fog moves first. It is not a still fog. The cloud river pours over the saddle continuously, fast, so that the visible world is in constant revision — a façade assembles out of whiteness, holds for ten seconds, dissolves; a whole district appears below you in a tearing of the overcast, four hundred roofs and a funicular line and the great mesh-fences flexing in the wind, and then it is gone again as if it had been an opinion the weather briefly held. You never see the same city twice. You rarely see the city at all, only the current installment of it.

And inside the moving fog, the city rebuilds itself, perpetually, everywhere. This is not decay and not growth exactly — it is metabolism. The damp eats everything: mortar, timber, mesh, moss frame, pipe joint. So the city has organized its entire industrial life around continuous replacement, and the replacement never pauses. Block and tackle on every third building. Scaffolding so common it is effectively a permanent layer of the architecture, lashed bamboo-pale poles slick with condensation. Crews moving along the rooftops at all hours swapping moss panels, re-leading gutters, winching down a corroded fog-fence and winching up its successor in the same motion. Material trams — small, frequent, rubber-wheeled — sliding along the terrace edges with loads of brick, soil, mesh, coiled pipe. The grinding houses run day and night, milling demolition rubble back into aggregate; the foundry on the lee spur recasts gutter-iron in a continuous slow loop, the same metal cycling through the city's gutters every few decades like blood.

But all of it muffled. The moss does it, mostly — a city upholstered in three inches of wet moss does not echo. The fog does the rest. Sounds arrive damped, directionless, intimate: the tick of a million droplets falling from eaves and leaf-tips, which is the city's constant undertone, its tinnitus; the soft pneumatic sigh of a tram braking somewhere unseen; a single hammer, far off, patient, blows spaced like a slow clock; the deep felt-more-than-heard hum of turbines under the streets; voices that come to you at a few paces' distance as murmurs even when the speakers are shouting across a courtyard. The most frantic activity you will ever witness here — a forty-person crew racing a failing retaining wall, cranes swinging, trams queued — proceeds at the volume of a library. The inhabitants have stopped noticing this. They speak quietly because the city has taught them that everything carries and nothing carries far.

Animals are scarce, and the scarcity is felt. The cold and the wet and the total constructedness leave little room. There are salamanders — black, glossy, gold-flecked — in the gutter runnels, and they are loved, considered lucky, never disturbed; a worker re-laying a channel will pause and move them by hand. There is one species of small grey-brown bird that nests in the weep-holes of the oldest walls and whose thin two-note call, dropping through the fog at dawn, is for many residents the only proof of morning. In the deep cisterns, pale fish nobody introduced and nobody can explain the ancestry of. That is nearly the whole bestiary. The green is everywhere but it is a quiet green, a green without buzzing, and visitors sometimes find that the silence inside such abundance is the eeriest thing about the place — a garden the size of a region, holding its breath.

Stand at the crest in the evening, by the fences, where the cloud comes over hardest. The mesh sings very faintly in the wind, a sound like distant wires. Water runs past your boots in its appointed channel, off to turn a turbine, fill a cistern, feed a terrace, soak a moss wall, and eventually weep back out of the masonry lower down and rejoin the fog it came from. Below you the lamps are coming up amber in the murk, and on a dozen rooftops crews are still working, lowering the day's spent panels, raising the new, silent at this distance, tireless, ant-busy in the grey — the whole enormous made mountain dismantling and reassembling itself by handfuls, forever, inside its river of cloud, so settled in its own turbulence that the turbulence has become a kind of stillness, and the city, never the same from one hour to the next, has not really changed in five hundred years.
