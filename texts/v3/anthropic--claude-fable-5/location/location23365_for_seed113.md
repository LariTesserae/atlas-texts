# The Antechamber District

You reach it by descending — not far, maybe forty meters below the old surface, down a stairway whose treads are still warm from their own making. The stone smells of rain and solder. At the bottom, the passage opens, and you stop, because the space ahead does not behave the way enclosed spaces are supposed to behave.

It is a single chamber, roughly the volume of a small cathedral — you could walk its length in two minutes — but it has been *built* the way a whole city is built. That is the first strangeness, and it takes a while to absorb. Every surface carries infrastructure. The walls are terraced with balconies, conduit galleries, stairs that switch back on themselves, doorways stacked six and seven high, arcades, cisterns, a bridge that crosses the upper dark at a slight diagonal for no reason you can name. Streetlamps — actual streetlamps, slender and slightly drooped, like the necks of wading birds — line walkways barely wide enough for two people. It is a downtown for a population that does not exist. The density of construction is total; the density of *presence* is almost zero. Nothing moves on the bridges. No door has ever been opened. You are the largest living thing in the district, and possibly the first.

The chamber is young in a way you can feel through your boot soles. Edges everywhere are sharp — cornices that haven't shed a single grain, gutters with machining marks still crisp in them, stone (if it is stone; it has the heft of basalt and the faint translucence of fingernail) that hasn't yet learned where it will crack. In places the architecture is visibly unfinished, not in the ruined sense but in the *emerging* sense: a stairway resolves out of the wall at its top and dissolves back into undifferentiated material at its bottom, the lowest steps still soft-looking, like candle wax deciding to become geometry. If you watch one of these zones for ten minutes — and you will, it's hard not to — you can see it advance. A millimeter, maybe two. The city is still being exhaled out of the rock.

How, exactly, is not clear, and this is where the place exceeds explanation. There is no machinery. There are no fabricators, no arms, no swarms of anything visible. The material simply *organizes*. Sometimes, at the growing edges, the air goes faintly thick — a heat-shimmer without heat, a region where straight lines passing behind it arrive very slightly wrong — and afterward there is more city than there was. The locals' word for it, if there were locals, might be *weather*. It behaves like weather. It has fronts and lulls.

It is also, emphatically, wet. Warm water moves through everything. It weeps from the fresh walls in slow dark fans, beads on the undersides of the bridge, runs in the gutters with a constant conversational gurgle, and gathers in a long shallow canal that bisects the chamber floor — a canal with stone steps down to it, mooring rings, everything a canal should have, holding water so clear and so still between surges that it reads as a second, inverted district hanging below the first. The air is at skin temperature and fully saturated; within minutes your clothes are heavy, your hair is wet, and condensation crawls down every lamppost in bright threads. Periodically, somewhere overhead in the dark above the bridge, something releases, and a brief warm rain falls across half the chamber — two minutes, no more — drumming on awnings that were grown specifically, it seems, to be drummed on.

The sound is the thing visitors mention first, when there are visitors. For an empty room it is *loud*. Water accounts for much of it — the canal, the gutters, the intermittent rain, drips landing in cisterns with deep ceramic notes — but underneath the water there is the sound of the growth itself: a low, granular, continuous roar, like surf heard through a wall, or a kiln, or ten thousand small decisions per second being made in solid matter. It rises and falls on a cycle of roughly an hour. At its peak you'd have to raise your voice. The acoustics are urban — every sound returns from balconies and arcades with the reverb signature of a real city street — so the empty district is permanently full of its own echo, a crowd-murmur made of plumbing.

The light is dim and comes from below the threshold of any obvious source. The streetlamps are lit, but barely — each one holds a soft amber point, the brightness of an ember, enough to define the lamp and a meter of wet pavement and nothing more. Between them the dimness is brownish, warm, like a city at the very last edge of dusk, held there. The canal collects all of it and gives back a faint band of gold down the chamber's center. There is no day cycle. There has not yet been a first day.

Vegetation arrived before anyone did, or was provided. It is generous but disciplined: planters along the balconies already overflowing with broad-leaved, dripping growth; a fig-like tree, surprisingly mature, anchored in a plaza no bigger than a living room, its canopy spread flat beneath a low ceiling as if it had been grown to fit, which it had; moss in every seam; ferns colonizing the spray zones around the gutters; long curtains of something vine-like descending from the bridge into the dark, not yet reaching the water. The leaves are deep green going black in the dimness, all of them glossed with condensation. The plants are the only things here that move in an ordinary way — nodding under drips, trembling in the warm convection drafts — and your eye goes to them gratefully, the way it goes to a fire.

No animals. Not one. No insects circling the lamps, no fish in the canal's perfect water, no birds for the lamppost necks to imitate. The fig drops no figs; there is nothing to carry the seeds. The ecology is a stage set with its botany installed and its zoology pending, and the silence inside the loudness — the absence of any *living* sound, any rustle that isn't water or growth — is the detail that eventually unsettles people, more than the self-assembling stairs, more than the shimmer.

The rules, as far as anyone has worked them out: the district grows toward completion at the pace of a slow tide. Doors finish before what's behind them. The water is potable, blood-warm, and arrives from nowhere that has been found. Nothing here will harm you, and nothing here will acknowledge you, and if you carve your initials into a fresh cornice the cornice will, over a day or two, gently un-carve them — not erasing you, exactly, just continuing to become what it was already becoming, the way a wave doesn't notice a name written on it.

It is a city of perhaps three hundred dwellings, perfectly empty, half-born, lit like dusk, raining indoors, and roaring quietly to itself in the warm dark under the ground — waiting, with no apparent impatience, for whoever it was made for.
