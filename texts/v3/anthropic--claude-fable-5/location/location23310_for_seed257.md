**The Cistern at the Roof of the World**

You reach it by climbing, though by the end the climbing does not feel like ascent. The plateau has been worn so long that it has no edges anymore, only a vast gradual swelling of dark stone under a thin warm wind, and somewhere near the highest point of that swelling — you would walk past it if you didn't know — there is a lip of fitted masonry flush with the ground, and below the lip, water.

Everything past the lip is made. There is no natural rock inside; or rather, there was once, but the builders cut it all away and lined the cavity they'd opened with their own work, course upon course of hand-shaped blocks, each one dressed with stone tools, no metal anywhere, the tool-marks still faintly legible under your fingers like a script you can feel but not read. The chamber is not large. Perhaps the size of a small chapel, a room you could shout across if shouting were a thing anyone did here. A roof of corbelled stone closes over most of it, each ring of blocks leaning slightly further inward than the last until they meet, leaving only one opening: a square mouth at the apex, the width of a person's shoulders, through which the climbing light falls — when there is light to fall.

There almost never is. The mouth admits a column of weak grey illumination at midday, a pillar standing in the black water for an hour or two, and the rest of the time the cistern keeps its own darkness, a darkness with body and temperature, blood-warm. The stone of the plateau drinks sun all day and gives it back all night, and the water, sealed in its made vault, never cools past the warmth of a sleeping animal. You could lower yourself into it and feel nothing change at your skin.

The water fills the chamber nearly to the corbels. Whatever the builders kept here — water itself, most likely; water was the treasure, up here above everything — the cistern has not been emptied in so long that emptiness is no longer one of its possible states. The masonry below the waterline has fused. Mineral seep has crept through every joint and sealed it, smoothed it, so that the courses of stone now read less like construction and more like geology that happens to have corners. The steps that once descended along one wall are still there but rounded into a series of soft swellings, like the vertebrae of something very old lying down.

Nothing green grows. Nothing has ever grown here that could be called a plant — the light is too scarce, was always too scarce, and the builders seem to have wanted it that way, a place of pure water and pure stone. What lives here instead lives on the mineral film, the slow chemical patience of the walls: mats of pale rust-colored growth, simpler than moss, simpler than lichen, a kind of breathing velvet that coats the lower courses and is the bottom of everything else's hunger.

And everything else is *animal*. The cistern is crowded — that is the surprise of it, the thing the silence conceals. Lower a hand into the warm dark and within a minute something will find it: blind shrimp the color of candle wax, walking on the backs of other shrimp, the population so dense in places that the wall itself seems to shiver. Snails with shells gone translucent from generations without sun, stacked two and three deep on the velvet mats, grazing at a pace measurable only in days. Flat soft-bodied things like white leaves (they are not leaves; nothing here is a leaf) gliding along the fused mortar lines. In the deepest corner, where the original floor dips toward a drain that mineral growth swallowed centuries ago, there are fish — a handful of small eyeless fish, pale as the inside of a wrist, who have been the only large presences here for so long that they move like landlords, unhurried, brushing past your fingers without alarm because alarm has no function left in their world.

None of it makes sound. Or almost none. The cistern's acoustics are exquisite — the corbelled vault gathers and returns the smallest noise — and so the smallest noises are all there are: a single drip from the apex mouth after rain, falling every few minutes, each drop a soft round note that the chamber holds for a surprising while. The dry tick of a snail's shell touching another snail's shell. Once in a long while, a faint stony *clink* from deep in the masonry, the building settling another fraction of a degree into its final shape — a sound the fish do not react to, having heard it their whole blind lives.

Change here happens on the scale of dynasties of shrimp. The water level rises a hand's width in the rain season, falls a hand's width in the dry, and that breathing — one breath per year — is the fastest thing the cistern does. The mineral seep advances along a joint perhaps a fingernail's depth in a generation. The light-pillar at noon migrates across the floor with the seasons, and the velvet mats grow thickest in the band it crosses, a pale ring on the dark floor like the memory of a sundial.

What is strange about the place is mild, and you only notice it after a while: the warmth has no source you can find. No vent, no spring, no current. The water is simply warm, has simply always been warm, as if the builders had cut their chamber down into some old stored heat the plateau has been holding since before it was worn flat — heat with nowhere to go, paying itself out so slowly that the cistern will be warm long after everything else up here is not.

The animals know the steps. If you sit on the worn vertebrae of the stair with your feet in the water and stay still through the falling of two or three drops from the roof-mouth, they will gather at your ankles in the dark — the wax-colored shrimp, the soft white gliders, eventually one of the eyeless fish, resting its blunt face against your skin with the absolute calm of a creature that has never once, in all the generations behind it, been wrong to trust the dark.

The builders are gone, of course. They left no images, no marks but tool-marks. But they made one thing that held: a small warm room full of water at the top of the world, sealed, settled, finished — and the world they made it for moved into it, and stayed.
