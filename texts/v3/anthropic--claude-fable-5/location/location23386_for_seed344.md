The basin is not large — you could cross it in forty paces if the ground would let you — and you do not find it so much as descend into it, the way you descend into a held breath. It sits below the surrounding flats like a thumbprint pressed into wet clay, and from the rim it announces itself first as sound: a roar, constant and total, the sound of something enormous exhaling and never needing to inhale.

The roar comes from the borehole.

At the basin's center stands the wellhead — a flanged iron collar maybe a meter across, sunk into rock so new it still looks poured. Someone drilled here, decades ago by the look of the rust, and what they tapped has never stopped coming up. Superheated water and steam vent from the collar in a continuous white column that frays apart three or four meters up, never higher, never lower. The pressure does not vary. The pitch does not vary. You could tune an instrument to it. People who have stood here for an hour report that the roar stops feeling like sound and starts feeling like a property of the air, like humidity, like a color.

And it is humid — ferociously so. The basin holds its own weather. Steam condenses on every surface and runs back down toward the vent in thin, hot threads, so that the whole pocket of land weeps continuously toward its own center. The rock underfoot is slick, blood-warm at the rim, scalding within ten meters of the collar. Touch a stone and your hand comes away wet. Touch the same stone an hour later and your hand comes away wet. The light overhead is ordinary daylight, but it arrives through the drifting vapor diffused and doubled, so that shadows here are soft-edged and slightly plural, and at certain hours a small, private rainbow forms about waist height and stands there like a resident.

The geology is young — embarrassingly young, geologically speaking. The basin's walls are sharp, unweathered, fractured in clean planes that have not yet learned to crumble. But the truly new rock is the rock the vent is making. Mineral-rich water deposits its freight as it cools, and the basin floor is terraced in pale, hard formations — silica and carbonate in shelves and lips and frozen drips, white shading to faint orange, like a cave's interior turned inside out and left in the sun. The terraces grow. Slowly. A millimeter a season, perhaps. A rusted pipe abandoned near the vent is already half-swallowed, its lower flank sheathed in smooth white stone, becoming a fossil of the industrial age in real time. Give the basin a century and it will have eaten everything that was ever built here, politely, without haste.

What was built here is not much. The wellhead itself, its bolts fused into single masses of rust and mineral crust. A length of pipe that once led somewhere and now leads into a wall of new stone. A small gauge-house at the rim — corrugated metal, door long gone, interior fogged and dripping, a pressure dial behind cracked glass whose needle still trembles faithfully at the same reading it has shown for as long as anyone has checked. A few iron stakes. A bootprint, pressed into mineral mud and then mineralized, now permanent, now stone. Whoever drilled here either got what they wanted or understood quickly that they had opened something that did not require their further participation, and left.

Vegetation survives only in the basin's compromises — the band where the heat softens but the moisture still reaches. A few stiff, salt-tolerant grasses at the rim, perpetually beaded with condensation, perpetually slightly cooked, the green of something deciding daily whether to continue. Closer in, nothing green at all, but the wet stone within the warm zone carries films of thermophile mats — bacterial life in streaks of mustard, rust-orange, and a green so dark it reads as black. These are the basin's only gardens, and they are laid out in precise temperature contours, each color a thermometer, banding the terraces like the rings of a tree that grows outward in heat instead of time.

No animal comes here. Not because the basin is poisonous — the air is breathable, merely thick — but because the roar fills the entire acoustic world, and nothing that survives by listening can afford to stand inside a sound that erases all other sounds. Birds route around the rising vapor. The basin is, in effect, deaf, and so it has been left to itself, a loud and lonely room.

The unusual thing — the thing visitors mention quietly, afterward, as though unsure it counts — is the steadiness. Hot springs pulse. Geysers cycle. Pressure systems breathe. This one does not. The column of steam has the fixed quality of a flame painted on a wall. The dial in the gauge-house trembles but never drifts. The roar, recorded and analyzed, shows a waveform of almost mechanical regularity, as though somewhere far below, beneath the young rock, something is running — not erupting, not venting, but running, the way an engine runs, patient and provisioned, partway through a task measured in spans no one on the surface uses.

Change comes to the basin anyway, the only way it can: by accumulation. The terraces thicken. The pipe sinks deeper into its white sheath. The bootprint hardens further into the record. In a thousand years the basin will be a low white mound with a column of steam at its crown, still roaring at the same pitch, still holding its small wet weather, still waiting — if waiting is the word — with the perfect composure of a process that has never once been interrupted and sees no reason to expect interruption now.

You climb back out the way you came. The roar diminishes behind you in steps, then all at once, and the silence of the ordinary flats lands on you like weight. For an hour afterward you can still feel the sound in your sternum, a faint sustained note, as if the basin had pressed its thumb there too, and signed you.
