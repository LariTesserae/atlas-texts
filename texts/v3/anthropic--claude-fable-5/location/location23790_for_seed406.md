High on the shoulder of a dry mountain, where the air is thin and blue and the stone has forgotten the last rain by ten thousand years, there is a pocket in the rock.

You would not find it unless you were already lost. The cliff face here is the color of old bone, wind-planed, its surfaces so long-settled that even the dust has a permanent address. Then a seam — a place where the stone once cracked and healed badly — and behind the seam, an opening no wider than your shoulders. You have to turn sideways. You have to want it.

Inside, it is bright. That is the first wrong thing, the first true thing. The hollow is perhaps three arm-spans deep and two across, a stone room the mountain made for itself, and light stands in it the way water stands in a bowl. The ceiling is riddled with fine flaws in the rock — veins of some clear mineral, quartz or something older than the name for quartz — and the high-altitude sun drives down through them all day, refracted, folded, doubled. The walls throw the light back and forth between themselves until it has nowhere left to go and simply stays. Noon lasts hours longer in here than outside. Even the shadows are only a paler kind of brightness.

And the pocket is full. Not decorated — *full*. Every surface, floor and wall and the underside of every ledge, is carpeted, crowded, stacked with growth, and none of it has ever tasted water.

Call them plants because the mind needs a word. They are flora of the dry chemistry: they root in the mineral itself, drawing what they need from the stone the way trees elsewhere draw from soil and rain. The lowest layer is a moss of pale green glass — millions of hair-fine filaments, each one translucent, each one tipped with a bead of something that catches light and holds it faintly after your hand passes over. Above the moss rise the ferns, if ferns is the word: fronds of layered silica thin as mica, green shading to blue-green shading to a mineral gold at the tips, unrolling their spirals so slowly that a single frond takes years to open. Some of them are open now, and some have been open, unchanged, since before anyone alive was born.

Between the ferns: rosettes. Fat, cabbage-like whorls of leaf-stuff with the heft and translucence of jade, packed shoulder to shoulder, each one grown against its neighbors until their edges have flattened into shared walls, like cells in a comb. There is no bare rock visible anywhere. Growth stands on growth. The older rosettes have younger ones budding from their centers, and lichen-like crusts of chalky lavender climbing their flanks, and the glass moss lapping at their bases, so that the whole hollow is a single interlocked body wearing itself in layers.

They drink the light. That is what the brightness is for, or what the brightness has made possible — cause and effect long ago married each other here and stopped keeping separate accounts. Watch a frond for an hour and you will see nothing. Watch it across a season and you would see it lean, by degrees, into the path the refracted sun takes across the chamber, the whole population turning together like a very slow crowd following a very slow procession.

The silence is total in a way that outdoor places never manage. The wind cannot make it past the seam in the rock. There are no insects — nothing here has ever needed pollinating by anything with wings, nothing has blood, nothing breathes in the animal sense. Your own heartbeat becomes an event. If a filament of the glass moss snaps under its own age — this happens, perhaps, a few times a year — it makes a sound like the smallest bell in the world, a *tink* almost below hearing, and the sound dies before its echo, absorbed into the packed softness of the growth. Then the silence closes back over.

Nothing was ever built here. But something was once *done* here.

Look closely at the largest rosette, the ancient one at the back of the hollow, big as a shield, its jade leaves gone milky with age. Along the edges of three of its outer leaves there are notches — clean, angled, deliberate. Cut marks. Someone crouched where you are crouching, long enough ago that the notches have half-healed, the mineral flesh grown back over the wounds in smooth scar-tissue whorls, and took slivers of the leaf away with a stone blade. Harvested it. For what — medicine, pigment, a lens, an offering — the hollow does not say. In a crack near the floor, if you dig your fingers into the glass moss, you can feel it: a flake of chert, worked on one edge, dropped or left, now grown into the carpet like a fossil in reverse. The moss has climbed over it. The moss will finish the job in another century or two.

Whoever they were, they came, they cut carefully — three leaves, no more, from a plant that could spare them — and they never came back, or their children never did. The pocket has been alone since, being bright, being full, being quiet.

It is cool despite the light. The stone holds the altitude's chill and the light here is somehow spent by the time it reaches your skin — the flora takes the warmth out of it in passing, leaves only the seeing part. Your breath does not fog, quite, but you can feel the edge of where it would.

Change happens here on the calendar of decades. A rosette dies — goes fully white, then chalky, then is slowly disassembled by the crusts and the moss, its minerals passed hand to hand back into the commons. A new vein of clear mineral is exposed in the ceiling as the stone around it sighs and settles, and a new blade of light enters the room, and over the following thirty years the growth beneath it thickens and turns its faces up. The pocket is not becoming anything. It became, long ago. What it does now is continue — a small bright room the mountain keeps, packed to its walls with patient green glass, holding its light like a held breath, in no hurry to exhale.
