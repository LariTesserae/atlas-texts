# The Natal Vault

You come into it from above, through a fissure still warm at the edges, and the first thing is the light — which should not be here, this far under, and yet it fills the space the way daylight fills a valley. It comes from the rock itself. The stone of this place is young, so young it has not finished deciding what it will be, and whole reefs of it glow with the residual heat of its own making: a broad, even, amber-white luminance, like late afternoon trapped in mineral. There is no sun. There has never been a sun here. But you could read by this light, and everything that lives here — and nearly everything here is alive — navigates by it.

The vault is enormous. Not a cave: a *region*. The ceiling, where you can find it, hangs perhaps half a mile up, ribbed and unfinished, and the floor rolls away in every direction into haze — ridge after ridge of new stone, basins, half-formed arches, columns that are still lengthening, drop by mineral drop, toward floors that haven't set. The geography is provisional. A traveler who crossed this country a year ago would not recognize it now. The land is being extruded, folded, dissolved, and re-deposited continuously, and you can hear it happening: a deep, irregular groan under everything, the sound of stone accepting new stone, punctuated by the crack and long rattling slide of some formation that grew too ambitious and came down.

It is warm the way a kitchen is warm. Wet heat. Water is everywhere but never abundant — it sweats from the ceiling in a slow, perpetual not-quite-rain, beads on every surface, gathers in the low places into hot, mineral-clouded pools the color of milk and rust. The air tastes of iron, of egg, of warm wet chalk. Mist stands in the basins in the early part of the cycle (the rock-light has a rhythm, brightening and dimming over long hours as heat moves through the deep country, and the inhabitants treat this as day and night). When the light swells, the mist burns off and rises and condenses on the ceiling and falls again. The vault makes its own weather, small and constant.

There are no plants. There has never been a green thing here, no leaf, no moss, not even the idea of one. What grows instead is *appetite*.

The fauna is beyond counting. It is the dominant fact of the place — more present than the stone, louder than the stone, in some basins literally outweighing the stone. Everything here feeds on the planet's own freshness: on the chemical wealth of rock that is days or weeks old, on the sulfur and iron and dissolved metals bleeding out of every new surface. The newest formations are the richest, and so the life of the vault crowds the frontier — it surges toward whatever is being born.

Begin small. The walls themselves are furred — not with plant growth but with grazers: flat, tile-shaped animals the size of a thumbnail, armored in their own waste-minerals, packed shell to shell in their millions, rasping the bloom of fresh deposit off the stone with a sound like distant surf. They are the pasture-layer. Above them, in the literal sense, climb the things that eat them — segmented, many-legged, glossy as wet obsidian, ranging from finger-length to the size of dogs, moving through the grazer-fields in slow harvesting lines. The pools boil with pale swimmers. The air — and this is what overwhelms a newcomer — the air is *thick*: gliders, floaters, small membranous things riding the convection columns off the hot stone in spiraling thousands, rising on the heat, raining slowly back down through the cooler air, an endless vertical circulation of bodies. Stand still and they land on you. Stand still longer and the wall-grazers begin to test your boots.

And there are the large ones. In the basin country you will see them from a ridge before you hear them, though they are not quiet: broad, low animals like ambulant boulders, ten or twenty feet across, that feed by pressing themselves against the newest stone and dissolving it — they leave smooth, glazed scars across the landscape, and the landscape, growing always, heals over the scars within a season. They move in herds. Where a fresh extrusion of mineral-rich rock breaks through the floor — and this happens constantly, the ground splitting with a gunshot crack, hot new material welling up glowing — the herds converge on it from miles around, and the gliders converge on the herds, and the predators converge on the gliders, and for a few days that spot is a roaring, shoving, shrieking congregation of thousands of animals of fifty kinds, stripping the newborn rock down to its bones before it has finished cooling. Then the rock is spent, the crowd disperses, and the vault grows quiet there — quiet meaning only the surf-sound of the grazers and the groan of the deep — until the ground splits somewhere else.

The sound of the place, taken all together, is the sound of a port city heard from offshore: no single voice, but an unceasing composite din — chitin clicking against chitin in numbers too great to separate, the bell-tones of the columns being struck (something strikes them; you will learn what), the herds' tidal lowing, the rattle of bodies in the air, and beneath all of it the patient, enormous creak of a world still under construction.

There are builders here. Not many — that is what makes them notable. Among the swarming kinds is one that has begun, only just, to do something different: a knee-high creature, bipedal in a hunched way, with clever forelimbs and eyes adapted to read the rock-light's subtleties. They are rare and they keep to the older, stabler ridges, the ground that has mostly stopped moving. You find their marks before you find them: cairns of selected stones at the safe fords between basins; windbreak walls of stacked slag, chest-high to them, curved against the convection drafts; shallow catch-bowls chipped into boulders to gather the ceiling's slow rain into clean water, away from the fouled pools. They knap the volcanic glass that the vault produces in abundance into blades and scrapers. They have learned to read the ground — to feel through their feet the particular tremor that means a new extrusion is coming, and to be early to it, prying the mineral-crusted grazers off the fresh stone in the rich first hours before the herds arrive and make the place lethal. Their settlements are never more than a few structures: a wall, a roofed crevice, a cache, a ring of glass-knapping debris glittering in the amber light. They build nothing they cannot abandon, because nothing here can be counted on to stay. Their whole architecture is a hedge against the vault's restlessness.

And the strangeness of the place — beyond the strangeness of an underground country lit like an overcast noon — is this: the land seems to *answer* the life. Not with intention, nothing so clear. But the great feeding-scars left by the boulder-herds regrow in patterns subtly shaped by the feeding — ridges form where the herds habitually pass, basins deepen where they congregate, as if the dissolved mineral the animals exhale and excrete is steering where the new stone chooses to rise. The vault is being terraformed by its own livestock, generation by generation, the geography slowly molded into a shape more convenient for the things that eat it. The little builders seem to understand this dimly. Some of their cairns stand in places with no ford, no cache, no obvious purpose — set out on the raw new ground in deliberate lines, as if marking where they would prefer the next ridge to grow. As if they have begun, in their hand-tool way, to farm the planet itself.

When the rock-light dims toward its low ebb, the mist rises, the air-swarms settle in glittering drifts along the warm seams, the herds go still and steam gently in the basins, and for a few hours the loudest thing in the vault is the world being born: the tick and groan and slow drip of a country adding itself to itself, drop by drop, in the dark-amber half-light, crowded to its furthest haze with things that cannot wait for it to finish.
