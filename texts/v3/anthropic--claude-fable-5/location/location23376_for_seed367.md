The plateau has been done with change for a very long time. It was a mountain range once, the kind that casts weather-shadows across continents, and now it is a warm tableland of bare red-brown stone worn so low and so smooth that walking across it you can no longer tell which direction the peaks once ran. The rock is old in the way only shield rock gets old — billions of seasons have taken everything from it except its height. No river has run here in geological memory. There is nothing to erode it anymore. It has settled into its final shape, and its final shape is almost nothing: a high, hot, level surface under a hazy sun, varnished dark by ages of dry air.

And then there is the seam.

You'd miss it a hundred times. A crack in the stone perhaps an arm's length wide, half-hidden under a lip of rock, breathing faintly — actually breathing, a slow warm exhalation you can feel on your face when you crouch over it. Climb down through it, two or three body-lengths, and you arrive in the only living place for a hundred miles in any direction: a pocket chamber the size of a modest room, sealed inside the dead summit of a dead mountain, and absolutely, deafeningly full.

The light comes down through the seam as a single dim blade, gold-grey, swimming with dust and wings. Everything else is gloom — but gloom with texture, because every surface of the chamber is colonized. The walls carry a forest in miniature: dry-country lichens grown thick over thousands of undisturbed years, ruffled and layered like coral, in sage and ochre and a deep singed orange, some of them standing out from the rock in shelves a hand-width deep. Among them, rooted in nothing but cracks and accumulated dust, are wiry dryland plants — papery, silver-leaved, alive without water in any form you could pour. They live on the chamber's breath alone: the humid exhalation of the bodies below. There is no spring here, no seep, no condensation you could lick from stone. The only water in this world is inside the living things, passed body to body, never once free.

The floor is the city.

It rises in terraces from wall to wall — masonry of cemented dust and stone-flour, built grain by grain, pellet by pellet, by the mandibles of a termite civilization that has occupied this chamber for longer than the surrounding rock has had its current shape. The construction is layered like an ancient tell: the lowest galleries are abandoned, fossil-hard, packed solid, the city of a hundred thousand generations ago; above them, newer arcades, buttressed towers, ventilation chimneys angled to catch the chamber's convection; and on top, the living surface, dark with traffic. Arched covered roads run up the walls and across the lichen shelves. There are granaries. There are nurseries you can hear before you see — a fine, ceaseless rustle, like rain on dry leaves, in a world that has never known rain.

The fauna is not a feature of this place. It is the place. The builders themselves number in the millions, and around them has gathered everything that can live on a city: glossy black ants running raid-columns along the upper terraces; pale, eyeless silverfish that live as pickpockets inside the galleries themselves; assassin bugs wearing cloaks of dust and the husks of the eaten, stalking the road-arches with terrible patience; ant-lions in the loose flour at the chamber's edges, their pit-traps arranged in fields, like agriculture run in reverse. A dynasty of fat geckos — perhaps thirty of them, all descended from one ancestor that fell through the seam in some unrecorded era — holds the upper walls, and they are the closest thing this world has to weather: when a gecko moves, alarm ripples across the whole city in a wave you can hear, soldiers drumming their heads against tunnel walls in a dry staccato that passes through the masonry like thunder through floorboards. Above everything, in the vault around the blade of light, spiders have strung a grey canopy across centuries, a sagging sky of silk loaded with the small dust of everything that ever flew.

It is loud in the way a marketplace is loud — not one sound but a thousand near-sounds fused: the rasp of chitin on stone, the click and tick of mandible-work, the drumming alarms, the papery shiver of the silver plants when the warm air turns over. And the air does turn over, on a schedule: the chamber heats through the long day, exhales hard through the seam at dusk, draws in cool dry plateau air through hairline fissures at night. The city is built around this breathing. Chimneys open and close — workers plugging and unplugging vents with pellets, a technology no more advanced than a pebble in a doorway and no less effective than any machine.

Everything here is in motion at once. Foraging columns going out along the lichen shelves and coming back loaded; a raid in progress on the third terrace; ant-lion pits collapsing and being rebuilt; a gecko digesting in the light-blade, jeweled with dust; eggs being carried, walls being raised, the dead being walled politely into the architecture, where they become the architecture. The pace never slackens, and it never has. The plateau outside finished its story eons ago and lies flat under the sun, perfected and empty. All of its remaining unrest — every process the dead mountain still owns — has drained down into this one warm, dim, crowded room, and stayed.
