**The Gullet**

You find it by sound before sight. Half a day's walk into the flat country — that worn-down mineral plain where the hills gave up their argument with the sky a hundred million years ago and lay down — there is a low hum in the ground, like a held note under the boot soles. Follow it, and the plain opens a mouth.

It is not large. Forty paces across at the rim, perhaps. A sinkhole, ancient past the meaning of the word: the limestone of its throat has been dissolved, redeposited, dissolved again, until the walls have the slumped, candle-wax finish of stone that has stopped changing because there is no shape simpler left to take. The bowl drops only a few body-lengths before flaring into a chamber the size of a modest barn — a pocket, a single contained room embedded in the dead flat of the world. The collapse that opened it happened so long ago that the rim has rounded over like an old scar, and daylight falls through the opening in a broad honest column, ordinary noon-colored light, pooling on the floor and bouncing soft and gray into every corner. Nothing down here is hidden in darkness. Everything is visible. That is part of the horror, or the wonder, depending on your constitution.

Because the chamber is *full*.

---

**The inhabitants.**

The ceiling is upholstered in bodies. Bats — or something in the bat family that has been alone with itself long enough to drift — hang shoulder to shoulder, wing folded over neighbor's wing, in a continuous shifting nap of brown-gray fur that covers the stone so completely you must watch for a long minute before you can find a patch of actual ceiling. They are small, snub-faced, with ears like torn paper, and there are — there is no counting them. Tens of thousands in a room you could shout across. They breathe together. The ceiling rises and falls.

The sound of them at rest is already loud: a continuous chittering rustle, a dry social static, punctuated by squabbles that ripple outward through the colony like a stone dropped in fur. At dusk it becomes deafening. The whole ceiling peels itself loose in a rotating column — they spiral up through the skylight in a smoke of wings that takes the better part of an hour to empty, the air in the chamber physically *moving*, a downdraft of displaced wind and animal heat, and the noise is a single solid thing you stand inside of.

They go out across the dead plain — out to wherever, far beyond the flats, there is still something living to eat — and they come back before dawn carrying the only sunlight this place will ever metabolize. Not one green thing grows here. Not moss, not lichen, not a thread of algae; the air is too dry, the stone too clean, the water reduced to a single weeping seam in the north wall that produces perhaps a cupful a day, gathered in a polished hollow the size of two hands. The entire ecology runs on imports. The bats are the harvest fleet, and what they bring home, they drop.

---

**The floor.**

The floor of the chamber is not stone. It has not been stone for geological time. It is guano — meters deep, the oldest layers compressed to a pale chalky rock of their own, the surface a soft dark loam that swallows footfalls — and it is *moving*. Always, everywhere, in every patch the eye rests on: a glittering traffic of beetles, black and bronze, in numbers that make the surface seem to simmer. Pale crickets with antennae longer than their bodies stilt across the beetle-traffic like cranes over a harbor. Mites in drifts. Centipedes the color of old brass hunting the mites. Blind, silk-white spiders strung between fallen rocks, harvesting whatever flies low. There are small things eating waste, smaller things eating the small things, and a whole guild of scavengers waiting under the densest part of the roost for the regular soft thud of a bat that has died in its sleep and let go — a body lasts perhaps an hour down here before it is a clean architecture of bone, and the bones themselves are gnawed for minerals until they go to powder and join the floor.

It is an entire biosphere in a single room, cool and gray-lit and roaring, packed nearly to touching, every cubic foot of it occupied, ranked, contested. The temperature never strays far from cellar-cool; the bodies above warm it a few degrees, the ancient stone keeps it there. The smell is enormous — ammonia and dust and warm fur — and it has the strange effect, after the first few minutes, of smelling *organized*, like a working place. Which it is.

---

**The machinery.**

Because people found this room. The evidence is scattered through it, half-digested by the ecology.

There was an extraction operation here, generations back — guano is fertilizer, fertilizer was money, and money will follow a smell a long way across a dead plain. A short run of narrow-gauge rail enters the chamber through a hand-bored side adit, its rails furred orange with rust, its single remaining ore-cart filled to the brim with floor — beetles included — as though the room had simply resumed depositing into the container left out for it. A timber gantry leans against the south wall, more shape than structure now. There is a hopper, a chute, a winch drum wound with cable gone stiff as root.

And there is the engine.

This is the hum you felt through the plain. In a niche cut into the west wall sits a squat compressor or ventilator — riveted casing, a flywheel taller than a person, fed by a line that runs off down the adit toward some power source nobody living has inspected — and it is *still turning*. Slowly, patiently, at maybe a tenth of its working speed, lubricated by who-knows-what, doing who-knows-what residual job: pushing a faint breath of air through the chamber, perhaps, which may be the only reason the ammonia hasn't long since made the room unbreathable. Its sound is the bass note under everything — *whum… whum… whum…* — and the colony has incorporated it. The bats roost thickest directly above its warmth. The crickets call in the gaps of its rhythm. A machine left running so long that the ecosystem has mistaken it for weather.

That is the only real strangeness of the place, and it is a quiet one. Not impossible — just unaccounted-for. The engine runs. No one feeds it. The floor-life avoids its casing in a precise circle, the way animals avoid a thing they have collectively decided about.

---

**The day of the room.**

The pocket has its cycles, dense and punctual as anywhere alive. Dawn: the returning column, the chamber refilling with noise, the floor-guilds surging toward the fresh fall of droppings like a tide answering a moon. Midday: the long gray-lit drowse, the ceiling murmuring, the beetles working, the engine's *whum* holding the beat. Dusk: the exodus, and with it the predators — a few rim-snakes, pale and patient, that live in the cracked stone at the skylight's edge and strike into the rising column, taking their one bat a night the way a tollbooth takes its coin. Then the strange emptied hours, when the room holds only its floor-life and its machine, and the chamber rings with insect sound and the volume somehow does not drop at all.

Outside, the plain runs flat and barren to the horizon, finished, settled into its final form, supporting nothing. And set into it like a geode: this one loud, crowded, breathing room — a furnace of life with no flame visible, burning sunlight gathered fifty miles away, keeping time on a forgotten flywheel, deafening, hidden, going about its dense and ancient business whether anyone ever finds the hole again or not.
