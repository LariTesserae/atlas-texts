**The Condensate Gallery**

You reach it through a gap between two pipes, a gap not meant for passage — your shoulders scrape on both sides, and the metal is so cold it feels wet before it is wet. Then it *is* wet. Then the gap opens, and you are inside.

The chamber is perhaps eight meters across. It is impossible to know for certain, because the far wall is only intermittently visible through the haze of breath and steam and falling water, and because the chamber is not empty. The chamber is almost entirely full.

There is no stone here. There never was. You are high inside something — a tower, a massif of plumbing, an industrial mountain whose outside you have never seen — and everything around you is cast metal, and all of it is *new*. That is the first strangeness, before the louder strangeness asserts itself: nothing here has worn. The mold seams on the great iron ribs still stand proud and sharp, like the sutures of a skull that has not finished closing. Where the casting flash was never ground off, it remains, a thin metal fin you could cut your palm on. The bolts have no rust collars. The rivets are bright under their film of condensate, each one a small unblinking eye. The chamber feels less like a room than like something just unmolded — geology before its first weather, a place that has been *poured* but not yet *lived*, except that this is wrong, because it is being lived in furiously, and has been, apparently, forever, and the living has left no mark at all.

The cold is total and exact. Somewhere far below, the structure burns and churns — you can feel it in your teeth, a drone that comes up through the floor plates like the structure's pulse — but none of that heat arrives here. This is the place where the heat goes to die. Vapor rises through grilles in the floor, meets the high-altitude chill that the walls hold like a grudge, and gives up. Every surface weeps. Water beads on the ceiling ribs in long ranked rows, swells, and falls. The drips are not random. You watch long enough and you understand: the same drop falls from the same rivet at the same interval, has always fallen, will always fall. There is a drip near the east wall that strikes a loose plate with a flat *tok*, and you could set a clock by it, except that nothing here needs a clock, because nothing here is going anywhere.

Vegetation has barely found this place. In the seams between floor plates, where the runoff gathers before draining away through slots you cannot see the bottom of, there is a green-black felt — an algae or a moss or something between, no thicker than a coat of paint, glistening, sparse, defeated. It is the only thing in the gallery that looks like it belongs to the world you came from.

And then there are the inhabitants.

They cover the upper half of the chamber the way barnacles cover a hull. Thousands of them — small bodies, fist-sized, pale as boiled bone, packed wing to wing along every pipe, every rib, every cable tray, hanging in dense overlapping shingles so that in the dim light the architecture itself seems feathered, seems to *breathe*. They resemble swifts, or what swifts would be if swifts had been designed by the same hand that cast these walls: eyes sealed over with smooth skin, feet fused into hooks that fit the pipe diameters exactly — *exactly*, as though the pipes were gauged to the birds or the birds to the pipes. They do not fly. There is nowhere to fly to. They shuffle, densely, constantly, a slow rotation of bodies from the cold outer layers of each cluster toward the warm interior and back out again, an endless circulation that moves and moves and goes nowhere, like the water, like the drone.

And they sing. That is the wrong word. They *emit*. Each one produces a single sustained tone, a metallic shriek pitched somewhere between a kettle and a cutting wheel, and the thousands of tones do not harmonize so much as fuse — with each other, with the turbine-drone rising through the floor, with the percussion of the eternal drips — into one solid chord that fills the chamber the way water fills a flooded room. It is deafening in the precise sense: within a minute you cannot hear yourself, cannot hear your own footsteps or your own voice, can only feel sound, in the sternum, in the sinuses, in the wet film on your eyes. The chord never crescendos. It never falters. People who have spent time here report that after the first hour it stops sounding like noise at all and starts sounding like silence — a silence with mass, a silence you have to lean into.

The strangest thing is the suspicion, hard to shake, that the birds are not incidental. That the chamber was cast around them, or for them. The condensate runs down the ribs in channels that pass directly beneath the largest roosts; the birds drink without moving. The grilles exhale warm vapor at exactly the rate the clustered bodies seem to require. No droppings accumulate — whatever they excrete, the floor seams take it, and the green-black felt is fed by it, and the felt is all there is to show for it. Input, output, closed. You can find no nest, no egg, no juvenile, no carcass. The colony does not appear to be a population. It appears to be a *component* — a living gasket installed in a cold joint of the machine, vibrating at its operating frequency, maintained at equilibrium since the day of the pour.

Stay as long as you like. The light will not change — it comes from a few caged filaments burning at quarter strength, dim and orange, and they have evidently been burning that way without interruption, because their glass is clean and their glow does not flicker. The temperature will not change. The chord will not change. The drop near the east wall will strike the loose plate, *tok*, and a pale body in the third roost will shuffle one place inward, and another will shuffle one place out, and the great structure below will go on burning at whatever it burns, and up here in its cold pocket the consequence of all that fire will go on being this: one small loud unchanging room, packed with blind singers, raining gently on itself, forever.

When you squeeze back out through the gap between the pipes, the silence of the corridor hits you like a wall. Your ears ring for days. The ringing, people say, is the chord — you carry a little of it out with you, and it never quite changes, either.
