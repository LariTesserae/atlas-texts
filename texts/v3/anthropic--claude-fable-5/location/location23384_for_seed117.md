**The Sump**

It is a low place, and low places gather. You come down to it the way water does — by no longer having anywhere else to go.

The basin is small. You could shout across it, if shouting felt appropriate here, which it does not. Perhaps eighty paces rim to rim, walled in by shoulders of grey stone that have been settled so long they've forgotten they were ever raw. Their faces are rounded, dished, run with the dark stains of centuries of seep. The rock weeps. Not dramatically — there is no waterfall, no rush — but everywhere, all the time, in beads and threads and slow varnishing sheets, so that every surface below the rim carries a skin of moving water too thin to see and too constant to ignore. You hear it before you understand what you're hearing: not dripping exactly, but a kind of dense, soft, mineral breathing. The whole bowl respires.

The floor of the basin is flooded to the ankle in most places, to the thigh in the channels. The water is cold without being bitter — the cold of a cellar, of stone that hasn't seen direct sun in a long while. Light comes down from the overcast sliver of sky above the rim, arrives already tired, and pools in the water as a dull pewter glow. There are perhaps two hours a day when a low angle of sun finds its way over the western lip, and in those hours the wet walls flare briefly into bronze, and then it's over, and the basin returns to its preferred condition: dim, damp, deliberate.

There are no plants. This is the first strange thing, and it takes a while to register, because the place does not *feel* dead. The walls are crowded — saturated — with growth, but none of it is green. Mineral bloom: cauliflower heads of pale flowstone, curtains of calcite thin enough to glow when the light catches them, rusty terraces where iron-rich seeps have built their slow shelves. And below the waterline, mats. Olive-grey, ochre, occasionally a deep oxblood — bacterial felts that ripple very slightly in the current, soft as wet velvet, layered over one another in successions that record decades. Lift the edge of one (you shouldn't) and underneath is another, darker, and under that another, like wet pages. Whatever ecology this is, it predates leaves and has never needed them.

And it is *full*. That's the second thing. The density of the place is almost social. Every cubic foot of water has something in it. Pale shrimp-like creatures, eyeless and translucent, hang in the channels in loose congregations, drifting against the seep like commuters. Snails — thousands of them — graze the mats in slow herds, their shells crusted so thickly with mineral deposit that the older ones look like moving pebbles, indistinguishable from the floor until the floor walks. There are flatworms the color of tarnished copper. There are small armored things in the deep pockets that you will only ever see as a settling cloud of silt where they were. On the weeping walls above the waterline, amphipods cling in the films of moisture, packed nearly shoulder to shoulder, feeding on the seep itself. Nothing here is large. Nothing here is fast. But stand still for a minute and you realize you are outnumbered on a scale that is hard to hold in the head — that the basin is less a landscape than a single crowded interior, a room with a population.

The machinery is the third thing.

Half-sunk in the deepest channel stands a pump house — or the masonry shell of one, the size of a garden shed, its roof long gone, its brick gone soft-edged and furred with flowstone until it reads more as a grown thing than a built one. Cast-iron pipework runs from it in two directions, riveted, painted once, the paint now a memory under orange bloom. One pipe climbs the basin wall toward the rim and ends in a sheared flange. The other descends into the water and keeps going, down into a flooded shaft whose mouth is a perfect dark circle in the basin floor, ringed with worn coping stones. Mine, well, drain — whatever it served is below conversation now.

The pump still runs. This is the thing you will not mention to people later, or will mention and then regret. Inside the shell, knee-deep in water, a single-cylinder engine of antique pattern turns over at a pace slower than a heartbeat — *thump*... and a long pause... *thump* — each stroke sending a faint pressure ring across the flooded floor, a pulse the shrimp congregations visibly answer, drifting toward it and away in time. There is no fuel line. There is no smoke. The flywheel is sleeved in an inch of calcite and turns anyway, with the smooth inevitability of something geological. Whether it lifts any water, and from where, and to where, is not apparent. It may simply have become part of the basin's circulation, the way a heart belongs to a body — adopted, mineralized, kept.

Scattered around the bowl are the smaller remains: a tipped ore cart, now a planter for flowstone; a row of ceramic insulators on a fallen post, each cupping its own clear pool; lengths of cable that the seep has turned into stalactite cores, hanging from the wall like the beginnings of teeth. Whoever worked here worked at industrial scale somewhere *else* — this was an outpost, a sump, the place where their deeper works exhaled. They are long gone. The basin has filed their leavings under *stone* and moved on.

The sound, mostly, is quiet — but it is the quiet of a full room where everyone is being considerate. The seep's breathing. The engine's patient thump. Now and then a calcite curtain sheds a single drop into a still pool with a note like a struck glass, and the note hangs, because the bowl is a fine resonator and has nothing else to say. Twice a day, when the temperature shifts, the whole basin fogs: the cool water meets cooler air, and a knee-high mist forms and pours slowly around the pump house, and the snail herds keep grazing inside it, and the pulse keeps coming up through the water.

It is not eerie, exactly. It is *occupied*. It has its arrangements. The water comes in through the stone, the mats eat the water's minerals, the small things eat the mats, the engine keeps its time, and the shaft in the floor takes whatever is finished. The basin settled this all long ago, before anyone arrived and after everyone left, and it will go on holding its small, crowded, dripping session — quorum always present, no business ever concluded — for as long as the rock keeps weeping. Which, by the look of the walls, is a unit of time the place no longer bothers to count.
