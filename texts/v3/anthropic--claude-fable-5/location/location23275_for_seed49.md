# The Shallows

There is no ground here, only depth that has been spoken for.

Begin at the water, because everything begins at the water. It stands warm and shin-deep over most of the world — a continental sheet of it, blood-temperature, the color of weak tea where it's still and the color of hammered tin where it's moving, which is almost everywhere. It is not a sea and not a marsh. It is a working fluid. It rises off every surface as haze, condenses on every overhang, runs down the flanks of the towers in permanent weeping sheets, and falls back into itself in a rain that never quite organizes into storms because the city makes its own weather faster than the sky can. The air is a thing you wear. Within a minute of arriving you are wet, and you stay wet, and the wetness is warm, and after an hour you stop noticing the difference between yourself and the atmosphere.

And out of this water, in every direction, to every horizon and past it: the city.

Not *a* city. Calling it a city implies an edge, a countryside it could be contrasted with. There is no countryside. The construction runs to the curvature of the planet and over it. From any elevated point — and elevation here is modest, the whole world is low, a delta-world, nothing taller than what tide and load-bearing patience will allow — you see the same thing in all directions: a packed, breathing crust of structure standing in shallow water, fading into its own exhaled steam. The haze is merciful. Without it the scale would be unsurvivable to look at.

---

**What it is made of** is harder to say than what it looks like.

The oldest material — and there is *old* here, old in the way riverbeds are old — is a gray-gold ceramic that has been worked and reworked so many times it has developed something like grain, like the rings of a tree, visible where structures have been sheared open. Above and around and through it runs the newer fabric: a matter that does not hold still. It is engineered at a scale below seeing, and it behaves the way physics suggests it shouldn't — load paths that route around damage in real time, walls that are walls until enough traffic insists they become doorways, bridges that exist for the duration of a crossing and then redistribute themselves elsewhere, needed more urgently somewhere else. Watch any block for ten minutes and it will not be the same block. A spire is digested and re-extruded as an arcade. A plaza floods deliberately, becomes a basin, becomes a channel, becomes a market floating on its own former floor.

And yet — this is the thing a visitor takes longest to understand — none of this is *new*. The turbulence is not construction. The city is not being built. It was finished long ago, if "finished" means anything here; what you are watching is its settled state, the way a river's settled state is motion. The chaos is weathered. The instability has eroded into balance. There are channels through the churn that have carried traffic for ten thousand years, and the churn respects them the way floodwater respects a canyon. The constant collapse-and-reformation has its own ancient grammar, worn smooth. Nothing here is permanent and everything here is established.

---

**The sound** is total.

It is the first thing and the last thing. It comes up through the water and in through the sternum: the grind and sigh of matter rearranging itself at city scale, a noise like surf if surf were made of masonry. Under that, the hiss — square kilometers of warm water flashing to vapor against working surfaces and raining back down. Under *that*, traffic: the slap and drone of a million things moving through shallows, the percussion of cargo, voices or things like voices, calls, signals, the whole bazaar-roar of a fully occupied world. There is no quiet district. There is no night shift. The sound has the texture of weather; locals navigate by it, reading the pitch of a neighborhood's reconstruction the way sailors read swell. Newcomers lose their hearing for the first few days — not damaged, just *overwhelmed* — and then something adjusts, and the roar resolves into information, and after that silence is what frightens them.

---

**Nothing green grows here.** This is the strangest absence, and it takes a while to register, because the world does not feel barren — it feels crammed. But look: no moss on the weeping walls, no algal bloom on the warm water, no weed in any seam. Whatever role plants played, this world reassigned long ago. The base of everything is mineral and machine: the city itself exudes a nutrient film, a faint iridescent slick on every wet surface, sugars and stranger molecules synthesized somewhere in the fabric and sweated out like the water is sweated out.

**And the animals graze on the city directly.**

They are everywhere, woven into the density rather than added to it. Flat, tile-shaped things the size of dinner plates that shingle the lower walls in their thousands, rasping the mineral film off the ceramic, lifting and resettling in slow waves when a wall decides to move. Long pale swimmers in the channels, eyeless, reading the water's chemistry, schooling through the legs of pedestrians with the indifference of pigeons. Things like herons, if herons were jointed differently and patient on a geological scale, standing in the shallows for hours waiting for the reconstruction to turn over the substrate and expose the burrowers. Scavenger swarms that arrive within seconds wherever a structure is being digested, gleaning the briefly loose matter before the city reclaims it. Whether all of these were ever wild, or whether some are the city's own circulating organs grown independent, is a distinction the locals stopped finding meaningful a long time ago. The ecology and the infrastructure share a metabolism. The fauna are the closest thing the place has to a memory of biology, and they thrive — sleek, loud, well-fed, utterly at home in a world that rebuilds itself underneath them hourly.

---

**The light** is ordinary, which is its own small mercy. A pale strong daylight comes down through the steam-haze, diffuse, shadowless at noon, going copper in the evenings when the suspended water catches it and the whole vapor-ceiling ignites at once. The towers don't glow, mostly. The water doesn't shine with anything but reflected sky. In a world this strange, the honesty of the light is almost moving: it falls on the churning fabric and the grazing animals and the warm flat water exactly the way light has always fallen on things, and it asks nothing, and it explains nothing.

---

**The rules**, as far as they can be observed:

The water table is sovereign. Everything is built to stand in it, drink it, breathe it; nothing is built to escape it. There are no heights here worth the name and no one seems to mourn this — depth and density are the dimensions that matter, the city packed shoulder-to-shoulder across the flooded plain like a reef that chose breadth over reach.

Nothing is owned for longer than it is used. The fabric reabsorbs what idles. A dwelling maintained by occupancy persists; an abandoned one is fluid again within a day, and the scavenger swarms attend the dissolution like gulls behind a plow.

The old channels are inviolable. Whatever intelligence runs in the city's deep matter — and something does; the load-routing alone implies a mind, or a process indistinguishable from one — it has learned, over its long erosion into equilibrium, which veins must never close. These thoroughfares of water and traffic are the only stillness in the place: not still in themselves, roaring with movement, but still in *form*, the same shape they were ten thousand years ago, the riverbeds of the storm.

And one more rule, the one visitors learn last: the turbulence is not hostile. The wall flowing toward you will pour around you. The bridge will hold until you are across. The city's chaos has had time — vast, patient, weathering time — to learn the shapes of its inhabitants, and it churns past them the way a river churns past a standing heron: violently, deafeningly, and with something that, after long enough acquaintance, you would have to call care.
