**The Hollow Borough**

You come to it through the green, because there is no other way to come to anything here. The lowlands are one plant — or so many plants grown so long into one another that the distinction has rotted away — a vegetable mass that fills the valley floor the way water fills a lake. Stems thick as towers, leaves broad as roofs, all of it laced and felted and grown shut. There is no ground in the usual sense. There is only plant, warm and faintly breathing, going down further than anyone has dug.

And then, inside it, there is the hollow.

It is not large. You could cross it in a few hundred slow paces, if the paces behaved, which they do not always. But after the press of the green — after squeezing between stems with your shoulders, after the wet dark of the grown-shut places — the hollow opens around you like a held breath finally released. A bubble in the mass. A room the size of a hamlet, and shaped like one.

That is the first thing that stops you: it is *shaped like one*. The walls of the hollow are not the chaotic inner surface of a thicket. They are facades. Grown facades — there is no carpentry here, no stone, no tool ever touched any of it — but facades nonetheless: flat green frontages rising two and three storeys, with regular dark openings where windows would be, with lintels of woody rib, with doorways at street level, dozens of them, opening onto a network of lanes that wind between the frontages exactly the way lanes wind in old towns that were never planned, only worn. There is a small square at the middle with a raised growth in its center like a covered well. There are stairs of stacked leaf-shelf climbing to upper openings. There are gutters — actual gutters, channels of curled leaf running along the eaves, carrying condensation in steady trickles down to ground level, where it pools in cisterns of cupped bract.

Nobody built it. That much is legible everywhere. The "cobbles" underfoot are the rounded knuckles of roots, polished smooth — but polished by what? Nothing with feet large enough lives here. The doorways are sized for inhabitants who never existed. The whole borough has the settled, weathered, slightly slumped look of a town that has stood for six hundred years: corners gone round, thresholds dished as if by generations of passage, the facades leaning companionably toward each other over the lanes. It did not grow yesterday into the shape of a town. It grew into the shape of a *very old* town, and it has been quietly maintaining its own ruin ever since. Where a wall sags, new green fills the sag and then weathers itself to match. The place is in balance with its own decay, like a face that has grown into its wrinkles.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere — daylight, ordinary in strength, soaked through the leaf-mass overhead until it arrives a warm, clear green-gold, shadowless, like the light inside a greenhouse at midmorning. The air is blood-warm and damp without being heavy. Everything sweats gently. Droplets stand on the lintels, slide, fall. You can watch a single drop travel a whole facade, window to window, like something going from room to room.

And the borough is *empty*. That is the second thing, and it takes longer to land than the first. All those doorways, and the rooms behind them — you can lean in and look — are hollow chambers of vein-ribbed green, vaulted, swept-clean, containing nothing. Lane after lane of nothing. The square holds nothing but the well-shaped growth, which holds water, which holds the reflection of the canopy and occasionally, briefly, the reflection of a canopy that is not the one above it. The emptiness is not abandonment. Abandoned places fill up — with debris, with collapse, with weeds. This place is empty the way a shell is empty: shaped around an absence so old the absence has become the architecture.

Empty of inhabitants. Not of life.

Because the third thing — and it arrives not as an observation but as a physical pressure, and in truth it was the *first* thing, present from the moment you entered, so total you mistook it for silence — is the sound.

The hollow roars. The entire vegetable mass of the lowlands is colonized, wall and root and canopy, by singing things: cicada-like creatures seamed into the bark, frog-things in every gutter and cistern, uncountable birds packed into the vaults above the leaf-ceiling, and the hollow itself is a resonating chamber set in the middle of all of it, a drum's interior, an ear turned inward. The noise is not loud the way a storm is loud. It is loud the way the inside of a bell is loud. It is structural. It comes through the root-cobbles into your feet, through the warm air into your chest, and it never stops, never dips — a layered, churning chorus: the bass thrum of something enormous and slow deep in the mass below, the metallic shriek-sheets of the wall-singers in overlapping phases, the gutter-frogs hammering out rhythms that the lanes catch and pass along, so that a pattern begun in one alley arrives in the square a half-second later from three directions. After a while you stop hearing it as sound at all. It becomes the medium. You move through it as through water.

The animals are the borough's only traffic, and there is a great deal of traffic. Swifts pour through the lanes in slate-grey ribbons, navigating the street-grid at full speed, banking around corners as though they have flown these exact turns for a thousand generations — and perhaps they have; the routes they fly are worn into the air the way paths are worn into grass; you can almost see the channels. Beetles the size of fists trundle the gutters. Small furred things with too many joints flow up the facades and vanish into the window-holes, and reappear from window-holes on the *opposite side of the square*, instantly, without crossing the intervening space.

Which brings us to the matter of the streets.

The borough does not hold still in the way places are supposed to. It is not that the walls move — they are as settled and weathered as anything you have ever leaned against. It is that the *connections* between things have come loose. Walk the lane that curves left from the square and you will arrive back at the square, entering from the right, having turned no corner you can account for. There is a doorway on the north frontage and a doorway on the south frontage that are, by every test, the same doorway: stand in one and your shadow falls out of the other. The echo of your footstep sometimes arrives before the footstep. Once, the square contains two of the same wren, both singing, half a beat apart, and the wrens are aware of it and unbothered, each tilting its head at the other with the mild interest of a creature looking in a mirror it has long since stopped finding strange.

And that is the deepest thing about the hollow: even its impossibilities are *old*. The breakdowns are not violent, not new, not spreading. They are worn smooth like the thresholds. The lane that loops back on itself loops back the same way every time — the animals know it and use it as a shortcut. The doubled doorway has moss grown identically around both of itself. Whatever happened here — whatever fold or fault opened in the order of things — happened long enough ago that the borough has grown around it, weathered it, made it load-bearing. Reality broke, and then the break eroded, and now the break is just another feature of the architecture, like the leaning frontages, like the gutters, like the roar.

You stand in the square, in the warm green light, drenched in sound, drops falling around you from the eaves of a town that grew itself for no one, watching a swift exit a window it has not yet entered.

It is, in its way, completely at peace.
