## The Hollow Mountain

You climb for most of a day to reach it, through cloud forest that thickens as it rises — which is the wrong way round, you think, until you understand where the moisture comes from. The mountain breathes. Warm, wet air sighs out of a hundred mouths in the high karst, and the forest crowds those exhalations like animals around a spring: tree ferns with fiddleheads the size of a child's fist, moss in pelts four inches deep, orchids stacked on orchids stacked on the rotting limbs of trees that died still standing and never fell, held upright by everything growing on them.

Then the ground opens, and you go in, and you realize the mountain is not stone anymore. Not where it matters.

---

**The substrate.** Whatever rock once made these caverns has not been touched by anything living in ten thousand years — not because it is bare, but because it is buried. Every surface is built. The floors are guano, thirty, forty feet deep in the great chambers, laid down dropping by dropping over millennia, compacted at depth into something like dark soft sandstone, loose and seething at the surface. The walls are nests — swiftlet nests of hardened saliva and moss and feather-down, mortared one onto another, abandoned and reoccupied and built over so many times that they form a continuous rind, a masonry of generations, in places a yard thick. The ceilings are upholstered in roosting bodies, and where the bodies thin out, in old wax and old mud and the daubed cells of wasps and the paper galleries of termites who came up from the forest and never left.

There is no tool in any of it. No cut mark, no joint, no plan. And yet nothing here is *found* — everything is made, accreted, placed by a beak or a jaw or a hind gut, the way a reef is made. You are walking through architecture with a hundred million architects, none of whom has ever seen the whole.

**The light.** Near the mouths there is a green twilight, and the building continues out into it — moss takes the nest-rind, ferns root in the upper guano terraces, lianas drop a hundred feet from the sinkhole rims and hang there furred with epiphytes, so that the boundary between forest and cave is a long, dim, dripping gradient rather than a line. Deeper in, the light fails by degrees. There are chambers where the only illumination is a coin of sky far overhead at the top of a shaft, a single slanted column of pale gold standing in the dark like something structural, with bats stitching across it. Beyond that: nothing. You move by hearing and by the heat of the air on your face.

**The sound.** It does not stop. It has not stopped, the locals will tell you, in living memory or in the memory of the memory. The base note is the colony itself — a deep, granular roar of wings and respiration, millions of small lungs and small hearts in a confined warm space, a sound like heavy rain that never varies and never relents. Over it: the clicking. Swiftlets echolocate in the dark, dry wooden clicks by the tens of thousands, and the bats overlay this with their own sonar, most of it above your hearing but enough of it audible to make the air feel *textured*, crackling, granulated. Then the screech-layer — disputes over roosts, nestlings begging, the shriek when a snake takes someone off the wall. Then below everything, felt in the sternum more than heard, the slow percussion of water: condensation gathering on the ceiling rind and falling forty feet into the guano with a sound like a soft drumbeat, thousands of drips, arrhythmic, eternal. In the great chamber the total volume is enough that two people cannot speak. You communicate by touch, or not at all.

**The fauna.** It is not that there are many animals here. It is that there is nothing here that is not animal. The floor *moves* — a brown-black tide of cockroaches grazing the fresh guano, so dense they overlap, flowing around your feet like a granular liquid. Among them: cave crickets with antennae longer than your forearm, blind white crabs picking through the drip-pools, centipedes the color of old amber. On the walls, racer snakes hang in the nest-masonry like lengths of cable, patient, striking blind at the wingbeats they feel passing. Crabs in the seep channels. Whip spiders flattened in the crevices between nests, ancient-looking, motionless for hours. Every ledge has its frog; every frog has its constellation of flies. The food web is a single column: light falls on the forest above, the swiftlets and bats go out and harvest it as insects, and it comes back down as guano, and the guano feeds everything else, forever. The cave imports its sunlight in stomachs.

**The slowness.** For all the roar and crawl of it, nothing here changes fast. The guano rises perhaps a finger's width in a year. A nest-wall thickens by the width of a feather. The drip-pools have terraced themselves in pale mineral lips over centuries, calm as garden ponds. The colonies wax and wane on cycles no single life is long enough to witness — a chamber slowly abandoned over generations, its nest-rind drying and softening, while a new chamber three hundred yards through the dark is slowly claimed. The mountain is loud the way the sea is loud: a roar that is, on any scale that matters, perfectly still.

**The cycle.** Twice a day the whole system turns over, and this is the only fast thing it does. At dusk the bats pour out — a smoke of them, hours of it, spiraling up the shafts and out the mouths in ropes and rivers visible from the valley floor, while the swiftlets pour *in*, clicking down through the columns of failing light to their walls. At dawn the rivers reverse. For those two hours the mouths of the mountain are solid with bodies moving in both directions, sorted by sound alone, no collisions, no light, no plan — and then the changeover completes, and the roar settles back into its single sustained note, and the warm wet dark goes on building itself, dropping by dropping, nest by nest, as it has, as it will.
