# The Seethe

It is the oldest flat place there is. Whatever stood here once — ranges, plateaus, anything with the arrogance of altitude — was ground down so long ago that the land has forgotten the concept of height. What remains is a plain of warm, dark sediment, planet-wide, sloping nowhere, a surface that has reached its final shape and will never have another one. The geology is finished. The geology is, in fact, the only finished thing here.

Everything on top of it is in convulsion.

The plain lies under a skin of water — ankle-deep in most places, knee-deep in the hollows, never absent — and the water is *busy*. It does not behave like standing water. It heaves. It crowds itself. Across the whole visible reach of it, the surface is rising into shapes: domes, ridges, brief towers a few feet high, columns that twist as they climb and collapse before they finish twisting. Each form holds for a breath, two breaths, and then folds back into the general churn, and the water that made it is already busy making something else. There is no part of the surface at rest. Stand still long enough and you understand that the plain is not flooded — the plain is *occupied*, fully, by an activity, and the activity has no edges. There is no gap between one upheaval and the next. The forms touch shoulders. The world is packed with them to the horizon, like a crowd in which everyone is mid-gesture forever.

The light is the color of dusk that never resolves. Above the churn hangs a ceiling of warm vapor — the water is blood-temperature, and it breathes — and this vapor is dense enough to be a presence rather than a weather. It dims everything to a brownish, copper-shot gloom. There is no sun, only a region of the haze that is slightly less dark, and that region moves, though not always in one direction, and sometimes there are two of it.

## Sound

You hear the Seethe long before you understand it, and you never stop hearing it. The collapse of ten million transient forms per second, planet-wide, produces a roar with no rhythm and no gaps — a white thunder, the sound of an ocean falling onto itself continuously, everywhere, at once. It comes from all directions including, faintly, below, conducted through the ancient mud. Up close, individual events resolve out of it: the *whump* of a dome subsiding, the hiss of a column unwinding, a strange wet syllable when two forms meet and merge. Speech is impossible beyond arm's length. Thought is possible, but it acquires the rhythm of the roar, which has no rhythm, which is a difficult thing for thought to survive.

And the sound is unreliable in a way that takes time to notice. Sometimes you hear a collapse a moment before the form it belongs to has finished rising. Sometimes a sound arrives twice — once thin, once full — as if the air had to attempt it. In the worst stretches, your own footsteps splash a half-beat before your feet come down, and you learn to stop listening for yourself.

## What the water makes

Mostly the forms are abstract — bulges, blades, slow whirlpools that climb out of their own funnels. But the longer you watch, the more you catch the Seethe attempting *things*. A column rises and, for a moment, has the suggestion of a trunk and branching. A ridge develops a regularity along its crest that looks deliberately like a wall, like coursed stone, before it sags back into slurry. Once in a long while a form holds longer than it should — a rough arch, a kneeling figure-shape, something almost architectural — and then you notice the actual remains.

Because there *are* remains, real ones, scattered thin across the plain: stumps of fitted stone standing a few feet above the churn, single doorframes opening from nowhere into nowhere, a fallen lintel furred with mineral bloom. They are unimaginably old, polished by the warm water to the smoothness of teeth. No tools survive near them, no marks of metal; they look less built than *grown and abandoned*. The unsettling part is that the Seethe's accidental shapes resemble them. It is not clear whether the water is imitating the ruins, or whether the ruins are simply the few attempts that ever set — forms the Seethe made in some calmer age that hardened and were left behind, the way a dreamer occasionally says a word out loud.

People who stay too long develop a private conviction about which way it goes. It is best not to ask them.

## Vegetation, such as it is

Life has a foothold here, but only just, and only where the churn relents. Around the stone remains — which break the water and create lees of relative calm — grow stands of reed, dark-bladed, rubbery, anchored impossibly deep in the old sediment. They are sparse: an island of them every mile or so, a green-black smudge in the copper dimness, hissing and lashing as the forms shoulder past. The reeds are the only things in the landscape that move *in response* to the turbulence rather than as part of it, and the eye clings to them with gratitude. There are also mats of something pale and gelatinous that ride the surface, tearing and reforming, possibly alive, possibly just water that has begun to congeal out of exhaustion. Nothing flowers. Nothing here has spare energy for advertisement.

## Fauna

Animals are rare and seem embarrassed by it. There is a long-legged wading bird, ash-grey, that walks the calmer lees with exaggerated care, spearing at the gel-mats; it is almost always alone, and when two are seen together, the seers report that the birds stood facing each other for a long time without feeding, which is probably nothing. Something large moves through the deeper hollows — a back, occasionally, breaking the churn, distinguishable from the water's own backs only because it goes *against* the surrounding motion. And there are the small translucent swimmers that gather in your footprints, in any depression that remembers a shape for more than a moment, as if shape itself were the resource they feed on.

## The breakdown

The deep strangeness of the Seethe is not the churn. It is that the churn has begun to work on things that should be exempt.

Distance behaves like the water. The horizon is not at a fixed remove; it crowds in during the loud hours until the world feels rooms-sized, then withdraws until the ruins a mile off look like remains on another continent. A doorframe approached in a straight line will sometimes be reached, and sometimes the walker arrives instead at a *different* doorframe, of the same stone, with the walker's own earlier footprints already silting closed around it. Reflections in the rare flat patches lag, or lead, or show the vapor ceiling lit by a light that is not currently happening. The warm rain that falls intermittently from the saturated air sometimes falls upward in slow fat drops, rejoining the ceiling, and the roar swallows whatever sound that should make.

The simplest way to say it: this is a world whose ground finished long ago, and everything that was supposed to stay finished with it — space, sequence, the difference between a thing and the image of a thing — has come loose and joined the water. The plain settled into its final form. Nothing else got to. The Seethe is what remains in motion when matter itself has retired: a continental act of restlessness, deafening, packed shoulder to shoulder with its own attempts, making shapes and unmaking them in the warm dim, occasionally — by accident, or by very long patience — making one that stays.

The stone doorframes stand in it, here and there, opening onto more of it.

Some of them, in the worst light, are ajar.
