**The Resin Town on the New Spire**

Begin with the rock, because the rock is young enough to remember being liquid. A spire of dark mineral — pushed up through some older crust no more than a few centuries ago — stands alone in warm air, and everything about it is still sharp. The edges of its ledges would cut a hand. Nothing has had time to soften here: no rain has ever fallen on this stone, no frost has pried at its seams. The fractures are clean as broken glass, and where the spire's flanks catch the light they show a faint conchoidal shine, the gloss of stone that cooled fast and has not yet been touched by weather.

The summit is small. You could walk its whole extent in a quarter hour if walking here were easy — a tilted crown of terraces and chimneys perhaps the size of a hamlet, bounded on all sides by drop. The air below is a haze you don't look into for long. The place has the self-contained feeling of an island, or a walled garden, or a room: everything that matters is *here*, pressed close, and the edges are absolute.

And it is crowded. That is the first surprise, after the sharpness — that a place so new and so dry should be so full.

---

**The flora that does not drink**

There is no water on the spire. Not a seep, not a dew, not a film of damp in the deepest crack. The air is warm and utterly arid, and the stone is dry to its core. The vegetation — and it is genuinely lush, layered, near-touching — runs on something else.

The plants here are *resin-bodied*. Their tissues hold not sap but a heavy amber oil, drawn up (no one watching could say how, but the roots go deep into the young stone, into veins where the mineral is still chemically restless) and concentrated into trunks like candles, leaves like blades of cloudy wax. They photosynthesize, or do something near enough to it; their foliage is a deep oily green shading to honey at the edges, and in the warm light of midday the whole canopy glows faintly from within, the way a thin slice of agate glows.

The dominant growth is a thing like a low tree — call it a *lampwood* — with a squat translucent trunk and a crown of stiff fronds. Lampwoods grow nearly trunk-to-trunk across the terraces, so that moving among them means turning sideways, and their fronds interlace overhead into a broken ceiling. Beneath them grow tiers of smaller things: cushion-plants like mounds of yellow glass beads, climbing cords of black-green resin that ascend the rock chimneys, and a ground layer of brittle moss-analog that crunches very softly, very softly, underfoot — and that is almost the loudest sound this place ever makes.

Where a lampwood is wounded, it bleeds slowly — a tear of amber that takes a season to descend an inch, hardening as it goes. The whole spire is hung with these frozen drips, centuries' worth in some sheltered chimneys, glowing like stored afternoons.

---

**The town**

The summit is built upon — and this is the second surprise, because nothing here has hands in the sense you'd expect, and there is not a tool, a hinge, a worked edge, or a fire anywhere on the spire.

The architecture is *secreted*. Generations of the spire's animals — chiefly a kind of soft-bodied, many-legged builder, palm-sized, the color of the stone — gather the resin tears, chew them warm, and lay them up in courses. What they have made, over the slow centuries, is unmistakably a town: lanes, chambers, stacked galleries three and four stories high, ramps that switchback up the rock chimneys, rounded doorways graded in size from finger-width to shoulder-width. The walls are amber and honey and smoke-dark brown, translucent in their thinner sections, so that in strong light you can see the shadows of occupants moving inside them like figures behind frosted glass.

It is dense the way an old medina is dense. The lanes are barely wider than the bodies that use them. Buildings lean together and fuse; new chambers are budded off old ones; an abandoned gallery becomes the foundation course of the next. There is clearly *organization* — the lanes connect, the ramps go where ramps should go, certain large communal chambers occur at intervals like squares or wells (dry wells; what they hold is pooled soft resin, the town's common stock) — but there is no plan in any drawn sense. The town has the logic of a thing that grew, argued with itself, and settled.

Lampwoods grow up *through* the town, deliberately spared, their trunks incorporated into walls — living pillars that keep bleeding building material straight into the architecture. A street here is a tunnel of amber wall on one side, glowing trunk on the other, waxy fronds for a roof.

---

**The inhabitants**

The spire is abundantly, almost oppressively alive, and almost perfectly silent.

The builders are everywhere — on the walls, in the lanes, working the soft resin with their mouthparts in unhurried shifts. They make no calls. They speak by touch and by scent: two meeting in a lane will pause, lay forelimbs along each other's flanks for a moment, and move on. Watch long enough and you see that the whole town is one continuous slow conversation conducted through trembling contact, wall-taps too faint to hear, and chemical script smeared at doorways.

Above them, in the canopy layer, live the *gleaners* — small gliding things with skin flaps and enormous dark eyes, who harvest the lampwood fronds and pay for the privilege, in the loose accounting of ecosystems, by carrying pollen-analog from crown to crown. They are silent too; they signal by flashing pale throat patches. At any moment a dozen of them are drifting between trunks on stiff outstretched flaps, soundless as falling leaves.

In the deep chimneys and the oldest amber galleries live longer, slower things — wax-pale, eyeless, seldom seen, the town's tunnelers and recyclers, who eat the dead resin of abandoned quarters and excrete it fresh. The town is digested and rebuilt from below at the rate of perhaps a wall a year.

And everywhere, in every crevice and under every cushion-plant, the small fauna: bead-shelled grazers, thread-thin climbers, things like animate droplets that move by flowing. The density of life per cubic yard rivals a reef. The total sound it all produces is less than a library.

The silence is not eerie once you understand it. Sound is expensive and useless here — the air is thin at this altitude, there is nothing to hear *over*, and every important message travels better through touch or through the faint warmth-and-scent gradients of the resin itself. The loudest regular events on the spire: a hardened amber tear, finally too heavy, detaching from an eave and landing on moss with a sound like a fingertip on felt. The dry whisper of a gleaner's flaps. Wind across the summit's sharp edges, which produces not a howl but a single low, almost subsonic tone, like the spire humming to itself.

---

**Time here**

Everything moves, and nothing moves quickly. The resin descends its walls at a season per inch. The builders lay perhaps a course a week. A lampwood takes forty years to reach the canopy and may stand four hundred. The young rock itself is still settling — once or twice a decade a crack extends with a deep silent flex felt through the feet rather than heard, and the town patiently amends its walls around the new geometry, sealing the fresh sharp edges into smooth amber.

The light has a cycle but the place barely registers it: warm bright hours when the trunks glow and the gleaners drift, and dimmer hours when the town's translucent walls hold a residue of stored light, a faint honeyed luminescence that takes half the night to fade — so that from a distance, in darkness, the spire's crown would show as a single soft ember, hamlet-sized, far above the haze.

It is a complete world, and a small one. A town with no water, no fire, no tools, and no voices — built drop by drop out of patience, on a stone too young to have learned anything but sharpness, and already, in its quiet way, fully inhabited.
