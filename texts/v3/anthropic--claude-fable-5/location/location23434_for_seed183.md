**The Weeping Tableland**

It begins, as most descriptions of this place do, with a correction: you are higher than you think you are. The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky has stopped being a ceiling and become a wall — the blue overhead is so saturated, so close to indigo at the zenith, that it seems to press downward, and the light comes through it with a kind of violence. There is no haze here. There is nothing between you and the sun except a thin scarf of atmosphere, and so every shadow is hard-edged and black as poured tar, and every lit surface burns. Snow-blind brightness, except there is no snow. There is only stone, and water, and the things the water does.

The stone is old. Whatever uplifted this tableland did so long ago that the violence has entirely left it. The surfaces are rounded, settled, worn into broad backs and shallow basins — pale grey rock with a faint rose cast, polished in places to a dull mineral sheen by ages of wind and meltwater. Nothing juts. Nothing is sharp except the shadows. The plateau extends in every direction past comfortable seeing: not infinite, but regional, a country of stone perhaps a day's walk across, ringed at its far edges by a drop into cloud. The clouds stay below. They lap at the rim of the tableland like a white sea that has agreed not to come up.

And everywhere, the stone weeps.

This is the first thing the ear understands. The whole plateau is audible — not roaring, but insistently, layeredly loud, the way a cathedral full of whispering is loud. Water seeps from cracks in the rock at every elevation: it beads on overhangs, it strings itself into threads down the faces of low scarps, it gathers in the basin-hollows into hundreds of pools, near-touching, pool beside pool beside pool, so that walking here means navigating a dense mosaic of water and stone. Each seep has a voice. Drips strike pools in irregular polyrhythm. Thin falls hiss. Somewhere — several somewheres — larger flows mutter inside the rock itself, in passages no one has found the mouths of. The combined sound is a constant wet conversation, and at this altitude, in this thin clean air, it carries strangely far. You will hear a drip from a hundred paces as clearly as one beside your boot.

You will also, sometimes, hear the drip before the drop falls. This is the second thing to understand.

The reality of the tableland is worn through in places, the way the stone is worn — not torn, not catastrophically broken, but *eroded*, thinned by long existence into a state of gentle disrepair. The breakdown here is old and settled. It has weather patterns. In the cold blue hour after dawn, sound runs ahead of its causes: echoes arrive first, then the events that made them, so that the plateau seems to be remembering forward. By midday, when the light is at its most brutal, the trouble moves into the water. Pools begin reflecting things that are not above them — a stretch of sky from some other hour, a stone formation from the far side of the plateau, occasionally a figure standing at the pool's edge when no one is standing there. The reflections are not sinister. They are simply *misfiled*. The water has stopped being strict about what it mirrors.

In the afternoons, the seeps reverse. You can watch it happen if you sit still: a thread of water descending a rock face will slow, hang trembling, and then begin to climb, beading upward against the pull of the world, returning into the crack it came from. The stone drinks itself back. Whole basins drain upward into the rock over the course of an hour and refill at dusk. The locals — there are no locals; there have not been locals for a very long time — but whoever once lived here clearly understood this cycle, because their works are built around it.

The works are the third thing. They are scattered, modest, and very old: dry-stone structures raised without mortar by patient hands. Low circular shelters with corbelled roofs, half-collapsed, their fallen stones lying where they rolled. Cairns — many cairns, far more than navigation would require, set in lines that bend in ways that do not seem to correspond to the terrain. Channels chipped into the living rock with stone tools, you can still see the peck-marks, guiding seep-water from one basin to another. And here and there, more enigmatic things: a slab balanced on three small stones above a pool, positioned so its shadow falls across the water at a particular angle; a ring of upright stones around a basin that never reverses, the one pool on the plateau whose water always falls down.

The impression is not of a settlement but of a long, careful, manual negotiation. Someone with nothing but hands and hammerstones spent generations learning the plateau's misbehaviors and building small accommodations for them — channeling the obedient water, fencing off the disobedient, marking with cairns the places where (one slowly gathers, walking the lines) distances do not add up correctly. Follow a cairn line and the far rim approaches at the proper rate. Strike out across open stone and you may walk an hour and gain nothing, the same pool-mosaic repeating under your feet with small variations, like a hand recopying a passage of text and drifting. The cairns are not decoration. They are stitches, holding the worn fabric closed along its seams.

Nothing lives here. This needs saying plainly, because the plateau is so *busy* that the absence takes time to register. There is no moss in the seeps, no algal stain in the pools — the water is mineral-clear, faintly milky with dissolved stone, and tastes of cold and flint. No lichen maps the rock. No insect dimples any pool. Whatever the pools reflect when they misfile their images, they never reflect anything alive. The figure sometimes seen standing in the water's surface casts no breath into the cold air, because there is no figure, and possibly never was — or the figure is from so long ago that the water is the only thing that still remembers it, and the memory has worn smooth like everything else.

The cool of the place is the cool of altitude under hard sun: your shadow-side shivers while your sun-side warms. The air is thin enough that exertion costs double, and the dense crowding of the pools means there is no straight walking — everything is a stepping, weaving progress, stone to stone, with water muttering on every side and the light hammering down and the sound of your own steps sometimes arriving a half-second early, announcing you to the plateau before you have moved.

Toward evening the misfiled reflections settle. The pools grow honest again as the light goes amber and then a deep mineral red, and the cloud-sea below the rim catches fire all the way to the horizon. The reversed seeps release their water back down the rock faces in a long, gathering, plateau-wide exhalation — the loudest hour, hundreds of threads falling at once, basins refilling, the whole stone country running with sound. The cairn lines throw shadows a hundred paces long, and for a few minutes the shadows of the cairns and the cairns themselves do not quite agree on where the cairns are.

Then the sun drops into the clouds, the brightness finally relents, and the plateau does what it has done for longer than its builders were here to watch: it sits, wet and stony and slightly wrong, balanced in its worn old way, weeping quietly to itself in the dark.
