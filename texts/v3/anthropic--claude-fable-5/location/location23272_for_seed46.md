High on the flank of a mountain that has not finished happening, there is a hollow in the rock about the size of a chapel. You would not find it by looking. You would find it the way you find a wasp's nest in a wall — by the hum.

The mountain itself is young in a way that has nothing to do with years. It is *under-resolved*. Its outer surfaces show no weathering at all, no scratch of wind, no varnish, no patina — the stone has the wet-poured look of something cast last night and not yet struck from its mold, except there was no mold, and in places you can see where the rock simply stops being committed: a cliff face that is sharp basalt for fifty meters and then continues as a kind of dense gray *intention*, matte and slightly translucent, geology still loading. The mountain is being extruded from below at a pace no one could watch. A centimeter a season, perhaps. It is patient industry. The whole massif rings very faintly, sub-audibly, like a transformer behind a wall.

The hollow opens off a high shelf, far above where weather would be if this world had finished installing weather. The air up here is thin, hot, and absolutely dry — not desert-dry, which still remembers rain, but *factory*-dry, the dryness of a kiln interior, of air that has never held a molecule of water and has no architecture for it. Heat comes up through the floor in slow waves. Stand still and the soles of your feet learn the mountain's duty cycle: warm, warmer, hold, ease. Hours per pulse.

Inside, the hollow is lit well — clean, ordinary daylight, the kind you'd trust to read by — except the light has no aperture. There is no crack in the ceiling, no glowing vent. Illumination is simply one of the chamber's properties, the way coldness is a property of a cave elsewhere. Shadows behave almost correctly. Almost: they arrive a half-beat early when something moves, as though the chamber computes the consequence before the event, and occasionally a shadow lies at rest on the floor with nothing above it, queued, waiting for its object to be manufactured.

And things *are* being manufactured here. That is the function of the room.

The walls are veined with bright metal — copper-gold filaments, fine as nerve, running in branching schematics through the stone. They were not laid by anyone. They are how this rock grows, the way other stone grows crystal. Current moves through them; you can feel it as a faint pressure behind the eyes, and when the mountain's pulse peaks, the veins brighten just slightly, and the room smells of hot dust and ozone and something resinous, like solder and sap together.

The vegetation grows off these veins.

It is sparse — eight, maybe ten presences in the whole chamber, with generous emptiness between them, each plant alone in its own pool of floor like an exhibit. But each one is *lush* in itself, extravagantly so: ferns the height of a person, dense with fronds, except the fronds are vitreous, a deep bottle-green silicate, warm to the touch, slightly flexible, and threaded down every midrib with that same hair-fine conductive metal. They do not photosynthesize. They run on the wall current and the floor heat, and they grow the way product comes off a line — in discrete increments. Every long while there is a small, precise *click*, like a relay closing, and a new frond exists at the crown of one of them, fully formed, faintly hot, its tip still soft for an hour before it anneals. Between clicks: nothing. No swaying, no rustle. There is no wind in here and the plants would not answer it anyway.

The largest fern, near the chamber's center, is not entirely decided. It exists in about three drafts at once. Look at it directly and it is one plant; let your eye drift and you catch the others superimposed — a version with broader fronds, a version curled in tighter spirals, a version that is mostly the *idea* of a fern, sketched in glowing vein-metal with no glass grown over it yet. The drafts do not flicker. They simply cohabit, patiently, while the chamber works out, over what may be decades, which one it means.

There is no animal life and there is no provision for any. No seed here expects a carrier. No frond expects to be eaten. The ecology is a closed loop between rock, current, and glass: old fronds, after some immense interval, dim, go brittle, and are reabsorbed — not falling, not rotting, but sinking by fractions of a millimeter back into the floor, their metal reclaimed up the veins, their silica returning to inventory. There is no death in the chamber, exactly. There is *recall*.

The sound of the place rewards stillness. Under the hum: the tick of stone cooling somewhere deep, on a schedule of its own. The occasional manufacturing click of a frond. Very rarely, from the unfinished side of the chamber, a soft sound like a page turning — and that is the most unnerving thing here, because the unfinished side is where the room runs out of itself. The far wall, beyond the last fern, does not end in rock. It ends in a region where the chamber has not been compiled: a depth of warm gray, faintly grained, in which you can see — if you stare, and you will — the *plans*. Vein-routes laid out in light with no stone around them. The schematic of a fern that is sixty years from its first click. A shadow, folded flat, filed against future need. The page-turning sound is the boundary advancing, completing another sliver of wall, and it happens perhaps once a day, perhaps once a month.

The one rule of the place, learned quickly by anyone who lingers: do not stand where the room is not finished. Nothing dramatic occurs. You simply begin, very gently, to feel *provisional* — your edges going matte, your shadow arriving earlier and earlier — and the chamber, patient, electrified, half-built and entirely sure of itself, begins quietly drafting a better version of where you stand.

Step back toward the ferns and the heat of the floor, and you are restored to the part of the world that has already been decided. The hum holds steady. Somewhere below, the mountain takes another century-long breath, and the room grows realer by one click.
