**The Cleft at Weir's Standing**

You would walk past it. Most do. The plateau at this altitude is a scoured table of grey stone, frost-shattered, gleaming faintly wherever meltwater has smeared it, and the crack in its surface is no wider than a farm gate. There is a handrail — old iron, rope-thick with rime — and a stair going down. That is the only announcement the place makes.

The cleft descends perhaps sixty metres and is never more than thirty across, but it runs deep enough back into the rock that the whole of it cannot be seen from any single point. And in that space, over some centuries, people have built a town.

It is a vertical town, stacked rather than spread. Buildings of dressed stone stand on ledges and on each other's shoulders, their walls continuous in places with the living rock, so that it is not always possible to say where the mountain ends and the masonry begins. The architecture has the settled, load-bearing patience of things built once and repaired forever: lintels replaced within older lintels, doorways half-swallowed by later walls, iron staples green and weeping down the stone beneath them. Nothing here is new. Nothing here has been new for a long time. The town has reached the shape it was always going to reach and now simply maintains it, the way a river maintains a pool.

The light is the light of the bottom of things. The sky is a pale band far overhead, and what falls from it arrives already tired — a dim, even, blue-grey illumination that casts no shadows, only gradations. Lamps burn in some windows, small electric points behind wet glass, and their light does not spread so much as sit, each one a held coin of amber. In the deepest recesses the town keeps its lamps lit at noon.

Everything is wet. Not raining — it rarely rains down here, the cleft is too narrow to catch weather cleanly — but the rock itself gives water. The walls sweat. Seams in the stone weep in slow, dark fans, and the town has spent generations organising that weeping: channels cut into every ledge, stone gutters running along the fronts of houses, lead pipes descending in long deliberate zigzags from level to level. The water is collected, dropped, spun, and collected again. Halfway down the cleft it turns a wheel — a broad, slow, iron overshot wheel, its blades furred with black moss — and the wheel turns a generator in a shed whose windows glow the colour of a banked fire. This is the town's whole industry, or the heart of it: pressure and gravity, converted quietly into light and the slow rotation of machinery further in — a pump, a mill of some kind, a lathe that runs unattended in a workshop whose door stands open on darkness.

The cold is total but not violent. It is the cold of stone that has never once been warm, cold that comes up through boot soles and settles in the wrists. Breath hangs. The perpetual moisture means the whole lower town is glazed — steps worn to a shine and then iced, iron rails wearing sleeves of clear frozen drip. Where warm air escapes from a chimney or a machine-house vent, it makes a small standing cloud that does not disperse so much as get slowly absorbed, and the wall above every vent is bearded white with hoarfrost grown fat as coral.

Vegetation is a rumour the rock occasionally repeats. Lichen, mostly — burnt-orange and bone-grey discs, some of them the size of dinner plates and therefore very old, older perhaps than the buildings they grow on. Moss in the wettest seams, black-green, dense as felt. In one place, on a ledge that catches the noon band of sky, someone maintains a garden: eight stone troughs of some grey-leaved cushion plant, netted against the wind that sometimes pours down the cleft. It is not clear whether the plants are food or observance.

For all its stacked construction the town does not feel crowded. It feels the opposite — half-empty, or perhaps exactly as full as it means to be. Many windows are dark and shuttered. The ledges between occupied houses run long and bare. You will see a figure crossing a high footbridge with a covered pail, or a lit doorway with someone's shadow in it, and then nothing for a long while. The population and the town have eroded together, and both are at rest.

The quiet is the deepest thing about it. High places are often loud with wind, but the cleft is below the wind; the plateau roars somewhere overhead and none of it comes down. What remains is water, articulated: the fine constant hiss of the weeping walls, the plink of gutters, the low wooden groan of the wheel making its turn — one revolution roughly every eight seconds, the town's true clock. Under all of it, if you stand still, a faint mineral ticking from everywhere and nowhere: the rock itself, contracting in the cold, or the pipes settling, or something else. People here can apparently read this ticking. There are marks chalked beside certain seams — dates, small tallies — as if the mountain's dripping were being kept like accounts.

Animals are scarce and specific. A kind of pale chough nests in the upper cleft and comes down in ones and twos to walk the ledges, tame in the way of birds that have never been troubled, its footsteps clearly audible on the wet stone. In the tailrace pool below the wheel there are said to be white, eyeless fish that came up out of the mountain through the same seams as the water; a wire creel hangs beside the pool, so somebody believes it. Once in a while, at the dim change of the light, something moves along the highest ledges too smoothly to be a bird — but the town does not remark on it, and neither, after a while, do you.

The strangeness of the place is mild and structural rather than dramatic. It is in the way machines run with no one tending them, and have evidently done so for years, oiled by someone, adjusted by someone, never seen. It is in the fact that the cleft holds more town than its dimensions should allow — every corner turned reveals another short street, another stair, another lit window further back in the rock, and the map you build in your head never quite closes. It is in the accounts kept on the weeping walls. The town has an arrangement with the mountain; the terms are old, the payments are made in maintenance and attention, and everything you see — the wheel turning, the lamps lit, the gutters clean, the frost fed by warm breath from the vents — is the arrangement being kept.

You climb back out the way you came, up the iron-railed stair, and the wind hits you at the lip like a door opening onto weather. Behind and below, the amber points burn on in the blue-grey dimness, and the wheel completes another turn.
