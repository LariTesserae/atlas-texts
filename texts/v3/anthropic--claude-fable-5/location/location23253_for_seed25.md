**The Hollow Country**

There is a region — it would take three days to walk across, if walking were possible, if you could see — where the land long ago gave up on the sky.

It begins as a flaw in a limestone plateau and descends. Not a single cave but a *country* of them: collapsed dolines linked by galleries, chambers the size of valleys strung together like vertebrae, sunken canyons whose roofs fell in a million years ago and then were roofed again by overhangs creeping back toward each other. The whole system sits low, sunk below the water table's memory, and the rock here is old in the way river stones are old — every edge taken off, every surface settled into the shape it was always going to be. Nothing is raw. Nothing is freshly broken. The stone has the finish of a thing handled for ten thousand generations, though no hand has ever touched it.

Light enters the way water enters a sealed jar: barely, and by accident. In a few chambers a shaft from some distant skylight arrives so diluted, so scattered by its long fall through mist, that it doesn't illuminate so much as suggest. A pillar of slightly-less-darkness, gray on black. Your eyes, given hours, can make out the difference between *near* and *far*. That is all the seeing this country allows.

So the country runs on sound instead. And the sound is *total*.

---

The first thing — before the smell, before the wet — is the noise. It has the physical pressure of weather. From the ceilings, which in places are eighty meters up and entirely invisible, hang the colonies: millions upon millions of winged bodies packed wing-fold to wing-fold across acres of overhead stone, a living upholstery so dense the rock itself has not felt open air in millennia. They are pale, eyeless or near-eyeless, leathery, restless. They never fully sleep. The colony churns constantly — shifting, squabbling, dropping and recatching the stone — and the collective shriek of it, every individual voice pitched into and over its neighbors, builds a standing roar that fills the chambers wall to wall. In the largest galleries the sound has nowhere to go, so it folds back on itself, harmonics stacking until the air feels solid, until you can feel specific frequencies in your sternum, your teeth, the fluid of your eyes. There is no quiet here. There are only different registers of deafening.

And the strange thing — the thing that takes a while to understand — is that the stone *shows* it. The walls and ceilings near the great colonies are fluted, rippled, carved into shallow scalloped waves with a regularity no water explains. Sound, sustained over geological time, is a kind of erosion. A million years of screaming, of ultrasonic pulse hammering the same surfaces day and night, has worked on the limestone the way a river works on its bed. The chambers have been tuned by their own inhabitants. Certain galleries ring. Certain alcoves are dead spots, acoustically smothered, and the animals avoid them the way surface creatures avoid shadows — to a thing that sees with sound, a dead spot is a place of blindness, and blindness here is death.

---

The floor is the second world, and it is worse, or better, depending on your sympathies.

Everything that falls from the ceiling — droppings, dropped prey, the dead and dying — lands on the floor, and the floor receives it with appetite. Beneath the colonies the ground is a soft, breathing terrain of accumulated guano, meters deep in places, terraced and gullied like badlands, warm with its own decomposition even in the cool air. And it *moves*. The entire surface is a carpet of life so dense it has the texture of a single organism: white crickets in such number that they flow like grain, beetles in shifting plates, centipedes the length of a forearm hunting through the crowd, blind millipedes, mites in fogs, pale crabs picking through the slopes of the mounds. Step anywhere — there is no anywhere that is not occupied. The density approaches the theoretical limit: bodies near-touching, layered, the eaters of the falling matter being eaten by the eaters of the eaters, three and four trophic levels stacked within a single handspan of ground.

There are no plants. There have never been plants, not within reach of memory. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a green cell. The entire economy runs on two imports: what the winged colonies carry back in their bellies from the unseen night-country above, and what the water brings. The colonies are the sun of this place. Their droppings are its rainfall of light, converted. Where a colony abandons a chamber, that chamber starves within a season — the carpet thins, goes still, goes to husks — and the silence of a dead gallery, after the roar of the living ones, is its own kind of horror.

---

Water is everywhere and nowhere. The air is saturated; the cool stone sweats constantly, and condensation gathers into beads, into trickles, into thin films that slide down the fluted walls and have been sliding down them long enough to dress everything in flowstone — smooth, dripping, pale draperies of redeposited mineral, the country slowly digesting and rebuilding itself. There is no open sky to rain from, but the country has weather anyway: when the surface world far above takes a storm, the system *breathes*. Pressure shifts move through the galleries as wind; the seeps quicken; whole walls begin to run; and in the lowest passages, sumps fill and black rivers rise without warning, sliding through the dark with a sound you feel before you hear it under the colonies' roar.

In the rivers and the standing pools live the third nation: fish without eyes and without color, salamanders gone translucent so that their slow hearts are visible as a flutter behind the skin, shrimp that navigate by the taste of the current. The pools are crowded too — nothing here is sparse, nothing is rationed by space, only by food — and where a river passes beneath a colony, the fish gather under the rain of droppings in such numbers that the water itself seems to boil.

And in a few deep chambers, where the dark is most absolute, there are the lights. Sparse, faint, easy to mistake for a trick of starved eyes: constellations of blue-white points across an unseen ceiling, the lures of small soft predators that fish for the flying insects with threads of sticky silk. They are the only stars this country has. Animals are drawn to them and die for it. Light, here, is bait — that is one of the rules. Light is never free, never neutral, never safe. Anything glowing wants something.

---

The other rules, as they assemble themselves:

That sound is sight, and the loudest places are the most legible, and a creature's territory is defined by its acoustics — what it can hear, what can hear it.

That nothing is built and nothing needs to be. The country provides its own architecture, weathered into permanence; the animals do not modify it, they only inhabit and, over eons, *polish* it. There are corridors where the passage of bodies — millions per night, for hundreds of thousands of years — has buffed the stone to a dark gloss, a sheen laid down by fur and chitin the way a stair is worn by feet.

That everything falls, eventually. The fundamental direction of this world is *down*: nutrients down, bodies down, water down, and only the winged ones move against the current, hauling the energy of the surface back into the dark, the country's single upstroke.

That the place is finished. Not dead — furiously, deafeningly alive — but settled, complete, at equilibrium with itself, the way a river in old age meanders without urgency. Nothing here is becoming. Everything here simply, enormously, *is*: the roar that never stops, the carpet that never stills, the water that never quite arrives and never quite leaves, the dark that was here first and will be here last, holding the whole shrieking nation of it like a cupped hand.
