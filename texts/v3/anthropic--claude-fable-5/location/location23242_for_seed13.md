# The Terraces of Vell

The plateau rose recently, as mountains measure things. You can tell by the edges: the cliffs that ring it on three sides are sheer and unsoftened, pale grey stone with fracture-faces still sharp enough to read like split slate. No talus skirts have had time to gather at their feet. The rivers that drain the highland haven't yet learned their courses — they run in shallow, braided channels over bare rock, undecided, pooling in basins the land hasn't finished tilting. Where they spill off the rim, they fall in thin white threads that the wind takes apart before they reach the lowlands, so that from below the plateau seems to be permanently exhaling.

Up here the air is cool and faintly wet. Not rain, most of the time — more a condition of the air itself, a dampness that settles on stone in the morning and beads on the undersides of leaves. Clouds drag their bellies across the plateau several times a day. They arrive, dim the light to pearl, leave everything glazed, and move on. Between them the light is generous: a high, clean, white-gold daylight with the particular clarity of altitude, where shadows have hard edges and distant things refuse to blur.

The vegetation took to the young stone with surprising appetite. Cushions of moss thick as bedding upholster every north-facing surface. Tree ferns stand in the stream-basins, and a kind of broadleaf evergreen — low, wide-crowned, with bark like grey rope — grows wherever soil has pocketed in the fractures. The green is not continuous. It comes in islands: a dense grove here, a quarter-mile of lichened pavement there, another grove. Walking the plateau means alternating between the hush of leaf-cover and long open crossings where there is nothing but rock, sky, and the small sound of your own feet.

And scattered through all of this, the city.

---

Vell is not compact. It was never built to be. It spreads across the plateau the way the groves do — in islands. A district of perhaps forty houses on one terrace; an empty half-mile of moss and channel-stone; another district; a long stair cut into a fracture-wall; a cluster of granaries roofed in slate; emptiness again. Causeways of fitted stone connect the islands, raised a hand's height above the wet ground, wide enough for two people to pass with baskets. Taken altogether it is unmistakably a city — thousands of structures, organized, planned, with cisterns and stairs and retaining walls and boundary cairns — but it has the population density of a constellation.

Everything is dry-laid stone. The plateau's youth means the rock breaks clean, in flat blades and rhomboid blocks, and the builders learned to read the fractures rather than fight them. No mortar anywhere. No metal anywhere — the tools are stone on stone, antler picks, wooden levers, fiber cordage, and an apparently inexhaustible patience. The walls are works of obsessive fitting: blocks shaped by percussion and grinding until they sit against their neighbors with joints you could not slide a leaf into. Roofs are slabs of the same pale slate, overlapped like fish scales, green-furred with moss along every seam. Doorways are low and trapezoidal. Windows are rare and small, because the cool never really leaves, and the houses hold warmth the way the cliffs hold the morning fog.

The largest structures are the cisterns: round, open-topped basins of fitted stone, some thirty paces across, fed by channels chiseled into the living rock. They catch the cloud-water and the intermittent rain. Their surfaces are dark and very still, and they double the sky.

---

What a visitor notices first is the quiet. What they notice second — slowly, over days — is the stillness underneath the quiet.

The city is full of life and almost empty of noise. The people of Vell speak softly, when they speak; sound carries strangely in the thin air, skipping across the open rock, and the culture long ago folded itself around that fact. Communication across distance is done with smoke, with arrangements of pale stones on dark walls, with a handful of low fluted whistles carved from bird-bone whose tones don't startle. The loudest regular sounds on the plateau are weather and water: wind combing the fern-groves, the threads of the falls at the rim, rain ticking on slate.

The animals are everywhere and similarly unhurried. Heavy-bodied grey doves nest in the wall-joints by the hundred, so that certain buildings seem feathered. A kind of high-country camelid — shaggy, cream and slate-colored, kept loosely rather than fenced — grazes the moss-terraces in scattered groups and sleeps in the lee of the granaries. The cisterns hold blind white newts that the people regard as belonging to the water rather than to anyone. Small foxes with dense smoke-colored coats walk the causeways at dusk with the unbothered air of fellow citizens. Hawks ride the rim-thermals. None of it is tame, exactly. It is more that nothing here has any reason to expect anything to change, and fear is mostly an expectation.

---

That is the strangeness of Vell, the thing travelers find hard to put words to: the plateau is in equilibrium, and the equilibrium is *deep*.

The clouds arrive on schedules the old people can recite. The braided streams flood to the same stones each season and no farther — there are flood-marks chiseled generations ago, and the water still kisses them exactly. The cliffs do not crumble; the young rock, which everywhere else in the world would be busily falling apart, seems here to have stopped mid-gesture, sharp-edged and patient, like a thing carved yesterday and somehow also forever. Masons repair walls less than once in a lifetime. The moss grows to the thickness of a thumb and grows no thicker.

The people do not find this eerie, because they have never known anything else. Their architecture reflects it: nothing in Vell is built for expansion, and nothing is built temporary. A house is laid out for the number of people it will hold, and somehow it always holds that number. Their counting of years is loose, almost decorative. They have no word that means *ruin*. Their word for the future is the same as their word for the far side of a terrace: a place that is simply elsewhere, fully formed, identical in kind to here.

Visitors from the eroding lowlands sometimes find that after a week on the plateau they have stopped checking the sky, stopped bracing for anything. The dampness beads, the clouds cross, the doves shuffle in the wall-joints, a fox crosses the causeway at the same blue hour. A few visitors find this the most restful place they have ever been. A few find it unbearable and leave by the rim-stairs in a hurry, back down toward weather that means something, toward rivers that go somewhere, toward stone that has the decency to wear away.

The city does not notice either reaction. The threads of the falls keep coming apart in the wind, and keep falling, and are never used up.
