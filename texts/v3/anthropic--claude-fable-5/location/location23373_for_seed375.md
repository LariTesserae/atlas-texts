**The Accreting Town**

It sits in a shallow bowl of land, lower than everything around it, as if the ground had dimpled under the weight of an idea. You come down a slope of dry grey scree — no streams, no gullies, nothing here has ever been carved by water — and the town simply begins: a wall, waist-high, then shoulder-high, then a street.

It is small. You could cross it in two hundred steps if the streets held still long enough to be crossed. From the rim of the bowl it looks like a hamlet of pale stone, maybe forty structures, packed close, with lanes between them and the suggestion of a square at the center. It looks built. It looks planned. There are lintels and thresholds, courtyards and stairs, a thing that is almost a bell tower, windowless, tapering.

No one built it. Nothing with hands has ever touched it.

The town is mineral, and it is growing. The walls are a dry chalky substance, gypsum-white shading to ash-grey, and they extrude themselves out of the ground at the pace of bone healing — a finger's width in a day, maybe less. The edges are everywhere sharp. Corners that would be softened in any old town are crisp as fresh-cut soap here, because nothing in this basin is more than a few decades old, and rain has never come to round it. Run a hand along a wall and a fine dust comes away, the powder of construction, not decay. The town sheds as it grows, like a snake too patient to finish molting.

The light is the dimness of permanent overcast, a sky the color of old pewter that never quite resolves into weather. It is cool — the cool of a cellar, of a north-facing room — and the chill seems to come up out of the ground rather than down from the air, as if whatever chemistry assembles the walls runs cold, drawing heat into itself the way frost does.

And the silence is nearly total. That is the strangest part, given how much is *happening*. Stand in one of the lanes long enough and you will see a doorway slowly narrowing, its jambs creeping toward each other over hours, sealing a house that was open yesterday. Across the square, a new wall is rising out of the paving in a thin blade, already taller than your knee, partitioning the open space according to some logic no one has ever decoded. The almost-bell-tower leans a degree further each season, and the streets around its base have widened, the buildings there politely withdrawing. The town revises itself constantly — opening, closing, thickening, abandoning whole blocks which then powder down and are reabsorbed — and all of it happens below the threshold of hearing. Sometimes, at night, a settling tick. Sometimes a sound like a held breath when a sealed room finally closes its last gap. Mostly nothing. A town under construction, forever, in perfect quiet.

The interiors are empty. That has to be said plainly, because the exteriors promise so much: every threshold suggests a hearth, every window-slot suggests a face. But step inside (where the doorways still admit you) and there is only a room — floor of the same dry mineral, swept-looking, holding a cold grey light and a smell like struck flint and old chalk. No furniture, because nothing furnishes. No soot, because nothing has ever burned here; there is no fuel, no fire, no tool, no mark of any maker. The town generates the *forms* of habitation without any of its contents, the way a shell generates chambers the animal will never use. Some rooms are sealed and have been sealed for years. Knock — gently, the walls are young — and the sound dies an inch into the material. Whatever is inside a sealed room is presumed to be more room.

Life clings on at the margins, sparse and specific. In the older seams — the few walls that have stood ten, fifteen years — grow flat grey rosettes, something between a lichen and a paper flower, dry to the touch, drawing what little moisture exists straight from the night air. They only grow on aged surfaces; the fresh extrusions are too alive, or too caustic, or simply too new. So you can read the town's history by its flora: the bare blinding-pale walls are this year's, the lightly freckled walls are older, and the rare wall furred grey with rosettes is an elder, a survivor of many revisions, and the town seems — though this may be imagination — reluctant to reabsorb those.

The fauna is rarer still. There is a pale animal here, seen perhaps once in a long visit: low, the size of a hare, smooth-skinned, the color of the walls, with a blunt rasping mouth. It grazes the fresh extrusions, gnawing the soft new mineral the way deer take spring shoots, and its tooth-marks are the only carving the town will ever know — the sole erosion in a place rain has never found. It makes no sound. It watches you without alarm, the way things watch when they have never had a predator, then returns to eating the architecture. Above, very occasionally, something crosses the grey sky too high to identify, and does not come down.

What the town is *for* — whether it is the skeleton of something vast and slow beneath the basin, or a crystal habit grown fluent in the grammar of streets, or a settlement rehearsing for inhabitants who have not yet evolved — there is no way to know, and nothing here will tell you. There is no water to dissolve it, no heat to crack it, no machine to measure it, no voice to ask. There is only the dim cool light, the dust of construction underfoot, and the patient, soundless industry of a small town building itself, sealing itself, opening itself, in an empty bowl of land, for no one.

If you stay until dusk, you will notice your footprints in the powder of the lane have already begun, very slightly, to fill in.
