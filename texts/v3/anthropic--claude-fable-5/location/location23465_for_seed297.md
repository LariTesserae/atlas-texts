# The Verdancy

Begin with the smell, because that is the first wrong thing. A jungle this thick should smell of rot — of leaf-mold, of wet bark, of the sweet decay that water makes possible. This one smells of warm stone and crushed mint and something faintly like ozone after lightning, and underneath all of that, nothing. No damp. There is no damp anywhere in the basin, and there never has been.

The land is low and young. You can see its youth in the bones of it: the basin floor is a sheet of pale igneous rock so recently cooled that its edges still hold the geometry of fracture — clean hexagonal jointing, ridgelines like snapped chocolate, no rounding anywhere, no gullying, because gullying needs rain and rain has never fallen here. The rock is the color of unfired clay shot through with seams of something darker that catches the light at certain angles and seems, briefly, to be looking back. The whole basin runs perhaps forty kilometers across, ringed at the horizon by low scarps still sharp as the day they faulted, and every square meter of it that isn't bare stone is green.

This is the thing that takes longest to accept. The vegetation is *lush*. Not the spare, armored lushness of a desert in bloom — true rainforest density, canopy and understory and a tangled middle layer, fronds overlapping fronds, growth so saturated that in places you cannot see three meters into it. And it is entirely dry. Press a leaf between your fingers and it gives like leather over cartilage, supple, alive, warm with the ambient heat — but crush it and nothing weeps out. No sap. No moisture. The break smells of mint and hot glass and seals itself before you can examine it.

The flora here does not run on water. Whatever it runs on, the leaves drink it the way other leaves drink light. They are green — emphatically, gorgeously green, a hundred shades of it — but the green sits *in* the tissue strangely, the way color sits in an opal, shifting as you move. Some of the broad fan-leaves of the understory are very slightly translucent, and looking through one toward the sun you can see structures inside that branch too regularly to be veins: lattices, fractal and exact, repeating down past the limit of the eye. The plants are grown things, but they were grown the way a proof is constructed. Somebody — some process — engineered this biology at a depth where biology stops being the right word, then planted it, then left. There are no structures in the basin. No ruins, no foundations, no markers, no tools, not one made artifact anywhere. The makers built nothing except the garden itself, and the garden was apparently the point.

It is warm without source of warmth changing. The light is full and ordinary — a clear sky, a sun of unremarkable yellow-white — and it stands at mid-morning. It has stood at mid-morning for as long as anything here can attest. The shadows of the great whorled trees do not creep. Come back in what feels like hours and the same shadow lies across the same fractured slab, sharp-edged, unmoved. The basin has been *paused* at the moment of its perfection: every plant at full extension, every frond unfurled, no flower fading, no shoot emerging, no leaf falling. There is no litter on the ground at all. Nothing has ever died here, because nothing has ever had time to.

This is where the wrongness stops being botanical and becomes structural.

Walk the basin long enough and you learn its rules, which are not physics' rules. Distance behaves like an opinion. A grove you fix your eyes on may take an hour to reach or a minute, and the difference seems to depend on attention — places you study arrive slowly, places you forget about are suddenly underfoot. In the deepest thickets, where the growth presses near-touching and the green light comes down filtered through five layers of lattice-leaf, you will sometimes find a corridor of vegetation that repeats. The same fern, the same spray of red-stemmed climbers, the same forked trunk with the same opaline knot — three times, four times, identical to the leaf, as if the world ran short of detail here and tiled what it had. Push through and the repetition breaks, reluctantly, like a pattern letting go.

Shadows occasionally disagree with their objects. A tall whorl-tree may cast the shadow of a slightly different tree — same species, different branching — and both versions are clearly true, and the discrepancy never resolves, because nothing here resolves. The frozen equilibrium that stopped the leaves from falling also stopped the errors from healing. Whatever maintains this place maintains its flaws with the same impartial fidelity as its flowers.

It is mostly quiet. Not silent — silence would be easier. There is a low, intermittent sound that moves through the canopy without wind, a soft *shhh* like distant surf or like a vast quantity of sand settling, arriving from no direction, lasting a few seconds, stopping. The leaves do not move when it passes. Between these passages, the quiet is dense enough that you hear your own circulation. Your footsteps on the bare rock are loud and slightly delayed, as if the sound has to be computed before it can be released.

Fauna is rare, and what exists is uncommitted. You may walk a full day and see nothing. Then, in a clearing floored with hexagonal stone, something the size of a hare — pale, long-limbed, its outline never quite settling, like a creature seen through heat-shimmer though the air is still — watching you from beside a trunk. It does not flee. It does not approach. When you look away and back, it is in a slightly different position with no transit in between, the way a clock hand moves. There are perhaps a half-dozen kinds of these animals in the whole basin, all of them quiet, all of them ambiguous at the edges, and it is difficult to escape the impression that they are not residents but *remainders* — placeholders left running, sketches the makers never finished rendering before they paused the work and walked out of it.

Because that is, in the end, the feeling the basin gives you, once the smell and the dryness and the impossible green have been absorbed: that this is a finished thing abandoned at the instant of finishing. A world grown rather than built, by a technology so complete it left no machinery — only consequences. The rock is new because the basin is new; the features are sharp because no time has been permitted to pass across them; the garden is lush because lushness was specified, and the specification holds, mid-morning, warm, dry, dense, very slightly broken, forever.

Stand still long enough and you notice one last thing. In the absolute stillness of the foliage — leaves that never tremble, stems that never sway — there is, very rarely, a single exception. Somewhere out in the forty green kilometers, one leaf will turn. Slowly, deliberately, through ninety degrees, and stop. It is never the same leaf. It is never explained. It may be maintenance. It may be a cursor. It may be the only part of the makers that stayed: a gardener with no body and no hurry, tending a garden that no longer needs anything at all.
