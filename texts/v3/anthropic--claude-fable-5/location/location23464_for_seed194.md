# The Burl Town

There is no way to say where it is from the outside, because from the outside there is only the plant — the one plant, the only plant, the plant that long ago finished becoming everything. It covers whatever ground there once was in a continuous low mass of dry, gray-brown tissue, heaped and folded like cooled wax, rising nowhere higher than a person could climb in an afternoon. It has no leaves anymore. It has no need of them. It stopped photosynthesizing, or stopped needing to, in an age so distant that the distinction has lost meaning. It simply persists, in the way that the oldest things persist: by having already changed in every way it was ever going to change.

The town is inside it.

You enter through a seam — a place where two lobes of the plant's flesh meet but do not fuse, a gap perhaps the width of a doorway, perhaps narrower, breathing very slightly with a rhythm measured in days. Past the seam, the dark is total. Not dim. Not gloomy. Total, in the way of sealed places: dark like the inside of a seed.

## The Interior

The hollow is not large. You could walk its longest gallery in a few hundred paces, if you knew the way, and the people who made it did know the way, knew it by hand and ear and the smell of the different woods. It is a pocket, a bubble in the mass — a chamber the plant grew around, or grew empty, or was persuaded over centuries to leave hollow. Within it the air is cool and absolutely dry. It has the dryness of granaries, of long-closed cedar chests, of paper. Moisture does not occur here. The plant's chemistry runs on something else now — resins, slow sugars, an internal economy that asks nothing of rain — and so the town it shelters has never known damp, never known rot, never known a puddle. Thirst was the founding problem of the people who lived here, and the architecture remembers it: every surface is grooved with channels meant to gather a condensation that the channels almost never carried.

The town itself is carved, not built. There was never any other material. The walls of every house are the plant; the floors are the plant; the lintels and benches and stairways and the long central street are all the plant, worked with tools of hardened root-thorn and abraded with grit, because nothing here was ever metal. The carving is patient and beautiful in the way of things made slowly by hand in the dark — surfaces smoothed by touch rather than sight, doorways shaped to the body, corners rounded because a corner in the dark is an injury waiting. Generations of palms have polished the walls along the main passage to a texture like worn antler. You can read the town's age in that polish, the way you read a stone step worn into a smile.

It is, unmistakably, a town. There are tiers of dwellings cut into the chamber walls, three and four layers of them, linked by ramps and by ladders of carved peg-holes. There are larger excavations that can only have been communal — a hall with a vaulted ceiling of exposed grain, ribbed like a palate; a market floor with shallow carved basins, each one labeled in a script of incised dots meant for fingertips; cisterns, dozens of cisterns, immaculate and empty. There is infrastructure of a strange, dry kind: tubes bored through the living tissue, plugged with carved stoppers, through which the inhabitants tapped the plant's slow internal sap — not water, something thicker, faintly sweet, the only drink and the only food this place ever offered.

And there is no one.

## The Emptiness

The density of presence here approaches zero. The town is hollow in both senses. No animal lives in it; no animal ever did — the plant admitted nothing with a heartbeat except the people, and the people are gone, gone so long ago that even their absence has aged, settled, lost its edges. There are no remains. There is no debris. The dry air preserved everything they left and they left almost nothing: a few thorn-tools in a wall niche, smoothed by their last hands. A coil of fiber rope, still supple. A carved bowl in a carved alcove, holding dust that was never anything but dust.

You can stand in the central gallery and feel the volume of the dark around you — the specific, pressing largeness of an empty room you cannot see — and there is nothing in it. Nothing moves through it. Nothing has moved through it, except air and sound, for longer than the town stood occupied.

## The Sound

But it is not silent. That is the thing that no description of the dark and the emptiness prepares you for: the Burl Town is *loud*.

The plant is alive, and it is enormous, and it is dry, and dry living tissue under slow internal pressure does not keep quiet. The walls tick and crack constantly — sharp percussive reports, like knuckles, like split kindling, near and far, above and below, an irregular fusillade that never fully stops. Beneath the ticking runs a deeper register: long groaning glissandi as some buried mass of tissue shifts a hair's width against another, sounds that begin below hearing and surface slowly, sweep through the hollow, and sink again. The chamber is a resonating body — it was probably chosen, or shaped, for exactly that reason — and it takes the plant's noises and rounds them, sustains them, hands them from wall to wall. A single crack in a far gallery arrives as a chord.

And on a cycle of roughly a day — though nothing here marks days — the whole structure exhales. Somewhere in the deep tissue a pressure equalizes, and air moves through the bored tubes and the breathing seam, and the town sounds, briefly, every pipe the carvers ever made. It is not music. It was probably, once, to them, music. Now it is a vast tuneless sighing through a hundred carved apertures, deafening at its peak, and when it fades the ticking resumes as if nothing happened.

The people who lived here lived inside this noise their whole lives. Their script is tactile; their architecture is acoustic; one suspects their language was half percussion. They navigated by it — every passage has a different voice, every chamber a different decay. The town can still be navigated that way. It is the only way it can be navigated.

## The Slowness

Change here has not stopped; it has merely lost all urgency. The plant still grows, at the rate of stone weathering. The hollow is, very gradually, closing. Doorways that were cut to a comfortable height now make tall visitors stoop. The fourth tier of dwellings has gone shallow, its rooms reduced to alcoves, then to recesses, then to faint dimples in the wall with a polished lip — you can find the whole sequence along the upper gallery, a row of houses in slow dissolve, the oldest already only a memory in the grain. In another span of ages the town will be a texture. Then a flaw. Then nothing: a denser whorl deep in the body of the plant, where the wood, if anyone ever cut into it, would show a strange small knot of worked surfaces, polished by hands, threaded with stopped pipes, and around it the rings going on and on and on.

The plant does not know the town is there. Or the plant has known for so long that knowing has become structure. In the dark, with the walls ticking and the great breath gathering somewhere below, it is hard to keep the two possibilities apart — hard not to feel that the hollow itself is a kind of held thought, very old, very patient, not yet finished being thought.

The seam at the entrance narrows by about a finger's width per century. There is still time to leave. There is, by the same measure, almost no hurry at all.
