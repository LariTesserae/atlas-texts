# The Hollow Under the Root

You come to it from above, the way everything does — through a fault in the cold ground, a crack barely wide enough for a body, and then the crack widens and widens until the walls fall away entirely and you are standing on a ledge at the rim of something with no honest name. *Cave* is wrong. Caves are made of stone, and there is no stone here. *Forest* is wrong too, though it is closer.

It is a hollow inside a plant. One plant, as far as anyone has ever been able to tell.

---

## The Vault

The space below the surface is vast in the way that makes the word stop working. The far wall — and there must be a far wall — is not visible. What you see instead, looking out from the rim ledge, is a luminous haze, the light of distances stacked on distances until they become weather. The hollow has its own clouds: slow banks of suspended pollen and spore-dust that drift on internal winds, catching the light and diffusing it, so that the air itself seems to be the source of illumination. There is no sun down here and no need for one. The interior is *bright* — painfully bright at first, a white-green brightness like snowfield glare, and it takes the eyes days to stop watering.

The light comes from the plant.

Every surface of the hollow — the floor of braided root, the buttress-walls, the colossal descending columns that anchor ceiling to ground like the legs of something kneeling — every surface is sheathed in a tissue that glows. Not the soft, romantic glow of phosphorescence. This is hard light, cold light, the spectral output of an organism that long ago turned photosynthesis inside out. Above the surface, in the frozen dark of the upper world, nothing grows. So the plant gave up on the sky. It burns its own deep chemistry to make light, floods its inner hollow with it, and then *eats that light back* through the leaves it grows facing inward. A closed loop, wasteful and magnificent. The hollow is the plant's own private sun, distributed across ten thousand kilometers of inner skin.

Touch any glowing surface and it is cold. Cold enough to numb fingers through gloves. Breath fogs constantly. Frost forms in the few shadowed creases — the undersides of root-arches, the lee of fallen columns — and these shadow-frosts are the only darkness in the entire world, thin blue lines sketched across an otherwise blazing interior.

---

## The Floor That Is Not Finished

The floor is young. This is the strangest thing to learn about a space so enormous: it is *new*, and it is not done.

Underfoot, the braided root-mass is green at its broken edges — not the grey of old wood but the wet jade of fresh growth, sharp where it has split, beaded with sap that freezes into pale amber pearls. There are no worn paths. There is no soil, no humus, no accumulated litter of ages, because nothing here has had time to rot, and the cold forbids most rot anyway. The hollow is being grown *right now*, expanding outward at its edges like a bubble blown in slow motion through the planet's root-heart, and the interior surfaces still bear the marks of their own emergence: tear-lines where tissue split to make room, ridges like healed-over wounds, columns that taper to raw growing tips still soft enough to dent with a thumb.

You can watch it move. Not constantly, not everywhere — but stand still for an hour and somewhere in your field of view the floor will shift. A buttress thickens visibly. A new tendril, pale as a tooth, breaks through the wall-skin and begins its weeks-long reach across a gulf of open air toward an anchor point it has somehow already chosen. And elsewhere, with equal indifference, the plant *unmakes* itself: a column the size of a city tower is abandoned, its glow guttering out section by section over a few days, and then it drops — sheared off at the base, falling silently through the bright haze for a long time before the impact arrives as a soft, enormous, faraway *whump*, felt in the soles of the feet more than heard.

This is the turbulence of the place. Not storm, not violence — *revision*. The hollow is a draft the plant keeps rewriting. Travelers' maps are useless within a season. Whole districts of the floor — fields of root the size of small nations — are swallowed back into the mass, digested, regrown elsewhere in new configurations. The plant builds bridges across its own gulfs and then forgets them. It raises towers of itself and lets them fall. No one knows whether this is metabolism or thought.

---

## Air, Water, Quiet

The dampness is everywhere and nowhere. There are no rivers, no pools that last. Instead the cold air, saturated with the plant's slow exhalation, sheds its moisture onto every surface as condensation — a perpetual fine sweat on the glowing skin of the world. The frost-beads grow fat in the shadow-creases, merge, and slip; you hear them sometimes, the single tap of a falling drop, hugely magnified by silence, landing somewhere out of sight. In the high reaches of the vault, where the spore-clouds bank against the ceiling-roots, the moisture occasionally organizes itself into actual rain — brief, drifting curtains of it, falling kilometers through lit haze, evaporating or freezing before most of it reaches the floor. Watching rain fall *underground*, lit from all sides, casting no shadow, is one of the things that makes new arrivals sit down for a while.

And it is quiet. Profoundly, structurally quiet. The plant makes its enormous changes without speech. The dominant sound of the hollow is a kind of textured silence: the tick of frost, the distant drop, an occasional creak running through the floor like a thought through a sleeping body — a sound so deep and so slow it registers as pressure. When a column falls a hundred kilometers away, the *whump* arrives minutes later, soft as a closing door. Voices carry strangely in the bright haze, flattened, intimate, arriving from no particular direction. People who live here long enough begin to whisper without deciding to.

---

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing. That is the second strangeness, after the light: a space the size of a continent, brilliantly lit, wet, alive in every fiber — and nearly empty. The gulfs between the great columns are voids of glowing air, kilometers across, crossed by nothing for days at a time.

But not crossed by *nothing*, quite.

There are the **gliders** — pale, membranous animals the size of bedsheets, rarely seen closer than silhouette, riding the internal winds between spore-banks. They feed on the drifting pollen, or possibly on the light itself; no one has held one. They appear in the high haze as slow translucent shapes, two or three at a time, and are gone for weeks.

There are the **trimmers**, blind and slow, jointed like enormous woodlice, which follow the dying columns. They appear only where the plant has withdrawn its glow, eating the abandoned tissue, and they vanish when the eating is done. Some say the plant grows them. Some say they grow the plant — that the falling columns are not abandonment but *feeding*. Nobody has settled it.

And once — in stories, always once, always somebody's grandmother — something very large was seen moving in the far haze, at the threshold where distance becomes light, and it was shaped like a person, and it was the size of weather. The stories agree on nothing else.

---

## The Marks of Hands

People do live here, thinly. They have to be thin: the hollow does not permit accumulation. Build a town and the floor will rewrite it within a generation.

So what the travelers find instead is *scatter*. A platform of woven root-fiber lashed into the crook of a buttress, hand-knotted, frost-rimed, empty — a way-shelter, used a few nights a year by people following the old route between two springs of drinkable melt. A windbreak of stacked fallen tendrils, their cut ends showing the bite of a sap-hardened blade, because there is no metal in this world that anyone has found; tools here are made from the plant itself, dead tissue cured in the cold until it takes an edge like dark glass. A drying-rack hung with frozen pollen-cakes, abandoned mid-use, the floor beneath it already beginning to bulge with new growth that will swallow the rack by spring — if *spring* meant anything here, where the seasons belong to the plant's moods rather than any sky.

The most common artifact is the **scar-sign**. Travelers score messages into the glowing skin of the columns with their glass-edged knives — route marks, warnings, names, the locations of shelters. The plant heals these cuts, but slowly, and as it heals them the scar tissue glows brighter than the surrounding skin, so that for a few years every message burns. An experienced traveler reads the age of a sign by its brilliance: a blazing fresh cut means *someone passed this season*; a faint warm line means *years*; and the oldest signs are not visible at all except as a slight smoothness, a healed-over silence, words the world has finished forgetting.

There is a custom, among the route-walkers, of cutting a single short vertical stroke before sleeping anywhere — no name, no message, just *I was here and I was alive*. In the well-traveled corridors near the rim, the buttresses carry hundreds of these strokes at every stage of healing, a column of light dimming downward into smoothness, generations deep, the brightest at the top. It is the closest thing the hollow has to a city.

---

## The Edge of It

Walk inward — away from the rim crack, away from the cold ground above — and the hollow does not end. The haze thickens; the columns grow larger and farther apart; the floor's revisions become more frequent and more sweeping, as if the deeper interior is where the plant does its real thinking. The route-walkers have limits they don't cross, marked with the brightest, most often re-cut signs of all: a circle, slashed through. Past those marks, they say, the changes come too fast to map at all — corridors that close behind you, light that pulses, floors that are floors in the morning and walls by night.

No one says the plant is hostile. The route-walkers are careful about this. The plant does not notice you, they say, the way you do not notice what your own blood is doing. The hollow is not a place that was made *for* anything. It is the inside of a decision the plant is still making — vast, bright, freezing, almost empty, and not yet, not nearly, finished.

You can stand at the rim a long time, with the frost forming on your collar and the white-green light filling your eyes, and watch a column fall in perfect silence forty kilometers away, and feel the soft drum of it arrive through your feet, and understand that you are standing in the lit interior of something alive at a scale where your whole history is a scar-sign — a single bright stroke, already beginning, very slowly, to heal.
