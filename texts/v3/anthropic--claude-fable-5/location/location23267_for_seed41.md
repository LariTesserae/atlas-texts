# The Steaming Room

You descend to it through a slot in the ground — a crack in red earth no wider than your shoulders, worn smooth at the lip by centuries of hands that gripped the same stone in the same place. The descent is short. Twenty feet, maybe thirty, down a throat of rock polished the color of old honey. Then the throat opens, and you are standing in the room.

It is not a cave, exactly. It is a chamber the size of a small chapel, a single hollow in the bedrock with no passages leading on. The world above has worn a ragged eye in the ceiling — not directly overhead but off to one side, so the light that falls in arrives already tired, already filtered through the leaves of whatever grows up there at the surface. What reaches the floor is a dim green-gold dusk that never brightens past dusk and never quite goes out. Noon here looks like the last ten minutes before a summer storm. It stays that way for hours.

The heat is the first thing the body understands. It rises from below — from a seep at the chamber's low end, where water the temperature of blood wells up through a bed of pale gravel, pools in a basin no larger than a bathtub, and disappears again into the stone. The water never runs. It breathes. The pool's surface lifts and settles with a slow internal pulse, and from it a constant faint steam unwinds upward through the dim air, so the whole chamber hangs in a warm haze. Every surface sweats. The walls are slick with condensation that gathers and travels in long, slow beads, taking minutes to cross a hand's width of stone. When rain falls in the world above, some of it finds the eye in the ceiling and arrives as a thin, scattered drip — not a downpour, just a sparse curtain of single drops, each one falling its full distance in silence and ending in a sound too small to call a sound.

In that heat and wet and almost-light, the chamber has grown a garden.

It is not the pale, starved vegetation of most underground places. The plants here are *fed* — by the warmth, by the perpetual moisture, by the slow rain of leaf litter and droppings sifting down through the eye. Ferns crowd the floor in ranks, their fronds broad and dark, overlapping like roof shingles, so dense in places that the ground beneath them has not seen even this dim light in years. Mosses upholster every rock in deep green plush, some of it inches thick, spongy and hot to the touch like a living animal's flank. From the rim of the ceiling-eye, roots descend — the long blind roots of surface trees that found the chamber generations ago and never stopped reaching. Some hang free in the air like ropes, beaded with droplets. Others have touched down and thickened into columns, wrist-thick, ankle-thick, one as broad as a torso, furred entirely in moss, so that the chamber seems pillared, propped, half-architecture already. Vines have come down the same way and gone wild in the warmth, looping from root-column to wall and back, and the whole upper air of the room is a layered tangle through which the dim light has to negotiate its way down.

There is almost no sound. This is the strange part, because the room is *crowded* with life.

Look long enough and the walls resolve into inhabitants. Small frogs the color of wet bark sit spaced along the stone like ornaments, throats moving, utterly silent — whatever they say to each other here, they say it without voices, in some register of touch or stillness. Salamanders, glossy as poured glass, ease through the moss with a slowness that makes them seem like a feature of the moss itself. In the warm pool, blind white shrimp drift in loose constellations, and something longer — a soft-bodied fish, eyeless, the pink of an inner lip — turns over once in the depths and is gone. Crickets pale as candle wax cling under every fern frond, and they do not sing; generations in this hush have taken the song out of them, and they speak instead by trembling the fronds, a conversation you can see if you watch the ferns shiver in windless air. Snails the size of fists graze the moss columns, leaving trails that gleam faintly, and high in the root-tangle small bats hang in clusters, fanning themselves with one wing in the heat, their comings and goings through the eye as soundless as moths.

And the room hunts itself, quietly, all the time. A whip-thin centipede flows along a vine and a cricket's frond goes suddenly still. A frog's tongue takes something from the air with no more noise than a blink. Once in a while a snake — slender, copper-dark, a surface animal that learned the way down — pours itself along a hanging root toward the bats, and the whole chamber registers its passage as a wave of stillness moving through the green, presence after presence going motionless in turn, then easing back into movement after it has passed. Nothing here screams. Death in this room is a small rearrangement of the silence.

People knew this place. Not many, and not recently, but for a long time. The evidence is scattered and modest: three steps cut into the stone where the entrance throat meets the floor, their edges rounded back almost to nothing by water and feet. A niche chipped into the wall beside the warm pool, just large enough to hold a lamp or an offering, now holding a cushion of moss and one sleeping frog. Near the basin, half-swallowed by ferns, a single worked stone — a rough basin or quern, cracked through, with a fern growing from the crack. On the wall above the pool, where the steam keeps the stone dark and damp, there are marks: hand-shapes, a row of short lines, something that might be an animal, all of it faded to the edge of legibility, less like images now than like a texture the wall has. Whoever came here came for the warm water, or for the quiet, or for both, and they did not build anything the room couldn't absorb. The room absorbed it.

The one strangeness — beyond the silence of so much life, which is strange enough — is the moss at the low end, around the warm pool. In the deepest dusk of the chamber, where almost no surface light reaches, it carries a glow. Not bright. Nothing here is bright. A faint, even, blue-green luminescence, as if the moss remembered light and was repeating it quietly to itself, dimming when touched, recovering over long minutes. The animals orient by it. The pale crickets gather at its edges in the hours when the eye above goes fully dark, and the frogs arrange themselves facing it, the way creatures elsewhere face a fire. It may only be a fungus threaded through the moss. It may be the moss. No one has been down in long enough to ask the question, and the room offers no answer — only the warm breathing of the pool, the slow travel of drops down stone, the ferns trembling with talk you cannot hear, and the dim glow at the bottom of everything, steady as a banked coal, in a hot green hush that has held its shape, and its crowd, and its quiet, for longer than the steps in the stone have been worn.
