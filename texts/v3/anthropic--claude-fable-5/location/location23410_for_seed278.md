**The Altiplane of the Smelters**

Begin with the height. This is a plateau so large it has stopped behaving like a landform and started behaving like a planet's second surface — a shelf of grey-gold stone lifted two miles into the cold, extending past every horizon, generating its own weather in slow rotating sheets of cloud that take a week to cross from one edge of the visible world to the other. The air is thin enough that sound dies young here. A rock dropped from your hand makes a small, apologetic click and then nothing. The wind, when it comes, doesn't howl; it presses, a wide soundless hand against your back.

The stone itself is old in the settled way — not jagged, not raw, but *finished*, the way a riverbed pebble is finished. Every ridge has been planed down to a long ergonomic curve. Frost-shatter and ten million years of patient weather have left the plateau covered in a pavement of fitted hexagonal slabs, fractured and re-fused, glazed with a thin perpetual varnish of damp. It never quite rains. Instead the cloud-decks descend at intervals and sit on the ground for a day, and everything beads with condensation — every stone wearing its film of cold water, every depression holding a black mirror-pool a finger deep. By morning the pools have skinned over with ice the thickness of paper, and by afternoon the ice has sublimated into a low silver haze that hangs at knee height across hundreds of miles.

Vegetation is nearly an absence. What grows, grows flat: coin-sized rosettes of slate-blue cushion plants wedged into seams, lichens the color of old brass, and — rarely, in the lee of the larger boulders — a kind of dwarf sedge that lives perhaps four centuries and never stands taller than your thumb. You could walk a full day and touch every plant you passed.

And yet the plateau is *crowded*. This is the thing that takes time to understand, because the crowd is silent.

They are everywhere once you learn to see them: the smelter-fauna, the engine-life. The plateau supports an animal density that should be impossible at this altitude and cold, and it manages this because the animals here do not graze. They *process*. The dominant forms are the kilnbacks — slow, dome-shelled creatures somewhere between a tortoise and a foundry, ranging from the size of a bread loaf to the size of a small house. They sit half-sunken in the stone pavement, sometimes a dozen to the acre, sometimes massed in herds of thousands stretching to the horizon like a boulder field that breathes. Their shells are dull oxide-red, ribbed, and warm. Inside each one runs a metabolic furnace: they swallow gravel and frost-shattered scree, and within chambered gizzards lined with living ceramic they crack the rock chemically, drawing out iron, manganese, traces of copper. Their droppings are slag — small glassy black cones that litter the plateau in their millions, and which, weathering over centuries, return their minerals to the thin soil that feeds the cushion plants.

The waste heat of all this digestion is the plateau's true climate engine. Stand among a dense herd and the air is perceptibly warmer; the condensation pools around them stay liquid through the night; the haze rises off their shells in slow columns. They are the chimneys of a smokeless industry. On dim days — and most days are dim, the light here a perpetual overcast pewter, brightening to weak silver at noon — you can navigate by the heat-shimmer above the great herds, visible from miles away like the air over a city of forges that was never built.

Because nothing here is built. That is the strange discipline of this world: industry without architecture. There are no structures, no artifacts, no marks of intention on the stone except the marks of digestion. The kilnbacks do not make nests. The smaller fauna — and there is an *enormous* hierarchy of smaller fauna — make do with what the giants produce. Whole guilds of creatures live on the kilnbacks' output: armored grubs that mine the slag cones for missed metal; pale, many-legged things like animated wire that wind themselves around the warm shells in winter; flat, tile-shaped scavengers that fit themselves into the pavement cracks so perfectly you will step on a hundred before you see one. Above it all drift the condensers — soft-bodied gliders with wide membranous fins, riding the thermal columns off the herds, harvesting fog on their fins and dripping it, drop by drop, into their own mouths and, incidentally, onto the ground beneath, so that the richest pools and the greenest lichen always lie downwind of the largest animals.

The whole assemblage is mechanized in temperament if not in material. Everything runs on slow chemistry, on electrochemical gradients — touch two kilnback shells at once and you'll feel a faint tingle, a few volts of difference between an animal digesting iron and one digesting manganese. The grubs are drawn along these currents like filings. Some naturalists' instinct in you wants to call it a factory; the better word might be a *refinery without walls*, distributed across a continent, with a production cycle measured in lifetimes.

And it is almost perfectly quiet. The fauna here have no voices — sound is a poor currency in air this thin — and they signal instead with heat, with current, with the slow tilt of a shell. What you hear, walking the plateau, is this: the click of slag underfoot. The faint mineral *tick* of a kilnback's gizzard turning over, audible only if you press your ear to the warm shell. Once an hour, perhaps, a sound like a sigh, which is a herd of ten thousand animals exhaling more or less together, a coincidence of rhythm that the silence makes seem deliberate. Then nothing again, for miles.

Change here happens on the calendar of stone. The herds migrate, but a migration takes a generation — the kilnbacks at the leading edge of a herd were born there, and will die there, three hundred yards farther on. The plateau's surface is being eaten and excreted and re-weathered in a closed loop so old it has reached a kind of stillness-in-motion: every century the whole pavement has been swallowed and laid back down, and yet from any given afternoon it appears that nothing has moved at all.

In the long dim evening, when the cloud-deck lowers and the haze thickens, the only lights are biological — a faint furnace-glow through the thinnest seams of the largest, oldest shells, scattered red embers across a hundred miles of darkening grey, each one an animal quietly smelting the world, asking nothing, building nothing, going almost nowhere, forever.
