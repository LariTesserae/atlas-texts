**The Condensate Works**

The land here is younger than the machinery built on it. That is the first strange-but-true fact of the place: the basalt underfoot is fresh enough that its edges still cut, black and glassy, ropy where it flowed and froze, and nothing has had time to soften it — no lichen, no soil, only a skin of mineral white where the steam has been licking it for thirty years. The flow came up out of the lowland less than a lifetime ago, a slow tongue of rock easing into the marsh, and before it had fully cooled, someone had already surveyed it, staked it, and begun bolting things to it.

The Works occupies the whole of the flow and nothing beyond it. That's the boundary of the place — you can walk it in twenty minutes. Where the new rock ends, hot mud begins: a fringe of steaming brown marsh, water standing in pewter-colored sheets, bubbling here and there with a sound like quiet laughter. Beyond the mud there is only haze. The Works does not look outward. Everything faces in, toward the boreholes.

It is hot in the way of laundries and engine rooms — wet heat, total heat, heat with no edges. The air is at the temperature of skin or just above it, saturated, so that sweat doesn't dry and breath comes back at you off every surface. Water is everywhere without ever quite being *weather*: it beads on the undersides of the great pipes and falls in slow irregular drips, it sheets down the corrugated walls of the pumphouse, it condenses on the cold-line and runs in a permanent thin stream along the gutter cut into the rock for exactly that purpose. The whole settlement weeps. Channels of warm runoff cross the paths, bridged by single planks gone soft and dark.

Light comes through a ceiling of vapor. There is no direct sun here and there never is — the steam from the vents and the relief valves rises and flattens into a low silver lid, and the day arrives as a general dim pearliness, shadowless, directionless. At the height of noon the light is the color of weak tea. Lamps burn around the clock on the gantries, caged bulbs furred with halos, each one wearing its own small sphere of glowing fog. From the marsh edge at dusk the Works looks like a constellation drowned in milk.

The construction is dense but the place is not crowded. This is its particular character: an enormous amount of *infrastructure* and very few *things*. A turbine hall the size of a small church. A pumphouse. Three boreholes capped with riveted domes, each dome trailing its bundle of insulated lines. A water tower, a coal store standing nearly empty (the ground itself provides the heat; the coal is for the forge only), a row of workers' quarters raised on iron stilts above the runoff. Between these, long open stretches of bare black rock crossed by pipe-runs on trestles, so that walking the Works means passing under pipe after pipe after pipe, each one ticking and dripping, with fifty meters of steaming emptiness between buildings. The pipes are the streets. People navigate by them: *follow the eight-inch line past the second expansion joint, turn at the valve cluster.*

Vegetation has barely gotten a foothold and the heat discourages most of what tries. A few stands of horsetail in the warm gutters, ancient-looking, jointed, green-gray. Mats of orange and rust-colored algae in the runoff channels, thriving exactly where the water cools from scalding to merely hot, painting a thermometer in color along every drain. One tree — a single mangrove-like thing at the marsh edge, half its roots in the hot mud, stunted and tenacious, which the workers have named and will not cut.

The animals, by contrast, have decided the place is paradise. Warmth, standing water, light at night, and an endless bloom of insects: the Works hums with life it never invited. Midges in standing columns over every warm pool. Geckos — dozens, hundreds — patrolling the lamp cages and the warm pipe lagging, so habituated that they ride the maintenance crews' shoulders. Crabs the color of the basalt working the margins of the runoff channels, picking algae from the grates. Herons stand sentry on the pipe trestles at dawn, perfectly spaced, motionless as valve handles, waiting over the warm channels where small silver fish — descended, nobody knows how, from marsh stock — school in the discharge. Swallows have packed the turbine hall eaves with mud nests and fly sorties through the steam. At night, frogs in the marsh fringe perform at industrial volume, as if competing with the machines.

And the machines are loud. Not violently — there are no crashes, no irregular bangs — but totally, ambiently, *foundationally* loud. The triple-expansion engine in the turbine hall has been turning at the same speed for longer than anyone present has worked here, a deep four-part rhythm you feel through the rock, *whum-whum-whum-whum*, the heartbeat of the flow. Over it, the constant white hiss of the relief vents; under it, the bass percolation of the boreholes themselves. Steam traps fire at intervals so regular that workers time their tea by them. Conversation happens close-up, or in the lee of buildings, or in the established quiet spots that everyone knows — behind the coal store, under the water tower — small acoustic shelters in a landscape of sound.

What strikes a visitor most, in the end, is the stillness inside all that motion. Nothing here changes. The steam plume rises at the same angle it rose yesterday. The pressure gauges hold their needles as if painted on. The ground gives heat at a constant rate and the engine takes it at a constant rate and the condensate runs off at a constant rate, and the whole place sits in a balance so perfect it feels less like industry than like geology — as if the Works were not built on the flow but *grown from* it, the rock's own way of breathing. Shifts change, herons rotate, the algae renews its orange mats exactly as fast as the heat strips them. The works manager keeps a log, and the log is the same page over and over.

The basalt is sharp, the machinery is old, and somehow they are the same age: a place finished early and holding, dripping, droning, warm in the dim silver light, forever at the top of its breath.
