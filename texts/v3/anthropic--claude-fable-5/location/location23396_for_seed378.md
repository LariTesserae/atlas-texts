**The Terraces at Caldn**

The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky stops being a color and becomes a pressure — a white-blue weight overhead, sun so direct it seems to come from all sides at once. Below it, the land steps down in wide mineral terraces, hundreds of them, each holding a sheet of water the temperature of blood or hotter. The terraces are travertine: bone-white at their rims, then ivory, then a pale scalded gold where the water deepens, then — in the hottest basins — a startling chemical turquoise, opaque as paint. Nothing green grows here. Nothing has, for as long as the stone remembers. The plateau makes its own crust instead, a slow lithic gardening, each pool depositing its rim a hair's width per year.

Steam rises everywhere but does not hiss. That is the first thing a visitor fails to understand: the silence. The air is so saturated, so heavy and warm, that sound seems to drown a few feet from its source. The great pools simmer without bubbling — heat moves through them in slow visible welts, the surface flexing like the back of something breathing. When water spills from one terrace to the next, it does so in thin films over smooth lips of stone, and a film of water makes almost no noise at all. The whole landscape pours constantly and says nothing.

The town is built into this, and is being unbuilt by it at the same rate.

Caldn is not dense. Its structures stand far apart, scattered across the terrace-field the way boulders are scattered — a pumphouse here, a condensation tower a quarter mile off, a long low foundry on the third rim down, its roofline soft with mineral bloom. The builders learned early that anything placed near the water gets claimed: the travertine creeps up walls, fattens around pipe-junctions, seals doors that stand shut too long. So the town keeps its distance from itself. Each building is an island of dark iron and pale-crusted brick in an expanse of white stone and steaming water, connected to its neighbors by elevated pipe-runs and plank causeways propped on stilts that have to be re-cut free every season.

The industry here is the heat itself. Caldn farms pressure. Borewells driven into the plateau's hot heart feed steam to engines that run almost frictionlessly and almost soundlessly — great horizontal pistons in oil baths, flywheels ten feet across turning behind fogged glass, everything sealed against the corrosive air. The loudest noise in the foundry is a soft periodic exhalation, like a sleeper, as a relief valve breathes into a condensing chamber. The town's lights — and it is electrified, sparsely, a string of lamps along the high causeways that burn even at noon as markers in the steam — draw from turbines no one has needed to open in a generation. The machinery has reached the same accommodation with the place as the buildings have: half-entombed, mineral-jacketed, running anyway. Some of the older engines are so thickly crusted they look like geological features that happen to rotate.

For a place without a single leaf, it is densely, almost embarrassingly alive.

The food chain begins as color. Every pool below scalding temperature wears mats of thermophile film — orange, rust, ochre, a deep arterial red in the cooler shallows — laid down in streaming ribbons where the water flows. These mats are pasture. Pale flat crustaceans the size of a thumbnail graze them in herds of thousands, visible as a slow gray shimmer along the pool margins. Above them, the air carries a permanent drifting population of glasswings: small insects whose larvae live in the hot films and whose adults rise off the water in soft silent columns, catching the high sun so that the steam itself appears to glitter. Squat, smooth-skinned amphibian things — locals call them kettle-dogs, though they are neither — lie half-submerged at the pool rims with only their nostrils and the pale ridges of their backs showing, mineral-frosted, snapping glasswings out of the air with a motion too fast to make sound. Long-legged wading birds, white as the stone, stalk the terrace lips and are nearly invisible until they move. Everything here is quiet not by accident but by adaptation; in air this thick, this hot, stillness is the economy and the camouflage both.

The strangeness of Caldn is gradual, the kind you notice on the second day. The kettle-dogs that bask near the foundry outflows have begun to mineralize while alive — their dorsal ridges genuinely stone, grown like coral, shed and regrown. The oldest causeways, abandoned and resorbed, are now visible inside the travertine as ghost-lines, plank-shapes and ladder-shapes in cross-section where a terrace lip has broken, the town's own past held in the rock like fossils of a thing not yet dead. And the engines' slow exhalations, the only deliberate sounds in the basin, have synchronized over the decades — no one tuned them to do this — so that every few minutes the whole scattered town breathes once, together, a single hushed sigh moving across a mile of white terraces, and the wading birds all lift their heads at the same moment, and then everything settles, and the water keeps pouring its soundless films from rim to rim, and the steam goes up bright and silent into the enormous light.

People live here, a few hundred, in raised houses with louvered walls, their skin gone soft from the perpetual humidity, their voices habitually low. They will tell you the plateau is not hostile. It is simply patient, and it is fair: it gives heat, light, power, and abundance, and in exchange it slowly makes everything — pipe, plank, engine, animal — into more of itself.
