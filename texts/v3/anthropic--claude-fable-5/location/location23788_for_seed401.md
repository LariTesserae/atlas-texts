# The Void Behind Pillar Nine

There is a gap in the underworks of the flood barrier — a cavity between the new pour and the old retaining wall, roughly the size of a garden shed, that appears on no drawing. The concrete around it is young enough that it is still exhaling: the walls sweat lime, and everywhere the seepage runs, thin white straws of calthemite have begun to grow, brittle and sharp as first teeth. Nothing in here has been softened yet. Every edge is a formwork edge, every drip-line a fresh decision. The cavity is perhaps three years old. It is already a city.

You would find it — if you found it at all — by sound. Long before the access panel, through half a meter of aggregate, comes a noise like a sewing machine arguing with a kettle: rapid, percussive, overlapping rhythms, punctuated by the deep intermittent *whumm* of water hammer in the municipal main that passes through the void's ceiling like a god's forearm. Inside, the noise is total. The chamber is a resonating box. Everything built in it is built loud, because nothing here has ever known quiet and nothing is designed for it.

The light is the color of a dying appliance. Most of it comes from a single hairline fault in the conduit sleeve overhead, where a live cable's insulation has been patiently, deliberately abraded — not enough to fail, just enough to leak. From this wound the inhabitants have run their grid: filaments of scavenged copper finer than hair, strung in slack catenaries across the dark, each one beaded with condensation, each one terminating in a lamp the size of a lentil. The lamps flicker constantly. The voltage is stolen and unstable and the city has simply adapted its metabolism to brownout. Between the lit nodes lie long reaches of true dark — the city is not dense, it is *archipelagic*, five or six bright industrial clusters separated by black moss-plains and standing water, and the darkness between them is not empty so much as unclaimed. Yet. Claims here last about a week.

The builders are isopods. Or they were, or they mostly are — the taxonomy has gotten complicated. Woodlice the size of thumbnails, pale from generations in the dark, their segmented backs scored with tool-marks and, on the older ones, fitted with riveted plates of foil and zinc. They tend the machines. The machines are the strange part: assemblages of watch gears, valve springs, solder blobs, and grown calthemite, driven by drip-wheels — tiny overshot waterwheels mounted under every weeping point in the ceiling, so that the constant percolation of the barrier above is harvested drop by drop into rotation, rotation into hammering, hammering into product. What the product *is* remains unclear. The smelters — thimble-sized crucibles heated on the leaking cable — run day and night, pouring beads of reclaimed metal that are carried off into the dark spans and, as far as anyone can tell, buried.

The wet is everywhere and the city is organized entirely around it. The floor of the void slopes toward a sump in the northeast corner, a black pool maybe a meter across, its surface never still — it receives the runoff of the whole chamber and it thrums with the water hammer, standing waves crossing it in shifting interference patterns. Springtails graze its margins in glittering thousands, scattering like thrown sand whenever a wheel-house upstream releases its tailwater. Amphipods have colonized the sump itself. Something larger lives at its bottom; the isopods have built a crane over the pool and lower offerings, or bait, or rent.

The vegetation should not be possible in this light and is possible anyway. The lamps, dim as they are, never go fully out, and the moss has learned them. Thick pelts of it — luminous-green in the flicker, nearly black in the gaps — climb every surface the lime-water hasn't sealed. Liverworts fan out under the drip-lines like spread hands. Where a lamp burns steadily, the moss around it grows in concentric rings of increasing hunger, and the isopods farm these rings, cropping them in rotation, hauling the cuttings on rail-carts made of hypodermic tubing. Pale fungi rise between the settlements in the dark spans: tall, thin, sudden, gone within days. The whole ecology runs cool — the void holds at cellar temperature, and everything moves with the deliberate speed of cold-blooded industry except when it doesn't.

Because the defining condition of this place is that it will not hold still. The barrier above is settling. Every few days a stress-crack whispers open somewhere in the ceiling and a new seep begins, and a new seep means a new drip-wheel site, and a new wheel site means a district picks itself up — literally, the structures are made to be unbolted — and migrates across the dark to colonize it. Old districts are cannibalized within hours, stripped to their calthemite stubs, which keep growing anyway, blind white spires marking where industry used to be, the youngest ruins imaginable: ruins with sharp edges, ruins still under construction by mineral processes that never got the news. Twice since the void formed, the sump has jumped its banks entirely and drowned a quarter of the city, and both times the survivors rebuilt within the week, higher, louder, on stilts of insulated wire.

Stand at the access panel — if you ever find the access panel — and what you see is this: five islands of shivering light in a black wet room, moss burning green at their edges, a thousand small armored bodies in motion, waterwheels ticking at every ceiling-wound, the stolen-current lamps browning out in slow waves, the main overhead delivering its enormous irregular heartbeat, and out in the dark between settlements, single lights moving — prospectors, or scouts, or heretics — following the newest crack, dragging their machinery behind them toward the next drip, the next district, the next version of the city, which is already being argued into existence at full volume in the dark.
