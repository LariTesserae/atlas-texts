# The Rootworks

You come down to it the way water does — by seepage, through a slot in the hillside that was never cut so much as permitted, a gap between two buttress roots each thicker than a church door. The descent is short. This is not a deep place. It sits just under the skin of the world, the way a splinter sits, or a town.

The chamber is the size of a hamlet, no more. You could walk its longest axis in two hundred slow steps, if walking here were ever slow, which it isn't, because the floor hums.

Everything here is one plant. That is the first rule of the Rootworks, and the oldest. The walls are root. The vaulted ceiling — low at the edges, rising to maybe four storeys at the crown — is root, woven and rewoven over so long that the weave has fused, the way braided hair will felt if never undone. The floor is root planed flat by centuries of traffic, polished to a dark amber where the lanes run, still ridged and barked at the margins where nothing walks. There is no stone visible anywhere. There may be no stone at all. The plant has eaten the cellar it was given and become the cellar, and somewhere far above, in a daylight this chamber has never seen, there is presumably a canopy, a trunk, a weather — but down here that is theology, not fact.

What stands inside the chamber is sparse, and the sparseness is deliberate, maintained, mowed. A dozen structures, scattered with room to breathe between them: round-shouldered buildings that were grown rather than built, hollow burls the size of granaries, drawn up out of the floor generations ago by some art of girdling and patience, then fitted out with what could not be grown — iron, brass, glass, belt-leather. Each burl-house is a workshop. From each one comes a different register of the noise.

Because the place is loud. Loud the way a mill is loud, the way a hive is loud, both at once and impossible to parse into either. The deepest layer is the plant itself: a vascular groan, water and sugar moving through ducts wide as drainpipes inside the walls, a sound like a slow river heard through a closed door, never stopping, swelling slightly when rain falls on the surface world — which you learn to hear as a change in the walls' tone, a sweetening, hours before the condensation arrives. Above that, the machinery: flywheels, trip-hammers, the chuff of a sap-pressure engine, because the works here run on the tree's own circulation. Tapped lines of pressurized sap drive pistons. Bladder-valves of grown membrane open and shut with a wet percussive *thock* that the whole chamber keeps time to. Nobody fires a boiler in the Rootworks. The boiler is the hill.

And above *that* — woven through everything, the true voice of the place — the animals.

The fauna here is not a population. It is a condition. The walls are riddled with bore-channels, and the channels are never empty; put your palm flat against any surface and you feel traffic, a steady freight of bodies moving through the wood like blood cells, grubs and millipedes and pale shrews and things with too many names or none. They surface constantly: a wall will pucker, extrude a glistening beetle the size of a boot, and reseal behind it. Roosting bats fur the upper vaults so thickly that the ceiling appears upholstered, and when the shift-bell rings — a grown bell, a hollow gall struck by a sap-driven hammer — they pour down through the chamber in a single banking sheet, harvesting the midges that rise off the condensation pools, and for thirty seconds the whole hamlet is inside a moving body, and then it isn't.

The works depend on them. This is the second rule. The lathe-shed runs its finest spindle off a wheel turned by armored rollers — woodlice, more or less, bred up to the size of loaves, who walk the wheel for the privilege of the fungus paste smeared on its rim. Moths with wings like wet slate carry message-scrolls between burls along scent-lines painted on the ceiling. There is a creature in the southernmost workshop, behind the membrane door, that is spoken of as *the gauge*: it tastes the sap lines and its color tells the pressure, and it has held its post, or its lineage has, longer than any of the families who tend it. Nothing here was tamed recently. Every arrangement has the smoothness of a thing negotiated so long ago that both parties have forgotten it was ever otherwise.

The light is borrowed and dim. A few daylight-pipes — bored channels lined with something pearled, a cultivated lining the plant produces if injured in just the right way — bring down a grey, directionless glow from the surface, brighter at noon, gone by evening. The rest is lamp-flies in wicker cages, paid in syrup, and the low ember-orange of the sap engines' friction housings. Shadows in the Rootworks are warm and brown and total at the edges, and the sparse buildings float in them like islands, each one its own pool of work-light, its own pitch of noise, with long, soft darknesses between where the only thing happening is the wall, breathing freight.

It is warm. The plant runs warm — fermentation heat, metabolic heat — and the air sits at the temperature of a body, humid in pulses. Twice a day, roughly, the chamber sweats: condensation beads on every cool surface, runs down the iron, pools in the worn places of the floor, and is gone within hours, drunk back through the planks of the floor itself. The puddles are the closest thing to weather here, and the midges hatch from them, and the bats eat the midges, and the bats' guano feeds the fungus paste, and the paste pays the wheel-walkers, and the wheel turns the spindle, and the spindle is making something — it is always making something — for a surface world the chamber knows only as a flavor in the rain.

A place this small should not feel inexhaustible. It does anyway. Stand in the dark between two burl-houses, hand on the wall, and you can feel that the hamlet you can see is the least part of it — a clearing the organism keeps open, the way a body keeps a wound open if the wound is useful. Everything else, in every direction, is the living mass: traveled, inhabited, working, loud, and just under the surface of the world, and just under the surface of itself.
