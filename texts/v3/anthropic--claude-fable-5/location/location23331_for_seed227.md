# The Roaring Room

You smell it before you hear it, and you hear it long before you see it: a cold mineral breath rising out of the ground, and a sound that begins as pressure in the chest somewhere back among the trees, a half-mile off, where the land starts its long slow tilt downward.

The descent is gradual. This is low country, old country — a forest floor of root-buckled limestone that has been settling into itself for so long that nothing about it is sharp anymore. Every edge has been licked round by ten thousand years of rain and frost. The trees are not tall but they are dense in their lower stories, hazel and cold-stunted alder and an undergrowth so thick with fern and moss that the ground reads as green even in the dim — and it is always dim here, the canopy and the perpetual haze conspiring to keep the light at the level of a long dusk. Moss climbs the trunks to head height. It grows on fallen things and standing things alike, on the occasional waist-high cairn of stacked stones that someone built long ago to mark the way down, each cairn now just a green mound with the suggestion of intention underneath.

Then the ground opens.

It is not a gorge so much as a mouth — a sinkhole the size of a large room, maybe forty feet across, its rim soft with overhanging turf and dripping ferns, and from it the sound comes up like something solid. Down inside, a river that runs invisible through the forest above finds its way into the earth, and it does not go quietly. It falls.

The descent into the mouth is by steps. They are the most deliberate thing in the landscape: shallow treads hacked into the limestone with stone or antler tools, each one cupped and slick from age and footfall and the endless film of condensation, worn so smooth in the center that they hold little lenses of water. Someone maintained these once. Twists of rotted fiber rope still hang from a few wooden pegs driven into cracks — handholds, mostly gone. Halfway down there is a niche cut into the wall, soot-blackened, the size of two cupped hands: a place where a lamp once burned, fat and wick. The soot has been there longer than any living memory of who put it there.

And then the chamber.

It is small. That is the first surprise — that something this loud could fit in a space this intimate. The room behind the fall is perhaps the size of a modest barn, a single pocket of hollow in the rock, and it is almost entirely empty. Black air, black water, the pale curtain of the cataract sealing one wall like a sheet of moving glass faintly lit from above, where the daylight of the dim forest leaks down the throat of the sinkhole and dies a few feet in. Everything else is darkness — not menacing darkness, just the ordinary thorough dark of underground — and the emptiness is total in a way few places are. No clutter. No debris. The water has spent geological ages sweeping this room clean. The floor is polished stone sloping to a plunge pool whose depth no one has measured, and the walls are scalloped into smooth fluted curves, the signature of slow water working at limestone with infinite patience. The room is finished. It has been in its final shape for a very long time and is in no hurry to change.

The sound, in here, stops being sound. It becomes the medium you exist in. The fall is not especially huge — twenty feet, maybe twenty-five — but the chamber is a resonating body, and the roar comes off every surface at once, layered with itself, a white thunder so complete that it erases speech, erases your own heartbeat, erases the difference between hearing and feeling. You can put your hand flat on the wall and feel the room humming. People who came here — and people did come here, that much is written in the steps and the soot and the faint ochre handprints pressed onto a dry shelf of wall above the spray line, a small constellation of them, adult and child — people who came here came knowing they would be deafened, came perhaps *for* the deafness, for the way the roar empties the head out the way the water emptied the room.

The vegetation does not stop at the rim. It reaches down. Wherever the spray drifts and a thread of light follows, the rock is upholstered in life: deep wet bolsters of moss, liverworts in glossy black-green sheets, filmy ferns one cell thick that can only live in air this saturated. Around the bright throat of the entrance the growth is extravagant, dripping, almost tropical in its density and arctic in its temperature — the water is snowmelt-cold year round, the air a few degrees above freezing, and the plants here are the patient kind, the kind that add a millimeter a year and have been adding it since the ice left. The green fades into the dark in bands, lush to sparse to nothing, like a tide line of light.

Living things are scarce and singular. A white-throated dipper nests somewhere behind the curtain of the fall itself — you will see it once, maybe, a small dark bird walking calmly into the thunder. Pale crayfish, eyeless or nearly so, in the cold margins of the pool. In autumn, eels come up the underground river and can sometimes be seen sliding over the wet lip of the fall like animated cables. A few bats use a high crack in the ceiling, leaving and returning through the sinkhole's mouth at hours when the dimness above qualifies as day or night. That is nearly the whole census. The roar keeps most things away; what lives here lives here because of it, in the safety of a place too loud for anything to hunt by ear.

Change comes, but slowly, on the schedule of the rock. The fall is fractionally retreating, undercutting itself a hair's breadth a decade. The plunge pool deepens at a rate no instrument would bother with. After a week of rain the curtain thickens and whitens and the roar climbs a register; in deep winter the spray builds glassy collars of ice on the moss and the sound dampens, going from roar to a vast muffled drumming. These are the seasons of the room. Otherwise it simply continues — dark, cold, drenched, nearly empty, almost unbearably loud, finished long ago and faithfully repeating itself, with a worn stair going down into it and a few hands pressed in ochre to say that someone, once, stood in all that thunder and wanted to be remembered there.
