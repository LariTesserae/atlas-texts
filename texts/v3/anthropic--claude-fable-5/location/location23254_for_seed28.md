# The Choir Pit

You find it by ear long before you find it by eye. Out on the dry mineral flat — a plain of fawn-colored stone polished by millennia of wind into something like the skin of an enormous drum — there is a sound that begins as a pressure behind the teeth and becomes, as you walk toward it, a roar. Not a roar like water. Nothing here is like water. A roar like a thousand reed pipes blown at once, sustained, layered, beating against itself in slow interference patterns that you feel in your sternum more than hear.

Then the ground simply opens.

The pit is not large. Perhaps two hundred paces across, a rounded depression sunk into the plain like a thumbprint pressed into warm wax, its rim worn smooth and lipless so that the flat seems to pour gently down into it. You could walk past it at a quarter mile and never know. From the air it would read as a fleck. But it is *deep* enough — sixty, eighty feet of descent in soft terraces — and the whole interior is a city.

A city built by hand, and a city built to scream.

---

## The Architecture

Whoever raised it had no metal worth the name. The construction is dry-stacked stone and a fired pale brick, kiln-glazed to a buttery sheen, each one bearing the dimples of fingers. The walls lean inward at angles that took generations to learn. There are no straight streets. Every lane is a curve, every curve is a horn: the city is laid out as a system of resonating channels, alleys that narrow and flare like the bores of woodwinds, courtyards domed into kettles, towers that are nothing but vertical flues, open at both ends, their inner surfaces fluted with hand-carved ridges.

The wind does the rest.

The dry plain above breathes constantly — heat rising off the stone all day drives a slow ceaseless wind across the pit's mouth, and the pit drinks it. Air pours down the western terraces, threads the streets, compresses in the narrows, and is forced shrieking up through the tower-flues on the eastern side. The whole settlement is a single instrument that has been playing without pause for longer than anyone can say. Stand in the central court at noon and the sound is genuinely deafening — a wall of overlapping drones, the deepest of them below hearing, rattling loose grit off the windowsills, the highest a silver edge that makes your eyes water. The masonry is worn glossy along the streamlines, polished by centuries of air the way other cities are polished by rain.

Some of the worn places are clearly *tuned*. You can see where stones have been replaced, where a flue's lip has been re-carved a finger's width wider. The city was maintained, once, the way you'd maintain a flute. Kept in pitch.

---

## The Green

There is no water. There has never been water — no gutters, no cisterns, no channels, none of the architecture of thirst. And yet the city is lush.

Everything grows on the sound.

The walls are upholstered in a flora that drinks vibration. Thick pelts of what looks like moss but is closer to felt — dense mats of mineral-fibered filaments, each filament a hair-fine crystal whisker that trembles in the roar and converts the trembling, by some patient chemistry, into growth. Where the drone is loudest, the felt grows deepest: the resonant alleys are furred a hand-span thick in colors of saffron, oxblood, and a green so dark it's nearly violet. The tower-flues, the loudest places in the city, are choked with hanging gardens of it, long banners of growth streaming in the updraft, fattening on the very shriek they soften.

Larger forms stand in the courtyards: bell-trees, the locals must have called them, if there were locals — rigid trunks of layered resin, branching into wide parabolic leaves, every leaf a dish aimed at the nearest source of sound. They flex slowly through the day, tracking the city's shifting harmonics the way other trees track the sun. They are warm to the touch and smell of cinnamon and hot dust. When a strong gust changes the city's chord, the whole grove leans in unison, creaking, and you understand suddenly that the vegetation is not decoration. It is the congregation. The city sings, and the green came to listen, and stayed.

---

## The Emptiness

And here is the strangeness that takes longest to settle: the city is full of rooms and empty of everything else.

Hundreds of doorways. Stairs worn into hollows by feet. Hand-smoothed thresholds, niches at shoulder height in every house where something was once kept. But the houses hold nothing — not bones, not pots, not tools beyond a few stone scrapers fused into the felt-moss like flies in amber. The streets between the gardens are bare, bright, swept clean by the perpetual wind. You can walk for an hour and the only motion you'll see, besides the leaning trees and the streaming moss-banners, is light.

Because the light here is ferocious. The pit faces the open sky and the pale brick throws the sun back from every surface; the city at midday is almost too bright to look at directly, a bowl of glare, the glazed walls shimmering, the resin trees glinting like poured glass. Shadows exist only as thin blue lines at the bases of walls, and even those hum.

Fauna is nearly a rumor. Once a day, maybe, something crosses the sky-mouth of the pit — a single broad-winged shape that rides the rising shriek of the eastern flues, spirals once on the thermal voice, and is gone. Down in the alleys live small armored things the size of a knuckle, the color of the brick, which press themselves flat against the loudest walls and feed, it seems, the same way the moss does. They do not flee from you. Nothing here has needed to flee for a very long time.

---

## The Rule of the Place

You come to understand the rule slowly, the way the pit teaches everything: through the body, through the bones.

The city was built to make this sound, and the sound now keeps the city. The drone shakes dust from the joints before it can settle and seed cracks. The vibration-fed gardens shade the brick and cushion the gusts and, you begin to suspect, were cultivated for exactly this — planted as the builders' last act, a green caretaker that would never sleep and never thirst. The architecture feeds the wind, the wind feeds the sound, the sound feeds the gardens, the gardens preserve the architecture. A closed loop, hand-made, running on nothing but dry air and sunlight, in balance for centuries.

Whoever built it did not die out, you decide, standing in the central court near dusk as the cooling air drops the city's chord a slow half-step, the towers sliding downward in pitch like the whole settlement exhaling. They finished. They built a thing that could keep itself, tuned it, and walked up the terraces onto the bright plain, leaving the pit to sing its one endless note into the empty sky.

It is singing still. It will be singing tomorrow. You can stand in it for only so long before the sound stops being sound and becomes a place you are inside of — and then you climb out onto the silent flat, ears ringing, and the pit closes behind you into the plain, hidden again, a roar shrinking to a pressure, a pressure to a whisper, a whisper to a thumbprint in warm stone.
