# The Foundry Flats

The basin runs low and wide between two old hills that have been worn down to the patience of loaves. From either rim you can see most of it at once — a region of pale stone, miles of it, the color of unfired ceramic, dimpled and channeled and polished by an age of weather into shapes that no longer change. Whatever this place was going to become, it became it long ago. Now it simply continues.

There are no plants. There never seem to have been; the rock holds no fossils of leaf or root, only its own mineral grammar — flowstone, terrace, sinter, glaze. The ground is warm underfoot, not hot, the warmth of an engine block hours after shutdown, except that nothing here has ever shut down. Heat rises from below in a slow, even, perpetual exhale.

And the basin *works*.

This is the first thing a traveler understands, before understanding what they mean by it. Scattered across the flats — never crowded, a quarter-mile of bare stone between one and the next — are the vents. Each is a low mineral cone or a fissure rimmed in chemical color, ochre and bone-white and a green like old copper, and each one is doing a job. One chuffs steam in heavy double-beats, *thump-hiss, thump-hiss*, the rhythm of a piston that has found its cruising speed. Another, half a mile off, releases a long pressurized shriek every few minutes, exact as a shift whistle. Pits of grey mud turn over and over with the thick, gulping churn of a mixer that is never emptied. Somewhere out of sight, a deep percussive knocking carries through the stone itself — felt in the soles of the feet before it's heard — like a forge working at the bottom of the world.

There is no factory. There has never been a factory. No wall, no pipe, no bolt, no hand has ever shaped anything here. The basin built its own machinery out of pressure and water and stone, and runs it without product, without purpose, without pause. The sound is industrial in every register — hiss, clang, churn, whistle, the white roar of escaping steam — loud enough that two people must lean close to speak, and it has been this loud, in exactly this arrangement, for longer than anything that could remember.

The timing is the unsettling part. Not that the vents fire — geysers fire everywhere — but that they fire *in order*. Stand still for an hour and the sequence reveals itself: the double-beat cone, then the whistle, then the far knocking answers twice, then the mud rolls over with a sound like swallowing, and the cycle returns to its top. No drift. No variation. Travelers who camp on the rim report waking at night to the same passage of sounds they fell asleep to, in the same key, as though the basin were a single instrument playing the only piece it knows, forever, at full volume.

Water is present the way grease is present in a machine — functional, distributed, never abundant. Condensation beads on the cooler stone in the lee of every cone and runs down in thin permanent trickles that have cut their channels finger-deep over the millennia and will cut them no deeper. Shallow pools collect in the terraces, blood-warm, faintly mineral-sweet to the smell, ringed in white. When rain comes, it comes briefly and vanishes into the warm rock within the hour, steaming up again from a thousand pores, recycled back into the work. Mist stands in the hollows on still mornings and burns off by the time the light goes full and ordinary — clear plain daylight, neither golden nor strange, as if the sky declines to comment.

Life is nearly absent, but not quite. A kind of flat, leathery creature — something between a salamander and a question — lives in the warm seeps, slate-colored, eyeless, the length of a hand. It moves only when the deep knocking sounds, one small advance per beat, and is otherwise still as the stone it resembles. And once or twice a season a lone bird crosses the basin high up, never landing, its call swallowed instantly by the machinery below. Whether anything here eats, or what, no one has stayed long enough to learn. The noise wears visitors down. The sameness wears them down faster.

Because that is the basin's real character, beneath the racket: equilibrium so total it approaches stillness. The steam rises in the same plumes to the same heights. The mud turns over and is the same mud. The channels are cut and will not deepen; the cones are built and will not grow. Erosion finished its argument with this place ages ago and the two of them settled. What remains is a vast, warm, roaring, empty workshop with no workers and no output and no clock — or rather, a place that *is* a clock, keeping perfect time, telling it to no one.

Leave by either hill at dusk and the sound follows you up the slope, organizing itself with distance into something almost like music: thump-hiss, whistle, knock, knock, swallow. Look back and the basin lies pale in the last ordinary light, breathing steam from its scattered mouths, mid-shift, as always, forever.
