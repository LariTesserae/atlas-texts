**The Drone Garden**

There is a crack near the summit of the mountain — a place so high the sky has gone the color of old slate and breathing feels like a transaction you must negotiate — and if you turn sideways and let the rock scrape both shoulders for eleven steps, you come out into a room.

It is not large. Thirty paces across, perhaps, roofed by a slab of leaning granite that lets in light only through a seam along its eastern edge: a long thin blade of grey illumination, dim as the inside of an eyelid, falling always at the same angle because at this height, in this hollow, the weather has stopped having opinions. The temperature holds at a clean, mineral cool — the cool of a cellar, of a tool left in shade — and it has held there, by every available sign, for a very long time.

The room is *full*. That is the first thing. There is no floor visible, no bare wall, no negotiable space. Every surface is occupied by growth, and the growth is occupied by further growth, epiphytes on epiphytes, until the chamber is less a space containing plants than a single vegetable mass with corridors of air worn through it.

And none of it has ever known water.

The plants here are dry the way a violin is dry. Their leaves are thin laminae of silica and cellulose, translucent at the edges, dust-colored shading into deep slate-greens and bruised purples in the dimness. Their stems are hollow. Their roots do not dig for moisture — there is none, has never been any; the rock here is anhydrous to its heart, and the air arrives already wrung out by four kilometers of climbing — instead the roots wrap and clutch and *press*, gripping the stone and each other like fingers around a railing.

Because what they drink is the sound.

In the center of the chamber, half-swallowed by growth, stands the engine. It is old. Whoever set it here built it from riveted iron and brass and a kind of dark ceramic, and they built it to run, and it runs. A flywheel taller than a person turns inside a husk of vines at a speed that has not varied in living memory — though nothing living here has memory, strictly speaking — fed by something geothermal or chemical or simply *committed*, deep in the shaft beneath it. Its housing is furred with oxide and grown over so thoroughly that only its gauges remain visible, kept clear, it seems, by the plants themselves, the way one might keep a window clean. The needles do not move. They have settled into their readings the way the mountain settled into its shape.

The engine makes one note.

It is a deep, layered drone — a fundamental somewhere down near the floor of hearing, with a stack of harmonics rising off it like heat off a road — and it is *loud*, genuinely loud, a sound you feel in the sternum and the sinuses, a sound that replaces silence so totally that after a few minutes you cannot remember what silence proposed to offer. The hollow leaves of every plant in the chamber are tuned to it. They resonate. Each leaf is a tiny sounding-board, each hollow stem an organ pipe, and the whole packed garden rings in sympathy with the engine, taking its single note and refracting it into a chord — a vast, motionless, unchanging chord that fills the room wall to wall the way the vegetation does, with no room left over.

This is how they live. The vibration shakes mineral dust loose from the granite, micron by micron, and the leaves catch it on their charged surfaces and ferry it inward along grooves worn smooth by repetition. The plants metabolize stone and sound. They photosynthesize, barely, on the one blade of dim light, supplementing it with the engine's faint electrical leakage, which crawls visibly along certain stems on the darkest days as a thin blue thread. It is a closed economy, austere and total, and it has been balanced so long that nothing in it can be called young or old anymore. The plants do not visibly grow. They do not visibly die. They have arrived, like the gauges, at their readings.

Here and there the makers left other marks: a catwalk of grated iron, now a trellis; a tin lamp housing, bulbless, packed solid with moss-analogue the color of verdigris; a ladder bolted to the wall that ascends into growth so dense it simply stops mattering halfway up; a single enameled sign, lettering long since abraded to ghosts, which the vines hold at a respectful distance — the only courtesy extended to anything in the room. There are no bones, no insects, no shed feathers, no webs, no droppings, no tracks. Nothing has ever eaten anything here, or fled from anything, or sung. The drone is the only voice, and it is not a voice; it is a condition.

Strangeness accumulates at the edges of attention. The shadows do not move with you quite correctly — the dimness is so even, so sourceless away from the eastern seam, that your own shadow seems negotiated rather than cast. If you press your palm flat against a broad leaf, you feel the note enter your hand and you will swear, afterward, that for a moment you understood something the plants understand — something about patience as a material, about how a single sustained tone, held long enough, becomes indistinguishable from quiet.

When you leave — sideways again, eleven scraping steps, out into the enormous thin grey altitude — the drone cuts off at the crack's mouth as if a door closed. The silence outside is a shock, vertiginous, almost rude. Behind you, in the rock, the chord goes on. It was going on before you came. It does not register that you were there. The needles hold their readings; the flywheel turns at its one speed; the dim blade of light lies across the leaves at its permanent angle; and the garden, packed and dry and ringing, continues being exactly, exhaustively itself.
