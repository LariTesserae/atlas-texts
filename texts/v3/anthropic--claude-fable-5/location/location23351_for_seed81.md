**The Condensing City**

You come up onto the plateau through cloud, and for a while it is impossible to say whether you have arrived anywhere at all. The air at this altitude should be thin and cold; instead it is thick, mineral-sweet, and hot — a heat that has nothing to do with the sun, which appears, when it appears at all, as a pale coin behind layers of vapor. The light here is permanently crepuscular. Not dark. Dim, the way the inside of a kettle would be dim if you could stand in one.

Then the first stack resolves out of the haze, and you understand that the city has been around you for some time.

---

**Terrain**

The plateau is volcanic basement rock — old, settled, done arguing with itself. Whatever violence raised it happened so long ago that the stone has gone smooth and dark and slightly soapy to the touch, polished by millennia of condensation running over it. The ground weeps. Everywhere, water beads out of the rock itself, fed by steam rising through hairline fractures from the hot strata below, condensing in the pores of the stone, emerging in slow tears that gather into rivulets, into runnels, into the broad shallow channels that thread the entire city like a circulatory system.

Nothing grows. The heat coming up through the ground sterilizes it; the water that runs everywhere is too hot, too laden with dissolved silica and sulfur, to host anything green. Instead the rock blooms with *mineral* growth — terraces of travertine like frozen foam, sinter aprons in white and rust and a strange muted gold, chimneys of precipitate that have built themselves drip by drip over centuries until they stand taller than the buildings they lean against. In dim light they look almost organic, almost like coral, and you have to remind yourself that they are only chemistry being patient.

**The city**

It is large — it takes the better part of a day to cross — and it is built with the confidence of people who expected to stay. Heavy masonry, basalt block and fired ceramic, walls a meter thick, everything sloped and guttered and channeled because here architecture is mostly the art of managing water that falls *upward*. Roofs are inverted: great shallow funnels of glazed tile that catch the condensate of their own rising steam and feed it down through internal pipes. Every building drinks itself.

The construction is industrial in temperament. There are foundries here, or things shaped like foundries: long halls with rows of squat iron engines, flywheels three stories tall, networks of brass and ceramic piping that climb the walls and cross the streets overhead in dense canopies — the only canopy this place has ever had. The whole city was built to run on the heat below it. Steam is its coal, its river, its weather, and its god.

And here is the strange part, the thing that takes a day or two to fully believe: much of it is still running.

Not all of it. Not most of it. But walk any street long enough and you will pass a building exhaling — a measured pulse of vapor from a roof vent, regular as breathing, eight seconds in, eight seconds out. Flywheels turn behind grimed windows, slowly, under no load, governed by mechanisms that bleed off exactly as much pressure as the ground provides. The city was engineered to idle indefinitely, and it does. Pipes knock softly as they expand and contract with the day's faint thermal tide. Somewhere — never findable, always two streets over — a pump cycles on, runs for a minute with a sound like a heart heard through a wall, and cycles off.

**Emptiness**

There is no one. The scale of the absence is the dominant fact of the place. Avenues sixty meters wide, built for crowds and freight and procession, hold nothing but drifting steam and the dark threads of warm water running down their center channels. The vapor enforces a kind of intimacy on the emptiness: you can rarely see more than a hundred meters, so the city reveals itself one vacancy at a time. A plaza with a dry ornamental basin (dry because its feed pipe failed; ten meters away an unintended fountain weeps from a cracked main, and has wept long enough to build itself a pedestal of white stone). A stairway, two hundred steps, ascending into haze. A factory door standing open on darkness and the smell of hot metal.

Nothing suggests catastrophe. There is no rubble beyond ordinary weathering, no burning, no haste. The city simply has no people in it, the way a shell has no animal in it, and like a shell it has kept its shape.

**Sounds**

Mostly quiet — but it is a *worked* quiet, a quiet with texture. Beneath everything, so constant you stop hearing it within an hour, is the white sigh of steam moving through stone and pipe. On top of that, intermittently: drips, in ten thousand voices, each surface tuned differently. The groan of an iron span easing as the afternoon warmth shifts. Once or twice a day, from far off, a heavy concussion — a pressure valve somewhere letting go its scheduled relief, the sound rolling across the rooftops and dying in the fog. Your own footsteps sound rude.

**Fauna**

Rare, but present, and worth watching for. There are the vent-moths — pale, palm-sized, with wings like waxed paper — that live their whole lives in the warm columns above the steam stacks, riding the updrafts, never seeming to land. No one knows what they eat; possibly the mineral film itself. There is something that lives in the warm channels, glimpsed only as a smooth back breaking the water's skin, dark and unhurried, perhaps an eel, perhaps not — the channels connect to the deep plumbing of the plateau, and what lives down there has had a long time alone with the heat. And in the highest district, where the pipes converge on the old central works, you may see the dwellers' only real successor: a kind of armored thing the size of a cat, slow, shingled in plates that have the same dull gold sheen as the sinter, grazing the precipitate off the warm pipes with patient rasping motions. It does not fear you. It has no reason to have learned how.

**Rules of the place**

The city maintains itself, within limits. Scale builds inside the pipes and the pipes, by some old engineering grace, route around their own blockages; condensation cleans the glazed surfaces; the mineral water seals small cracks even as it slowly entombs the architecture that contains it. Equilibrium, not preservation: every century the city is a little more stone and a little less machine. Eventually the travertine will finish what it has started and the whole place will be one continuous pale formation, with flywheels at its heart like fossils, still turning, perhaps, in their sealed chambers.

The water that runs in the central channels is hot enough to scald. The water weeping from north-facing walls is merely warm, and tastes of iron and faintly of eggs. After what passes for nightfall — a deepening of the dimness, the coin of the sun extinguished — the temperature barely drops, but the steam thickens until the streets fill wall to wall with slow white rivers of fog, and the only navigation is by sound: the exhaling buildings, each with its own period, eight seconds, eleven, thirteen, a city-wide arrhythmia of breath that the old inhabitants, surely, could read like a map.

Stand still long enough, anywhere, and the place performs its one recurring miracle: water condenses on you. You become, briefly, part of the system — one more warm surface in the great engine of rising and falling — beaded, weeping, slowly acquiring the thinnest imaginable shell of stone.
