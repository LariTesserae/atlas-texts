**The Heat Gallery**

You climb to reach it, which seems wrong for a place this hot. Two days up the shoulder of an old shield volcano, through scree the color of dried blood and fields of glass that ring faintly underfoot, until the air thins and the cold of altitude starts working into your joints — and then the fissure, exhaling.

The breath of the mountain comes out of a crack no wider than a doorway, and it is warm the way an animal is warm. Not furnace-heat. Body-heat, magnified. The temperature of something enormous and alive, sleeping just inside.

Inside is the gallery.

---

It is not large. That's the first surprise. The stories — and there are stories, because people came here once, a long time ago — make it sound like an underworld, but it is a single chamber, roughly the size of a small church, with a vaulted ceiling of black basalt and a floor that slopes gently down toward the source of the heat: a seam in the rock, hand-wide, glowing nowhere, lit by nothing, but radiating like an open oven door. The chamber holds maybe forty paces across at its widest. You could shout across it, if shouting felt permitted. It doesn't.

There is no light. None. The seam does not glow visibly; whatever it gives off sits below the eye's threshold. You experience the gallery through your skin, which reads the room as a map of warmths — the seam blazing at the low end, the walls a gradient of fever-heat fading toward the entrance, the ceiling a dome of trapped warmth pressing down like a hand. Through your ears, which find the place almost, but not entirely, silent. And through a sense you don't have a good name for, the one that tells you, immediately and without ambiguity, that you are not alone, that you are in fact massively outnumbered, that every surface in this room is occupied.

Because it is. All of it.

---

The walls are alive. Not metaphorically — there is no exposed stone in the heat gallery, or almost none. The basalt is upholstered in a continuous layer of bodies, packed shell to shell, a tessellation of living things that begins at the floor and continues without interruption across the ceiling. When you put a hand out in the dark (everyone does, eventually, despite themselves) you do not touch rock. You touch something dry and faintly ridged, like the back of a beetle, blood-warm, and it shifts very slightly under your fingers — not in alarm, just adjusting, the way a sleeper adjusts — and the shift propagates outward through its neighbors in a slow ring, a ripple in a pond of chitin, and then settles.

There is no water here. There has never been water here; the rock is bone-dry, the air is dry enough to crack your lips within an hour, and nothing in the gallery drinks. The creatures of the heat gallery run on the mountain itself. They press their undersides to the warm stone and to each other and they take what they need from the heat gradient and from the seam's slow exhalation of sulfur and mineral vapor. They are sealed things, waxy and rind-skinned, built to lose nothing. Some of them, the old ones, the ones grown to the size of dinner plates in the prime positions nearest the seam, have not moved in living memory and have begun to be mistaken for architecture.

The variety, if you could see it, would stagger you. Travelers who brought light into the gallery — there were a few, before lights were understood to be a mistake — described flat lobed things like dry anemones, blind moths with vestigial wings folded forever, segmented climbers in spiral processions, soft-bodied grazers moving across the colony's surface and harvesting its shed skins, and slender heat-snakes, pale as candle wax, threaded through the crowd like rivers through a city. The light made everything stop. The whole room went rigid, tens of thousands of bodies at once, and stayed rigid until the light was gone, and the travelers reported that the stillness was the most frightening thing they had ever stood inside.

So you keep it dark, and the gallery keeps moving.

---

And it does move. Slowly, constantly, on the pace of digestion and tide rather than the pace of flight. The seam breathes in long cycles — hours of swelling warmth, then a slow subsidence — and the whole population migrates with it, the colony creeping floorward when the seam runs hot, drifting up the walls and across the ceiling when it cools, so that the room's living lining is in perpetual rotation, a wheel turning over days. Predation happens, but at the speed of patience: a heat-snake easing through the packed bodies for an hour to position itself, the small dry click of a shell giving way, then nothing. Births happen in the warm crevices near the seam. Deaths are absorbed; nothing is wasted in a place this dry; the grazers see to it.

The sound of all this is almost nothing, and the almost is the texture of the place. A continuous faint friction, like wind in dry grass heard through a wall. Tick. A settling. The papery whisper of ten thousand minute adjustments averaging out to a hush. Occasionally — once an hour, maybe — a larger sound: a slough of bodies losing grip on the ceiling and sliding a hand-span before catching, a soft collective rasp like a sigh. Your own breathing is the loudest thing in the room. Your heartbeat feels like a rudeness.

---

People did come here. The evidence is slight and very old. Near the entrance, where the colony thins and a little bare rock survives, there is a sill carved into the wall at sitting height — a bench, worn smooth, polished not only by the people who used it but by centuries of small bodies traversing it since. Below the bench, a ring of fitted stones, fist-sized, set into the floor with a care that has outlasted whatever was set inside the ring. A few hand-shapes pressed in old pitch on the rock above, fingers spread. Nothing else. No tools, no markings that explain anything. Whoever they were, they came up two days of cold mountain to sit in the dark in a hot room packed with living things, and they sat on that bench, and they left almost no trace, and then they stopped coming.

It is hard, sitting on the bench yourself — and you will sit on it; the bench is obviously, magnetically, the place where one sits — not to develop a theory of why. The gallery has a quality that the parameters of the place don't quite account for. The heat is animal. The dark is total. The room is full, completely full, of quiet living attention, none of it directed at you, all of it oriented toward the warm seam at the center like a congregation facing an altar. The colony's slow rotation around the heat source is, if you sit long enough, indistinguishable from devotion. You begin to feel that you have walked into the middle of a rite that started before your species did and that the rite does not mind you, has a place for you even, on the worn bench, in the warm dark, as long as you bring no light and make no noise.

When you leave — backing out through the fissure into the shock of thin cold air and the unbearable brightness of ordinary starlight — the mountain's breath follows you a few steps and then withdraws. Behind you, in the gallery, the seam swells on its slow cycle, and the whole living lining of the room turns another degree on its wheel, and the dry hush goes on, as it has, as it apparently intends to.
