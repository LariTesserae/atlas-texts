**The Pumphouse Parish**

Forty meters down, where the cold stops being a temperature and becomes a pressure on the bones, there is a town the size of a churchyard, and it is still working.

You arrive at it from above, the way everything arrives now — sinking through black water so cold it has gone glassy and slow, water that hasn't felt sunlight in living memory. The dark here is not absence. It is a material, thick and settled, and the town sits inside it the way a fossil sits in shale. The only light comes from the town itself: a scattering of pilot lamps behind quartz lenses, burning a dim orange that does not flicker so much as *breathe*, brightening and fading on a cycle of about a minute, as if something below is inhaling.

The town is small enough to cross in a hundred slow paces, if pacing were possible. A dozen structures, no more, packed shoulder to shoulder with the dense intimacy of a medieval lane: brick boiler houses, a riveted iron stack now wearing a sleeve of pale anemone-flesh, a winding-tower whose headframe wheel still turns — very slowly, perhaps one revolution an hour — drawing a cable down into a shaft that goes deeper than the dark. Pipes connect everything to everything. They run between buildings at chest height, at ankle height, overhead in bundles of six and ten, so dense in places that the streets are more conduit than street. You move through the town by ducking. The pipes are warm. This is the first strangeness you can touch: in water a half-degree above freezing, the iron is warm, faintly, like a hand recently withdrawn.

Everything has had time to settle. The flood was not recent — the rivets wear smooth caps of mineral accretion, rust has bloomed and stabilized into a hard chocolate-colored skin, and the silt has long since stopped falling. The town and the water have reached terms. Doorways have softened at their edges. A staircase has dissolved into a ramp of orange concretion. Nothing is collapsing; collapse is finished. What remains is the durable skeleton of the place, in balance, maintained — and that word, *maintained*, is the one you keep returning to, because the valves are not seized. The valves turn. You can watch one turning now, a brass wheel the size of a dinner plate, rotating a quarter-turn with no hand upon it, then resting, then rotating back.

Sound carries strangely. Mostly there is silence — the enormous, upholstered silence of deep cold water — but underneath it, if you hold still long enough for your own noise to drain away, you find the town's pulse: a low, regular *thud... thud...* from beneath the silt, slower than a heartbeat, more patient. A pump, presumably. Pumping what, and to where, in a world entirely of water, is a question the town does not answer. Sometimes a pipe ticks as it warms. Once an hour, when the headframe wheel completes its revolution, there is a single soft chime, like a spoon touched to the rim of a glass two rooms away.

Life is sparse and deliberate. Vegetation amounts to a few colonies of white kelp-like fronds rooted in the warm seams between pipes, pallid and translucent, leaning toward the breathing lamps the way ordinary plants lean toward the sun — their slow heliotropism tuned to that one-minute pulse, so the whole grove sways in time with the light. Fauna is rarer still. A single eel, ancient and thick as a forearm, lives in the bore of a fractured outflow pipe and is seen perhaps once a day, threading the lanes on some errand of its own, unhurried, proprietary. Small blind shrimp graze the warm iron. Now and then something larger passes above the town — felt rather than seen, a displacement in the cold, a brief deepening of the dark — and for a few minutes afterward the pilot lamps dim, all together, and the pump slows, until it has gone.

The active life of the place is mechanical, and it is genuinely active — not haunted-house twitching but real process, ongoing. Flywheels in the largest building turn behind windows fogged with mineral film. A pressure gauge holds steady at a reading the dial was never printed to show. At intervals, a vent on the boiler-house roof releases a column of something — not air; it doesn't rise as bubbles but as a slow braid of clearer water, warmer water, visible only by its shimmer, climbing into the black until the cold takes it. The kelp leans toward these releases too. The shrimp gather. Whatever the town manufactures, the small economy of the dark has organized itself around the product.

There is one door in the town that stands open. It belongs to a narrow brick structure, chapel-proportioned, set at the dense heart of the lanes where the pipework is thickest, and every pipe in town, followed far enough, bends eventually toward it. Inside, the orange light breathes more deeply, and the warmth is enough to feel through a glove, and the *thud* of the pump resolves into something more articulate — not words, nothing so strange as words, but rhythm with intention in it, the cadence of work being done carefully by something that has been doing it a very long time and sees no reason, drowned or not, dark or not, alone or not, to stop.

The town does not mind being visited. The lamps brighten a little when you move among them. But the water above is heavy, and the cold is patient, and when you rise away the lights fade behind you in their slow respiration — orange, dim, orange, dim — until the dark closes over the parish and it goes on with its shift.
