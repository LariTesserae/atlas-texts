**The Cistern Town**

You reach it only after the mountain has finished with you. The climb takes days — up a massif so old it has stopped being a mountain in any dramatic sense, its peaks long since planed down to a vast, tilted tableland of naked stone, warm underfoot, wrinkled like the skin on the back of a hand. Nothing grows up here in the open. The rock is the color of old teeth. Whatever weather once carved this place has done all the carving it will ever do; the summit has settled into its final shape, and the shape is nearly flat, and it is patient in a way that makes the wind seem young.

Then there is the crack.

It is not wide — you could miss it, and almost everyone does. A slot in the summit stone perhaps four paces across, breathing. That is the first thing you notice: warm, wet air rising out of it in a slow exhalation, condensing in the cooler altitude into a thin, standing plume that never becomes a proper cloud. It smells of minerals and wet clay and faintly of smoke.

Climb down. There are handholds. They were cut a very long time ago, and the water has softened their edges into something like the sockets of a jaw, but they hold.

The pocket below is the size of a modest courtyard — you could cross it in twenty steps if crossing it were possible, which it is not, because the town is in the way.

There is no room in the cistern town. That is the first law of the place. Every surface of the chamber — the floor, the sloped walls, the underside of the great stone lintel that half-roofs it — is built upon, and the buildings press against one another like teeth in a crowded mouth. They are small. Doorways come to your waist. Stairs are cut at the width of a child's foot. Whole terraces of dwellings, granaries no larger than jars, a plaza the size of a table, all stacked and interlocked and mortared with pale clay, ascending the walls in tiers until the highest houses hang almost upside-down from the ceiling, their doorways opening onto air. The architecture is hand-work — you can see the thumb-marks in the clay, the tool-scars from stone chisels, the places where a builder patched a wall with the flat of a palm and the palm-print stayed. No metal anywhere, or almost none: a few small hooks of soft beaten copper, green now, holding nothing.

The whole town weeps. Water is everywhere and comes from everywhere — it beads on the ceiling stone and falls in slow, deliberate drops; it slides down the walls in films thin as breath; it wells up between the paving stones of the little plaza and runs off through gutters no wider than a finger, gutters that the builders cut with obvious care, that route the water down through the town in a hundred channels, under thresholds, through the hearts of houses, spilling from carved spouts shaped like open mouths. At the bottom of everything, in the lowest hollow of the chamber, the channels gather into a dark pool that steams gently. The warmth of the whole pocket comes up through that pool from somewhere deep in the mountain's old body. This is the furnace and the well and, one suspects, the reason for everything.

The light is the color of dusk at noon. It falls through the slot above in a single soft blade, moves across the terraces over the course of a day like a slow hand, and where it touches the wet clay the town briefly glows amber. Everywhere else is a brown-grey dimness in which shapes resolve slowly. Your eyes adjust. They keep adjusting. There always seems to be one more tier of houses just past where you thought the dark began.

Vegetation is scarce and precise. A black moss grows in the seams between buildings, only in the seams, as if it too obeys the law against taking up room. In the plaza stands the town's single tree — pale, leafless or nearly, no taller than a person, its roots gone down through the paving into the warm water below. Its bark is smooth and slightly translucent. It is impossible to say whether it is very young or the oldest living thing on the mountain. Small offerings sit at its base: cups of water, arranged with recent care.

That is the quiet unease of the place. The town is packed and the town is empty, and neither fact will yield to the other. No one comes to the low doorways. No smoke rises from the little clay chimneys. Yet the gutters are clear of moss. The offering cups are full and the water in them has not gone stale. Once, in a high tier, a doorway shows a faint interior glow, and by the time you have climbed toward it there is only warm darkness inside and the smell of a fire recently smothered.

Sound is nearly a substance here, there is so little of it. The drip of the ceiling into the pool — irregular, unhurried, each drop distinct. The undertone of water moving through a hundred small channels, less a sound than a pressure. Occasionally the deep stone gives a soft settling knock, the mountain shifting its ancient weight by a hair's width. Your own breathing becomes the loudest thing in the chamber, and you find yourself quieting it.

Life is rare and pale. A blind salamander, white as candle-fat, hangs motionless in the warm pool with its fingers spread. Once a day, roughly, a single dark bird drops through the slot above, drinks at the plaza, and leaves — it does not sing. Small translucent snails ascend the wet walls with geologic patience. That is nearly the whole census.

And yet the place is not still. It has weather of its own, a full climate in twenty paces: the warm pool exhales, the mist thickens against the cool stone of the ceiling, the ceiling sweats, the sweat falls, the gutters carry it home again. Morning, in the cistern town, is when the blade of light arrives and the mist thins to gold; evening is when the walls run heavier and every carved mouth-spout speaks in a low trickle. The cycle turns whether or not anyone is there to live inside it — though the cleared gutters, the fresh water in the cups, the smothered fire, all suggest that someone is, someone small and patient and unwilling to be seen, keeping the oldest town on the oldest mountain in working order, one handful of clay at a time.

You climb out eventually. The plume of warm breath follows you up through the slot and vanishes into the thin bright air of the summit, and behind you, faintly, a drop falls into the pool, and another, keeping the time the place has always kept.
