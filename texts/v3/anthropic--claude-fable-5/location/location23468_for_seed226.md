# The Risen Quarter

There is a saddle between two old peaks, high enough that clouds pass through it rather than over it, and in that saddle a tooth of new stone is coming up out of the earth.

It is not large — perhaps three hundred paces across at its base, a pale, blunt mass the color of bone left in water, rising from a moat of dark older rock. It grows. Not quickly: a hand's width in a good year, less in most. The motion is invisible to anyone watching, obvious to anyone returning. The stone is warm at its base, faintly, the warmth of a living animal rather than a fire, and where it emerges from the ground it is soft enough to take the mark of a thumbnail. A week of mountain air hardens that surface to something a flint pick can still bite. A generation of air makes it as hard as anything in the world.

This is the only fact you need to understand the place. Everything else follows from it.

## The City That Rises

People have been carving into the tooth for longer than the carving records itself — and it does record itself, because nothing here erodes. The stone is too new for weather to have learned its weaknesses. Edges cut four hundred years ago are still knife-clean. So the tooth has become a vertical settlement, a city read from bottom to top in the wrong order: the newest work is at the base, raw doorways and half-finished stairs hacked into the soft fresh rock, and as the stone rises it carries the finished rooms up with it, lifting them year by year into the cold bright air.

The oldest chambers are now sixty, eighty meters overhead — cells and halls and granaries carved by people whose names survive only as mason's marks, hoisted slowly out of reach of their descendants. The whole structure resembles a city built by a tide going the wrong way. Streets that were once at ground level are now balconies. A well-shaft carved straight down two centuries ago is now a chimney open to the sky at both ends, and the wind plays it.

That is the first thing you hear, long before you see the place: the tooth is *loud*. Hundreds of carved rooms, stairwells, light-shafts, and cistern mouths, almost all of them empty, all of them open to a wind that never fully stops at this altitude. The stone moans in long organ-tones from the big halls and whistles thin and urgent from the narrow vents. Beneath the wind there is a second voice: the rock itself cracking as it rises and dries — sharp reports like a knuckle popping, sometimes a deep settling groan that travels through the soles of the feet. The carvers say the tooth talks more in autumn. They have opinions about what it says.

## Materials and Hands

There is no metal here worth the name. The tools are picks of antler and fire-hardened wood for the freshest stone, flint and quarried basalt for stone a season old, and grinding slabs with wet sand for finishing. This is enough, because the stone meets the carver halfway — the trick of the whole settlement is *timing*, not force. A doorway is roughed out in the soft new rock in a week; the same doorway attempted in ten-year-old stone would take a lifetime. So the masons work in a permanent crouch at the base of the city, always at the waterline of hardness, racing a deadline that moves at a finger-width a month.

Scaffolding of lashed pine clings to the lower walls. Rope ladders, gray with age, hang from the older levels — some still trusted, most not. Tally-marks and ownership glyphs are pressed into wet stone with a wooden stamp, the closest thing the place has to writing, and they ride upward through the decades like names on a slow flood.

## Emptiness

Here is the strange arithmetic of the Risen Quarter: it has the rooms of a town and the population of a hamlet. Fewer than forty people live here. They occupy a narrow band of the city — the levels between five and twenty meters up, old enough to be dry and hard, young enough to still be reachable by sane stairs. Below them, raw stone and works in progress. Above them, an ever-growing volume of abandonment: chamber after chamber, swept clean by wind, lit by carved windows, holding nothing but the sound of themselves.

Why do they keep carving? Ask three residents and you get three answers. Because the stone is soft *now* and will never be soft again — waste of softness is the only real sin here. Because the rooms are owed to descendants, or to a returning people, depending on which lineage is talking. Because stopping feels like a kind of death, and the tooth would keep rising anyway, blank and meaningless, and no one can bear the thought of all that good stone going up uncut.

So the place is hollow on purpose. Vast interior volumes per living soul. You can walk an hour through the upper levels — climbing, mostly, hand over hand up stairs that have tilted slightly as the tooth leaned — and meet nothing but your own echo arriving from unexpected directions.

## Water and Green

The saddle sits inside the cloud belt. Mist comes through twice a day like a slow tide, and the stone, cool and dense, milks it: every carved lintel weeps, every window-sill has its drip-line, every interior wall sweats a fine condensate that gathers in channels the old carvers cut for exactly this purpose. The city drinks its own atmosphere. Cisterns on the middle levels are always a third full; the sound of water finding its way down through the structure — tick, tick, a sudden brief rush — runs under everything, the quiet counterpoint to the wind's noise.

The vegetation is dense but vertical. The fresh stone at the base is naked, too new and too faintly warm for anything to take hold. But each level upward is a step back in time, and life colonizes accordingly: first a film of green-black algae, then mosses in thick saturated pelts, then ferns rooted in the moss, and on the oldest, highest levels, actual shrubs — dwarf rowan and a wind-flattened juniper — growing out of windows that were carved as windows and are now hanging gardens no one tends. The abandoned upper city is the lushest place for a day's walk in any direction. The surrounding saddle is cool grass and stunted heath, but the tooth itself is a green tower wearing its own age as foliage.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, and what does is singular. A colony of dark swifts owns the upper levels, perhaps thirty birds, nesting in the oldest halls; at dusk they pour out of the high windows screaming, the only fast thing in the entire landscape. The carvers consider them a calendar — the swifts abandon a level when its windows rise above a certain height, moving their nests down into rooms newly come of age, and so the birds, like everyone else here, are slowly migrating *downward through the city as the city rises through them*.

There is one other animal anyone will mention: something that lives in the deep stair-shafts, heard and never clearly seen, that knocks. Two knocks, a pause, two knocks, answering loud sounds from outside. Probably it is the stone. The carvers knock back anyway, politely, because the cost of politeness is nothing and the tooth is, after all, still being born — and one does not stop being courteous to a thing while it is being born.

## Time

Nothing here happens fast. The drama of the place is geological in pace and total in consequence: rooms ripen, harden, rise, empty, and green over, in that order, always. A carver may begin a hall as an apprentice and gild its threshold-marks as an elder while standing on a ladder, the hall having climbed past her in the meantime. The city is a clock that builds itself, a record that writes itself upward, loud, wet, cold, nearly empty —

and underneath everything, patient as breath, the warm soft stone keeps coming.
