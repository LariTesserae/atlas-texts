**The Bright Understory**

You descend for half a day before the ceiling opens out, and then there is no more descending — only the floor of the world, a plain of pale stone under a roof so distant it reads as sky.

The light is the first wrong thing, and the most beautiful. There is no sun down here, but the cavern roof is a vault of calcite and rock-salt hundreds of meters thick, and somewhere far above, on the true surface, daylight falls on it and soaks through. The whole ceiling glows — not dimly, not the polite phosphorescence of caves in stories, but with a hard, even brilliance, like the inside of an overcast noon. The light has been filtered through a kilometer of crystal and arrives scrubbed of warmth and direction. Nothing casts a sharp shadow here. Everything stands in a soft, total illumination, the way objects look in heavy fog, except the air is clear and you can see for miles.

And the light *moves*. Water seeps through the vault from above — rains that fell on the surface weeks ago, descending grain by grain through the salt — and where the ceiling is wet it goes glassy and transmits more; where it dries it clouds over. So brightness travels across the roof in slow, enormous blooms, square kilometers of glow swelling and fading, sweeping over the plain below like the shadows of clouds in reverse: not darkness passing over a bright land, but light passing over a pale one. The inhabitants of this place, whoever they were, must have told time by it. There is no other clock.

The city is everywhere and almost nowhere. It was built by hand — you can see that in every surface: stone stacked on stone without mortar, lintels of single slabs levered into place, walls whose courses still carry the rhythm of individual days of labor, a meter or so per day, you can count them like tree rings. No metal was ever used here, or none survives. The tools lie where they were set down: flint adzes, antler picks, hammerstones worn into perfect ergonomic dents, grinding slabs hollowed almost through. But the construction is *old* in a way that hand-built things rarely get to be. The dripping ceiling has spent uncountable centuries dissolving and redepositing lime over everything, and the city has been candied. Walls have melted into smooth flowing forms, doorways have sagged into ovals, stairs have become ramps, ramps have become slopes, slopes have become the floor. Whole districts have been simplified down to suggestion — a grid of low ridges where streets were, mounds where granaries stood. The city has been settling toward this final shape for so long that it has nearly arrived. It will not change again. It is the oldest stable thing in a world of motion.

Because everything *else* moves.

The plain between the worn districts — and the districts are far apart, islands of construction separated by long reaches of bare flowstone, as though the builders feared crowding, or the cavern's geometry forbade it — the plain is alive with traffic. Pale things in their thousands. Blind crickets the size of a thumb travel in slow rivers, following moisture gradients only they can read. Translucent salamanders, big as cats, gather at the seep-pools and drip-basins that the builders carved — the basins still work; they are the only infrastructure that never stopped functioning — and the salamanders pile into them in writhing dozens, then disperse, then pile in again, governed by some pressure in the rock or the light. Flat, soft-bodied grazers, something between an isopod and a sheep, move across the lime-crusted floors in herds, rasping up the bacterial films that are this world's only harvest. There are no plants. There were never plants. The whole food web hangs from chemistry: mineral-eating films on every wet surface, and everything else eating the films, or eating the film-eaters.

The turbulence is real and constant and almost completely silent.

That is the thing that takes longest to accept. The plain at any moment carries the visual energy of a market at midday — herds wheeling, swarms dividing around the ruins like water around stones, sudden mass flights when a light-bloom passes overhead and the grazers, who hate the brightness, stampede toward the dimming districts in a soundless gray surge. Predators work the edges of the herds: long, low, many-legged things, white as the stone, that explode into motion and drag their catch down without a cry, because nothing here has ever had a reason to cry. Sound is expensive and the dark that shaped these lineages — before the vault thinned, before the light came through — never rewarded it. So the chaos unfolds in a hush. You hear the drip of seep-water into the old basins. You hear, if you stand very still, the faint papery whisper of ten thousand cricket legs, like wind in a field that does not exist. You hear your own blood. The visual noise is deafening and the air is nearly silent, and the mismatch makes the place feel like a memory of itself, or a place watched through glass.

The air is cool — cellar-cool, constant, somewhere a sweater would fix — and damp in patches. Moisture comes down from the vault unevenly: there are drip-lines, thin permanent veils of falling drops marking the fracture systems above, and the city was built along them. You can map the dead streets by the wet seams in the ceiling miles overhead. Between the drip-lines the stone is dry, dusty with old lime, and the fauna avoids it; the herds funnel along the moisture like caravans along oases, which means they funnel through the ruins, which means the dead city is the most crowded place in this world, its empty doorways thick with pale traffic that does not know it is walking through rooms.

Here and there are the unusual things, the details that don't quite resolve. Hand-prints in red ochre on the underside of a fallen lintel — ordinary enough — but they are pressed in concentric rings around the drip-basins, hundreds of hands, as if the basins were not plumbing but altars, or both. Niches in the walls at salamander height, worn glossy, as if the animals have been sheltering in them for so many generations that they have polished the stone — or as if the niches were carved *for* them. A district where every doorway faces the same direction: not toward anything visible now, but toward a section of the vault that, once or twice in a long watching, flares brighter than any other bloom, a sustained white blaze lasting hours. Whatever surface condition produces it — a lake above, a glacier, a salt flat under a clear sky — the builders oriented their whole quarter toward it, and when it blazes, even now, the herds stop. All of them. The plain goes still for the duration, every pale animal motionless and faced the same way, and the silence, which was never broken, somehow deepens.

Then the blaze fades, the light-blooms resume their slow migration across the ceiling, and the floor of the world churns back into its quiet riot — the finished city standing motionless in the middle of it, dissolving by millimeters, brighter than any underground place has a right to be, busy as a bazaar, and hushed as the moment before a word.
