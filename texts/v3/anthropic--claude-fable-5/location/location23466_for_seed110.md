**The Sweating Chamber**

You would not find it by looking. Looking is not a sense that works here.

High in the shoulder of a mountain so young it has no name yet — rock that hardened within living memory of nothing, because nothing has lived long enough to remember — there is a cavity the size of a small room. It formed when a pocket of gas was trapped in cooling stone and the stone closed around it like a fist around a held breath. The breath is still in there. So is everything else.

There is no light. Not dim light, not light filtered or failed — none. Light has never entered this place. The chamber was sealed in darkness before it was anything at all, and darkness is its native condition the way water is the native condition of the sea. To describe it, you have to put away your eyes and come in through the skin.

So: skin first. The air is warm, blood-warm, a fraction warmer than you, so that the boundary of your body goes soft and uncertain. It is wet air, saturated, air that has given up the distinction between itself and water. Every breath is half drink. The walls sweat — not metaphorically. The young rock is still close enough to the mountain's heat that groundwater migrating through its hairline pores arrives warm and beads on every surface, swells, hangs, and falls. The whole chamber is weeping at body temperature, slowly, everywhere, forever.

The ceiling is low enough to touch in places, and touching it tells you how new this place is. There are no stalactites. There has not been time. Instead the rock carries the first *intentions* of stalactites — soft rings of mineral no thicker than a fingernail, blisters of calcite the size of lentils, a fine pebbled texture like gooseflesh. The chamber is covered in beginnings. Thousands of them, dense, near-touching, a whole vault of formations in their embryonic state, each one a few centuries from becoming anything. Run your palm across the ceiling and it reads like braille written by something that has only just learned the alphabet. Nothing here is worn. Nothing has been smoothed, broken, redeposited. The geology is raw — edges still sharp from the original cooling, glassy in places where your fingertips slide and then catch on a crystal facet that has never been touched by weather, because weather does not come here.

That is the strangest property of the chamber, stranger than the dark: it does not change. The temperature does not vary by a degree between what would be noon and what would be midnight, between what would be summer and what would be the deep of the year. The drip rate is constant. The humidity is total and cannot become more total. The chamber long ago reached its equilibrium and settled into it like sediment, and now it simply *persists*, a held note. Time passes through it without leaving fingerprints. If you stayed an hour or a decade, the room would offer you no evidence of which.

Sound, then, because sound is most of what there is. Mostly the chamber is quiet — but it is the quiet of a sleeping body, not of a vacuum. There is the drip: irregular, unhurried, each drop falling from ceiling to floor-pool with a small round *tok* that the curved walls pass around twice before swallowing. There is a sub-audible warmth, a sense more than a sound, of the mountain itself — pressure, settling, the vast patient digestion of stone. And occasionally, rarely, a sound you cannot place: a soft mineral *tick*, like a knuckle cracking far inside the wall. The rock is still annealing. It is the sound of the chamber continuing, very slowly, to be born.

Life has found the place, but sparsely, the way light finds the bottom of the sea — in threads. Where the warm water seeps through the wall it carries dissolved minerals, sulfur, faint chemical food, and on those seeps grow pale mats: chemosynthetic films, white and faintly slick, like wet silk laid over the stone. Here and there a colorless filament hangs from the ceiling and sways in air currents too gentle to feel — roots, perhaps, of something growing far above on the mountain's outer skin, which have followed water down through forty meters of fissure to dangle blind in the dark, drinking. These hanging threads are the chamber's only vegetation, and they are dense in one corner and absent everywhere else, a single pale curtain in a room of stone.

The fauna is almost a rumor. There is one species of thing in here — small, eyeless, soft-bodied, pale as the mats it grazes. You will go hours without evidence of it. Then your hand on the wall will find a track in the mineral film, a clean grazed line a few millimeters wide, wandering, recent or ancient — impossible to say, since nothing here erases anything. Perhaps once, if you are very still, something the size of a thumb-joint will cross the back of your hand on too many legs, unhurried, and continue into the dark without registering you as anything other than a warm, temporary stone.

And then there are the marks.

Someone has been here. Not recently — but recency is a meaningless category in a room outside of time. Near the low entrance crack, where a person would have to flatten and crawl, the wall carries a cluster of cupules: small hemispheric pits ground into the soft new mineral with a harder stone, dozens of them, dense in one patch, arranged in a pattern that might be deliberate and might be the simple accumulation of a repeated gesture. Below them, on a natural shelf, sit three objects. A hammerstone, its working end rounded by use. A shallow dish chipped from a flake of the chamber's own rock, holding a residue of something fatty — a lamp, perhaps, once, though whatever it burned is long gone and the dark has closed back over it. And a small stack of river pebbles, smooth and foreign, carried up the mountain and in through the dark for no purpose that survives.

Whoever they were, they came with hands and stone and fire small enough to cup, into total darkness and blood-warm wet, and ground pits into the wall, and left their tools, and did not come back. The chamber has done what the chamber does: nothing. It has not buried the objects, not corroded them, not dignified them with ruin. The mineral sweat has begun, just barely, to glaze them — a film thinner than breath on glass. In ten thousand years they will be sealed under flowstone like insects in amber. For now they simply sit in the dark, mid-sentence, exactly as they were set down.

That is the chamber's whole nature, finally: it is a room where everything is beginning and nothing proceeds. The stalactites are begun. The mats are begun. The cupules are begun, the lamp was lit once, the creature's grazing tracks are written and never weathered away. It is a place packed wall to wall with first gestures, warm and wet and dark and almost silent, held at the exact moment before becoming —

— and the drop falls, *tok*, and the walls pass the sound around, and swallow it, and wait.
