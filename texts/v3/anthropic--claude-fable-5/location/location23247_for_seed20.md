**The Ringing Bowl**

It is a small place, and it should not be possible to drown at this altitude, and yet here the world has done both things at once: taken the sharp new summit of a mountain — rock so young it still has the gloss of its own breaking, edges that have never met a river — and filled the bowl of it with warm sea.

The water stands higher than the rim. That is the first wrong thing you notice, if you arrive from below, climbing the dry black slopes through thin cold air: ahead of you, cresting the broken crater wall, a lens of ocean perhaps two hundred strokes across, bulging upward like water held in a glass past the brim, trembling, lit from within by a dim grey-gold glow that has no source. The air around the bowl is humid and blood-warm. The air everywhere else on the mountain is freezing. The boundary between the two is about as wide as your hand.

You hear it long before you see it. Everyone does. The bowl *rings*.

---

Beneath the surface, the sound is no longer sound. It is the medium itself. A bronze bell lies on its side at the bottom of the bowl — the only finished thing here, green-shouldered, big as a small room, its yoke and frame collapsed around it in a tangle of fitted stone, the remnant of some shrine built by people who must have come up the mountain before the water did, for reasons the place no longer remembers. There is a stair cut into the rock that descends to it, eleven steps, very fresh, the chisel marks still crisp. There is a single cairn off to the east, leaning. There is half a doorway standing free, holding up nothing. That is the entire built inventory of the place.

The bell rings continuously. Nothing strikes it. Or rather — and this is the more honest way to say it — the *ringing exists*, and the bell appears to be where the ringing has chosen to live. Sometimes the bronze visibly shudders a half-second after a peal, the way thunder follows lightning, effect trailing cause. Sometimes you can watch a peal travel across the bowl as a visible thing: a wall of compressed water, silver-skinned, sweeping outward, and where it passes, the rock changes. Edges sharpen. New spires extrude from the floor like glass pulled hot, set, and are sheared off by the next peal. The young stone of this place is not young because it is recently made. It is young because it is *being remade*, constantly, on the rhythm of the bell — a summit in permanent first-formation, never permitted to begin eroding, never the same crag twice.

The loudness cannot be overstated. In water this warm and this strange, the ringing arrives in the bones before the ears, in the teeth, in the fluid of the eyes. It blurs vision at its peaks. It is the dominant fact of the environment the way gravity is a fact elsewhere, and the few living things here have organized themselves entirely around it.

---

There is not much life. A sparse fringe of red weed grows in the lee of the larger spires — only in the lee; anything that roots in open water is shredded by the standing waves of sound, which hang in the bowl as shimmering, almost-architectural planes, curtains of vibration you can see by their sheen and must not swim through. The weed has gone strange in the manner of everything here: each frond grows in a tight spiral matched to the bell's overtones, and when a peal passes, the whole sparse colony flexes in sequence like a thing breathing.

The fauna is one animal, possibly. A pale eel, long as an arm, blind, with skin like wet paper. You will see it perhaps twice in a day. The trouble is that you will sometimes see it twice *at once* — the same eel, identical scar behind the head, swimming in two places, and when the peal passes through the bowl the two of it slide back together without ceremony, as if the sound were a comb pulling the world's loose threads straight. It eats nothing anyone has observed. It may be the place's only resident, or its echo.

---

The dimness is constant and directionless. Up through the bulging surface, the sky shows as a smeared brightness, and down is darker, but light here behaves like everything else — turbulently. Glimmers pool in hollows of the rock and stay there after their source is gone, sloshing faintly when a peal goes by. Your shadow lags you. Near the bell it sometimes precedes you, hurrying down the eleven steps before you have decided to descend them.

Bubbles in the bowl fall. They gather at the bottom around the bell in a quivering silver carpet, and when the largest peals come, the carpet leaps and scatters downward into cracks in the floor, and the new rock grows over them, sealing the bell's breath into the mountain.

---

The rules, as far as they can be assembled:

The boundary of the place is the boundary of the ringing. Where you can no longer feel the bell in your sternum, the water ends — abruptly, a curved wall of sea standing against thin mountain air — and the world resumes its ordinary obligations.

Inside, cause and effect are loosened but not absent. Things still happen; they simply do not insist on their order. The eel arrives before it sets out. The stone breaks before it grows. The bell answers itself.

And the place is becoming more, not less. The spires are taller than they were. The standing curtains of sound have multiplied, subdividing the small bowl into chambers, a sparse drowned cathedral building itself out of noise on the roof of a mountain — turbulent, deafening, dim, almost empty, two hundred strokes across, and ringing, ringing, ringing.
