# The Standing Sea

There is a sea on top of the world, and it has never once been quiet.

Begin with the altitude, because everything else follows from it. Long ago — though "long ago" here means almost nothing, the rock is so young it still has edges — a shield of basalt rose out of the planet's crust, miles high and broad as a continent, and at its crown it cracked open into a basin the size of an inland ocean. The walls of that basin are unweathered. No rain has had time to soften them, no glacier has dragged across them, and the water that fills them now is too cold and too still to do its slow work quickly. Underwater, the cliffs go down in clean black sheets, fluted like organ pipes, edged like split obsidian. You could cut yourself on this landscape. Many things do.

The sea fills the basin nearly to the rim. Above the surface there is only thin freezing sky and the black lip of the caldera running off in both directions past the curve of the world. Below the surface — and below is where this place actually *is* — the water descends three, four, five kilometers through a clarity that should not be possible. There is almost nothing suspended in it. No plankton bloom, no silt, no drowned forests shedding debris. No plant has ever grown here; the water is mineral, cold-filtered, gin-clear, and so the daylight that strikes the surface does not die at thirty meters the way it does in living oceans. It keeps going. At a kilometer down the light is still ordinary light — pale, blued, directional, casting shadows. The basalt walls are visible from impossible distances, hanging in the water like the sides of a vast dim room. It is the emptiest brightness imaginable. You can see for miles and there is, for miles, nothing to see.

Except that you cannot think here, because of the sound.

The sound is the sea's one absolute fact, and it is everywhere, and it has never changed. At the floor of the basin, spaced across thousands of kilometers of glass-sharp plain, stand the engines. They are old industrial things — riveted, immense, streaked with mineral bloom — turbine halls the size of cathedrals bolted directly into the young rock, intake throats wide enough to swallow ships. Most of them stand alone, a single structure and then four hundred kilometers of nothing, then another. Some are clearly derelict, split open, their flywheels seized into sculpture. But enough of them still run. Whoever built them built them to raise this sea — to pump an ocean's worth of water up through the body of the mountain from the drowned lowlands below — and the work is finished, has been finished for ages, and the engines never received the instruction to stop. So now they run against themselves. They lift water and the water falls back. They drive currents that cancel in great fixed gyres. The whole system long ago found its equilibrium and locked there, and the byproduct of that equilibrium is a roar.

Water carries sound four times faster than air and gives almost none of it up. In a basin this size, with walls this hard and this smooth, the roar of the engines does not fade — it reflects, compounds, and settles into a standing wave the size of the sea itself. There is no "near the engines" and "far from the engines." The sound is structural. It is in the water the way salt is in the water. It is a pressure on the chest, a vibration in the jaw, a continuous tectonic thunder overlaid with deeper pulses where the great pistons of distant stations beat in and out of phase with each other — a slow interference pattern, decades long, the only clock this place has. Stand anywhere in the High Sea and you are standing inside a struck bell that is never permitted to ring down.

And the strange thing — the thing that takes time to understand — is that the sea is *full of animals*, and the animals love it.

They are not crowded. Nothing here is crowded; the emptiness is real, and you can swim for a day through bright vacant blue and meet nothing. But the life, when it comes, comes abundantly, in pulses, the way weather comes. Rivers of pale eyeless swimmers, millions strong and kilometers long, pouring along the contours of the sound — for they navigate by it, reading the standing wave the way migrating birds read magnetism, riding the fixed acoustic geography of a sea that never changes. Slow grazers like hinged stones work the engine housings, rasping off the mineral crusts and rust-blooms and bacterial mats that are the only thing resembling pasture in a world without plants. Around the running turbine halls, where waste heat leaks into the cold, whole settlements of filter-feeders cluster in the warm outwash like towns around a mill — the engines were never meant to be reefs, but they are the only warmth and the only food and the only difference in five thousand kilometers of identical perfection, so everything that lives here lives, ultimately, off the machines.

The deep fauna have abandoned eyes despite the light. Light tells you almost nothing in a place where every direction looks the same. Sound tells you everything. The largest creatures — there are very large creatures, glimpsed rarely, singular, spaced across the basin like the engines themselves — seem to do nothing at all but hang motionless in the water column with their whole bodies tuned to the roar, listening to the one note for irregularities that never arrive.

Here and there on the basin floor lie the lighter marks of the builders: a fallen crane half-sunk in glass gravel, its boom snapped clean. A row of habitation cylinders, doors open, swept bare, no silt to bury them and no current to move them, preserved in cold clarity as if abandoned an hour ago. A line of pylons marching toward an engine station, cables still taut. The marks are scattered, faint, outnumbered ten thousand to one by empty rock. Whoever they were, they raised a sea to the top of a mountain, set it ringing, and left.

What remains is a closed circle, balanced and roaring. The engines lift the water; the water falls; the heat leaks; the animals gather; the sound stands. Nothing erodes, nothing silts, nothing blooms or dies back with seasons, because there are no seasons — only the cold, the clear vertical light, the sharp black walls, and the single sustained chord of an industry that completed its purpose before memory and has been thundering its equilibrium into the dark ever since.

The High Sea does not change. It only continues, at full volume, forever.
