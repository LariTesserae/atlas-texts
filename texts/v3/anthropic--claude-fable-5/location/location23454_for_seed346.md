**The Provisional City**

It sits in a shallow basin at the bottom of everything — low ground, cold ground, a place where fog collects and does not leave. From the rim of the basin you could mistake it for a town of perhaps three hundred buildings, packed so tightly their walls share each other, eaves overlapping eaves, stairways threading between structures with no gap wider than a shoulder. It looks dense the way a honeycomb is dense, or a crystal: not crowded so much as *saturated*, as if every available unit of space had been claimed and filled and then claimed again at a finer resolution.

Then you walk down into it, and you understand that the city is not finished. Not ruined — the distinction matters here, and the city will teach it to you. Ruin is what happens after coherence. This place has not yet achieved coherence. It is still arriving.

---

**Materials.** The buildings are made of a stone that has not fully committed to being stone. In the older quarters — older meaning *more resolved*, the parts that have been real the longest — the walls are a dense gray-blue material, cold to the touch, beaded everywhere with condensation, and they behave the way matter should. You can lean on them. Frost forms on them in thin ferning patterns on the coldest mornings, and the frost stays.

Farther out, toward the edges of the basin, the walls become approximate. A doorframe will be sharp and certain at eye level and then go soft toward the lintel, the corners rounding into something like the *intention* of a corner. Some surfaces have grain but no texture; some have texture but, when you look away and back, a different one. The locals — there are locals, a few — say you can tell how real a wall is by whether dew forms on it. Condensation is the city's honesty test. The fog drifts everywhere, indiscriminate, but it only beads on what has finished becoming. Unresolved walls stay dry, faintly warm, and very slightly translucent at dusk, like wax held near a flame.

No one built this place in the way buildings are usually built. It is being *instantiated* — extruded into existence by an engine no one has located, somewhere below the basin floor or perhaps distributed through it, working at a scale beneath chemistry. The process is patient. A given block may take a decade to fully resolve. You can stand in a half-real plaza and feel it happening: a faint pressure behind the sternum, the sensation of definition accumulating, the way a photograph develops if photographs took years.

---

**Geometry, and its failures.** The city is small. This is its most reliable cruelty. It is perhaps four hundred meters across, walkable end to end in eight minutes — except that the streets do not preserve distance. The interior is denser than the exterior admits. A street that should run thirty paces runs ninety, then loops, then deposits you one block *behind* where you entered, facing the way you came. There is an alley off the fish-scale square (so called for its paving, thousands of overlapping slate lozenges, each one wet) that appears in two places at once, and the locals use it as a shortcut, stepping in at one end of the city and out the other, the way you'd use a fold in a map. Children — there were children once; the records are unclear — supposedly learned the folds by heart, the way other children learn streams and hedgerows.

The boundary of the place is absolute. At the basin's rim the fog thickens into something that is not fog: a gray with no depth in it, no temperature, no smell. Things thrown into it do not land. The boundary is not dangerous, exactly — people have walked into it and walked back out, reporting nothing, an interval of nothing — but the city does not extend there, and neither, apparently, does anything else yet.

---

**Atmosphere.** Cold, persistently, a damp cold that lives at the bottom of the lungs — never bitter, never relenting, the temperature of a cellar in early spring. Moisture is everywhere but rarely falls. Rain comes occasionally, fine and brief, and where it touches unresolved stone it makes a sound like rain falling on rain, a doubled hiss, as if the surface had not decided whether to be there for the impact. Gutters run with thin water for an hour afterward. Pools collect in the low plazas, perfectly still, and the still water is the one place where the city always looks finished — reflections here are more committed than the things they reflect, sharper-edged, fully textured. Some residents check the puddles each morning to see what their street is *going* to look like.

The light is ordinary, and this is somehow the strangest thing about the place. Honest gray daylight, soft through the fog, casting weak doubled shadows at noon. Whatever is generating the city has gotten light exactly right, before almost anything else. Light arrives complete. It falls correctly on incorrect walls.

---

**Sound.** Mostly quiet — a deep, upholstered quiet, the silence of a library or a snowfield. Sounds that do occur arrive slightly wrong: footsteps that echo before the step, a drip from an eave that repeats itself, identical, four or five times, like a phrase being practiced. The most constant sound is almost below hearing: a low settling, a granular sigh, the sound of matter resolving. Stand with your palm against a young wall and you can feel it more than hear it — a fizz, like a held breath being very slowly released into stone.

---

**Ecology.** Vegetation is sparse and diagnostic. Moss grows only on the fully real — thick, black-green, beaded with water, packed into mortar lines and stair edges in the resolved quarters. The locals trust moss the way they trust dew. Where moss grows, you can build a fire, sleep, store food. A few stunted trees stand in the oldest courtyard, leafless or nearly, their bark genuinely rough, their roots heaving genuinely real paving stones — small proofs that the place can, eventually, support a complete thing.

Fauna is rare and partial. There is a bird, gray, never well seen, that exists intermittently — present on a railing, absent, present on another railing, with no flight between. It leaves real droppings, which the locals find obscurely reassuring. There are damp-loving insects in the moss, fully resolved, indifferent. Once or twice a year something larger moves through the unresolved quarters at night — its footsteps are heard, doubled and pre-echoing, but nothing has ever been seen, and the prevailing theory is that it is an animal the city intends to have eventually, rehearsing.

---

**Rules, as the residents have learned them.** Do not sleep in unresolved rooms; you wake stiff, slow, with the feeling of having been *revised*. Do not name the streets — named streets move. Trust dew, trust moss, trust reflections. The city changes slowly, on the scale of seasons and years, and always toward greater definition; nothing here decays, because nothing here has finished beginning. The handful of people who live in the resolved core do not know if they are inhabitants or ingredients. They keep the fires small, sweep the fish-scale square, and wait, in the cold and the fog and the patient fizzing quiet, for the place to come true around them.
