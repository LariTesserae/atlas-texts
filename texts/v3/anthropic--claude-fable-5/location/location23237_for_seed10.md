**The Loom Plateau**

Begin with altitude. You are high enough that the air has thinned to a cold, washed clarity, high enough that weather forms *here* rather than arriving from elsewhere — fog condenses out of nothing a few meters above the ground, beads, and falls back as a drizzle so fine it is less rain than a slow saturation of everything. The light is the gray-green of deep overcast at high latitude, a permanent hour before dusk. It never quite resolves into day.

And the ground is not ground. There is no rock here, not anymore. Whatever mountain this plateau was made of has been worn, over a span of time that mountains do not usually survive, down to a near-flat immensity — and what remains was claimed so long ago, so completely, that the plant *is* the terrain now. A single continuous mat of vegetation, meters deep in places, perhaps much deeper — cold-adapted, dark olive shading to black-green, dense as felted wool, soft underfoot and weeping water at every step. Press your hand down and your handprint fills with clear, freezing water that drains away again as the mat slowly exhales back into shape. The whole plateau is like this: a saturated, living surface stretching to a horizon that has been planed almost perfectly level by erosion so ancient it has nothing left to do.

This is the oldest stable state of the place. It is also, somehow, the least stable hour of its life.

---

**The works**

There are no structures here. Nothing was ever built. And yet the plateau is unmistakably *industrial* — you understand this within minutes of arriving, because you can hear it.

Beneath the mat, the plant runs machinery of its own flesh. Vast tubular vessels, evolved or arrived-at over the deep ages, pump water through the body of the plateau with a slow percussive rhythm — a *thud... thud... thud* you feel through your boots before you hear it, like the heartbeat of a building. At intervals across the flat — and the intervals are wide, a kilometer or more, so that each feature stands alone in great wet emptiness — the mat rises into structures the size of grain silos: hollow, ribbed, organically fluted towers of compacted living tissue. They vent. Columns of steam and metabolic gas roll out of their crowns into the cold air, and the venting is *loud* — a rhythmic, chuffing roar, each tower on its own cycle, so that the plateau drums and sighs with overlapping mechanical breath.

At night, or in the deeper dimness that passes for night, the towers spark. Bioelectric discharge — the plant runs current through itself, for reasons that may be signaling, may be metabolism, may be something else entirely — and pale blue-violet arcs crawl up the ribbing of the vent stacks and ground themselves in the wet mat with an audible crack. After a discharge, the surrounding moss glows faintly for a few minutes, a dim phosphor wash, and then goes dark again. There are no wires, no engines, no furnaces. There is only the plant, doing at planetary scale and biological pace what factories do: intake, process, pressure, exhaust, power.

No one built this. It built itself, over more time than building usually gets.

---

**The animals**

The plateau is full of animals and you will slowly realize that none of them came from anywhere.

The plant makes them. Across the wet flats you see them at distance — sparse, scattered, each one alone in its acre of dripping green: a thing like a heavy, slow heron, mottled the exact olive-black of the mat; a low, six-legged grazer with a hide of overlapping leaf-scale; flocks of small flickering fliers that pour out of the vent towers with the steam. Watch one long enough and you may see it *unfinished* — a heron with one wing still fibrous and rooted, trailing a green umbilical back into the moss, walking patiently at the limit of its tether. The plateau dreams fauna upward out of itself, lets them run loose for a while — hours, seasons, who knows — and then reabsorbs them. You will pass shallow depressions where an animal is being taken back down: half-submerged in the mat, calm, already mostly moss again, its eye still wet and watching you without alarm.

The abundance is real. The independence is the illusion. Everything that moves here is the plateau, briefly trying out a shape.

---

**The turbulence**

And it is trying out shapes *constantly*, faster than such an old thing should.

This is the paradox of the place: it has eroded into its final form, settled, ancient beyond ancient — and yet the surface is in a state of perpetual storm-like upheaval. The mat heaves. Slow waves a meter high travel across the flats like swells on a green sea, taking minutes to pass beneath you, lifting you and setting you down. Pools open without warning — the mat splits and a black, freezing eye of water gapes for an hour, breathing steam, and then knits shut. Whole hectares churn into temporary chaos: the moss boiling up into towers, ridges, half-formed colonnades, things that look for a moment like intentions — and then collapsing back into flatness, the water sheeting off them in cold curtains. The sound of a churning-event carries for miles: a wet, tearing roar, layered over the drumbeat of the pumps and the chuffing of the vents.

The erosion is finished. The *thinking* is not. The plateau reached its last stable shape ages ago and has spent the time since restlessly rehearsing every shape it can no longer permanently become.

---

**The breakdown**

Stay longer and the deeper trouble shows itself: the rehearsals do not respect reality.

Distance misbehaves first. A vent tower clearly three kilometers off resolves, after twenty minutes of walking, to be at arm's length — or recedes, or turns out to have been behind you. The plateau's flatness makes this hard to argue with; there are no landmarks except the ones the plant provides, and the plant rearranges them, or rearranges you among them.

Then time. You will watch a churning-event raise a green ridge, collapse it — and then watch the *same* event again, identical to the wave, an hour later, in the same place, like footage replayed. Some of the animals are repetitions too: the same heavy heron, with the same chipped beak, encountered at noon and at dusk and at noon again, performing the same seven steps. The plateau remembers, and it does not always distinguish remembering from doing.

At the worst of it — in the deep churn, near the largest vents, when the discharges come fast and the air itself goes faintly luminous — the rehearsals reach past matter. The mat raises shapes that are not green: a stretch of moss becomes, for a few seconds, gray stone, *actual* stone, the long-vanished bedrock of the original mountain, cold and hard and wet under your hand — and then is moss again, and was perhaps always moss. The plant is so old, and so total, that its memory of the rock it consumed has begun to outweigh the rock's absence. The plateau dreams its own dead geology back into brief existence, the way it dreams animals, and the boundary between the dream and the place has worn as thin as everything else here.

---

**Standing in it**

So: a vast cold table of land near the roof of the sky. Dim gray-green light, drizzle condensing out of the air itself. One plant, the size of a region, worn into the world's oldest shape. Wide silences of wet emptiness between its features — a vent tower roaring alone, a half-made animal grazing alone, a black pool breathing alone — and beneath all of it the industrial heartbeat of the pumps, the crack of living lightning, the periodic tearing roar of the surface remaking itself.

Nothing here was built. Nothing here is finished. The plateau ended long ago and has not stopped since.
