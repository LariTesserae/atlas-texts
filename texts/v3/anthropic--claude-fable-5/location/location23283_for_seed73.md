# The Caldera Stairs

At six thousand meters, the air should be thin and freezing. Here it is thick as breath on glass, and hot — a wet, mineral heat that settles on the skin within seconds of arrival and never fully lifts. This is the first rule of the massif, and the one travelers take longest to believe: *altitude does not cool this place.* Something underneath refuses to let it.

## Terrain

The massif rises out of cloud in a series of enormous tiered shoulders — not a single peak but a regional architecture of them, a dozen summits sharing one root, separated by hanging valleys, saddle-passes, and gorges so deep their floors have never been mapped except by sound. The rock is old. You can read its age in the way every edge has been rounded into agreement with the weather: ridgelines worn smooth as knuckles, cliff faces grooved by millennia of the same warm rain falling in the same patterns. Nothing here looks recent. The landscape has stopped arguing with itself.

The stone is a dark, iron-rich basalt veined with something paler that does not behave like mineral. The pale veins run warm to the touch — warmer than the surrounding rock, warmer than the air — and they branch the way circulation branches, narrowing as they climb, thickening as they descend into the mountain's interior. Geologists who came here generations ago called them *thermoclasts* and tried to core-sample them. The cores came up hollow. Whatever conducts the heat moves out of the way of drills, patiently, and returns when the drilling stops.

This is the engine of the place, as far as anyone understands it: a heat-distribution system threaded through the entire massif, older than any record, maintaining the mountains at a steady tropical temperature from root to summit. No one knows what it draws from. The mountain does not cool in winter. It does not warm further in summer. It holds, the way a body holds.

## Water

Because the air is hot and the heights are constantly shouldering through cloud, the massif drinks from the sky continuously. Every cold front that touches the warm stone condenses on contact. The result is that the rock *weeps* — everywhere, always. Cliff faces glisten and run. Overhangs hang with permanent curtains of drip, beaded lines of falling water spaced so evenly they look deliberate. In the hanging valleys, the water gathers into terraced marshes: broad, shallow, blood-warm pools stepping down the mountainsides in stairs hundreds of meters tall, each pool spilling its skin of water over a mossed lip into the next.

The pools are the loudest thing in the landscape after the insects. Their overflow is not a trickle but a regional sound — a layered white roar of ten thousand small spillways operating at once, varying in pitch with the day's condensation, never stopping. Underneath the roar there is a second sound, lower, felt in the sternum more than heard: a slow harmonic that rises from the stone itself, from whatever circulates in the pale veins. Long-term residents stop noticing it. Visitors describe it as the sound of something very large being very patient.

Steam stands over everything in the early hours, thick enough that the terraces vanish into white and you navigate by the sound of water alone. By midday the steam thins to a haze that softens every distance, so the far peaks float in pale gold and nothing in the landscape ever appears entirely solid.

## Vegetation

The plant life is dense to the point of contact — a high-altitude jungle that has had so long to settle into the heat and wet that it has stratified into permanent layers, like a society.

Lowest, the marsh terraces carry beds of reed and broadleaf aquatics, their stems sleeved in warm algal velvet. Above them, on every slope that holds soil, grows a closed canopy of trees that resemble enormous ferns more than anything woody — soft-trunked, water-fat, their fronds wide as sails and dark green going black in the folds. Their roots do not seek soil so much as the pale veins in the rock; you can find root systems wrapped around exposed thermoclasts like hands around a warm cup. The trees grow toward heat the way other forests grow toward light.

Everything is colonized. Trunks wear gardens; the gardens wear smaller gardens. Mosses here grow in slow waves — visibly banded, each band a generation, so that an old moss bank reads like tree rings laid flat. Lichens on the high cliffs have spread into patterns too regular to be accidental and too organic to be designed, broad geometric blooms hundreds of meters across, visible from the opposite valley wall. Some say the lichen is reading the vein-patterns beneath the rock and copying them onto the surface. Some say it is the other way around.

Nothing here grows fast. The heat is constant, the water is constant, and so urgency has been bred out of the ecology. A frond unfurls over a season. A terrace lip thickens its travertine by a fingernail's width per decade. The jungle is lush the way a deep archive is full.

## Fauna

The animal life is abundant and unhurried. Amphibians dominate — the warm pools breed them in enormous quantity, and at dusk the terraces erupt into a chorus that competes with the spillways and sometimes wins: deep, resonant calls from throat-sacs the size of melons, layered into chords that drift across whole valleys. Sleep here is something you learn to do inside the noise, not apart from it.

Above the pools move the gliders — long-bodied, frill-winged things that ride the columns of warm rising air between terraces and never seem to land for long. Flocks of them turn in the haze in slow spirals, following the thermals that follow the veins, so that on a clear afternoon you can map the mountain's hidden circulation just by watching where the animals circle.

In the canopy live the patient things: heavy, slow-moving browsers with prehensile everything, moving from frond to frond over hours, furred in the same moss that covers the trees until the distinction stops mattering. And in the gorges, in the unmapped deep, something large calls back when the frogs sing — a single answering note, once or twice a night, from far below. No one has gone down to look. There has never been a reason good enough.

## What Was Built

People have been coming to the massif for a very long time, and what they leave behind is modest, because the mountain provides almost everything and tolerates almost nothing. Scattered through the terraces you find:

— Stone shelters built into cliff overhangs, single-roomed, their door-lintels worn into smooth saddles by centuries of hands. Some are still used. Most are not. The moss has its banding well advanced across their floors.

— Markers at the terrace lips: upright slabs incised with measurements no one can still read, recording — it is assumed — water levels, or temperatures, or something the engine does that earlier visitors thought worth tracking.

— And here and there, half-swallowed by root systems, fragments of the *other* construction: surfaces of the pale vein-material shaped into deliberate forms. A perfect shallow basin. A doorway that frames nothing. A spiral channel that water enters and does not leave. These are warm like the veins, hollow like the cores, and old beyond the worn shelters by some unguessable factor. They were not built *on* the mountain. They give every impression of having been built *as* the mountain, with the rest of it raised around them.

## Rules of the World

Travelers learn the rules quickly, because the rules are gentle and the place enforces them without malice:

The heat is constant; dress for it and drink the pool water, which is clean — something in the terraces filters it, and no one has found out what.

Do not drill, cut, or pry at the pale veins. They will move away from you, and afterward, for a few days, the heat near your camp will be slightly withdrawn — a coolness like a turned shoulder. It always comes back. The mountain holds no grudges. But it notices.

Travel by sound when the steam is down. The spillways do not move; they are the most reliable map there is.

And the last rule, which is less a rule than an observation, passed between the few who stay: nothing here is in a hurry, and the mountain extends that courtesy to you. Whatever you came up the long stairs to escape or decide, the heat and the water and the chorus will hold steady around you for as long as it takes. The massif has been keeping itself warm for longer than anyone can measure, with no sign of effort and no sign of end.

It is, in the oldest sense of the word, *settled* — a place that finished becoming what it is long ago, and now simply, loudly, patiently continues.
