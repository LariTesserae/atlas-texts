# The Basin of New Ground

There is no light here. Not nighttime — nighttime implies a sun somewhere below the horizon, working its way back. This is a place where light is not among the available materials. Lanterns brought down into the basin burn correctly, consume their oil, give off heat you can hold your hands to, and illuminate nothing. The flame exists; the *shining* does not arrive. Travelers learn to stop striking matches after the third or fourth, the way you stop calling a name into a house you've realized is empty.

So the basin is known by other means, and it is known well, because it is full.

---

**The ground** is young. Not geologically young — *recently finished*. Underfoot it has the texture of something that set last week: a warm mineral crust, slightly tacky in the lowest places, like cooling slag or the skin on poured concrete. Edges here are sharp. Ridges of new stone stand up in clean fins, unblunted, and where the crust has cracked the fracture faces are glassy and exact. Nothing has had time to soften. Rain — and it rains often, a fine blood-warm drizzle out of the dark, condensing apparently from nothing — has only begun to write its first thin channels into the surface. You can trace them with a finger: shallow, hesitant, the opening sentences of erosion.

The basin is low and large. You feel its size in the air pressure and the way sound behaves — a dropped tool clinks once and the report comes back from several directions at staggered intervals, sketching a bowl of land miles across, walled by something at its rim that the echoes describe as *tall and ribbed*. Within the bowl: standing water everywhere. Sheets of it, knee-deep meres, threads of slow current moving between fresh stone fins. The water is warm as a held hand. It tastes faintly of iron and of something sweeter underneath, like sap, like the inside of a green stem, though almost nothing grows here. Vegetation is sparse and strange — isolated stands of pale reeds you find only by walking into them, rubbery and rootless, easy to pull free, and here and there a single tree-shaped thing whose bark is smooth and seamless, as if it were cast rather than grown, in a mold of a tree.

---

**The machines** are the great mystery and the great fact of the place. They are scattered — never clustered, never a complex, always one at a time, a quarter mile apart or more — and they are running. You come upon them in the dark first as heat, a furnace-warmth radiating across the water, then as vibration in the crust underfoot, and finally as shape under your hands: huge riveted housings, slick with condensation, flanks of warm iron taller than a house. Pistons move inside them at the tempo of breathing during sleep. One stroke per long minute, sometimes longer. Pipes run from their bases down into the ground and the pipes are hot, and they pulse.

It is widely believed, by those who have spent time in the basin and come back able to speak about it, that the machines are making the ground. That this whole low warm country was extruded, poured, set, and that the engines are still at it — that the sharpness of the stone is the sharpness of a product not yet shipped. The land is fresh from the works. If you press your ear to the crust between machines you can hear, very far down, a sound like slow industry: a continuous low manufacturing, patient as a tide.

There are smaller artifacts too, half-sunk in the meres: a flooded turbine, a stair of iron grating that ascends eleven steps and stops, a single electrified cable strung between two stone fins, humming faintly, connected at neither end.

---

**The animals.** The basin is *crowded*. This is the thing no description prepares you for — the density of living presence in the warm dark. Wading through the shallows you are never more than an arm's length from something. Soft bodies bump your shins and move on without alarm. The water is thick with slow swimmers; the air carries wings that pass close enough to fan your face; the stone fins are upholstered with clinging things that shift their grip as you pass. Reach out anywhere and your hand finds fur, or shell, or the cool pleated hide of something large and stationary that does not object to being touched.

And they are quiet. That is the second thing. A population this dense should roar, and instead the basin holds a hush like a full theater before the curtain — small sounds only: water dripping off a moving body, the click of many small feet on new stone, an occasional single call, low and brief, answered after a long pause from far across the water. The animals here do not seem to compete or flee or hunt, or if they do, they do it at a speed below perception. They are waiting, perhaps. Or grazing on something in the warm water. Or simply *inhabiting*, the way a finished building fills up with tenants.

No one has ever described one. In darkness this complete, a creature is only its texture, weight, warmth, and smell — wet wool, copper, crushed reeds — and no two encounters quite agree.

---

**The breakdown.** It is the geometry, mostly. The basin does not hold its shape between visits, or even between hours. Walk a straight line away from a machine, counting paces, and the machine's heat will sometimes reappear in front of you at pace four hundred — the same machine, you can tell by a dent in its housing your hands have learned. Distances are advisory. The echo-map of the basin rim never returns the same outline twice. Some surveyors maintain there is exactly one machine in the whole basin, encountered many times; others, that the basin manufactures distance the way it manufactures ground, and that the supply is uneven, pooling in some places, running thin in others — so that two stone fins an arm-span apart may take twenty minutes to walk between, and the far rim may be reached, occasionally, by accident, in nine steps.

Time pools too. The drizzle falls at slightly different rates on the backs of your two hands. A word spoken aloud will sometimes arrive at a companion three or four seconds late, intact, in your voice, while you both stand still. None of this happens quickly or violently. The basin's strangeness has the same temperament as its engines: one stroke per long minute. It is a *slow* impossibility, a reality settling unevenly as it cures, like the crust underfoot — full of stresses, cracking along clean glassy lines, but in no hurry.

---

What stays with visitors is the warmth and the company. You stand in shallow blood-warm water in absolute dark, in a country only weeks or centuries off the production line, and around you in every direction is the patient breathing of an iron engine and the patient breathing of ten thousand quiet animals, and underfoot the slow industry of the world being added to. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is in any hurry to be. The basin gives the strong impression of a place delivered ahead of schedule — stocked, heated, populated, running — and waiting, in the dark, for whatever the light was going to be for.
