**The Weir**

There is a bowl of rock above the cloud line, no wider across than a town square, and it is full.

Full of water, first: the basin holds it everywhere, in sheets and lenses and braided runnels that never sit still. Full of light: the overcast here is not a dimming but an amplifier, a white ceiling pressed low and lit from within, so that the whole sky glares like the inside of an eggshell and everything wet — which is everything — gives the glare back. There are no shadows in the bowl. A person standing in it casts only a faint blue smudge that follows them like an apology. Visitors who arrive without smoked lenses or slit goggles of carved horn go down to the cells with their eyes streaming, and stay indoors for a day.

Full, third, of bodies.

---

The rock itself is old beyond argument. Whatever violence raised this place finished its work long ago; the cirque walls are rounded, polished, fluted with ten thousand years of meltwater, scalloped like the inside of a shell. The stone weeps. Not in places — everywhere. Water comes out of the rock the way warmth comes out of a body, a continuous sweating, beading on every face of every boulder, gathering in the flutes, sliding down. At this altitude it should freeze, and it half-does: every surface carries a skin of ice thin as a fingernail, forming and shedding, forming and shedding, so that the walls of the bowl are perpetually glassed and perpetually shrugging the glass off in sheets that slide into the water below. This should be deafening. It is not. The ice comes off the rock the way a leaf comes off a branch — a slip, a settling. The loudest thing in the basin most hours is the click of it landing.

That is the first strangeness, and the one residents stop noticing soonest: the bowl eats sound. The air here is dense, cold, supersaturated — you can watch your breath fall rather than rise, sinking off your lips like poured milk — and in it, sound dies within arm's reach. A shout carries three paces and drops. Stone struck on stone makes a sensation more than a noise, a knock you feel in your teeth. The people of the Weir have given up on the voice for anything past intimacy. They speak hand-to-hand, palm against forearm, a pressure-language. At distance they signal with water: a paddle slapped flat on a pool sends a ring that the eye reads where the ear cannot. The basin is laced with their signal-pools, kept clear of ice for exactly this purpose, a telegraph of ripples.

---

The floor of the bowl does not hold still. This is the second strangeness and the central fact of life here. The meltwater braids reorganize themselves hourly — a channel that ran along the eastern terraces at dawn will have abandoned its bed by noon and opened a new one through what was, that morning, dry ground. Pools migrate. A tarn the size of a room will, over three or four days, walk itself across the basin floor, its leading edge dissolving gravel, its trailing edge leaving a glazed pavement behind. Sometimes the water mounds. There is no wind to explain it and no slope: the surface of a pool will simply gather itself upward into a smooth swelling, hold the shape — a dome, a ridge, once famously a long low wall of standing water that bisected the settlement for half a day — and then let go, all without a sound louder than rain.

The people do not pretend to understand this. They have a word for the water that translates roughly as *the neighbor*. You do not fight a neighbor's habits; you build around them.

And build they do, endlessly. The settlement — perhaps forty drystone cells, corbelled beehive domes stacked two and three deep against the southern wall of the cirque where the rock weeps least — is the most heavily maintained structure most travelers will ever see. There is no mortar at this altitude and no timber; everything is stacked stone, hand-fitted, and the water unbuilds it as a matter of course. A migrating pool undermines a lower course; an uphill runnel decides to come through a doorway; ice grows in a joint overnight and shoulders it apart by morning. So the Weir is never finished and never ruined. At any hour, a third of its population is rebuilding it — silently, in the white glare, figures in oiled wool and birdskin moving stone in long bucket-lines of hands, the whole hamlet humming with motion and producing almost no noise at all, like an anthill behind glass. The walls show their history in strata: courses laid by grandmothers at the bottom, courses laid this morning at the top, all of it weathered to the same wet grey within a season. The settlement has stood here, by its own count, ninety generations. Not one stone is where it started.

Their tools are the bowl's own: hammerstones, bone pry-bars, gut cordage, sledges of braided hide for moving slabs across the ice-skin. Heat comes from rendered fat burned in stone lamps, and from the strangest economy of the place — the warm seeps, fist-sized vents in the basin floor where the weeping water comes up faintly heated, around which the cells cluster and over which, by ancient agreement, no one may build a roof.

---

Almost nothing green grows here. A black crust of lichen on the drier overhangs; cushions of moss like drowned velvet at the pool margins; one species of pale, fleshy cress that grows only at the warm seeps and is eaten ceremonially, a leaf at a time. That is the flora entire.

The life, instead, is animal, and there is shockingly much of it.

The cirque walls host a colony of white swift-like birds — thousands of them, nesting in the water-fluted grooves, glazed each morning into a faint shine by the ice-skin and shrugging out of it with the same soundless slip as everything else. They are mute, or near it; they fly in dense rotating columns above the basin, threshing the bright air, and the only sound of the whole gyre is the air itself, a breath. They feed on the midge-swarms that hang over the warm seeps in standing pillars, visible from across the bowl as smudges of grey in the glare. The pools are crowded — pale crayfish, blind and the color of candlewax; black newts that overwinter frozen solid in the ice-skin and walk out of it in the thaw, unbothered; small silver fish that arrive in any newly opened pool within a day, by routes no one has ever traced, as if the water carries them folded up inside itself and unpacks them where it pleases. The people regard the fish the way they regard everything the water does: as the neighbor's business. They eat them anyway.

And there are the molts. The birds shed, the newts shed, the crayfish shed, and in this airless cold nothing rots quickly, so the basin floor between the channels is drifted with castings — feather, skin, translucent shell — a pale wrack that the migrating water gathers, sorts, and arranges. It deposits these leavings in lines. In rings. Once, the elders say, in something that resembled the settlement itself, laid out in feathers on a gravel flat, accurate to the doorways, a day before a pool came through and took the eastern cells.

They rebuilt to the east anyway. You do not read too much into a neighbor's gestures. You stack the stone, you watch the ripples, you keep your goggles on against the light, and you live in the one quiet place in the world where nothing ever stops moving.
