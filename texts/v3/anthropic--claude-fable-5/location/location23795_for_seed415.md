**The Rookery at Nine Thousand Meters**

Begin with the mountain, because the mountain is not finished.

It is young the way almost nothing on Earth is young — extruded, not carved. The summit ridge went up faster than weather could get purchase on it, and so the rock has no history written on it at all: no frost-shatter, no scallop of wind, no lichen stain older than a decade. The basalt is glassy in places, still faintly conchoidal where it cooled against air, and its edges are sharp enough to open a glove. Climbers who reach this altitude on old mountains describe a landscape ground down to essentials. Here it is the opposite. Everything is excess, everything is first-draft. Seismographs bolted lower on the flank record the peak gaining height — centimeters a year, sometimes centimeters a week — a mountain still being spoken.

And near the top, where the ridge splits like a badly healed seam, there is the pocket.

You would miss it from thirty meters away. From the outside it is only a dark crease in the young stone, exhaling. From the inside it is a room — that is the only honest word — perhaps twenty paces across at its widest, walls of new rock rising and closing overhead into a slot of ordinary grey daylight. The light that falls in is unremarkable, mid-morning light, the light of an overcast Tuesday anywhere. What it falls *on* is not.

The floor of the pocket is warm. Not hot — the deep vents that feed it are throttled by the same fresh geology that made it, and what reaches the surface is a breath, a constant low exhalation of humid air at the temperature of skin. It meets the alpine cold coming down through the slot above and the two argue endlessly, and the argument condenses. Every surface drips. Water beads on the glassy rock, runs in threads no wider than string, gathers in cupped hollows the size of a palm, and never accumulates into anything you could call a pool. There is no stream here, no ice, no snow — just a permanent fine sweat over everything, a room that never dries.

On this sweat, the garden.

The vegetation is not sparse alpine cushion-plant survival. It is *lush*, obscenely so for the altitude — the geothermal breath keeps the pocket ten degrees ahead of the mountain around it, cool but livable, a cellar climate hoisted into the sky. Moss goes up the walls in continuous pelts, deep enough to sink a hand into to the wrist, banded in greens so saturated they read almost black in the folds. Filmy ferns — leaves one cell thick, translucent as insect wings — colonize every seep line. There are liverworts layered on liverworts, a shag of tiny sedges where guano and mineral water mix, and hanging from the overhung sections, curtains of some pale epiphytic growth that no survey has formally named, because no survey has stayed long enough. Nothing here is spaced out. Everything touches everything. The plant community is stacked like a crowd in a lift, and it is all young — first-generation vegetation on first-generation rock, an ecosystem with no ruins in it, no dead layer underneath. It is all foreground.

Then the birds.

The swiftlets found the pocket before anyone else did — before the rock had fully finished ticking as it cooled, if you believe the drill crew's stories. They nest in the upper walls where the vent-warmth pools, and there are not hundreds of them. There are not thousands. The colony saturates the pocket the way water saturates the moss: every ledge, every vesicle in the basalt large enough to hold a saliva-cemented cup of a nest, occupied, contested, defended. At any moment a measurable fraction of the air in the room is bird. They pour up through the slot at dawn to hunt the insect drift on the high winds and pour back down through the day in rotating shifts, and when they are inside they navigate the dim lower reaches by clicking — a hard, dry, ratcheting sonar, thousands of individuals ranging at once.

This is why the pocket is loud, and it is loud beyond the word's usual duty. The click-chorus alone would be deafening; layered under it is the wingbeat, a continuous fluttering roar like rain on a metal roof scaled up until it lives in your sternum; under *that*, the vent's steady hydraulic hiss; and threaded through all of it, mechanical and out of place, a fourth voice: the turbine.

Because someone built here. Not much — the built presence is a scatter, an afterthought, gear rather than architecture. A geothermal survey crew came up years ago, drawn by the anomaly of a warm wet room near a growing summit, and they sank a slim borehole in the pocket's floor and capped it with a compact turbine housing no larger than a chest freezer, painted a safety orange now furred over entirely with moss except where the vibration keeps one panel clean. It still runs. It was never decommissioned — the crew left in a weather window and the funding left with them — and the vent has been spinning it ever since, feeding a string of instruments bolted to the walls: strain gauges across the seam, a tiltmeter on a tripod half-swallowed by fern, a data logger in a steel box with a hairline antenna threading up through the slot toward a relay no one is certain still listens. Conduit runs along the rock in dripping loops. A single caged work-light burns above the borehole, redundant in the daytime, unkillable at night. The swiftlets nest on the warm turbine housing. Two of the strain gauges have nests built directly onto them, which means somewhere a dataset shows the mountain's growth contaminated, very slightly, with heartbeats.

The insects are the layer below the birds, and they are why the birds can be so many: the vent-breath carries a constant bloom of midges and vent-flies up from the warm floor, a shimmering column of protein rising through the room like slow smoke, harvested from all sides. Below the insects, the snails — small, pale, thin-shelled things grazing the moss in densities that make the green surfaces glitter faintly when you look closely, as though the walls were beaded with wet seeds. Spiders string the fern curtains. Something small and mammalian — no one has caught one — leaves runs through the moss pelts and takes eggs from the lowest nests. The food web here is not a diagram; it is a pressure. Every trophic level is over-full, packed into a volume the size of a modest chapel, and the whole thing cycles daily with the swiftlets' shifts: the roar swelling toward dusk when the colony returns in force, the click-chorus peaking in the dark, the pre-dawn eruption upward through the slot like the room exhaling its entire population at once.

And underneath all of it, on a timescale the birds cannot perceive and the instruments barely can, the room itself is changing shape. The seam is a live structure in a live mountain. The strain gauges show the walls creeping apart at the pace of a fingernail — the pocket is widening, year over year, the mountain unfolding it like a slow bud. New cracks appear with small dry reports that vanish instantly into the general roar; within a season the moss finds them; within two, the swiftlets do. The oldest nests in the pocket sit on rock younger than the birds that built them.

That is the strangest thing about the place, in the end — not the noise, not the impossible garden at killing altitude, not the orphaned turbine spinning for no one. It is the inversion of the usual order. Everywhere else, life arrives late, onto old stone, into finished rooms. Here the room is not finished. The rock is the youngest thing present, and everything living in the pocket — the moss, the ferns, the glittering snails, the ten thousand clicking birds, even the moss-furred machine faithfully logging it all — is, in a sense, older than the ground. They are not inhabiting the mountain.

They are attending its birth, at the top of the sky, at the top of their lungs.
