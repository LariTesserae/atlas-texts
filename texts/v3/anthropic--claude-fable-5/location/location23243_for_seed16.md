**The Kiln Ward**

You come down into it through a crack in the slag — a seam no wider than a doorway, and then the ground opens like a throat, and you are inside.

It is a city the size of a courtyard. That is the first strangeness: the whole settlement, all its terraces and stairs and chimney-lanes, fits within a single hollow in the rock, a bubble cooled into old lava long before anyone thought to live in it. The chamber is perhaps eighty paces across at its widest, but it has been built *upward* and *inward* for so long that it feels larger than it is — galleries stacked seven and eight deep against the curved walls, bridges of fired brick arcing across the central void, ladders of bone-dry wood lashed with cord that has fossilized into something like wire. The middle of the chamber is empty air, top to bottom. The builders kept it that way deliberately. Everything important here happens in that emptiness.

There is no water. There has never been water. The floor is a mosaic of brick and cracked basalt, swept clean — swept by *something*, the dust lies in long deliberate drifts at the edges — and every surface radiates heat. Not the heat of sun; the chamber is dim, lit only by what filters down the entrance seam and by a sullen amber glow from fissures low in the walls, where the rock is still warm from whatever made it. The air sits at the temperature of a body with a fever, and the deeper galleries are hotter still. You sweat, and your sweat vanishes before it can run.

And yet the place is green. Or not green exactly — the vegetation here is the color of dark honey and oxidized copper, and it grows thick on every south-facing surface, which in this inverted world means every surface that faces the warm fissures. These are heat-drinkers: plants with roots of black glass that wedge into the brickwork and pull thermal current up through their stems the way other plants pull water. Their leaves are stiff, waxy, faintly translucent — hold one to the fissure-light and you can see fluid moving in it, slow and amber, something thicker than sap. They flower constantly. The flowers are small ceramic-looking cups that open with an audible *tick*, like cooling pottery.

That ticking is everywhere, and it is only the quietest layer of the sound. The Kiln Ward is *loud* — startlingly loud for a place with no animal in it, no bird, no insect, nothing with a heartbeat. The brick itself talks all day, expanding and contracting as heat pulses through the walls in slow waves, and the talk is a constant percussion: pops, groans, the long shearing creak of a bridge taking up thermal slack. The seed pods of the heat-drinkers detonate when ripe — a sharp *crack*, then the rattle of glassy seeds skittering down through the galleries, sometimes for ten or fifteen seconds, bouncing brick to brick toward the floor. And under everything there is the chamber's own voice: the hollow acts as a resonator, and the fissures breathe, drawing air in and pushing it out on a cycle of some minutes, a deep organ-note that you feel in your sternum before you identify it as sound.

The construction is old and humble and very good. Whoever built here had no metal worth mentioning — you find tools abandoned in wall-niches, and they are basalt hand-axes, obsidian scrapers, mallets of dense scorched wood. But they understood fire and they understood brick, and they had endless heat for free. Every block in the place was fired in the fissures themselves; you can see the kiln-mouths, brick collars built around the hottest cracks, blackened and glazed from generations of use. The mortar is fused glass. The masonry has settled into itself over what must be centuries — joints worn round, stairs dished by feet, lintels sagging into stable curves. Nothing is new. Nothing is ruined either. The place is in balance, weathered the way a riverbed is weathered, except no river ever ran here.

There are no inhabitants. There are no remains of inhabitants. The galleries stand empty — and the emptiness is profound; you can walk the whole ward and touch nothing but brick and plant — but the city does not feel dead, because it has never stopped *working*. The heat-drinkers grow measurably day to day, colonizing along the warm seams, dying back where a fissure cools, their dropped leaves curling into brittle scrolls that the breathing of the chamber herds into the corner-drifts. The fissure-glow waxes and wanes on a cycle like weather. Pods burst, seeds rain into the void, the few that land on warm brick root within a season. The bridges flex. The organ-note rises and falls.

If there is a strangeness beyond the obvious, it is this: the layout of the galleries follows the vegetation, not the other way around. The builders shaped their city around where the heat-drinkers chose to grow — doorways skirt root-masses, whole terraces bend to accommodate a single ancient plant whose glass roots now hold a wall together better than any mortar could. It is hard to say, looking at the oldest quarters, whether the people tended the plants or the plants permitted the people.

Either way, only one party stayed.

Stand in the central void, on the swept floor, in the dim amber, and listen. The cracking, the ticking, the seeds falling like sparse hard rain, the slow breath of the rock. A city of eighty paces, hollow at the heart, hot as a banked fire, talking ceaselessly to no one.
