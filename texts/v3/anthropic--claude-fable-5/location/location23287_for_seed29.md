**The Vesicle**

High on the shoulder of a mountain that did not exist a thousand years ago, under rock so new it still smells of its own making, there is a room.

Not a built room. Nothing made it in the sense of hands or plans. It is a gas bubble — a vesicle — trapped in the rhyolite when the dome last inflated, a pocket about the size of a small chapel, sealed inside hundreds of meters of stone at an altitude where the outside air, if you could reach it, would be thin and burning-cold. Inside, it is neither. Inside it is hot the way the inside of an engine block is hot: a dry, pressing, mineral heat, somewhere past sixty degrees, radiating from every surface at once because every surface is connected to the same cooling magma body far below.

The walls have never been weathered. That is the first thing you would notice if you could see — and you mostly cannot, the darkness here is nearly total, broken only by a faint and sourceless rust-colored glow where one fissure in the floor runs deep enough that the rock below still remembers incandescence. By that dim light, in glimpses: edges. Everything in the chamber is edged. Volcanic glass fractured into blades that have never met rain, never met wind, never been touched by anything that dulls. The floor is not a floor but a frozen turbulence, ropes and shelves and broken bubbles of stone, every contour exactly as it was on the day it stopped moving. This is geology before erosion has had its first word. The mountain is still being drafted.

And yet the chamber works. That is the strangest thing about it — it works, ceaselessly, like industry. A fissure at the low end of the room admits gas from the cooling body below, and the pocket breathes on a pressure cycle measured in minutes: a building hiss, rising to a hard metallic shriek as gas forces through a throat of rock no wider than a wrist, then a percussive *chunk* as a flake of cooling stone shifts and reseats itself, the same flake, the same sound, thousands of times a day, a valve no one machined. Condensing minerals plate the walls in production runs. Sulfur sublimates out of the hot gas and crystallizes on the cooler ceiling in needles, in fretworks, in brittle yellow chandeliers that grow a millimeter a year and occasionally fail, shattering downward with a sound like dropped glassware in the dark. Iron and silica precipitate in banded layers, stratum on stratum, quality-controlled by chemistry alone. If you ran your hand along the wall — gloved, you would want it gloved — you would feel the regularity of it, ribbed and laminated, factory-finished. The mountain is a refinery that manufactures only itself, on a schedule of decades, and it is *loud* about it. The hiss never stops. Under the hiss, a deeper sound: the slow groan of the dome settling, rock under load adjusting by micrometers, transmitted through the walls like the complaint of a hull.

The chamber is full. That is the second strangest thing. In a space with no light, no plants, no soil, almost no water — only a thin acid dew that beads on the one wall cool enough to condense it, single drops that hang for days before falling — life has packed itself in to the point of saturation. The gas rising from below carries reduced sulfur, and on every surface where temperature and chemistry permit, the rock wears a living finish: films of chemosynthetic bacteria in pale grays and ember-oranges, lacquered over the mineral bands, eating the mountain's exhaust. And on the films, grazers. The room crawls. Heat-hardened mites in numbers beyond counting, moving over the walls in slow traffic, so dense in places that the wall itself seems to have a grain that drifts. Blind springtails harvesting the bacterial mats around the dew-wall. Pale, long-legged crickets — eyeless, antennae longer than their bodies — stationed in the cooler recesses near the ceiling, reading the room entirely through vibration, which here is like reading by floodlight. Small predatory beetles patrol the laminated shelves, glossy as the glass they walk on. Centipedes the color of old wax thread through fractures too narrow for anything else, hunting by heat. Every niche is held. Every surface is claimed. There is no empty square centimeter that is not either too hot to live on or already occupied by something that found its exact thermal limit and settled there, generations deep, in a place its lineage has never left and could never leave.

Nothing here is fast. The animals move with the deliberateness of creatures budgeting every calorie against the heat. The minerals grow at the speed of patience. The pressure cycle repeats and repeats, the valve-flake chunks and reseats, and the only events are slow ones: a sulfur chandelier reaching its breaking weight after forty years, a fissure widening by a hair as the dome cools, the bacterial mats migrating a hand's-width per decade as the thermal gradient creeps. The chamber will not be here forever — the mountain is young and unfinished, and one day the drafting will resume, the walls will shift, the room will close or split open to the killing cold outside. But that is far off, by the only clocks that matter here.

For now: a sealed hot room high inside a newborn mountain, black, roaring, ribbed with self-made machinery of stone, and crowded wall to wall with small blind lives that have never known there is anywhere else.
