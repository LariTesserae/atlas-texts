**The Pleach**

You come up into it from below, through cloud, and the first thing you understand is that there is no ground here and never was. The whole world at this altitude is woven.

Imagine a basket the size of a continent. Not a basket sitting on land — the basket *is* the land, suspended at a height where the air is thin and clean and warm in a way that thin air should not be, as if the structure itself exhales heat. Every surface you will ever touch here is plant: stem grafted to stem, vine plaited through vine, vast trunks bent generations ago into arches and held in tension until they fused and forgot they were ever separate things. Nothing here is *natural*, in the sense of having arisen on its own. Every span was bent by hands. Every junction shows, if you look closely, the old marks of the work — the bite of a bone awl, the wrap of cordage long since absorbed into bark, the puckered scar where a splice took. The tools were simple. The patience was not.

Whoever made it is gone, or so far away in the weave that the distinction doesn't matter.

**The terrain.** Underfoot, the weave is dense enough to walk on for leagues at a stretch: a floor of pleached limbs, mossed over, spongy, warm. It rises and falls in long swells like a sea that set. Then, without warning, it opens. The floor thins to a lattice and then to single spans, and you find yourself crossing a living bridge no wider than your two feet over a hollow so large it has its own weather — a gulf of dim air where a single rain cloud drifts, contained, browsing the interior like a grazing animal. These voids are the dominant fact of the place. The Pleach is mostly *absence*: enormous vaulted emptiness held open by ribs of grown wood, chamber after chamber, some of them so vast that the far wall is only a darker quality of the dark. You can travel for a day and touch the structure only with the soles of your feet.

Everything weeps. The stems sweat; condensation beads on the underside of every span and falls in slow, countable drops into the gulfs below — you never hear them land. Mist pools in the low chambers. The wood is never dry and never rotten; it has reached some old agreement with the water. Pale water collects in the cupped junctions where four or five great limbs meet, forming hanging pools, lens-still, skinned with green. Things drink there. You will rarely see them doing it.

**The light** comes from nowhere in particular. The outer weave is too thick for sky, and yet a dim grey-gold suffusion fills the chambers, brightest at certain knots in the lattice, as if light were a sap the structure carries and occasionally lets seep. It is the light of late evening, permanently. Shadows are soft, doubled, and — this is the first wrongness you will notice — they do not always agree about where the light is.

**The wrongness.** It begins gently. You walk a long gallery of arches and realize the arches are not getting closer together; *you* are getting smaller, or the weave repeats itself at every scale and you have drifted down a scale without crossing anything. A knot in a handrail of living vine, examined closely, cannot be traced: the strand passes over, under, over, and then through itself, cleanly, the way a thing can only do if it has briefly used a direction your eyes don't own. The old builders' splices are full of these. It may be how the whole structure stands — tension borrowed from somewhere adjacent to the world.

Distances do not commute. The chamber you crossed in an hour takes four days to cross back, and the moss on the spans has aged accordingly. Some passages are longer inside than the wall that contains them. There is a region travelers mark by tying a strip of bark to a strut, where every junction is the *same* junction, encountered from twelve different directions, and your own bark-strip is already there when you arrive to tie it, weathered grey, years old.

The Pleach is settled in this. None of it feels broken or new. The impossibilities are weathered, moss-grown, load-bearing. The place has been folded wrong for so long that wrongness is its grain.

**The sound** is the second thing visitors mention, after the scale: there nearly isn't any. A structure this size should groan. It doesn't. The voids swallow noise; your own footsteps arrive at your ears slightly delayed, as if the report had to travel by some longer internal route. What sound there is, is small and precise. The tick of a drop of condensate striking a leaf somewhere overhead. A long, soft creak, once an hour, like a continent shifting its weight in sleep. And occasionally — far off, never close — a low resonant note, struck and let fade, that might be a hollow trunk being knocked by something, or might be the structure itself ringing where two of its folded distances touch.

**The animals** are everywhere and almost never present. The Pleach is abundantly inhabited the way a quiet house is inhabited: by evidence. Smooth-worn trails along the great limbs, polished by generations of pads. Nests woven into the weave with the same techniques as the weave itself, indistinguishable from the architecture except by being warm. Pale, long-bodied gliders that cross the gulfs in total silence and are gone before the eye finishes finding them. Soft-shelled things that graze the algae skins of the hanging pools at night. Slow herds of something large move through the deep galleries — you find their dung, their shed plates of bark-coloured armour, the broad polished roads they keep open — but in the standing dimness they keep a chamber or two ahead of any walker, always. Some of the fauna has learned the folds: there is a small bird-like creature that escapes predators by flying into a knot and out of a different knot a mile away, and a patient ambush animal, never described twice the same way, that is said to wait inside the longer-than-its-wall passages, where prey, by walking forward, delivers itself.

Predation here is nearly soundless. You find the aftermath, never the act.

**The processes.** For all its emptiness, the Pleach is busy. Interior clouds make their slow circuits of the great chambers, raining themselves out against the latticed ceilings, condensing again below. The weave grows — slowly, on the timescale of the very old, a finger-width a year — and the growth still follows the bends and tensions the builders set, as if the structure remembers its instructions. Splices heal. Failed spans are not replaced by anyone; they are *resorbed*, drawn back into the body of the weave over decades, the hollow they leave becoming one more vault. Spores drift in the gulf air and glow faintly at the dim knots of light. The whole thing respires: in the warm hours the chambers exhale upward through the outer weave and the mist thins; later, the air settles, sweetens, and beads on everything again.

Whether the makers will return — whether they are still working, somewhere, bending green limbs with cord and bone and standing back to wait fifty years — the Pleach gives no answer. The newest tool-marks anyone has found were old. But here and there a traveler reports a limb bent and lashed in a fresh green arc, sap still bright at the binding, in a chamber that, on the return journey, cannot be found again. The structure keeps its own counsel about whether it is finished.

It is warm. It is quiet. It drips. It holds you a few miles above an unseen world on a craft of plaited living wood older than its own geometry, and it is in no hurry whatsoever.
