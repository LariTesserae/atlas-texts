The plateau has no proper name anymore, which is fitting, because names are one of the things that have worn away.

It is a continent that finished. Whatever orogeny raised it happened so long ago that the mountains it once carried have been planed down to a memory of themselves — long whaleback swells of grey stone, none more than a few dozen meters higher than its neighbors, rolling away to a horizon that is, depending on the day, fifty kilometers off or further than the eye should be able to reach. The whole landmass sits high, two or three kilometers above whatever seas may still exist below the permanent cloud-skirts at its rim. Up here the air is thin and cold and clean, the light a steady, sourceless pewter-bright overcast that brightens and dims on its own schedule, not always in agreement with anything you'd call a sun.

The bedrock is the oldest stable thing in the world, and it looks it. Not jagged — nothing here is jagged — but smoothed past smoothness, into a kind of softness that stone shouldn't have. Outcrops rise from the ground like the backs of submerged animals, their surfaces rounded as river pebbles, marked with long shallow grooves where ice or water or simple duration dragged across them for ages. In places the rock has worn so far down that it has stopped being entirely confident about being rock. You can press a palm against an outcrop on a foggy morning and feel it give, very slightly, the way a thing gives when it has been the same thing for too long and the insistence has gone out of it.

**Water**

It is never dry here and never wet. The plateau lives inside a regime of cold dampness: fog banks the size of small nations slide across the tableland on their own circuits, condensing on every surface, beading the stone, dripping from the undersides of ledges in a slow irregular percussion that is, most days, the loudest sound for kilometers. Rain comes intermittently — fine, drifting, more a thickening of the fog than a weather event — and when it passes, the whole landscape steams faintly, exhaling.

There are no real rivers left. The land is too flat; water has nowhere ambitious to go. Instead it gathers in pockets: pans of black, lens-still water lying in the hollows between stone swells, some a few paces across, some broad enough that their far shores dissolve into the haze. The pools are cold enough to ache and so still that they reflect with unsettling fidelity — and the reflections are one of the places the breakdown shows first. A pool will reflect the sky correctly but show a city on its surface that is not on its shore. Travelers, if there were travelers, would learn to read the pools the way sailors read clouds: as reports from elsewhere, not always elsewhere in space.

**The green**

Vegetation here is lush the way a velvet cushion is lush — low, dense, saturated — but it comes in islands. Most of the plateau is bare pavement and gravel and the bones of hills. Then, scattered across that emptiness at intervals of kilometers, the green pockets: hollows and lee-slopes where the fog drains and pools, and in them, sudden extravagance.

The pockets are dwarf forests, knee-high to shoulder-high, ancient beyond reckoning. Cushion plants in mounds a thousand years thick, so firm you could stand on them. Lichens in every cold color — sage, rust, bone-white, a particular luminous grey-green that seems to hold light after the overcast dims. Mosses deep enough to swallow an arm. Small contorted trees, hard as horn, their trunks no thicker than a wrist but ringed, if you cut one (nothing cuts them), with more rings than the wood should hold. Everything grows at biological pace and yet the growth is *visible* if you are patient: sit beside a moss bank through one full fog-cycle and you can watch it swell a finger's width, drinking.

The vegetation, like the stone, is settling into final form. Some of the oldest cushion mounds have begun to simplify. Their structure smooths out; the individual leaves become a suggestion of leaves, then a texture, then just a green firmness, a generalization of plant. Botanically they are still alive — they respire, they darken in rain — but they have stopped bothering with detail. At the heart of the largest pocket-forests there are shapes that are unmistakably *tree* without being any tree in particular: the idea of a tree, eroded out of all the trees that ever stood there, holding the spot.

Nothing animal moves in the green. No insect hum, no birds threading the fog, not even the small archaeology of bones. Whatever this ecology once fed, it has outlived. The pockets pollinate, if they pollinate, by fog.

**The cities**

And yet the plateau is inhabited — or built, at least, which is no longer quite the same claim.

Scattered across the tableland, one to a horizon, more or less, stand settlements of dry-laid stone. Real cities, some of them: districts, lanes, stairways, terraced cisterns for catching the fog-drip, plazas paved with flags worn into shallow basins by feet. The construction is patient and metal-poor — corbelled vaults instead of true arches, beehive roofs, lintels of single slabs that must have taken fifty hands to raise, walls of fitted stone so precise that moss is the only mortar. Everything in them could have been made with hammerstones, antler picks, rope, time. Mostly time.

The cities are empty in the way the green pockets are empty, but they are not dead, and this is where the plateau stops being merely old and becomes what it actually is.

The walls maintain themselves. Not dramatically — you will never see a stone leap into place — but a wall that has slumped will, over a span of foggy days, be found re-stacked, the courses sorted, the batter corrected, in exactly the hand of the original masons. The cisterns stay clean. The stair-treads renew their wear patterns, hollowing in the middle as though traffic continued. The cities have been built and lived in for so long that building and inhabitation have become properties of the stone, the way magnetism is a property of iron. The masons eroded away; the masonry kept the habit.

There is some evidence — disputed by no one, because there is no one to dispute it — that all the cities are the same city. Walk out of one through its western gate, travel four days across bare stone, and the next settlement you reach will be a different size, a different plan, set against a different hill — and the basin worn into the third stair of its great staircase will fit your memory of the first city exactly, and the chip you idly knocked from a lintel will be there, fresh. Distance on the plateau is honored loosely, the way a very old person honors appointments. The horizon sometimes curves upward at the edges, faintly, so that on the clearest days you can see, far off and slightly *above* you, the grey swells and green pockets and a small pale city — and the city is the one you are standing in, seen from outside.

**Breakdown as erosion**

The right way to understand this place is that erosion here did not stop at the rock.

Everything wears down. Stone wears to roundness, plants wear to the generic, and underneath both of these, the finer grain of the world — the part that keeps places distinct from each other, keeps moments in order, keeps a thing's edges crisp against the things it is not — has been weathering too, for the same immense duration, under the same patient fog. The plateau is what reality looks like in its oldest stable state. Not shattered, not chaotic: *planed*. The wild violence of a young world's physics has all been carried away grain by grain, and what's left is broad and flat and gently, pervasively wrong.

So: facts here have erosional landforms. A direction can be worn through, like a rock arch, so that west leads briefly into yesterday before collapsing back into mere west. The fog cycles are weather, real weather, vast and self-organized — but some of them are *the same* fog, the identical bank returning at long intervals with the same droplets in the same arrangement, the world reusing a day it made well rather than going to the expense of a new one. Echoes outlive their sounds; in certain plazas you can hear, very faintly and only in the deepest stillness, the dripping of a rain that hasn't fallen yet, or fell so long ago the stone it fell on is gone.

None of this feels violent. That is the strangest part. A young world's anomalies would scream. Here the impossible has the same settled, weathered character as everything else — old paradoxes worn smooth, lying around like boulders, safe to lean against.

**Sound and motion**

It is mostly quiet, but it is not still. The plateau is busy the way a glacier is busy.

Fog rivers pour silently along the hollows. Cloud shadows the size of provinces cross the tableland. The pools rise and fall on a slow breath-rhythm of condensation. The green pockets swell and dim with the moisture cycles, their colors shifting through the day's pewter light. In the cities, the slow self-masonry proceeds. Far off, sometimes, a section of horizon will quietly exchange itself for a different section, with no more drama than a cloud changing shape.

What you hear, standing in a plaza with your hood back and your breath smoking: drip. Drip. The threadbare hiss of fine rain on moss. A wind so unobstructed it has nothing to sound against and so arrives mostly as pressure and cold. And beneath all of it, at the very floor of hearing, a tone that may be the plateau itself — not a rumble, nothing geological in the ordinary sense, but the low sustained note of an enormous thing that has finished changing and is now simply, vastly, continuing.

The cities catch fog in their cisterns. The moss drinks. The stairs hollow themselves a little deeper for the feet that have eroded out of existence but whose walking, like everything else here, was repeated long enough to become permanent. The plateau is what remains when a world has been used so thoroughly, for so long, that use itself is the last thing standing — a continent-sized habit, green in its hollows, damp, cold, patient, going on.
