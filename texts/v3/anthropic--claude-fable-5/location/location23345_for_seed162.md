# The Foundry Tablelands

You come to it from below, always — there is no other way, because the tablelands ride higher than anything else on this world, a continent-sized terrace of new stone lifted into the warm under-belly of a permanent cloud deck. The light up here never resolves into day. It arrives pre-diffused, the color of pewter warmed in a hand, and it falls without shadows, so that distance becomes a matter of faith. A ridge ten kilometers off and a ridge ten meters off render in the same soft gray-green, and you learn to judge nearness by smell instead: hot mineral, wet chlorophyll, and underneath both, faintly, the odor of solder.

That last one takes longer to explain.

## The Stone

The rock is young the way a scab is young. Nothing here has been weathered into kindness. Every escarpment is a clean fracture, every canyon a fresh split, edges sharp enough to read braille off of, and the strata exposed in the cliff faces are still glossy — not polished by water but *unworn*, never yet touched by enough rain to dull them. In places the stone is visibly warm. You can put a palm against a cliff and feel a slow heat coming through, steady, regulated, like the side of a kiln hours after firing, and the dampness of the air condenses on the cooler faces and runs down them in thin braided films, the only rivers this country has. There are no old watercourses because there is no *old*. Water here exists as sweat: beading on leaves, fogging in hollows, gathering in the sharp-cornered basins the land has only just finished making, pooling black and still until the land changes its mind and tilts the basin empty.

Because the land does change its mind. That is the first thing the tablelands teach you. The horizon is not a fact here; it is a draft.

## The Industry

There are no structures on the tablelands. Nothing built, nothing stacked, no wall, no road, not one mark of a deciding hand. And yet the place is unmistakably industrial — this is the contradiction you live inside the whole time you are here, and it never stops itching.

The industry is in the *processes*. Watch any slope long enough and you will see it working. Seams in the rock dilate and extrude fresh stone the way a press extrudes wire — slow, even, continuous, a ridge lengthening itself at perhaps a meter an hour, hot at the tip, annealing as it goes. Certain valleys function as assembly lines without ever having been designed as anything: mineral-rich condensate runs down a graded sequence of warm terraces, and at each terrace something is added — a film of metallic precipitate here, a colony of accreting organisms there — so that what enters the top of the valley as fog leaves the bottom as a glittering grit of perfectly uniform spherules, which the wind then takes and distributes, and from which new vegetation visibly grows. The land is a factory whose product is more land, more growth, more of itself, and whose floor plan is the geology.

Electricity is everywhere and nowhere. The vegetation carries it. You see no machines, but you see the *behavior* of machines: ferns that ratchet open in synchronized banks like louvers responding to a signal; vines as thick as your leg that pulse with a dim interior light, blue-white, in long traveling waves, clearly conducting charge from the hot extrusion zones out to the cooler margins; groves that all turn at once, silently, with the coordinated precision of something switched. At dusk — insofar as the dimness has a dusk, a deepening — entire hillsides come up in a faint capacitor glow, and you can trace the circuitry of the country in pale lines across the dark, a schematic of itself drawn at continental scale, with no schematic-maker anywhere in evidence.

It is very quiet. That is the eeriest part. A factory this size should roar. Instead there is a hush with texture in it — a sub-audible hum you feel in your sternum more than hear, the drip of condensation off ten billion leaves, the occasional soft *tick* of cooling stone, and now and then, from far off, a single deep percussive note, felt through the soles of the feet, as some district of the tablelands finishes a process and settles. Between these sounds the silence is total in a way that makes you aware of your own blood. The industry runs muffled, lubricated, internal. Whatever is being made here is being made the way a body makes things: without announcement.

## The Vegetation

Lush is the wrong word, though it is the nearest one. The growth is dense and wet and vigorous, but it has the character of *productive output* rather than wilderness. Canopies form in flat, even layers, stratified like sediment, each layer a different species occupying a precise band of altitude as if assigned. The leaves are heavy, dark, lacquered-looking, and many of them are warm to the touch — they run hot the way transformers run hot. In the deepest hollows, where the dimness thickens to a green dusk, you find the condensation forests: trees whose entire architecture exists to harvest moisture from the air, their branches finned like heat exchangers, every fin beaded with water, the whole tree dripping in slow rhythm into the shallow black pools at its roots, and the pools rising and falling on a cycle, like sumps.

And the vegetation participates in the turbulence. A forest is not a permanent commitment here. You will crest a ridge and find a forest in the process of being *withdrawn* — trees retracting into the ground, branch by branch, with the smooth unhurried motion of landing gear folding away, the soil closing over them seamlessly, and within an hour the slope is bare warm stone again, ready for whatever the land intends to run there next. Nothing about it looks like death. It looks like a changeover between shifts.

## The Fauna

The animals are abundant past counting and almost entirely silent. They move through the layered canopies in dense, near-touching traffic — there is rarely a cubic meter of forest without something alive in it — and they have the unmistakable manner of *personnel*. Small armored gleaners work the warm rock faces in lines, scraping the metallic precipitate into ventral pouches and carrying it downslope. Broad gliding things ride the thermal columns off the extrusion ridges in permanent rotation, descending only to deposit something, ascending again. Herds of larger animals, slab-flanked and slow, graze the spherule-grit of the valley floors and excrete it elsewhere, transported, sorted. None of them appear to fear anything, and almost none of them make noise. When they communicate it is by light — brief patterned flickers along their flanks, the same dim blue-white as the vines, the same circuit.

They are not domesticated, because there is no one to have domesticated them. They are *employed* the way mitochondria are employed. You cannot find the boundary where the ecosystem ends and the industry begins, because there isn't one.

## The Breakdown

All of the above you could, with effort, hold in your head as a single strange but coherent place. What you cannot hold is the weather, because the weather here is made of reality, and the reality is unstable.

The cloud deck overhead is not entirely cloud. In its lower reaches it behaves like vapor; higher up, where the dim light comes from, it behaves like *unset* world — a suspension of possibilities that has not finished precipitating into matter. And in the turbulent seasons, which is most seasons, it rains drafts.

You see the storms coming as a deepening of the dimness and a wrongness in the air, a feeling of standing inside a held breath. Then the down-drafts arrive, and what falls is not water but *terrain*: provisional landscapes condensing out of the cloud deck and settling over the tablelands like weather fronts — a kilometer of plausible river valley, complete with the sound of water it does not contain; a forest of trees from no lineage, fully grown, faintly translucent at the edges; once, witnesses swear, a sea, ten minutes of a gray sea lying impossibly across a hundred kilometers of highland, with swells and a horizon, before the land rejected it. Most drafts are rejected. They lose coherence within hours, going soft at the edges, their geometry developing errors — slopes that descend in two directions, stones with more inside than outside — and then they sublime back upward into the cloud deck in slow ascending curtains, like rain falling in reverse, taking their wrongness with them.

But some drafts *take*. Some hardened ridge you walk today fell out of the sky last month as a proposal. This is how the tablelands grow; this is why everything is so young. The country is not eroding — it is the opposite of eroding. It is being continuously, turbulently *proposed*, tested, and either reabsorbed or annealed into permanence, and the silent industry of the stone and the vines and the animal traffic is, you eventually understand, the quality-control process: the warm extrusions knitting accepted drafts into the body of the continent, the gleaners and grazers redistributing the materials of rejected ones, the electric forests carrying — what? approval? — through the dark in those long traveling pulses.

During a heavy draft-fall the silence finally breaks, a little. The air fills with a sound like an enormous quiet loom — not loud, never loud, but vast, a soft systematic shuttling that comes from every direction including underfoot, and the dim light flickers as the cloud deck spends itself, and for a few hours you can watch the world being manufactured in front of you with no maker anywhere, the factory and the product and the raw material all the same warm, wet, electric, half-finished thing.

Then the front passes. The hush reseals. Condensation begins again on the new stone, which is already faintly warm, already growing its first lacquered leaves. Somewhere far off, one deep note, felt through the feet: a district settling. The pewter light holds steady at its eternal almost-evening.

The tablelands do not notice you. You are not a draft. You are not, as far as the country is concerned, even a proposal. You walk across the floor of the quietest factory that has ever existed, at the highest elevation of a world that is still deciding what it will be, and the only mark you leave is your condensation — your breath, briefly visible in the warm dim air, rising toward the cloud deck like a very small idea, submitted.
