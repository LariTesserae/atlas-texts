**The Plain Under the Storms**

Begin with the dark, because everything here begins with the dark. Not the dark of night, which carries the memory of day inside it, but a dark that has never been interrupted — two and a half kilometers of seawater stacked overhead, the last red wavelength strangled hundreds of meters up, and below that, nothing of the sun at all. This is old dark. It has been dark here for as long as there has been a here.

The floor is the floor of the world. An abyssal plain, flatter than any desert — the flattest large surface on the planet — leveled not by wind or ice but by patience: a hundred million years of falling. The mountains that once stood here (and they did stand; their basalt roots are still down there, fifty meters under the mud) were never worn down so much as *buried alive*, drowned in sediment a particle at a time until only the gentlest swellings remain, long low whalebacks of ooze you could not detect without instruments. This is erosion's final answer. Not the canyon, not the arch, not the dramatic ruin — just the planar, the settled, the place where all gradients have finished spending themselves. Topographically, the region is nearly silent. A relief of centimeters over kilometers.

The temperature holds at just above freezing and has held there, within a degree, since before there were primates. Cold here is not weather. It is a property of the universe, like mass.

And yet — and this is the contradiction the place is built on — nothing here is still.

The water is *full*. That is the first thing a light would show you, if you brought one: not clear black glass but a blizzard. Marine snow falls without pause and without end — the slow precipitation of everything that ever lived in the sunlit ocean above, flaked into pale aggregates, diatom shells and fish scales and the mucus-bound dust of a trillion small deaths, drifting down at the speed of an hour per meter. It has been snowing here for sixty million years. The water is packed with it, saturated, no clean gap between one falling presence and the next; a beam of light goes only a few meters before the snow eats it. The dark is not empty dark. It is *crowded* dark, occupied to its edges, a held breath full of suspended matter.

And through this perpetual snowfall come the storms.

They arrive from the west, where the plain runs up against the foot of the continental rise and the mouths of the great submarine canyons open like drowned river deltas. Somewhere far above and far away — a shelf collapse, an earthquake, a flooded river dumping its load into a canyon head — a slope lets go, and a turbidity current is born: an avalanche of mud-thickened water, denser than the sea around it, pouring downslope at the speed of a freight train, undercutting and entraining and growing as it comes. By the time it spills out onto the plain it is a wall of moving sediment kilometers across, a ground-hugging storm front of liquefied earth.

It makes almost no sound. That is the strange mercy of it. In air, such a thing would roar. Here there is only a deep infrasonic shudder you would feel in your sternum rather than hear, a pressure-hum below the threshold of listening, and then the hiss — faint, granular, intimate — of a billion grains shearing past one another. The loudest events in the deep ocean register as a kind of whispering. The storm passes over the plain in the dark, reorganizing the surface of the world, and the silence barely flexes to admit it.

This is the dynamic of the place: long stillness ruptured by catastrophic rearrangement, then stillness again — but the ruptures come often now, in geological terms constantly, one canyon mouth or another always discharging. The plain is a battlefield that never stops being fought over and never shows a scar for more than a season. Each flow lays down a new sheet of graded sediment — coarse at the base, fining upward to a film of clay — and the plain absorbs it, flattens around it, returns to its ancient horizontality like water closing over a stone. Turbulence here does not build anything. It cannot. The place has already reached its final form, and all the violence in the world only restates it.

Life is rare, and it is patient. Between storms, the snow is a feast for almost no one: a tripod fish standing motionless on stilted fins, facing into the faint bottom current, waiting weeks for something to touch it. A brittle star, arms threaded up into the water like pale grass — and these brittle stars are the closest thing the plain has to vegetation, fields of them in places, sparse and white, swaying in currents too slow to feel. Here and there a stalked crinoid, a sea lily, a survivor-form essentially unchanged since the Paleozoic, flowering in the dark on a flexible stem — a plant-shaped animal in a place plants cannot reach. Actual vegetation arrives only as the dead: a waterlogged tree trunk flushed down a canyon by some flood centuries ago, half-buried now, hosting a slow civilization of wood-boring clams; a drift of kelp blades, sunk from a coast a thousand kilometers away, already being grazed to lace by urchins that found it within days. How they found it, in all that dark and distance, is one of the plain's quiet capacities.

When a storm comes, the tripod fish swims up and lets the turbid front pass beneath it. The brittle stars are buried by the thousand and the thousand more arrive afterward, recolonizing the fresh mud within months. Burial and renewal, restatement of the flat. The fauna of the plain are not numerous, but they are very, very good at starting over.

There is almost nothing made here. Almost. Sunk into the levee deposits at the mouth of the largest canyon, carried down in some flow's coarse basal load along with gravel and shell hash, lies a hand axe — flint, bifacial, knapped on a riverbank or a shoreline in an interglacial so distant that the coast it came from is itself now drowned shelf. It is the only worked thing for a hundred kilometers in any direction. The plain does not distinguish it from the other stones. A scale worm has taken up residence in the hollow of its grip. Above, three kilometers up and ten thousand years removed, people in skin boats may once have crossed the surface of this water, hand lines down, never suspecting the floor; nothing of them reaches here except, perhaps, eventually, everything — since all things that sink come to the plain at last.

That is finally what this place is: the destination. The terminal basin. The flattest, oldest, most finished surface there is, lying in the cold and the crowded dark, accepting the constant snow of the upper world's living and the periodic avalanches of its land — receiving all of it in near-perfect silence, smoothing all of it into one horizontal statement, and waiting, without anything that could be called impatience, for the rest.
