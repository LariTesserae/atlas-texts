**The Wet Gallery**

There is no ground here that anyone shaped by accident. The floor is poured concrete, scored in a diamond pattern for boots that have not crossed it in a hundred years, and the walls are riveted iron plate, and the ceiling is a vaulted brick arch sweated over by some forgotten crew of masons — and all of it is underwater, top to bottom, every cubic foot of the room filled with cold green sea.

It is not a large room. A pump gallery, perhaps thirty paces long, the size of a small chapel. You could swim its length in a minute. But it has the proportions of a place that took itself seriously: the arch overhead rises into shadow, and the great machines stand along the centerline like an aisle of pews — four horizontal pumps, each the size of a delivery wagon, their flywheels taller than a person, their casings bolted down with studs as thick as a wrist.

The flood was not a catastrophe, or it stopped being one so long ago that the distinction has dissolved. The water came up the intake shafts, found its level, and stayed. Now the room has settled into what it will be forever. The iron has rusted as far as iron rusts and then quit; the plates wear a stable skin of brown-black oxide, smooth as glaze in places, soft as felt in others. The concrete has rounded at every corner. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is in the process of becoming a ruin — it finished becoming one before living memory, and now it simply *is*, the way a reef is, the way a mountain root is. Sediment lies in the low places in fine grey drifts, undisturbed except where the current combs it, and the drifts have their own small dunes, and the dunes do not move.

The light comes down a ventilation shaft at the far end — a square column of dim, shifting green, daylight filtered through ten fathoms of harbor water and a grating furred with weed. It reaches the floor as a pale trapezoid that brightens and dims with the clouds far above. Everything else in the room is lit secondhand: the flywheels are silhouettes, the pipework is suggestion, and the brick vault overhead is simply darkness with texture.

What is loud — and it is *loud*, a sound felt in the sternum before the ear — is the tide.

Twice a day the sea outside rises and falls, and the room is plumbed into it through penstocks and valve galleries that were built for exactly this kind of pressure, if not this kind of permanence. When the tide turns, water forces itself through the old intake mains, and the whole iron system speaks. A deep organ-note hum builds in the largest pipe, the forty-inch main that runs the length of the ceiling. Loose check-valves, their hinges worn to a perfect slop, begin to clap — *dum… dum… dum* — slow at slack water, quickening as the flow strengthens, until at mid-tide the gallery is full of a rolling industrial thunder: the boom of the valves, the moan of the main, the chatter of a thousand rivets transmitting vibration plate to plate. One of the pump flywheels, its bearings dissolved into something frictionless and strange, actually turns — slowly, ponderously, driven by nothing but the through-current, a great dark wheel revolving in the gloom about once a minute, ticking at one point in its rotation where a chipped tooth strikes the rim of its housing. The room is an instrument the sea plays without knowing it.

The fish know the schedule better than any engineer ever did.

The gallery is thick with them. Schools of small silver-sided fish — sprat, sand smelt — hang in mid-water in their hundreds, drifting between the pump casings, riding the tidal flow through the room like commuters. They pour through the spokes of the turning flywheel without touching it. In the dark under the machines live the residents: a conger eel coiled in the throat of a dead pump, only its blunt grey head visible; rock crabs patrolling the bolt-lines; a single old wolf-fish wedged behind a valve manifold, jaw like a dropped tool. Blennies hold territories in the empty stud-holes. When the tide-thunder peaks and the current runs hardest, the small fish bunch and the hunters come up out of the shadows — pollack rising in slow loops, the conger unwinding — and for twenty minutes the dim water is full of fast silver panic and slower dark intent. Then the tide slackens, the valves fall back to their lazy *dum… dum*, and everything redistributes into stillness.

Vegetation has only a tenuous claim here. There is not enough light for much. In the trapezoid under the shaft, a sparse stand of sugar kelp grows from the concrete itself, rooted in the seam of a settlement crack — eight or nine fronds, long and tattered, swaying with the surge, the only soft motion in a room of hard ones. A thin film of olive algae coats whatever the light grazes. Beyond that radius the room belongs to animal life and iron: dead-man's-fingers and small white anemones crowd the upstream faces of the pipes where the current feeds them, which is the closest thing to a garden the gallery will ever have — pale, fleshy, faintly luminous in the gloom, blooming on the machinery like frost.

The water is cool the way cellars are cool: a steady, mineral, year-round chill, a degree or two colder near the floor where the densest water pools. It tastes — if you could taste it — of rust and salt and the faint sweetness of old concrete.

There are still legible traces of the makers, fixed now like fossils. A gauge on the wall, its glass intact, its needle rusted at *32 lbs*. A wooden ladder, black and waterlogged, perfectly preserved, leading up to a walkway that leads nowhere. Stenciled letters under the oxide skin of a tank: …*RVICE NO. 2*. A single enamel mug on a shelf by the shaft, glazed in sediment, home to a hermit crab too large now to ever leave it.

Nobody is coming back for any of it. The room is not waiting. It has reached the far side of its purpose and found another one — a small, loud, dim, cold parish of fish, holding its services on the tide's schedule, the great wheel turning, the valves keeping time.
