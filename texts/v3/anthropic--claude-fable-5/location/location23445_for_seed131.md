**The Wet Room**

You find it by sound before you find it by sight. Somewhere behind a service door that no longer remembers being a door — its hinges fused into one continuous brown mineral, its handle worn to a soap-smooth nub by hands that stopped coming generations ago — there is a roar. Not a roar that rises or falls. A roar like a fact.

Inside is a single chamber, and the chamber is the whole world. It is perhaps fifteen meters across, round-shouldered, vaulted in poured concrete so old that the concrete has stopped behaving like a material and started behaving like geology. Every edge in the room has been rounded off — by spray, by lime, by a century or three of warm condensation rolling down the walls in unbroken films. Form lines from the original pour are still faintly visible, but softened, the way ripples survive in sandstone. Nothing here was ever natural, and nothing here is new. The room has finished becoming. It is in its last shape, and its last shape is gentle.

At the center, sunk into a circular well in the floor, the machine runs.

It is a single turbine — or it was a turbine, when there were words for it — a squat iron volute the size of a small whale, painted some color that has long since unified into the universal wet brown-green of everything else. Water enters it from below through a penstock no one has seen the other end of, spins it at a speed that has not varied within living memory of anything alive, and exits through a channel in the floor, sliding away under a low arch into the dark. The machine does not labor. It does not strain or surge. It turns at exactly the speed it has always turned, and the sound it makes — a vast, layered, full-body howl of moving water and spinning mass, pressure and iron, harmonics stacked on harmonics — fills the chamber completely, floor to vault, without remainder. You cannot speak here. You cannot think in sentences here. The sound is not an event in the room; the sound *is* the room, the way water is a lake.

Above the turbine, a single round light well pierces the vault — a drum of concrete rising to an iron grille and, beyond it, ordinary sky. Daylight comes down the shaft in a soft, even column, swirled with mist. It is warm light, unremarkable light, and it lands on the wet floor in a pale disc that has, over the ages, polished that one circle of concrete to a faint shine. The rest of the chamber lives in the spill: a green-gold dimness, never dark, never bright, never different.

And in that constant warmth and constant wet, the room has grown a garden.

Every surface that holds even a film of mineral silt holds life. The walls are upholstered in moss so deep and saturated it looks black until the light finds it, then flares emerald. Ferns crowd the seams of the concrete, fat and glossy, their fronds trembling perpetually in the machine's breath — not wind, exactly, but the standing turbulence the turbine stirs into the air. Liverworts tile the lower walls in overlapping scales. Long beards of algae stream from the lip of the tailrace channel, combed permanently downstream by water that never slackens and never floods. Hart's-tongue. Maidenhair. A single fig — somehow, impossibly, ordinarily — has rooted in a crack near the light well and grown sideways along the wall for what must be decades, its trunk flattened against the concrete like a vein, its leaves turned upward into the column of day.

The plants do not encroach on the machine. Or rather: they encroached long ago, were burned back by vibration and spray to a precise boundary, and the boundary holds. There is a clean iron margin around the turbine housing, scoured bare and rust-dark, beyond which the green begins. Equilibrium, drawn as a line on the floor.

Otherwise, the chamber is empty. That is the strange luxury of it — all this volume, all this warm humming air, and almost nothing in it. No catwalks survive; their stubs are rusted teardrops on the walls. No tools, no crates, no debris. The roar sweeps through unobstructed space. You can stand in the middle of the floor with the sound pressing on your sternum and feel that the room is hollow the way a bell is hollow: emptiness as a functional component.

Life of the moving kind is rare and shy. A pale salamander — eyeless, the color of wet paper — lives somewhere in the tailrace and is seen perhaps once a season, motionless on the algae, gone when you blink. Now and then a bird falls down the light well, panics in the roar, and finds its way back up. There are no insects to speak of; the vibration discourages them. The garden pollinates itself, or doesn't need to, or has stopped bothering with futures altogether.

Because that is the deepest fact of the wet room: nothing here is going anywhere. The water has flowed at this rate since before the moss. The turbine will turn tomorrow as it turned during whatever forgotten administration first closed the door. The temperature is the temperature; the light is the light; the roar is the roar. Whatever the machine powers — if it still powers anything, if its wires run to anything but earth — is not this room's concern. The room has settled into its final form, and its final form is this: a warm green throat in the body of something larger, full of thunder, full of ferns, perfectly at rest while spinning at full speed.

You leave the way you came. The door-that-was-a-door eases shut behind you, and the roar drops away so completely that for a long while afterward, silence feels like a substance — something thick you are walking through, something the room kept, and you, briefly, borrowed.
