**The Foundry Fens**

Begin with the sound, because the sound arrives before you do. It arrives, in fact, before itself — but we will get to that.

It is a low country, the lowest the land can get without becoming sea. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down so long ago that even their roots have relaxed. What remains is a vast cold plain of dark stone, planed nearly flat, lying just above the water table — and the water table presses up through it everywhere, so that the whole region is a lacework of black rock and standing water: sheets, channels, sumps, weeping seams. The stone sweats. Touch any surface and your hand comes away wet and faintly warm with friction, though the air is a few degrees above freezing and your breath hangs in front of you in a fog that does not always dissipate downwind. Sometimes it dissipates upwind. Sometimes it dissipates yesterday.

The light is the color of pewter and never changes much. There is no visible sun, only a general dim radiance through cloud, as if the sky were lit from behind by something tired. The brightest things in the landscape are below the horizon line: the pools glow faintly, a smothered orange-grey, like coals seen through frosted glass.

**The machinery that no one made**

There are no structures here. Nothing was ever built. Understand that clearly, because everything you see will argue against it.

The plain *runs*. It runs like a factory.

Erosion this old does strange things. Given enough time — and this place has had nearly all the time there is — water and stone stop fighting and settle into mechanism. The tide, miles distant, pushes through subterranean galleries and drives columns of trapped air up through bore-holes in the rock; the bores have been polished by a billion years of this into true cylinders, and the air-columns rise and fall in them with a deep pneumatic *chunk… chunk… chunk*, regular as a beam engine, eight strokes a minute, never missing. There are thousands of them. They do not all keep the same time, and the interference patterns of their beats roll across the fens like weather.

Basalt slabs the size of barges sit balanced on worn pivot-stones, rocking as groundwater loads and unloads their hollows — slow seesaws that *boom* against their seats with a noise you feel in your sternum. Mineral veins, leached and re-deposited over eons, have organized themselves into long conductive threads through the rock; the rocking and the pumping drag them through the planet's magnetic field, and the plain generates current. You can hear it as a hum under everything, a 50-cycle drone coming out of the ground itself, and on cold nights pale arcs crawl between outcrops like slow lightning that forgot how to hurry. Steam vents exhale on schedules. Sulfide chimneys in the deeper pools cycle through pressure-builds and releases with the hiss-and-clang rhythm of a foundry floor.

It is industry without intent. The landscape eroded past randomness and out the other side, into pattern. This is what *settled into final form* means here: the final form of this country is an engine.

The smell is engine-smell, too — wet iron, ozone, sulfur, and underneath it something organic and cold, like the inside of a shell.

**The inhabitants**

Nothing green has ever grown here, or if it did, no trace survives. There is no soil, no moss, no lichen even — the rock is bare, black, wet, and clean. But the fens are *crowded*. Saturated. There is not a pool without occupants, not a crevice without eyes.

The life here feeds on the machine. It eats current, heat-differential, mineral flux. It is animal, unambiguously animal — it moves, hunts, flees, calls — but it grazes on electricity the way other worlds' creatures graze on grass.

In the shallows: **rasps**, palm-sized armored things like trilobites cast in graphite, packed shoulder to shoulder in their thousands along the conductive veins, all aligned the same direction like iron filings. When an arc crawls past, the whole congregation shivers and rings — a sound like ten thousand small bells made of slate.

In the channels: **flue-eels**, pale as candle-fat, two or three meters long, that ride the pneumatic bores. They wait in the cylinders for the upstroke and let the air-column launch them into the open pools, where they snap up rasps before the water closes over them again. Their bodies bear regular ridges, like threading, and they spin as they fly.

On the rocking slabs: herds of **counterweights** — heavy, slow, tortoise-bodied grazers the size of oxen, who position themselves on the slabs to alter the rhythm of the rocking, slowing a slab's beat until the magnetic harvest in the underlying veins is richest, then pressing their soft undersides to the stone and drinking the current directly. They moan to each other across the fens in frequencies that beat against the ground-hum and make the standing water shiver into standing-wave patterns, perfect concentric geometry, pool after pool.

And above, in the pewter air: **stitchers**, ragged dark fliers that feed on the arcs themselves, swallowing the slow lightning mid-crawl. When a stitcher feeds, the light in that part of the fens dims a shade further, and everything below it pauses.

Predator and prey, graze and ambush, congregation and panic — a full ecology, dense as any reef, running at the cold deliberate pace of things that live a very long time. None of it could survive anywhere else. The fens are the only socket it fits.

**Where it comes apart**

Now: the part that resists description, and must be described anyway.

The fens are old enough that they are wearing through *more than rock*. Erosion, continued long past the point where there is anything left to erode, begins to abrade the underlayment — sequence, distance, identity, the ordinary load-bearing assumptions. The plain has worn thin in places, the way a riverbed wears down to bedrock, except the bedrock here is causation, and in the deep-worn places it is exposed and polished and slippery.

So:

The pneumatic *chunk* of a bore sometimes arrives at your ear several seconds before the air-column rises. The fens have eroded the gap between event and sound, and the two have come slightly loose from each other, like a film with the audio drifting. Old flue-eels exploit this: they strike at the sound of prey, and the prey arrives.

There are pools that hold their reflections at a small angle to what they reflect — a stitcher passes overhead and its image crosses the water heading the other way, or crossed already, an hour ago, and is still crossing. The rasps avoid these pools. The counterweights seek them out, and stand at the rims for days, watching, in their slow way, things that have not finished happening.

Distance is worn smooth in the middle ranges. Walk toward a rocking slab half a mile off and you will reach it in forty paces or four thousand; the fens have stopped keeping careful track. The horizon occasionally repeats — the same outcrop, the same three vents, the same balanced slab, recurring at intervals like a pattern stamped from a die — not because anything was duplicated but because *over here* and *over there* have eroded into each other and the land no longer fully distinguishes its own regions. The animals navigate by rhythm instead, by the interference patterns of the beats, which remain reliable when geography does not. A flue-eel finding its way home is not going somewhere. It is finding the right part of the song.

In the deepest sumps, where the wearing-through is most advanced, the water is perfectly still and perfectly loud — it roars without moving, a cataract's voice in a millpond's body, sound that has been ground free of its source and pooled there like sediment. Things live in those sumps too. They are abundant and various and difficult to count, because counting them changes the number, and they have evolved to take advantage of that.

**The weather of it**

The whole system breathes. Tides drive the pistons, the pistons drive the launches and the feedings, the feedings drive the migrations, the migrations re-tune the rocking of the slabs, the slabs re-shape the magnetic harvest, and twice a day the entire plain swells into a crescendo — every bore stroking, every slab booming, the herds moaning, the rasps ringing, the arcs crawling thick and pale across the black wet stone, the loosened echoes of it all arriving early and late and layered — a shift-change roar, a foundry at full production, with no foreman, no product, no purpose, and no beginning that anyone or anything remembers.

Then the tide turns, and it eases, and the fens idle: dim, cold, dripping, humming, packed with patient life, the oldest working thing in the world, running on, wearing thinner.
