**The Anvil Plateau, Night Without End**

You arrive in darkness and at first you think the darkness is total. It is not. It takes the eyes — or whatever you are using instead of eyes — perhaps an hour to find the difference between the black of the ground and the black of the sky, and then the plateau begins, very slowly, to disclose itself.

It is high. You can feel that before you can see anything: the thinness of the air, the way warmth arrives in pressure-fronts rather than sitting still, the long cold draw of wind that has crossed nothing for a thousand kilometers and carries no scent but mineral. This is a tableland the size of a small continent, lifted two or three thousand meters above whatever lies below it, and it has been here long enough that nothing about it is sharp anymore. The rock — a dark, fine-grained basalt, almost soapy to the touch where wind has finished with it — rolls in low, settled swells, like a sea that stopped moving a million years ago and then was patiently rubbed smooth. There are no cliffs at the interior. There are no young edges. Everything that was going to fall has fallen, and been ground down, and become the dark gravel that crunches faintly underfoot.

The warmth is the strange thing, given the altitude and the dark. It comes from below.

This is the world's industry, and the world built it itself. No structure stands anywhere on the plateau — no wall, no ruin, no mark of a made thing — and yet the place *runs*. Under the basalt cap is a vast, slow, pressurized circulatory system: chambers of magmatic heat, brine aquifers, gas seams, all plumbed together by ten million years of fracture and seal and re-fracture, until the geology has organized itself into something with cycles, schedules, throughput. Stand still and you feel it through your soles — a deep, irregular thrum, less a sound than a texture, like standing on the deck of an enormous ship several floors above the engine room. Every so often, on a rhythm of hours, somewhere out in the dark a vent exhales: a long, soft, pneumatic sigh, steam released through a stone throat polished perfectly round by ages of use. The sigh rolls across the flats, fades, and the quiet closes back over it. It is the quiet of a working facility at night — not silence, but suppression. Machinery behind walls. Except the walls are a kilometer of rock, and there is no machinery, only the planet doing what the planet does, at industrial scale, with industrial regularity.

The vents are where the light is, what little there is. Each one — and they are scattered sparsely, one every few kilometers, glowing like portholes across the dark plain — holds a dull red-orange ember down in its throat, and breathes a column of steam that condenses almost immediately in the cold thin air. The steam is where the water lives. There is no rain here in the ordinary sense, or almost none; instead the plateau makes its own weather in patches. Downwind of every vent lies a fan of dampness, a few hundred meters of beaded condensation on the stone, shallow warm pools in the hollows, a film of mineral dew that never quite dries before the next exhalation renews it. Between the fans: dryness, dust, the soft abrasive dark.

And in those damp fans, life — startling amounts of it.

The vegetation is meager and strange: low chemosynthetic mats, rust-colored and charcoal-colored, that feed on vent gases rather than light, since light is not on offer. They grow in rings around the warm throats like lichen around a chimney, and in the cooler reaches they thin out to scattered crusts, a stain here, a cushion there, long stretches of nothing. But the animals — the animals are everywhere, once you learn to perceive them. The plateau supports an entire fauna that has never needed sight. They navigate by heat. They read the ground's thrum the way other creatures read a horizon. There are small grazers, dense-furred and nearly spherical against the cold, that move from vent to vent on memorized routes, cropping the mats, timing their crossings of the dry dark gaps to the vent schedules — arriving just as the steam sighs out, to drink the condensation while it is still warm. There are long, low-slung hunters that lie in the cold gravel between vents, their bodies chilled deliberately to ambient temperature so that they cast no heat-shadow, waiting. There are diggers that live partly inside the plumbing itself, in the warm fracture systems just below the surface, and emerge at the seams; you see their spoil-heaps everywhere, small dark cones, the only construction on the plateau and not really construction at all.

Mostly what you hear of them is almost nothing. A click, conversational, answered by a click a hundred meters off — the grazers keep contact by sound in the dark, sparse calls across sparse country. The faint gravel-whisper of something repositioning. Then long minutes of only the deep thrum and the wind. The ecosystem is busy but it is not loud; loudness, here, is dangerous, and everything has learned economy.

Twice a night — if "night" means anything in a place that has no day — comes the plateau's one great event. Somewhere along one of the major seams, pressure peaks, and a chain of vents fires in sequence: a line of red throats brightening one after another across forty kilometers, each with its long exhalation, the sound arriving staggered and overlapping like a slow cannonade heard through felt. The steam from a chain-firing is enough to make actual weather — a low warm cloud that drifts downwind for an hour, dropping a thin, brief, blood-warm rain on the dark stone, and behind it the whole fauna of the region moves at once: grazers converging, hunters shifting, the diggers' cones steaming gently. Then it passes. The pools shrink back to their accustomed margins. The clicks grow sparse again.

It would be easy to mistake the place for desolate. It is the opposite. It is a finished thing — a system that has run long enough to wear off all its rough edges, the geology and the biology meshed like gears that have lapped themselves smooth against each other over deep time. Nothing here is new and nothing is failing. The plateau breathes, and is drunk from, and is grazed, and thrums on in the dark, vast and warm-blooded and almost entirely silent, an engine with no maker and no purpose except to continue, which it does, and has, and will.
