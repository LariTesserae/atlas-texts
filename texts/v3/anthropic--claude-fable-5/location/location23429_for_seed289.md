## The Bright Pocket

You would never find it on purpose. It sits maybe forty feet under an unremarkable hillside — under cold clay, under a seam of grey mudstone still soft enough to take a thumbprint — a single hollow no larger than a small room. A cavity. A pocket. The earth's held breath.

And it is *full of light*.

Not lamplight, not fire, nothing carried in. The light belongs to the chamber itself. Every surface — wall, ceiling, the knuckled floor — is sheathed in mineral that has not finished becoming mineral: half-set crystal, milky and wet-looking, like the inside of an egg held to the sun. It glows from within, evenly, fiercely, a white-gold radiance with no source and no shadow. Stand anywhere in the pocket and you cast nothing. The brightness comes from all directions at once, the way it might in the first second of a world, before anything has decided where the sun goes.

The chamber is caught mid-formation, and has been caught there for an unmeasurable time. Crystals stand frozen in the act of growing — you can see the direction they were reaching, the taper of a point that will never sharpen further. Stone drips hang from the ceiling with droplets of mineral milk suspended at their tips, *about* to fall, never falling. Run a finger along the wall and it comes away damp; the moisture beads back instantly, the film restoring itself, the same water in the same arrangement. A thin sheet of condensation slides perpetually down one curved wall into a pool the size of a washbasin, and the pool never rises, and the wall never dries. The water is cool. Everything is cool — the bright pocket gives off tremendous light and no heat at all, a cellar-cold radiance that makes your breath visible in the glow.

It is also, impossibly, *loud*.

The chamber rings. A continuous chord — part waterfall-roar, part struck bell, part the hum you hear when you press your ear to a hive — pours out of the glowing stone without pause or variation. It does not swell. It does not fade. Singers who hold a note must eventually breathe; the pocket does not. After a few minutes inside, the sound stops registering as sound and becomes something more like pressure, like weather, like the medium you are now suspended in. Some who have crouched in here say it is the noise of crystallization itself — the sound a mineral makes growing — slowed and held at the exact moment of greatest intensity, the way the light is the flash of formation that never finished flashing.

There is no room in here. That is the other thing. The pocket is *packed*.

Every inch of glowing surface carries life, shoulder to shoulder, shell to shell. Pale snails with translucent coils sit so densely on the lower walls that they tile it like a mosaic, each one motionless, each one alive — touch one and its eyestalks retract, then extend again to the precise position they held before. Blind, ghost-white salamanders lie stacked in the basin pool, three and four deep, gills blooming like small red ferns, perfectly still in the cold bright water. The ceiling crawls — slowly, so slowly — with flat copper-colored beetles arranged in spirals, and in the crevices between half-grown crystals, colonies of soft grey things like animate lichen pulse in rhythm with the chamber's single endless note. Nothing eats anything. Nothing seems to need to. The creatures of the pocket appear to live on the light the way moss lives on stone, and they have arranged themselves into a configuration that has no gaps and no turnover. If one of the snails has ever died, something identical occupies its place.

The vegetation is sparse and strange: a few ferns, bone-white and rigid as carvings, growing from cracks where condensation gathers. They are not fossils — they are pliant, they are damp, they are unmistakably living — but no one has ever seen a new frond, or a fallen one. They are the ferns the pocket has, the ferns it has always had.

And someone has been here. That is the unsettling part — not the light, not the noise, but the small evidences of visitation scattered through the crowding. A cairn of five flat stones, each one fitted to the next, stacked in a niche and now half-engulfed by crystal growth that stopped engulfing it long ago. A handprint pressed into the soft mineral of the floor, small, perhaps a child's, glowing the same as everything else, the lines of the palm rendered in light. Near the pool, a shallow clay bowl, fired by no kiln anyone could name, holding a single quartz point and a film of cold water that never evaporates. Whoever left these things left them gently. There is a sense — hard to defend, impossible to shake — that the objects were *offerings*, and that they were accepted, and that the acceptance is ongoing: the cairn, the handprint, the bowl have been folded into the pocket's eternal arrangement and are now as permanent as the salamanders, as the suspended drip, as the note.

The strangest property reveals itself only when you try to leave. The narrow throat of mudstone that admitted you is exactly as you left it, of course — everything here is exactly as you left it, exactly as you found it, exactly as it is. But as you crawl back up toward the dark and the ordinary cold clay, the light does not dim behind you gradually. It simply ends, at a precise boundary, like the edge of a page. One inch inside that line: the roar, the radiance, the thousand patient creatures, the chamber forever one second from finishing its own birth. One inch outside: silence, blackness, wet earth, and the faint conviction — fading even as you climb — that you have just stepped out of the only room in the world where nothing has ever gone wrong, because nothing has ever happened at all.

The hillside above grows thin grass. Rain falls on it sometimes. The grass doesn't know what it's standing on.

Neither, really, does anyone.
