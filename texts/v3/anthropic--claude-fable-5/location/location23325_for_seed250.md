**The Terraform Sea**

Begin with the water, because the water is almost everything.

It is an ocean that has been placed rather than gathered — that is the first thing you would understand about it, eventually, after days of drifting. It sits at an altitude where oceans should not be: a sea the size of a continent perched on the shoulders of a world, two and a half kilometers above the old datum, held in a basin that did not exist a century ago and is, in fact, still arriving. The rock beneath the water is being made *now*. You can hear it. That is the dominant fact of this place: you can always hear it.

The sound comes up through the water in long, freight-deep groans — basalt extruding itself in ridges across the seafloor, slow as toothpaste, fast as geology has ever moved. Pressure-cracks travel for tens of kilometers and arrive as a single percussive *knock* against the hull of anything floating, against the ribcage of anyone swimming. Between the knocks there is a continuous granular roar, like gravel poured forever down a chute, which is more or less what it is: new stone, cooling and shattering and being overgrown by newer stone. The sea is loud the way the inside of a working engine is loud. There is no silence here, only distances at which the roar becomes texture.

The water itself is cool, just above the temperature where your hands stop being useful, and it is astonishingly clear — clear in a way natural seas never are, because there is almost nothing alive in it. No plankton bloom, no drifting larvae, no fish, no krill, nothing with a nervous system anywhere in its volume. The clarity gives the light a strange formality. Ordinary sunlight comes down through the surface in broad shafts and just keeps going, sixty, eighty meters, illuminating a blue that deepens without ever clouding, so that you can hang in the water column and see the seafloor being born far below you: dull red seams opening in the dark, flushing the water above them with shimmer, then crusting black. At night the seams are the only light, a slow constellation underfoot.

What is doing the making is not visible, and this is the part that takes longest to accept. There are no rigs, no platforms, no pipes, no machines. The technology is *in* the water — distributed through it at a density of perhaps one working unit per liter, each smaller than a silt grain, none of them detectable except by their consequences. The sea is its own factory floor. The water chemistry shifts in slow regional pulses, dissolved minerals herded across hundreds of kilometers toward wherever the next ridge is scheduled to rise; heat appears where heat is needed and is gone where it isn't, in violation of nothing provable but the intuition of every thermodynamicist who has studied the gradients. Sometimes a column of water a kilometer across will go faintly opalescent for an hour — countless invisible agents doing something coordinated — and then clear again, and afterward there will be a new shoal where there was abyss, raw and steaming and already cooling into permanence.

The turbulence is constant and it is not weather, or not only weather. The sea makes its own storms; it is large enough for that. Cold katabatic winds pour off the unfinished rim-mountains that wall the basin — bare, angular, geometrically suspicious peaks, their strata too regular, like rock that was *decided* rather than deposited — and these winds drive a swell that crosses the open water unobstructed for a thousand kilometers, building into gray ranges of its own. But beneath the weather there is the deeper instability: the floor moves. Upwellings erupt without warning, domes of warmer water that breach the surface in smooth glassy boils fifty meters wide, hissing. Whole regions of the sea slowly rotate. Whirlpools open over subsidence zones, hold for a day, and close. Charts of this place are dated in hours.

And it is empty. That is the other fact, the one that settles in slowly. A sea this size on Earth would be crowded with weather of life — birds working the surface, the smell of it, the noise of it. Here the air above the water carries nothing. No wings, no calls, no insects. The horizon is bare in every direction except where the rim-mountains show, snow-dusted, remote, and the space between you and them is filled only with wind and the engine-roar of the deep and the long gray architecture of the swell. The emptiness has a pressure of its own. People who have crossed it report that they began, without meaning to, to whisper.

The vegetation, what little there is, is the first honest thing in the landscape — the first thing that arrived rather than being installed. On the older shoals, the ones that have had a decade to cool, there are mats of something dark olive and rubbery, a seeded pioneer alga, clinging in patches to the new basalt like lichen on a fresh grave marker. In the shallows of the rim, where wave-light flickers over black cobble, a sparse meadow of pale eelgrass has taken hold, each blade combed flat by the surge, thin and tentative, growing in a sea that groans and shifts beneath it. The mats tear loose in the upwellings and drift for weeks, the only floating things, small green rafts on an enormity of gray-blue.

Whether the sea is being built *for* something is not written anywhere on it. There are no signs, no markers, no claim. Only the ongoing fact of it: stone rising in the dark, water shouldering itself into storms, the invisible trillion-handed labor proceeding at every scale at once, and the first thin grass holding on at the edge — a world at the moment of its pouring, loud, cold, high, and almost perfectly alone.
