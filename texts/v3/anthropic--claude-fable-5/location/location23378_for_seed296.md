**The Tableland of Vell**

There were mountains here once. You can tell by what's left of them: not peaks but the memory of peaks, a high country worn so far down that it has become a kind of plain again — only a plain three miles into the sky. The Tableland rolls in long, low swells, like a sea that stopped moving and forgot it had ever moved. Nothing juts. Nothing breaks the line of the land except where the land itself has split, slowly, into broad shallow troughs where the cloud collects. Every sharp thing this place ever had was ground off ages ago. What remains is round-shouldered, settled, final. Geologists who came up here — there were some, generations back — said the rock had reached its last stable form. There was nowhere lower for it to go that mattered. It would simply *be* now, for whatever remained of time.

And on that finished rock: green. Improbable, total green.

**The Pile**

The cold up here should forbid it, and the cold has tried. What grew instead is a vegetation that gave up on height the way the mountains did. Nothing on the Tableland stands taller than your knee, but within that knee-high stratum the growth is ferocious, layered, nearly solid — cushion plants centuries old and hard as upholstered stone, dwarf forests of birch and juniper whose "trees" are two hundred years old and spread flat along the ground like spilled hair, mosses in a dozen saturations of green-black and gold-green and a green so deep it reads as blue in the dim light. The locals call this whole living layer *the pile*, as in the pile of a carpet, and they walk on raised plank-ways rather than on it, partly out of practicality — the pile can swallow a boot to the thigh — and partly out of something closer to manners.

The pile is never still. This is the strange thing visitors take longest to accept. It grows at a visible pace when the cloud comes through: stand on a plankway in heavy mist for an hour and you will see the moss swell, the cushion plants flush darker, tiny pale threads extend and braid. When the dry wind comes off the western swells, the whole surface contracts and pales over the course of an afternoon, audibly — a faint, vast ticking, like a cooling engine, millions of plant fibers tightening at once. The Tableland breathes on a cycle of days, and the pile breathes with it.

**Water, in its current form**

There is no open water here in the ordinary sense — no lakes, no rivers, though the troughs in the land are old riverbeds, you can read them, drainage from when the mountains still had height enough to wring rain from the sky. Now the water arrives sideways. The Tableland sits at the altitude where a great regional cloud-stream passes, and several times a day a wall of mist rolls across the swells, dense enough to dim noon to dusk. Everything condenses. Plank-ways drip. The pile beads silver. The town's roofs run with water that never technically fell.

Between cloud-passages the air goes startlingly dry and the light lifts a little — never bright, the sun up here is a pale coin behind permanent high haze — and you can see the whole region at once: green swells to the horizon, the dark seams of the trough-lines, and the towns.

**Vell and its sisters**

There are five towns on the Tableland within a day's travel of each other, strung along the deepest trough like beads, and Vell is the largest. The towns are built *down*, not up — there is no timber for towers and no shelter from the wind, so Vell is sunk into the trough-wall, terrace under terrace, stone galleries quarried from the soft rounded bedrock, their fronts faced with slate and old riveted iron. From above, the town is nearly invisible: a long darkening in the green, a hundred low roofs furred with deliberate moss for insulation, and the condenser-fields.

The condenser-fields are Vell's industry and its reason for existing. Great frames of woven wire and treated mesh, hundreds of them, stand in ranks across the windward swells, each the size of a barn door, angled to comb the cloud as it passes. The water they strip from the mist runs down through channels into cisterns, and from the cisterns it drives the works: low waterwheels turning in stone galleries, line-shafts running under the plank-ways, the slow heavy machinery of the wool-mills and the moss-presses and the dynamo hall that gives the town its light — dim, amber, flickering with the rhythm of the wheels, brightening when the cloud is thick and the cisterns are full, fading in dry spells. The people of Vell can tell the weather by the brightness of their lamps. Light here is literally weather, stored.

When the cloud-wall arrives, the whole town changes register at once. The condenser frames begin to sing — a faint metallic sigh as wind moves through the mesh — the channels start to chuckle, the wheels pick up, the lamps swell, and the streets empty as everyone goes inside to work by the good light while it lasts. Vell is busiest when it is least visible.

**Quiet, mostly**

For an industrial town, Vell is almost silent. The machinery is water-driven and slow; it thuds and breathes rather than roars. The pile soaks up sound the way it soaks up water — voices die within a few yards, footsteps on the plank-ways make a soft knock and nothing more. The dominant sound of the Tableland is the ticking of the pile drying, the sigh of the condenser-fields, and underneath everything a deep, irregular settling — the rock itself, people say, finishing its last unfinished business, an inch a century.

**What lives here, besides the green**

Animals are rare and consequently famous. There is a kind of hare, slate-grey, that swims through the pile rather than over it — you see only the wake, a line of trembling moss crossing a swell. There are the *gleaners*, dark long-bodied birds, perhaps forty in the whole region, that ride the cloud-wall in and pick condensed droplets and trapped insects off the mesh of the condenser-frames; the mill-workers know each one individually and consider it bad luck to start the wheels while a gleaner is still on the frames. And there is something larger that nobody has seen entire — only the impressions it leaves, long shallow channels pressed into the pile overnight, which heal closed within a day or two. The pile remembers it briefly and then forgets. Vell has decided, collectively and without ever quite voting on it, not to investigate.

**The strangeness, plainly stated**

It is this: the Tableland is *finished*, and everything on it knows. The rock has reached its last form; the towns build downward so as not to argue with the sky; the plants grow flat; even the machinery is designed to take what passes through rather than extract what lies beneath. And yet nothing here is dead or even slow — the cloud comes and goes on its hours, the pile swells and ticks, the lamps breathe with the cisterns, the hare-wakes cross the green. It is a place where geological time stopped and biological time kept going, lushly, in the resulting calm — life flourishing on the deck of a ship that has, after an unimaginably long voyage, finally and permanently come into harbor.

The people of Vell have a phrase for the weather, the light, the works, the green, all of it together. They say the Tableland is *living quietly in its last shape*. They mean it as a description. After a few days on the plank-ways, in the amber lamplight, listening to the moss tick, you begin to suspect they also mean it as an ambition.
