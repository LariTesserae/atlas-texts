## The Shelf at Vantar's Notch

There is a place near the top of the world that is the size of a room.

You reach it — if you reach it — by a stair of forty-one steps cut into bare gneiss, each tread worn into a shallow spoon by feet and meltwater. Nobody knows who cut them. They are pre-iron work: you can see the peck-marks of stone-on-stone all along their risers, thousands of small bruises in the rock, patient as rain. The stair climbs out of cloud. Most days the cloud is below you long before the last step, a white floor stretching out in every direction, and you come up over the lip of the notch into clear cool light with the sensation of surfacing.

The shelf itself is perhaps thirty paces long and twelve deep — a flaw in the mountain, a notch bitten out of the summit ridge where two rock masses of different hardness meet. The softer stone has been weeping itself away for an age, and what's left is a hollow with a high back wall, an uneven floor, and a front edge that simply stops. Beyond the edge is nothing. Not valley, not slope: *nothing*, two vertical miles of empty air, so much vacancy that the eye refuses it and slides back to the near and the small. The emptiness out there is the largest thing in the landscape and it contains, on most days, no object at all — no bird below a certain line, no drifting seed, nothing but the slow rearrangement of cloud. The shelf is a full room hung on the wall of a void.

And the room is full. That is the strangeness of it — the contrast you never stop feeling. Step back from the edge and you are in a dense, dripping, clamoring little world.

**The wet.** The back wall weeps. It has always wept; the whole face is striped dark and pale with mineral tears, and the dark stripes are alive — beards of moss a hand-span thick, liverworts the color of old bottle glass, a fine constant beadwork of water moving through all of it. The mountain exhales here. Air forced up the great face below arrives saturated, hits the cool stone, and gives up its water, so the wall condenses even on cloudless days. Drops gather at the tips of the moss and fall with an audible, irregular ticking, all day, all night, onto stones worn into cups exactly where each drop lands — cups deepened over centuries by nothing heavier than water falling six feet. The floor of the shelf is never dry. It is not flooded either; it has reached its settlement with the water long ago. Rivulets thread between tussocks along channels they have kept for so long that their banks are cushioned and sealed with moss, and at the lowest point of the shelf a pool the size of a sleeping ox holds black water and a permanent slow turning of foam. The pool drains through a slot in the lip of the shelf and falls. You cannot see the falling water once it goes — the wind below tears it apart within a few yards and carries it back up as mist, so the shelf partly drinks its own outflow. Stand at the edge in the afternoon and the spray rises *past* you, upward, fine and cold, which is the kind of small wrongness the place deals in: water that falls and then returns.

**The green.** Everything that can hold soil holds a garden. Cushion plants mound up like green skulls between the stones, some of them — the big ones, the ancients — surely older than any tree at lower altitude, growing a fingernail's width a decade. Dwarf willow runs along the rivulets, true trees only ankle-high, with trunks you could circle with thumb and finger and rings too fine to count. There are sedges in the wet ground, dense and combed flat by wind into a single direction like the fur of an animal. Tucked under overhangs, ferns; on the ledges of the weeping wall, hanging mats of saxifrage that bloom, briefly and all at once, in a season that lasts nine days. The lushness is real but it is all *low* — nothing on the shelf stands higher than your knee except the wall itself. The green is a pelt, not a forest. It muffles the floor and makes every footstep silent and slightly treacherous.

**The cold.** Cool rather than cruel. The sun does reach the shelf — it faces the long light of afternoon — and on a still day the hollow gathers a fragile warmth you can feel on one cheek while the other stays cold. But the stone never warms through. Put your palm flat on the back wall and the chill comes up into you from somewhere very deep. The pool carries a skin of ice on most mornings, thin as a leaf, gone by midday. Breath shows year-round.

**The noise.** For all its emptiness of objects, the place is *loud* — outrageously, continuously loud, and from several throats at once.

First, the wind, which does not blow across the shelf so much as past its mouth, the way breath passes the mouth of a bottle. The notch resonates. There is a standing note, low and woody, that rises and falls with the wind's strength out in the void; locals of the valleys below — those who know the place exists — call it the mountain humming, and on certain nights it can be heard two ridgelines away.

Second, the water: the ticking wall, the chuckle of the rivulets, the steady gulp of the pool's outlet slot.

Third — and chiefly — the birds.

The shelf is a colony. The whole back wall above the moss-line is drilled and pocketed with old erosion holes, and nearly every hole is owned. Slate-winged choughs, hundreds of them, nest in the wall and spend the day pouring in and out of the notch in shrieking strings, riding the updraft out over the void, tumbling, falling deliberately for hundreds of feet just to climb again. They are the only things that populate the great emptiness, and only briefly — a flung handful of black flecks against the white cloud-floor, then gone, then back. Their voices are metallic, conversational, ceaseless. Beneath them, in the moss itself, lives a second nation: a small fat thing like a cross between a pika and a wren-mouse, dozens of them, each with a territory the size of a table, each defending it with a piercing two-note whistle. They whistle at the birds, at each other, at falling drops, at you. In the pool live black newts with cold gold eyes that surface, sip air with a tiny *pock*, and sink. At dusk, swifts arrive from below the cloud — nobody knows from how far — and scythe through the notch at speed, screaming, threading the gap between the wall and the edge so close to your head that you feel the air part.

There is a roughly half-hour period before dawn when everything goes quiet at once — wind, birds, even the wall seems to tick slower — and the silence is so total and so unlike the rest of the day that people who have slept there describe it as a pressure, almost a sound of its own. Then a single chough calls, and the whole machine starts again.

**The marks of hands.** Beyond the stair, the human presence is thin and old. There is a cairn at the south end of the shelf, taller than a man, built of flat stones so neatly corbelled that it has shed two thousand winters of ice without losing its shape. Wedged into its crevices, here and there, are offerings: a bird skull, a disk of polished slate with a hole bored through it, a twist of cord gone green. Against the back wall, half-swallowed by moss, stand the remains of a single small structure — three courses of dry stone outlining a space just big enough for one person to lie down in, roofless now, its floor a bed of sedge somebody, sometime, must have cut and laid. A hermit's cell, or a watcher's, or a place for the dying or the dreaming; the architecture doesn't say. Cut into the lip of the shelf, right at the edge of the void, is the only deliberate ornament in the whole place: a spiral, hand-width, ground into the stone, its grooves softened by weather into something that looks almost natural — except that the chough colony, seen from the cell at certain hours, wheels out over the void in the same turning direction, and has, as far as anyone has ever recorded, always done so. Whether the spiral copies the birds or the birds were taught by something older than the spiral is the kind of question the shelf does not answer.

No one lives here now. Someone still climbs the forty-one steps — once a season, perhaps — and leaves something in the cairn. The moss is thick on the cell walls but the sedge bed, when found, is always strangely fresh.

**The motion of it.** Nothing on the shelf is still. The cloud-floor below heaves and reforms all day; sometimes it rises, pours over the lip, fills the notch with blind white wet for an hour, and drains away again, leaving everything beaded and the birds furious. The choughs raid the moss-nation's territories for nestlings; the moss-nation whistles its sentry-chains; a falcon — there is exactly one, or one at a time, holding the airspace of the entire void as her territory — comes through perhaps twice a day like a thrown knife, and for the ninety seconds of her presence the colony goes up in a screaming smoke of black wings and the whistlers vanish underground and the shelf is, briefly, hers. Then she drops off the edge into the nothing, and the room refills with noise, drop by drop and call by call, the way the pool refills: from sources too small to see, into a fullness that never overflows.

A small place. A loud, wet, crowded, green, ancient room — nailed to the top of the sky, with all the emptiness in the world for a view.
