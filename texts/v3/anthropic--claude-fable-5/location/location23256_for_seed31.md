**The Caldera Towns of the New Shield**

Begin with the rock, because the rock is younger than memory pretends. This is high country — a broad volcanic shield lifted nearly three kilometers into the warm middle air, its summit not a peak but a wide, shallow basin many days' walk across. The stone here has not had time to soften. Every ridge keeps its edges. The lava that built this place cooled into long black blades and rippled aprons, and the rims of the old vents still stand as crisp as broken pottery, unrounded, unbothered. There are no river-carved valleys yet, no gravel fans, no patient terraces of sediment. Erosion has barely begun its first sentence. When the rock breaks, it breaks fresh and glassy, and the fracture faces glitter faintly in the gray-bright light.

And yet the place is soaked. The shield is tall enough to stand inside the cloud belt, and the clouds arrive each day with the regularity of breathing. By mid-morning the air thickens; by noon the whole basin is wrapped in a warm, drifting whiteness that beads on every surface. It is not rain so much as condensation made geography. The young rock, being unweathered, holds the water strangely — it cannot soak in deep, so it weeps. Every dark cliff face runs with thin silver threads. Every overhang drips at its lip in slow, countable intervals. Pools collect in the lava's own dimples and stay there, glass-still, scattered across the black ground like coins dropped from a great height: a pool here, then a hundred paces of bare stone, then another. The water is warm to the touch, blood-temperature, faintly mineral.

The green came on the wind. Spores travel where animals cannot, and so the basin is colonized by everything light enough to fly without wings: mosses in dense emerald cushions wherever the drip-lines fall, tree ferns standing alone or in small congregations in the wetter hollows, their fronds unfurling over black rubble like green umbrellas opened above a ruin that was never a building. The vegetation is lush where it occurs and absent where it does not — the land is too young for soil to be general, so life clusters at the seeps and skips the dry blades entirely. The effect, seen from any high rim, is a vast dark plain stippled with vivid green islands, each one marking where the rock weeps.

There are towns. This is the strange grace of the place: people came before the animals did.

You see the first one from a long way off — a terraced settlement climbing the inner wall of a secondary crater, built entirely of the land's own stone. The builders had no metal worth speaking of; everything here was made by hand, basalt shaped against basalt, walls raised dry-stacked from the angular blocks the lava conveniently shatters into. The masonry is rough but enormously patient. Cisterns are everywhere — that is the towns' true architecture, more cistern than house. Broad catchment aprons of fitted stone funnel the daily condensation into deep tanks; channels of hollowed rock, pecked out over generations with hammerstones, carry water from the weeping cliffs down through the terraces. The houses are low, thick-walled, round-shouldered, roofed with overlapping slabs and fern thatch, their doorways facing away from the drift of the afternoon cloud.

The settlements are real towns — hundreds of dwellings, plazas paved in fitted black slabs, retaining walls three men high — but they are spread thin across the basin, one to a crater, one to a seep-field, with long empty distances between. A path of white pumice cobbles, laid by hand stone by stone, connects them across the dark plain, visible for miles like a chalk line drawn on slate. You can walk half a day on that path and meet no one and hear almost nothing.

The quiet is the thing visitors remember. There are no birds here. No insects. Nothing has crossed the heights and the distances to reach this raw young shield except spores and people, and so the basin has the acoustic character of an empty room. The loudest regular sound is water: the metronome drip beneath the overhangs, the threadlike trickle in the carved channels, the occasional soft collapse of a moss cushion grown too heavy with its own wetness. Human sounds carry absurdly far. A mason's hammerstone tapping in one town can sometimes be heard, faint and dry, from the rim of the next crater over. People here speak quietly out of habit, the way one does in a place that returns every sound with interest.

The climate does not change. That is perhaps the deepest fact of the basin. There are no seasons at this height and latitude; the warmth is constant, the cloud arrives and dissolves on the same schedule every day of the year, the pools neither flood nor shrink. The towns' calendars are social inventions, kept by tally-cords and the counting of cistern levels, because the sky offers no help. Children grow up understanding the day as a fixed shape: clear morning, white noon, weeping afternoon, clear dusk. Elders say the basin has looked exactly like this since the first walls were raised, and the rock — sharp-edged, unweathered, unchanged — does not contradict them.

The people grow ferns and tuberous moss-garden crops in built soil — soil they make themselves, crumbled pumice and composted frond-litter packed into walled beds, since the land has not yet made soil of its own. Without animals there is nothing to herd, nothing to hunt, nothing to fear in the dark; the towns have no fences against anything but wind. Their tools are stone, fiber, and water. Their wealth is measured in cistern depth.

At dusk the cloud burns off and the basin opens to its full extent: black plain, green islands, white path, the scattered amber points of cook-fires in three or four distant towns, and above it all an enormous clear sky pressing down on a landscape that is, by the standards of mountains, scarcely born — and that intends, by every still pool and unrounded edge, to stay exactly as it is.
