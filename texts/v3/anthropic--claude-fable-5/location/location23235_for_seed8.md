**The Long Shallows**

The land here gave up its argument with water a very long time ago. Whatever this country once was — and it must have been something once, because everything was — has been worn down past hills, past slopes, past even the memory of slopes, until what remains is a plain so flat and so old that the horizon seems less like a line than a rumor. The bedrock lies just under everything, planed smooth, and the water lies just over the bedrock, and between them there is almost nothing left to lose.

It is warm. Not the aggressive heat of desert noon but a deep, settled, bath-water warmth that rises out of the ground itself and hangs in the air as haze. The water — and water is most of what there is — sits in immense shallow sheets, ankle-deep across miles, broken by low whaleback domes of stone the color of old bone and rained-on rust. These domes are the last vertebrae of mountains. They rise a few feet, no more, their surfaces polished and pitted, weeping at their bases where groundwater pushes back up through hairline seams. You can put your hand on the stone and feel it sweating.

The light is generous and slightly thick, the way light gets when it has to pass through a great deal of warm wet air. Everything wears a soft sheen. The water sheets act as a second sky laid flat on the world, so that clouds drift below your feet as well as above your head, and walking here feels like walking the seam between two identical heavens, each one faintly steaming.

Vegetation is sparse, and what there is keeps low and apologizes for itself. Mats of dark moss in the lee of the stone domes. Rings of rusty-green algal crust where the water laps and retreats with the seasons, building structures so slowly that a thumb-width of growth might represent a lifetime — yours, not its. Here and there a stand of reeds, never more than a dozen stems, standing in the shallows like a small committee that arrived early and is waiting for the rest of the plants to show up. The rest of the plants are not coming. This place sorted out its arrangements ages ago, and the arrangement is: stone, water, light, and a thin film of stubborn green where the three agree to overlap.

But the animals — the animals never got the message that this is an ending. The shallows are *loud*. That is the first surprise of the place, the gap between how empty it looks and how full it sounds. The water sheets are a single continuous feeding ground, paved with snails, ribbed with the burrows of pale crustaceans, clouded in places with fry so dense the water seems to shiver of its own accord. Wading birds work the flats in loose scattered congregations, never crowding, each one given its acre, and their calls carry without obstruction across miles of open water — a constant cross-hatching of cries, whistles, the percussive slap of a thousand bills probing mud. At dusk the frogs take over, and the frogs are uncountable. Their chorus comes up out of the warm shallows like a physical pressure, a sound you feel in your sternum, rolling unbroken from horizon to horizon. There is nothing to absorb sound here. No forests, no walls, no hills. Every noise made anywhere on the plain belongs to the whole plain.

Beneath the chorus there is a quieter, older sound, audible only when you stand still on one of the stone domes and let the animal noise become wallpaper: the seep. Water moving through the rock, out of the rock, over the rock — a faint universal trickle, the sound of the land's last slow exhalation. The domes weep, the seams drip, the sheets drain imperceptibly toward sheets infinitesimally lower. There is current here, but it moves at the speed of seasons. Drop a leaf — if you can find one — and come back in a week to see whether it has traveled.

Nothing has ever been built here, and standing in the shallows you understand why the idea feels almost grammatically wrong. There is nothing to build *against*. No slope to terrace, no resource to corner, no shelter the warm air doesn't already provide. The place is finished in the way a riverbed stone is finished — not abandoned, but completed. Even the animal architecture is modest: burrows, scrapes, a heron's careless platform of reeds on a dome top, washed away and rebuilt across generations in the same spot, which is as close to a monument as the plain permits.

Rain comes in slow warm columns you can watch approaching from a great distance — gray pillars walking the flats, announced an hour ahead by the smell of struck stone. When the rain arrives it barely changes anything, because the land is already as wet as it can be; the sheets deepen by a finger's width, the frogs sing louder, the domes weep a little faster, and within a day the water has redistributed itself back into its ancient equilibrium. Change here is not absent. It is simply conducted at a register too low for a single life to hear: the domes losing a grain a year, the algal rings adding a layer a decade, the whole vast plain settling another fraction of an inch into its final, comfortable, deafening rest.

It is the loudest quiet place there could be. A country that finished becoming itself before almost anything else began, and that now does nothing all day but lie flat in the warm light, full of water and birds, and ring.
