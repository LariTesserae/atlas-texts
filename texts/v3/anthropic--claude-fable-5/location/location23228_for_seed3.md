**The Provisional City**

The mountain is not finished. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing anyone here stops understanding.

At this altitude — well above the cloud deck, in air thin enough that the foundry workers wear breathing bladders strapped under their chins — the rock has not yet fully decided what it is. The lower slopes, kilometers down in the white, are real in the ordinary way: granite, schist, the usual stubborn certainties. But up here, near the growing tip, the stone is still *precipitating*. You can see it if you watch a ridgeline long enough: the edge of it wavering very slightly, like a word on the tip of the tongue, before settling — for an hour, for a decade — into one particular arrangement of itself. The geologists call it green rock, the way one speaks of green wood. It is cool to the touch, faintly damp, and it remembers pressure. Press your palm against a wall of it and come back in a season; the print will have deepened, as though the mountain considered your hand and decided to keep it.

The city was built here anyway. Of course it was.

---

It is not one city so much as a constellation of districts strung across the unfinished summit country, separated by long reaches of empty proto-stone — a smelter quarter here, a residential terrace two kilometers away across a saddle of half-formed ridges, a rail junction perched alone on a spur that did not exist eighty years ago. The emptiness between them is not wasteland; it is *pending*. Surveyors mark it with iron stakes painted vermilion, less to claim it than to give the rock something definite to organize around. A stake driven into green rock will, over years, gather true stone about itself the way a string gathers rock candy. Whole foundations have been grown this way: forests of stakes, patiently rusting, and then one morning a generation later there is a hill of honest granite where the city can finally set its weight.

This is the principal industry. The foundries do not smelt ore — they smelt *certainty*. Enormous brick works, blunt and windowless, squat at the edges of the unfinished zones, exhaling slow columns of steam that do not rise so much as lean, drifting downslope into the cloud sea. Inside, they burn coal hauled up the cog railways to heat great iron formes — molds the size of houses — which are then craned out, still ticking with heat, and pressed against the raw flanks of the mountain. Where hot iron meets green rock, the rock sets. It commits. The contact line glows a dull mineral orange for days, and when the forme is pulled away, there is bedrock where there was only proposal. The whole upper city stands on cured ground like this, plate by plate, the seams between curings still visible everywhere as long welts in the stone, like scars from an enormous slow surgery.

The sound of the place is the sound of this work, and it never fully stops. It is loud, but it is a patient loudness: the deep respiratory thud of the forme-presses, one stroke every few minutes, felt through the soles of the feet before it is heard; the long groan of crane chains; and beneath everything, lower than the machines, the mountain itself — a continuous bass murmur, like a kettle an hour from boiling, which is the sound of rock becoming. Newcomers cannot sleep for it. Old residents cannot sleep without it, and when a district goes quiet — when the murmur beneath a particular street falls silent — they evacuate, because silence means the ground there has stopped deciding, and stone that stops deciding sometimes decides to be *absent* instead. There are gaps in the city. Streets that end at a railing and a long drop into grey unresolved air. They are fenced, lamplit, and left alone.

---

The light is dim in a way that has nothing to do with weather. The sun is up there somewhere — its position can be calculated — but light arriving at the unfinished summit comes through air that is itself slightly provisional, and it arrives tired, diffused, the color of pewter and weak tea. Noon is a brightening of the general dusk. The city compensates with electricity: festoons of carbon-filament lamps strung between buildings, burning ember-orange around the clock, and the great arc lights of the foundry yards, which hiss and flutter and throw shadows that take a perceptible moment to follow the things that cast them. Lamplight behaves better than sunlight here. It is local, it is intentional, and the green rock seems to respect intention. The brightest-lit streets are the most solid ones. This is doctrine, written into the municipal code: *illumination is infrastructure.*

It rains, but only in pockets — small private rains, a hundred meters across, that wander the saddles between districts like grazing animals. You see one coming, a grey column drifting against the prevailing nothing, and you step into a doorway and wait the ten minutes for it to pass over, drumming on the tin awnings, sluicing down the welted stone, and move on. Where the rains linger, things grow.

And things do grow, improbably, generously, in the cracks of all this industry. The cured stone weeps condensation — it is always cool here, breath-fog cool, the air wet against iron railings — and in every seam between forme-plates there are gardens nobody planted: dense ropes of a dark moss called widow's wool; ferns with fronds like verdigris; a hanging vine, pale and luminous-veined, that grows only on the green rock and dies the moment the rock beneath it cures, so that gardeners read the vine like a map of where the world is still soft. Whole north-facing cliffs below the residential terraces are upholstered in vegetation, dripping, dim, rich-smelling — cold rot and crushed stems and coal smoke all braided together. The terraces farm in this: cabbages, blue potatoes, a bitter high-altitude barley, grown in plots of imported finished soil, fenced against the animals.

Because the animals are everywhere. This is the strange generosity of the unfinished country: life loves it. The pending zones between districts, useless for building, teem. Hares the color of slate, in numbers that suggest the rock produces them. Flocks of small crook-winged birds — choughs, or something the choughs are becoming — that roost by the thousand in the cooling vents of the foundries and rise at the shift-change whistle in a single dark sheet, wheeling through the lamp-glow. Goats with too many opinions, picking their way along ridgelines that did not exist last year and may not next, apparently untroubled by standing on geology that has not happened yet. And in the deepest unfinished places, glimpsed and argued about: larger shapes, slow ones, grey on grey, which the surveyors call *provisionals* and refuse to discuss further — animals, perhaps, that the mountain is still drafting, walking sketches, not yet committed to a species. They do not approach the lit streets. The lamps, again. Intention.

---

Change here is real but glacial. The mountain rises, they say, a hand's-breadth a year, and the city rises with it, jacking its rail beds, re-leveling its terraces, an endless unhurried maintenance. Districts are decades in the curing. Children are born on streets that were proposals when their grandparents arrived. The clocktower in the rail junction quarter — the tallest finished structure on the summit, its stone cured plate by plate over forty years, its faces lit, its bell the one sound that can briefly silence the foundries — leans two degrees out of true, and the engineers' guild has voted, twice now, not to correct it. The lean is recorded. The lean is *finished*. In a place where everything else is still becoming, a flaw that has fully committed to itself is the closest thing the city has to a monument.

At night — which is to say, when the pewter dim deepens to true dark and the cloud sea below catches the orange of the lamps and glows faintly back, like embers under ash — you can stand at one of the fenced railings at the edge of the cured ground and hear it all at once: the press-strokes, the chains, the choughs resettling, a far pocket of rain crossing an empty saddle, and under everything the mountain murmuring its long sentence, not yet at the verb.

The people who live here do not say they live *on* the mountain. They say they live *during* it.
