# The Hollow Crown

You do not climb this place so much as enter it. From outside — if anyone still remembers the outside — it would look like a mountain range, a green-black wall rising through the cloud deck and beyond, vanishing into thin cold air. But there is no rock in it. There never was. The whole massif is a single growth, or perhaps a colony of growths so old and interwoven that the distinction stopped mattering: a continent of plant, kilometers thick at the base, and almost entirely hollow.

Inside, it is night that never quite finishes being night.

## The Chambers

The interior is a system of vaults — vast lung-like cavities stacked and braided through the stem-mass, some of them large enough to hold their own weather. You stand on a floor of living tissue, slightly yielding, faintly warm-cold like the skin of something enormous and slow, and the far wall of the chamber is so distant that you cannot see it. You know it's there because sound comes back from it, eventually, changed.

Almost nothing fills these spaces. That is the first shock, and it never stops being shocking: a world made wholly of vegetation that is, by volume, mostly *absence*. The plant builds itself as a frame and leaves the rest as air. Columnar trunks-within-the-trunk rise out of the dark at intervals of a kilometer or more — pale, ribbed pillars of structural fiber, each one wider than a city block, sweating cold condensation. Between them: nothing. Black air. Drift. The occasional falling drop, taking a long time to land.

The chamber you are in is *young*. You can tell because the edges are still sharp. Older vaults, lower down, have gone soft and rounded, their surfaces furred with secondary growth — but here, near the crown, the walls show fresh tearing where the plant recently pulled itself apart to make new space. Pale green scar tissue, still wet. Ribs of new fiber, translucent at the tips, that flex audibly when the structure shifts. The floor in places is not fully formed: thin membrane over deeper hollows, drum-tight, and you learn to read its color in the gloom or you fall through into a chamber below, and the chamber below that, and so on for a very long way.

## Light

There is almost none. The rind of the great plant is meters thick and the sun is a rumor. What light exists comes in three forms:

First, the **seams** — hairline fractures in the outer wall where growth has outpaced itself, admitting threads of grey daylight so faint they read as scratches on the darkness, kilometers overhead. They drift over months as the structure flexes and heals.

Second, a dim biological smolder in the wounds: torn tissue here glows faintly as it knits, a low blue-green like the memory of a color. New chambers are therefore the brightest places in the world, which is to say barely lit at all — you can see your hands, the suggestion of the nearest pillar, and nothing else.

Third, the lights that the animals carry, which are not for your benefit.

## Sound

Because there is so little to see, the place arrives mostly through the ears, and it arrives *loudly*. The hollow architecture is one enormous resonating body. The plant grows fast at this altitude — fast for a plant, which means audibly — and its growth is a continuous structural complaint: long groans that travel through the floor and up your legs, sharp cracks like distant ice as a new rib splits free, deep periodic booms from far below where some vault is sealing itself or collapsing or being born. None of these sounds stays where it started. A crack in a chamber ten kilometers away will reach you as a soft round *whump*; a crack nearby will hit you in the chest.

Over the structural noise lies the wind. Cold exterior air finds the seams and pours through the hollows, and the hollows are flutes. Certain chambers have voices — sustained organ tones that hold for hours, shifting pitch as the seams stretch. Travelers navigate by them. *The chamber that sings in fifths. The shaft that moans before rain.*

And over the wind, the animals.

## The Animals

For all its emptiness, the hollow crown is inhabited — not densely, but *thoroughly*. Life here is distributed the way the plant is distributed: thin, far-flung, clinging to the frame.

The walls and pillars belong to climbers: pale, long-limbed things with hooked hands and enormous ears, who almost never descend to the chamber floors. They eat the new growth at the scar-lines, following the faint wound-glow from one fresh tear to the next, and they scream to each other across the voids — flat, carrying calls engineered by the acoustics of the place, each one returning seconds later as its own echo, so that a single animal sounds like a scattered chorus and a chorus sounds like weather.

The air belongs to gliders. You rarely see them; you hear the rip of membrane wings cutting the dark, feel the pressure-wave when something large passes close. Some carry light — paired lures along the leading edge of the wing, cold yellow points that drift through the black like slow sparks. The lures are bait. Smaller fliers, moth-like, condensation-drinkers, are drawn to any light at all, which is a thing the people here learned early and at cost: in the hollow crown, you shade your lamp, or you feed it.

Below the membrane floors, in the sealed and half-sealed chambers, live the things nobody has names for — heard only as movement, vast and slow, dragging through the under-vaults. They surface rarely. When the floor-skin of a chamber bulges upward in the dark and subsides, travelers stand very still and say nothing and move on.

Predation here is conducted almost entirely by sound. Everything listens. The loudest place in the world rewards the quiet.

## Water and Cold

The crown stands above the cloud layer, in air thin and bitterly cold, and the plant breathes. Warm moist air rises through the hollows from the living mass far below, meets the cold of the heights, and condenses against every surface. The pillars weep continuously. The ceilings — where there are ceilings — gather droplets along their ribs until the weight releases, and then it rains *indoors*: brief, local, freezing showers falling through perfect darkness, announced by a hiss that swells and passes like a hand dragged across the chamber.

Pockets of standing water collect in the floor-folds: black pools, dead-still, rimmed in places with thin pale ice. They are the only mirrors in the world, and they reflect nothing, because there is nothing to reflect.

The cold is constant and architectural. It is the cold of altitude, leaking in through the seams, fighting the faint metabolic warmth of the walls. Where the two meet, fog forms — long horizontal banks of it drifting between the pillars, glowing very slightly where they pass a healing scar.

## The People

Someone lives here. Not many — the emptiness would not be emptiness otherwise — and not grandly.

You find their traces at long intervals: a platform of cut fiber lashed into the lee of a pillar, just large enough for three or four sleepers. A rope bridge of braided bast spanning a floor-gap, its knots tight and competent. Steps chopped into a wall with stone adzes — the only stone in this world is what their ancestors carried up, generations ago, and each blade is an heirloom, resharpened until it is a sliver. Mostly they work in the plant's own materials: fiber, resin, the hard dried husks of old growth, needles of rib-tip, vessels carved from gall-swellings and sealed with sap.

Their structures are always small, always temporary in spirit even when old in fact, because the world they build on is *young* and still moving — a platform anchored to a wall this season may be carried thirty meters and tilted sideways by next season's growth. So they build to be abandoned. They mark routes instead: notches in the pillars, knotted cords hung at junctions, and above all *sound-signs* — hollow gourd-chimes tuned to specific pitches, hung where the wind will play them, so that a traveler in total darkness can hear *the third pillar of the singing chamber* from two kilometers off and know exactly where they are.

They shade their lamps. They speak in low tones, and across distance they use struck-fiber drums whose notes ride the resonant walls farther than any voice. They harvest the wound-glow scars (carefully, gratefully — there is something in their manner toward the plant that is not quite worship and not quite husbandry), drink the condensation, net the moth-things, and avoid, with elaborate inherited care, the chambers where the floor moves.

## What the Place Is Doing

It is not finished. That is the deepest fact of it. Lower down, in the warm crushed dark of the base, the plant is old beyond reckoning — but the crown is *new*, still erupting upward into the cold, adding chambers the way a lung adds breath. Stand still long enough on a fresh floor and you can feel it: a slow, continuous, directional strain, the whole black hollow world straining *up*.

Some nights — there are only nights — a new vault opens somewhere in the heights. It begins as a tearing groan that goes on far too long, swells into a sustained roar as tissue splits along a kilometer of seam, and ends in a pressure-change you feel in your ears, a great cold inhalation as the new emptiness fills with air for the first time. Then silence, comparatively. Then, faintly, from every direction, the climbers begin to scream the news to each other, and the echoes carry it on long after the animals have stopped, outward through the dark chambers of the crown, until the place itself seems to be repeating it:

*more room, more room, more room.*
