# The Basin of Vapor and Noise

You arrive at the rim first, because everyone does. The road climbs for a day and a half through warm, wet switchbacks — stone weeping at every cut, the air thickening as you ascend rather than thinning, which is the first wrong thing — and then the land simply stops pretending to be land and opens into the basin.

It is a caldera, or was, a long time ago. Whatever violence made it has been worn so smooth that the violence is no longer legible. The rim runs in a vast irregular ring, maybe thirty kilometers across, its edges rounded like the lip of a clay bowl handled by ten thousand years of hands. The rock is a pale rust-and-bone color, banded, soft-shouldered. Nothing here looks new. Nothing here looks like it is *becoming* anything. The basin has finished. It is in its own state now, and the city inside it has had to learn to be in that state too.

## The Roar

Before you see the city, you hear it — except *hear* is the wrong word, because hearing implies an event, and this is a condition.

The basin floor is fractured along old, settled fault lines, and through those fractures the mountain exhales. Superheated groundwater rises through a hundred natural throats and meets the open air in a continuous, overlapping, all-directional thunder. Steam columns stand across the basin like a sparse forest of white trunks — there are perhaps forty major vents, widely scattered, each one a permanent geyser that long ago stopped erupting in pulses and now simply *runs*, the way a river runs. The sound of one vent would be impressive. The sound of forty, reflecting off the curved stone walls of the rim, folding back on itself, harmonizing and beating against itself in slow interference patterns, is total.

It is loud in the way the inside of a waterfall is loud. It does not spike. It does not relent. It has not, by every account, changed in pitch within living memory, though the very old claim that it has dropped perhaps a quarter-tone in their lifetimes, the way a kettle deepens as it nears the end of its water. This is discussed, when it is discussed, with the unhurried dread of people contemplating an event their great-grandchildren may have to deal with.

Within an hour of arriving you stop perceiving it as sound. It becomes pressure, weather, a medium you move through. Within a week, travelers report, silence is what becomes frightening — there are sealed rooms in the city, padded chambers built for delicate instrument-work, and newcomers sometimes faint in them. The body interprets the absence of the roar as a held breath. As something about to happen.

## The City

The city — it has a name, but the name is a hand-sign, and writing it down has always been an approximation — does not cluster. It cannot. The basin floor is a patchwork of safe ground and unbuildable ground: scalded flats where the crust is thin and white-crusted with mineral bloom, warm shallow fens where the runoff pools and steams, and between these, long whaleback ridges of solid old stone. The city is built on the ridges, which means the city is built in *pieces* — fifteen, twenty districts, each a dense knot of construction, separated from its neighbors by a kilometer or two of fen, flat, and vapor.

So it is urban and empty at once. Stand in a district and you are in a true town: four- and five-story buildings of mortared rust-stone, their lower courses dissolved and re-deposited by a century of mineral-laden condensation until wall and bedrock are no longer distinguishable; streets channeled and guttered with deep stone troughs, because everything here drips; rooftops shingled in overlapping ceramic scales glazed against the acid damp. Then walk ten minutes and you are alone on a causeway of fitted stone, fog drifting across it in slow ropes, the next district a smudge of chimneys and turbine-houses far ahead, and nothing between you and it but warm wet emptiness and the roar.

The causeways are the city's skeleton. They are old — older, structurally, than most of the buildings — raised roadbeds running ridge to ridge across the fens, wide enough for the rail-trams that shuttle between districts. The trams are cable-hauled, driven from winding-houses at each district's edge, because no one would hear an engine coming and no one would hear a warning bell. Everything that moves in this city moves on rails or on schedules, and the schedules are carved into stone posts, because the schedules do not change. They have not changed in a long time. A tram that ran at the third hour a hundred years ago runs at the third hour now.

## The Works

The roar is not merely endured here. It is harvested.

Every major vent wears a collar — a vast, weathered assembly of riveted iron and stone, green-black with mineral scale, that captures a fraction of the rising steam and feeds it through penstocks into turbine halls. The turbine halls are the largest structures in the basin: long, windowless, barrel-vaulted buildings, half-buried, trembling faintly and perpetually. They were built by people with an industrial confidence the current inhabitants maintain rather than extend. Nobody builds new collars. The knowledge isn't lost, exactly — it is simply understood that the basin's output is what it is, the works are sized to it, and ambition beyond that would be a kind of rudeness toward an arrangement that has held for generations.

The electricity goes everywhere on the ridges and nowhere off them. Lights burn in the districts at all hours — not brightly, but steadily, a warm sodium-amber that hangs in the perpetual humidity as soft spheres of glow. From the rim at dusk, the city looks like embers scattered across the bottom of a bowl of milk: isolated points of warmth in a slow tide of vapor, each one connected to the others by the faint geometric lines of the causeways.

The factories — and there are factories, a few in each district — do quiet work loudly housed. Ceramics, mostly: the acid-glazed roofing scales, insulators, the great fired-clay penstock liners, and a famous export, a kind of mineral-cured porcelain that can only be finished in the basin's vapor and is found in cabinets a thousand miles away. The kilns never fully cool. Firing cycles run on the scale of weeks. Everything industrial here has been geared down toward the geological — slow flywheels, long cycles, processes left to run because the power never stops and there is no reason to hurry.

## Life, Such As It Is

Vegetation has made its peace with the place by mostly declining to attend. The scalded flats grow nothing but mineral crusts in cream, ochre, and arsenic-orange. The fens support a sparse stubble of wiry sedge and one notable plant — a low, leathery rosette the inhabitants call *kettle-leaf* — that grows only within the spray-radius of the vents, drinking condensation, its leaves cupped upward. It is bitter and medicinal and harvested once a year by people in waxed coats, walking out on the flats along paths of trusted stone.

Animals are rarer still, for an obvious reason: nothing that hunts or flees by ear can live in the roar. There are no songbirds, because there can be no song. What persists has gone visual or gone deaf. A species of pale, large-eyed gecko lives in the warm masonry and signals by throat-flash. White moths, big as a spread hand, navigate the vapor at dusk. And there are the basin doves — stone-grey, genuinely deaf, descendants of some ancient escape — that wheel between districts in slow flocks and have become, inevitably, the city's emblem: the creature that solved the place by giving up the sense the place destroys.

The people did something similar. Spoken language exists here but is an indoor, sealed-room, intimate thing. Public life runs on the hand-language, an old and complete signed speech with its own poetry, its own regional accents between districts, its own untranslatable jokes. Conversations happen across streets, across causeways, across the gap between a moving tram and a platform. The city is deafening and yet, watched from a distance, it appears profoundly silent — figures gesturing in the fog, lamplit, eloquent, making no sound anyone could find.

## The Strange Part

It is this: the roar is not quite uniform, and the inhabitants do not quite believe it is meaningless.

The interference of forty vents inside a curved stone bowl produces standing patterns — places on the basin floor where the sound thickens, beats, or thins almost to bearability. These nodes drift, but slowly, over months and years, and they are mapped with the seriousness other cities reserve for property lines. Certain buildings stand where they stand because a quiet node stood there when they were built; some of them now sit in full roar, abandoned, their doorways sealed with ceramic plugs, because moving out of the sound's way is simply what one does.

And in the deepest nodes — the still points, where the thunder cancels itself almost to a hum — people report that the residue of sound resolves into something with the cadence of speech. Not words. Cadence. The very old go and sit in the still points for hours. They do not say what they are listening to, and it is considered graceless to ask. The drifting of the nodes is tracked, charted, and discussed in the same patient registers as the quarter-tone drop in the basin's pitch: the city listening, across generations, to a single sentence being said too slowly to ever hear the end of.

The light through all of this is ordinary — that may be the most disorienting thing. Plain warm daylight, filtered gold through vapor, falling on rust-stone and amber lamps and white steam. A reasonable sky over an unreasonable floor. From the rim, at midday, the whole arrangement looks calm, settled, almost sleepy: a sparse city steaming gently in a worn old bowl.

Then you start down the switchbacks, and the pressure finds you, and you understand that you are not approaching a place so much as entering an utterance — one the basin began before anyone arrived, and will still be pronouncing, a quarter-tone lower, long after the last kiln cools.
