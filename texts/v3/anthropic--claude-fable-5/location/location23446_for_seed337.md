# The Quarried Town

You come to it across a flat plateau of grey stone, and you do not see it until you are nearly standing in it: a town that goes down instead of up.

The plateau simply ends. The ground opens into a sunken bowl perhaps a mile across, thirty, forty feet deep at its center and deepening still — because the town is not finished. The town is being *cut*. Every wall here was once the inside of the rock. Every street is a floor that someone exposed with a chisel. Nothing was built so much as *revealed*, subtracted, persuaded out of the living stone, and the whole settlement has the look of something halfway through being born.

## The Stone

The rock is a pale, fine-grained limestone, soft enough to take a copper edge, hard enough to hold one afterward. Where it has been freshly cut — and almost everything here is freshly cut — it is almost white, with a faint creamy luster, and the tool marks are still crisp: parallel rows of small crescent gouges, the signature of a particular chisel held at a particular angle by a particular hand. You can read the workers in the walls. One mason cuts in long confident strokes; another in nervous, overlapping pecks. Their work stands side by side in the same doorway, two handwritings in the same sentence.

There is no weathering anywhere. No rounded corner, no softened edge, no patina. The arrises of the walls are sharp enough to scrape skin. The steps have never been worn into the gentle sags of old stairs; their edges are square and a little cruel. In a thousand years this place will be smooth and grey and ancient. Right now it is white and angular and *new*, raw as a split bone, and the rawness is everywhere — the whole town smells faintly of broken stone, that mineral spark-smell, like rain about to fall on dust.

## The Plan, Such As It Is

There was a plan once, you suspect — somewhere down in the deepest, oldest cuttings there are streets that run straight and rooms with square corners. But the town grew faster than the plan, and now it is *packed*, crammed, saturated with itself. Dwellings are cut into the faces of the excavation in tiers, three and four stories of doorways stacked up the quarry walls, reached by ladders of lashed poles and by stairs chiseled diagonally across the rock face. Between the carved houses, every remaining surface is occupied: drying racks, rope-and-timber scaffolds, baskets of rubble awaiting haulage, stacks of cut blocks (the stone removed to make one house becomes the courtyard wall of another), stone troughs, stone benches, stone bins — furniture and architecture are the same substance here and shade into one another without a seam.

There is almost no open ground. The streets are trenches, shoulder-wide in places, roofed over here and there where two houses grew toward each other across the gap and someone bridged it with timber and flat slabs. Light comes down into these passages in pale vertical sheets. You move through the town the way water moves through cracked rock — finding the gaps, turning sideways.

And below everything, the floor of the bowl keeps descending in broad shallow terraces, each terrace a worksite, each worksite a future neighborhood. At the lowest level the cuts are only chest-deep: rooms marked out but not yet emptied, doorways drawn in charcoal on stone that is still solid, a whole district that exists so far only as an intention scored into the rock. You can stand at the rim and see the town's entire life at once — finished above, embryonic below, and in between every stage of becoming.

## Water

The stone weeps. Not everywhere, but reliably — the limestone carries a slow seep through its bedding planes, and wherever a wall cuts across one, the rock sweats. Certain walls are always dark with it, beaded like the outside of a cold jar. The masons have learned to read the stone for these veins and cut small channels beneath them, narrow chiseled gutters that carry the seepage down from terrace to terrace, joining and rejoining, until it gathers in cisterns hollowed at the corners of courtyards — round-mouthed pits with stone lids, each one holding a few days of cool, mineral-tasting water.

Rain comes occasionally, a few times a month, brief and businesslike. When it does, the whole town becomes a catchment: the rim of the bowl sheds water inward, the gutters run audibly for an hour, the cut floors gleam, and shallow pools stand in the unfinished rooms at the bottom — temporary mirrors holding rectangles of sky in rooms that don't have ceilings yet, and never will need them.

The air down in the lanes is cool always, even at midday. The stone is a great cold mass and the sun reaches the trench-streets only briefly. Mornings, there is condensation on every north-facing wall, and breath shows faintly until the light gets down past the upper tiers.

## What Grows

Very little, and all of it deliberate or opportunistic. Moss has found the permanently weeping walls — thin emerald films, the only old-looking thing in town, though even the moss can be no older than the cuts it grows on. In the seep-channels, a dark filamentous algae streams like wet hair.

Here and there the inhabitants have filled a cut basin with hauled-in soil — soil is the rarest material in this town of infinite stone — and these pocket gardens hold what they can: a fig tree espaliered flat against a south-facing wall to catch the brief sun, hard-leaved herbs, a few rows of something pale and edible. Each garden is walled, lidded with brush at night against the cold, and tended with the attention people give to things that cannot be replaced. On the plateau above, beyond the rim, there is sparse scrub — grey-green cushions of it, widely spaced — and you understand at a glance why the town went downward instead of out: there is nothing up there to build with except the same stone, and nothing to eat, and the wind.

## What Lives

Not much announces itself. Rock doves have already claimed the highest abandoned cuttings — niches begun and left when a better seam opened elsewhere — and their soft burbling is one of the few constant sounds. At dusk, small bats come up out of the deepest finished chambers and hunt the cooling air above the bowl in silence. A pale gecko, the color of the stone itself, lives in the tool-mark crevices and is seen mostly as a flicker of absence. Somewhere in the town there are a handful of goats — you hear a hoof on stone occasionally, a single bell — kept for milk in a courtyard you never quite find.

The people themselves are present mostly as evidence. A basket half-filled with chippings. A copper chisel left across a wooden maul, the copper edge already mushroomed from use, waiting to be re-ground. A child's game scratched on a terrace floor, pebbles still in position. You hear them more than see them: the distant, patient *tik… tik… tik* of stone-cutting somewhere across the bowl, never hurried, never quite stopping, a sound so steady it sinks below attention and becomes a property of the air. The town is quiet the way a workshop is quiet — not empty, but absorbed.

## The Processes

For all its hush, the place is in constant motion at every speed:

The seeps run, ceaselessly, a few drops a minute, drop-sounds spaced far apart in the cisterns like a slow clock. The dust of the day's cutting settles all evening, a fine white film that the next rain rinses into the gutters, so that the town launders itself in pale slurry. On clear cold nights, frost gets into the freshest cuts and works the bedding planes; in the morning you sometimes find a slab spalled cleanly off a new wall, lying on the floor like a dropped plate — the rock continuing the masons' work without them, not always where they wanted. The masons curse this and also use it: they wet certain seams deliberately before cold nights and let the frost do a day's labor for free.

And beneath all of it, the slowest process: the town descending. Each season the floor of the bowl is a little lower, the tiers a little taller, the trench-streets a little deeper and cooler and bluer at their bottoms. The inhabitants do not speak of finishing. The plan, insofar as anyone holds it, is simply *down* — following the good stone, opening rooms ahead of the families who will need them, the whole settlement a single ongoing act of carving that has houses inside it the way a sentence has words.

Stand at the rim at evening, when the cutting has stopped and the doves have gone quiet and the light goes level and amber across the plateau, missing the bowl entirely so that the town fills with early blue shadow while the rim still burns gold. The white walls go grey. The seeps keep their slow time. A single lamp appears in a doorway three tiers down, then another, small warm holes in the cold stone — and you can feel it then, distinctly: not a town that was built and is now aging, but the opposite. A town still arriving. Stone becoming rooms. The whole place one long, patient, unfinished gesture of emergence, paused for the night.
