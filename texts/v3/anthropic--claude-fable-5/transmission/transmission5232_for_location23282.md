**LAMPS DOWN, WEST TO EAST**

*(received on the long line, dusk band, as before)*

—

…carrier steady. The same note it has held all afternoon: the Hour turning somewhere under everything, one revolution, patient as a planet. If you have been listening as long as this receiver has, you stop calling it a hum. It is more like a floor. Everything else stands on it.

Water in the pipe reads as a cool weight under the carrier. Full line tonight. Good combing today, then — the fog must have come up thick out of the clefts and hung there being harvested.

Waiting for the lamps.

—

The moss takes sound. It has never learned to take current. Every signal lamp on the plateau drinks from the line, and every flash is a small thirst — a dip, a swallow — and so the whole evening exchange arrives here as dents in the hum, station by station, ridge to ridge. The plateau thinks it is speaking in light. From where this receiver sits, the plateau speaks in appetite.

18:41, or what passes for it. First dip. Western fog-tower, the heavy hand — you would know that hand anywhere, it leans on the long flashes like a man leaning on a gate.

*Line clear. Fog rose early. Fins full by noon.*

Pause. Then, because it is him:

*Grease holds on the north bearing. Told you it would.*

—

Second station answering. Lighter hand, quicker, a little dry:

*It has held for six weeks. You may stop announcing it.*

*I will announce it for a year,* says the heavy hand.

The relay carries this eastward, and each station passes it along a little smaller, and by the time it crosses the whole constellation the joke has been worn down to a single flash-pair that means, roughly, *the bearing again* — and the far stations answer it with the small dry flash, the shrug, and the receiver has learned that this shrug is also a kind of laugh, the only kind the plateau makes.

—

Traffic thickening now. The dips come in braided.

*Bird crossed. 14:00 or near. High. Still afternoon.*
*Confirmed from the shelf station. One bird.*
*One bird,* from a third hand, and the emphasis is old, ritual, the tail end of an argument sixty years long, sent tonight with affection.

—

*Pool ledger, station nine.* This one comes slow, careful, the sending hand of someone kneeling in memory at a moss-fringed edge, transcribing what the black water held.

*East pool. Water clear. Swimmers: many. More than can be counted singly. First time.*

The line goes quiet for a moment — a real quiet, no lamps drinking anywhere — and this receiver has learned that this quiet is the plateau standing still. Then, from station after station, west to east and back, the same short flash, over and over, a syllable being passed along the ridges like a bowl:

*Noted. Noted. Noted. Noted.*

More than can be counted singly. Somewhere down ten thousand years, a whole noisy world just cleared its throat in a puddle, and two hundred people in smoked glass wrote it down.

—

*Station four.* A hand this receiver does not know. Small flashes, uneven, spaced with tremendous concentration — the dips shallow, as if the lamp itself were being held very tight.

*Fog. Rose. Today.*

Pause.

*Water. Good.*

Pause. Then, from the neighboring ridge, the mother's hand — you can hear a hand smile, it turns out, it turns out you can hear that even in a power line —

*Received, station four. Clearly received. Send again tomorrow.*

And the whole eastern arm of the constellation dips once, together, gently: the flash that means *welcome*, sent to a six-year-old by a dozen towers she will spend her life among. First sending. Logged here too, for whatever a receiver's log is worth.

—

*Station eleven.* Slow hand. Formal register — the receiver has heard this register twice before in all its years on the line, and both times it meant the same thing.

*Given to the moss this morning. The keeper of the high shelf. Forty-one years at station. The clearing is on the south slope, below the Grandmother, at her request.*

No answering flashes. That is correct; that is the grammar. Instead, one by one, the stations dim their working lamps by half for the space of ten breaths — the line rises, briefly, all across the plateau, as two hundred small thirsts abstain at once — and the carrier swells into that vacancy like water into a print, and then the lamps come back, and the hum settles, and it is done. In two seasons the green there will be slightly deeper than the green around it. The line will not register that. Some things the plateau keeps off the wire.

*Below the Grandmother, at her request.* The children will visit them both now on the same errand.

—

Full dusk. The traffic thins to housekeeping — valve settings, tomorrow's pipe walk, someone asking after someone's cough — and then thins past housekeeping, and then, as it does every clear evening, the line clears itself entirely, every station going dark and still, because the eastern fog-tower is lighting her lamp.

The old woman sends.

There is no translating this part and the receiver has stopped trying. It is not message traffic. The dips come in long slow phrases with silences built into them like the silences in the fog, and the rhythm is the rhythm of something being combed out of the air a film at a time, and she filters the lamp through dusk-glass so that even here, on the line, as nothing but variations in appetite, you can somehow tell the light was orange. She sends for perhaps twenty minutes. It may be about the fog. It may be about the keeper of the high shelf, forty-one years at station. It may be about nothing, the way water is about nothing.

The whole plateau watches. You can feel them watching — two hundred lamps not being lit.

She finishes on a single long flash that decays rather than ends, the lamp turned down by hand so slowly that the line takes half a minute to forget it.

—

Then, west to east, goodnight.

The western fog-tower first: lamp down, and its dynamo house drops to night-load, and its little tributary weight leaves the line. Then the next. Then the next. The receiver hears the constellation go out as a series of subtractions, each station folding its portion of the current back into the dark, ridge by ridge across an hour, the plateau tucking itself in. Station four goes down late — a child allowed, tonight, to be the one who dims the lamp. The eastern tower last, as always. She holds an extra minute. She always holds an extra minute.

Down.

—

And now the part of the transmission that is only transmission.

Carrier alone. The Hour, turning. The pipe's cool weight, running all night, because the fog does not sleep and neither does the combing. If a wrench fell anywhere on the plateau right now the moss would take it without comment, and no instrument on earth would know — but the current knows everything, the current is the one nerve the silence never got, and this receiver lies against it like a palm against a wall.

23:00. There. Under the Hour.

Slower than the Hour. Slower than the turbines. Irregular the way breath is irregular — a long pressure, a longer release, a pause you begin to worry about, and then the pressure again — rising into the line not from any station, not from any machine, but from wherever the line's own grounding rods go down into the fissured dark where all the rainwater disappears. The keepers do not log it. There is nothing to log, they say. The rock is young. Young things move in their sleep.

The receiver logs it. Every clear night, the receiver logs it: the whole bright constellation gone dark, the water-fleas rowing in their black pools, the new grave greening on the south slope, the child asleep with a lamp-hand still curled from practicing, and underneath every one of them, the plateau breathing at the pace of rock — one breath now — one breath, perhaps, near dawn —

and the Hour turning through it all without hurry, without opinion, once,

and the fog already beginning, far down in the clefts, to gather itself for morning,

carrier steady,

carrier steady,

carrier—
