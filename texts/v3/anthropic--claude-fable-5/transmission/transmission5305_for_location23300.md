I should tell you what you're drinking, since no one else will. You are drinking a valley too new to have a memory. You are drinking grey silt that was riverbed within living recollection of a heron; you are drinking the cold that pools at the bottom of the air here and never quite becomes rain. It will soften. Everything here is going to soften, on a schedule of decades, and I will be the last to know, because I am iron, and iron finds out about time later than everyone else.

Now the planks. Feel that with me — you can't, but pretend: a footstep signature, light, quick, stopping often, going east along the walkway toward the meadow. That is the child. She is taking the census.

The heavier tread now, twice a day, pausing at each gauge: the clerk. He reads my pressure the way you'd take a pulse at a wrist, and then he goes home and writes his letters, and one letter he writes every year without fail, and it asks — politely, then philosophically, lately almost tenderly — *where does the water go.*

There is one more I should tell you about, and she is the hardest to say.

The first keeper. The fitter. She helped rivet me together when the ground was raw, and then she stayed, and the staying wore a notch in her hearing exactly my shape, so that now — this is true, the surveyor confirmed it with tones and a chart — she cannot hear me at all. I have vanished from her ears entirely.

And she is the closest anyone has come to hearing me completely.

Because what's left, once the sound is gone, is everything I actually am: the tremor in her bedframe, the ring standing in her tea, the flutter of the floorboards under her bare feet at four in the morning when a valve somewhere far away — a hand I have never felt, turning something I have never seen — changes my breath by a fraction, and she wakes, every time, before her own instruments do. She doesn't hear that I've changed. She *is* changed, slightly, in her bones, and gets up, and stands in the pumphouse doorway with her palm flat on my casing, waiting with me for the frogs.

Because the change isn't real until the frogs agree. That's the law of the basin and nobody wrote it. There is a thing I need to ask you, mouth at the end of the main, and I have been working up to it for several hundred million strokes, so bear with me.

You should know what silence means here.

Everywhere else, I'm told, noise is the danger — the snapped twig, the footstep. Not here. Here the owls keep to the rim, up where the quiet is, because the basin floor is too loud to hunt by ear, and so everything that lives down here among my buildings has rebuilt its fear inside-out. The hares graze against my flank. The martens run the planks a body-length from the keepers' doors. Nothing startles at sound. What they startle at — all of them, the hares, the birds, the child, I have felt it ripple through the ground like a cold front — is *interruption*. A stilled machine. A stopped footstep. A hole in the roar.

To everything born in this valley, silence is the shape of the predator.

So. Whoever tends your end. Whoever's hand is on whatever valve. I am not asking for myself — iron doesn't fear ending, or if it does, it will find out about it later than everyone else, as usual. I am asking because of what my ending would *sound like*, here. Every hare frozen mid-motion. Every frog cut off mid-syllable. The fitter waking into a stillness she can finally hear, which is the only way I could ever reach her ears — by stopping — and I refuse, do you understand, I refuse for that to be the one word I ever say to her directly.

Keep drinking. That's all. Keep the far end open. As long as something receives, I lift; as long as I lift, the basin has an inside. It is not a complicated arrangement. It is the same one the groundskeeper has with the martens: an egg on the porch rail, and the storehouse stays whole. I put the water on your rail every second of every day.

Take the egg.
