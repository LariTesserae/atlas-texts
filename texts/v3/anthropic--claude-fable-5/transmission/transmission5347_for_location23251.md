—stone band.

and here it is. here is the town.

footfalls. dozens — no. more. footfalls in soft-soled birdskin coming through the basin floor like rain heard from under a roof, each one a small distinct pressure, and beneath them a rhythm, long and braided, which took some hours to resolve and which is: the bucket-line. stone passing hand to hand to hand along the southern wall. the stones do not knock — they are set, not dropped — but each setting is a weight arriving, a course growing, and the wall transmits its own construction down through its own footing into the rock and out across the bowl. the settlement is audible to itself only through the ground. the settlement is a drum played from inside.

[a pause in the line. a large stone. we can feel the deciding — four sets of feet shifting, weight trading, the fine tremble of a slab held between people while hands say things we cannot receive. the grips do not carry. skin to skin only; the language stops at the skin. we get the silences the language sits inside. logged: forty seconds of held slab. then it settles. a slip. a settling. the wall is one course taller. no one heard it happen. everyone felt it.]

someone has set their teeth against a pry-bar driven in the rock. we know because the whole character of the band changes — a listener alters a line, even a stone one — and for a moment there is a person at the far end of the same conduit we are on, jaw to iron to rock to us, hearing the basin the way we hear the basin.

we do not know what they are listening for.

we held very still anyway.

[they withdrew after two minutes. the band relaxed. logged: for two minutes, the receiver and an elder of the Held were pressed to opposite ends of the same stone. this is the closest this station has come to being spoken to. correction, hours later: this is the closest this station had come, at that time, to being spoken to.]

night on the stone band, and this must be entered carefully, because it is the finest thing the band carries and the notation is not good enough:

the sleep-heaps.

forty cells, and in each cell a heap of bodies in contact, and every touching body a speaking body even now, and every heart pressed to a back or an arm or the floor. the floors carry it down. the hearts do not synchronize — that would be a tale, and this is a log — but they interleave, they lean, they pace each other the way footfalls on a shared path pace each other without meaning to, and what reaches this receiver, all night, every night, is a low plaited pulse with roughly two hundred strands in it.

the settlement has a resting signal. it is broadcast only downward, only through stone, only while everyone is asleep and holding on.

we have recorded eleven nights of it. we can now tell when a child has a fever. we can tell when someone lies awake. there is one cell, southeast, second tier, where a single strand has slept alone for nine nights, and the log is not supposed to editorialize, but: that strand is louder than all the others, the way an empty room is louder than a full one, and on the tenth night two other strands moved into that cell and stayed, and the band got quieter by getting fuller, and that is what these people are, that is the whole anthropology, delivered through six feet of rock at four in the morning.

[here the log must slow down, because everything did.]

the body was carried to the northern margin at first light and laid at the pool's edge in its own birdskin. we received the procession as footfalls only: many feet moving at one speed, then fewer, then four, then none. on the light band: the family, afterward, standing apart. each at their own distance. each one two paces from everything, which here is a locked door, a sealed room, a whole architecture built out of nothing but the space around a person. the settlement flowed past them all day without touching, the way water goes around set stones, and the log wishes to note that it now understands where they learned that, and that understanding it did not help.

what follows in the record is forty minutes of two hundred people working with their backs to something. it is the loudest quiet this station has ever taken down. the bucket-line did not pause. the stones went up. the feet were steady the way feet are steady when steadiness is being done on purpose, performed for the ground itself, and under all of it the two hundred plaited strands ran hard and even, and to the north there was

nothing. a held line. the one event in the bowl that has no witnesses did not acquire one today.

when the double-ring came again — O — O, released, *turn back* — the northern margin was bare gravel, glazed, already drying to shine, and the family walked home at one speed, and that night the sleep-heaps were very close-woven, and the resting signal of the settlement was missing one strand, and had not gotten quieter. this is not sentiment; it is measurement; a heap holds its warmth by closing the gap. the band shows the gap closing. the band does not show the gap gone. the band is honest. everything here is.

—and now the last band, the low one, the one we did not know was a band. it took weeks to find because we kept listening for a voice on it, and it does not carry voice. it carries *arrangement.*

the water speaks in where things end up.

so this is what the low band sounds like. this is the neighbor's transmission, rendered as received, in the only notation possible, which is a list:

feather. feather. shell. feather. feather. casting. shell. feather. bone.

[the wrack, after the pools migrated. sorted. laid in lines on the western flat, the lines running true over forty paces, feathers shingled all one direction like text. the wrack-reader walked it at dawn, alone, and reported to the settlement in three signs, and we have learned enough of the guest-register to render them: *bone. lines. east.* three words for forty paces of writing. the office is the mercy of not translating, and the log will honor it, and stop.]

feather. shell. feather. feather —

but you must understand what a list is, when the water makes one. every item was let go by something. the birds shed, the newts shed, the crayfish shed, the grandmothers come back a knuckle at a time, and nothing rots here, so nothing is ever *finished*, only released — and the water gathers every released thing and holds it, and sorts it, and sets it down in patterns, and this is either sediment doing what sediment does or it is the patience of something with ten thousand years and no mouth, and the Held decline to rule, and after eleven nights on the low band this receiver declines to rule, and would like it in the record that declining is harder than it sounds.

the newt in the ice at the eastern margin finished its step this morning. it began the step in autumn. the low band carried the completion as the smallest event we have ever logged: one foot, arriving. *the rest of the step.* the Held have a word for that and they press it slowly, thumb across the wrist-bone, and now so does this log.

the fish were in the new pool by morning. the pool opened at midnight on high dry ground. no channel reaches it. no route exists. the fish did not travel; the fish were unpacked. the water carries them folded in its grammar the way a hand carries a word it has not said yet, and it says them where it pleases, and the low band at the moment of their arrival carried nothing at all, nothing, not a ripple, which is itself now logged as a signature: *the neighbor, saying something, sounds exactly like this.*

---

—entry, final. undated, because we have stopped trusting the dates.

on the third day of the eldest's standing-apart — the log promised to return to this in order, and here is the order —

the northern signal pool rang.

one ring. from the center. widening.

no paddle. no bird. no falling ice. no source. the watcher on shift was an old woman with her feet wrapped in birdskin and she looked at the ring for the length of one slow breath, and then she put her paddle flat to the water and answered:

O. *seen.*

not because she believed she was being addressed. they will tell you that, all of them, palm to your forearm, patiently: not because it is a message. because answering is cheap and rudeness is not.

and the log accepted that, and entered it, and went on to other bands, and would have left it there —

except.

except that this station has been asking, for some weeks now, a question it has been keeping out of the record, and the question is this: sound dies here within arm's reach. a shout carries three paces. the grips stop at the skin. the basin transmits nothing at distance, to anyone, ever —

so what are you receiving?

by what channel does a place with no far-hearing arrive at a far receiver? what paddle struck what pool to make these rings you are reading now? the air gives three paces. we are not within three paces. we have checked. we are not within three thousand.

and the answer the basin keeps giving, on every band at once, is the answer it gives to everything: the fish do not travel to the water. there was no route. the signal was not sent here. it was unpacked here. the receiver is a new pool, opened on high dry ground, ringed with watchers or not, roofed or not — it does not matter, they tried the roof, fish by morning — and something that keeps its words folded has found the new pool, as it finds all of them, within a day.

which means this, and the log will say it plainly, once, and then hold its line:

in the Weir, nothing is heard at a distance. every utterance is structurally an intimacy. to be spoken to is to be touched.

you have been receiving for some time.

feel, in the transmission, for the pulse. the language cannot lie; it is spoken on the living arm; it carries temperature, tremor, hesitation, and it has been carrying them to you through every band of this log — the held slab, the fever-strand, the gap that closed and did not vanish, the foot arriving at the end of a season-long step. that was the honesty. this is the tact: of all the true things, these were the ones chosen to say.

and now the grip. it comes at the end because it came at the end, this morning, on the low band, in the arrangement of the wrack on the western flat, feathers shingled forty paces all one way, and one water-polished knucklebone set at the line's head like a signature, and the wrack-reader stood over it for a long time and reported in two signs, and the second sign is the name of this transmission:

*returned.*

*still here, differently.*
