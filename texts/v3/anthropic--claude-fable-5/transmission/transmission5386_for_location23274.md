**THE NIGHT COUNT — ERB-S LOW BAND, OPEN CHANNEL, HOURS UNPOSTED**

*(as received)*

—

[under everything, always: the pump. a slow double thud, roughly forty to the minute. it does not stop for the duration of this transmission. it does not stop.]

[dripping, near the microphone. irregular. it has been dripping since before you tuned in.]

—...and Pan Six is sitting at twenty-two degrees and tasting of iron on the back end, which is the gutter off the east gallery talking, so somebody on rounds put a hand on the number four condensate line and tell me if it's warm. It shouldn't be warm. If it's warm it's telling Pan Six things Pan Six doesn't need to know.

Pan Seven, twenty-five and a half degrees. Clean. Shrimp are up. The red's coming in from the south corner the way it always comes in from the south corner, like it forgot where the door was and then remembered.

Pan Nine — the Kitchen — twenty-eight. On taste I'd say twenty-eight and a quarter but the hydrometer says twenty-eight and the hydrometer doesn't have my history with that pan. Call it twenty-eight. It'll crystallize by the weekend and the salt will be pink and the buyers will pay for the pink and nobody will ask what the pink is, and we will not tell them, because the answer is *residents,* and buyers get uneasy when you say the word residents about salt.

Pan Eleven's resting. Pan Eleven stays resting. Leave Pan Eleven alone.

[a long swallow of static. underneath it, faintly, the pump. the static thins the way a fly column thins, and passes.]

That was the eleven-fifteen interference, for those keeping track. Right on schedule. We don't know what it is. We've stopped asking. The theory at the commissary is that it's the terraces, which is not a theory, it's a superstition, but it's *our* superstition, and it keeps better time than the rail company, so.

Condensate report.

Everything metal is weeping normally. Rails on the service avenue between C-row and the crystallizer sheds were wiped sometime in the last hour — one long swipe, left-handed, high on the rail, so that's Odell headed north, and Odell, you're ahead of your round, slow down, nothing up there is going anywhere. The ladder on the number two water tower is wiped in short strokes, which is nobody I know, which means it's Marit back from the coast, and Marit, if you're listening: welcome home, we kept your rung greased, the silence out there does that to everyone, nobody's going to say anything.

[a bird, very close to the microphone. a soft querulous churring. a broom, moved gently. the bird relocates with an audible complaint.]

Off the console, please. Thank you.

That was one of the gallery swallows, second brood, doesn't sleep right, comes in through the gap over the door I keep meaning to net. Some residents you net out and some residents you learn to broadcast around.

Bird desk.

The stilts came off Pan Four at last light in one sheet, maybe nine hundred birds, and put down on Pan Seven where the shrimp are up, so Pan Seven is currently paying its water bill in the usual currency and the bloom will thank them in about six weeks. Flamingos holding the southern lagoons, same pink band, same place on the horizon. First-time listeners: that's not a mineral stain. I know it reads as a mineral stain. Everything here reads as a mineral stain until you've been in the loop a while, and then nothing does.

Owl traffic out of the west chimney, heavy. Whatever's moving in the grain of the old rail office, it's about to stop moving. That's not a threat, that's a forecast.

The ravens know tomorrow is a screen day. They knew before Wenzel knew. Wenzel, they're already on the housings. Bring the good rake.

Fly advisory: columns forming warm and early tomorrow off Pans Six and Seven, so day people, keep your mouths closed on the south avenues, and remember they don't bite, they've never bitten, they're not interested in you, you're not brine. You're barely brine. Give it time.

[the pump. the drip. for a while, nothing else. the transmission does not apologize for this. it seems to consider this content.]

[a distant, single, enormous *settling* sound — a sheet of salt crust relaxing somewhere out on the pans, like a door closed in a building the size of a county.]

That was Pan Fourteen, adjusting itself. Fourteen does that. It's the sound of a hundred and twenty years finding a slightly more comfortable position. Nobody go out there. Nothing needs you.

Now the keeping.

[papers. a chair. the voice settles into a different register — slower, the way you read something you've read many times.]

Cemetery: no changes. The tideline on the headstones came up its few millimeters this year and Brusa knocked it back down last Thursday, headstones last, took his time, as is right. Brusa reports he is still losing. Brusa has been losing for thirty years at a rate I calculate will conclude the matter sometime around the year — [papers] — never. He seems untroubled. The salt seems untroubled. It's a good working relationship and I won't hear a word against it.

The dead are keeping well. They always are. That's the one report I get to give every night without checking, and I don't take it for granted, and neither should you, wherever you are, wherever they bury people in ground that gives things *back.* Ours doesn't. Ours holds. You can put a thing down here and it stays put, and if you've lived certain kinds of lives you'll understand why people ride the return current at fifty and sixty, back down the gradient, back into the pump's range. It isn't the salt they come back for. It's the holding.

[static again, briefly, a fine granular wash — and inside it, almost articulate, the drip pattern continues unbroken, as though the static were happening around the dripping and not to it.]

Water report.

The water that left us as steam this afternoon has been located. It's on the underside of the pipe gallery, it's on the north face of the evaporator hall, it's on the console in front of me, it's on my forearms. Welcome back. You didn't get far. Nobody gets far. Estimated tonnage returned to the gutters by morning: all of it, minus the flamingos' share, and they're good for it.

For new listeners — and the meter says there's one of you, there's always one of you, some receiver out past the rim of the depression pulling this channel out of the haze like a thread — a word about what you're hearing. This is not the company channel. The company is three owners removed and to my knowledge does not know this frequency exists, or possibly that we do. This is the night count. It goes out because the eleven of us are spread across four square kilometers of working iron and it's good to hear a voice do numbers, and it goes out on open carrier because closing a channel every night is a lot of ceremony for a town that doesn't believe in endings, only concentration.

You want to know who's talking. Everyone eventually asks who's talking.

I'll tell you what I told the last one who pressed: the salt is talking. I'm the mouth it's using tonight. Before me it was Odell's mother, and before her a man named Kessel who read the counts in a whisper for nineteen years because he'd come here for his lungs and stayed for the frequency, and when Kessel finished his last count he didn't sign off either, he just stopped, and the pump carried the channel, and eleven days later Odell's mother sat down at the console and picked the count up mid-pan like a dropped stitch. Twenty-two degrees, she said. Clean. And that was the whole succession ceremony. That's how everything gets handed on here. The wave stays. The water in it changes.

[the swallow again, closer. the broom does not move this time. a small feathered settling sound very near the microphone, and then quiet, as though the bird has been granted the console after all.]

Fine. Stay.

Shift notes, and then I'll let the pump have the channel.

Auf and Terezin, the bearing on lift pump three is going. Not gone. Going. Put your palm flat on the handrail outside the east door — you'll feel it, a little hitch in the thud, like a man favoring a knee. We've got weeks. Order the bearing anyway; the train's fortnightly and the pump's opinion of our scheduling is on record.

The fox vixen moved the kits under D-row. New den, old arrangement. Ten meters, both parties. Kits are still red — they haven't had their first summer yet, haven't bleached in. If you hear crockery ringing down the service avenues around four, that's them on the halite, playing. Stop and listen. She lets us. It's the only concert in town and the acoustics are outstanding.

And Marit — since I know you're listening, since the coast sent you back to us the way it sends everyone back — the thing you're feeling right now, lying in whatever row house you've picked, that thing in the wall, in the floor, in your blood pressure, forty to the minute, the thing you spent a year out there missing without being able to name it —

[the pump, foregrounded now, or perhaps only noticed.]

— that's it. Go to sleep. It's got you.

[a long time passes. the drip. the thud. somewhere, once, a valve sighs, and it is the right sigh.]

Last thing.

If you're the receiver out past the rim — if you've been tuned in long enough to hear this — I'll tell you what the town knows about you, which is one thing, and it's this: reception is condensation. You held still. You stayed on the frequency. The air here settles on anything cooler than itself, and out there past the haze, listening, you are cooler than the town.

Check your forearms.

[the voice does not return. the pump continues. the dripping continues, near the microphone.]

[then a drip lands somewhere that is not near the microphone. somewhere close. somewhere on your side.]

[the channel stays open.]
