[drip on broadleaf. uncountable, but at three rates at least: a fast line somewhere under the east crowns, a slow line along the bridge to the tanks, and one heavy single drop close to the receiver that falls every eleven seconds onto something hollow — a gourd, maybe — a note like a knuckle on a door, patient, all afternoon]

[under everything, as it has been under everything for the whole of this reception: the south fall. the drone. water leaving the town for the cloud]

[weavers, eastern trees, hundreds, continuous. the transcription gave up on the weavers some time ago. assume them under every line that follows. they are under every line that follows]

[two women on the tank stair, going up with empty gourds, pitched to carry]

"—and I told him, that basket held salt three seasons and now it holds his opinions, and neither one is dry—"

[laughter, the ringing forward kind, thrown like the voice]

"—so weigh it again. Weigh it in front of her. Let the scale talk, the scale doesn't have a mother in the west valley—"

[they pass out of range. their feet make no sound the receiver can find. their hands are moving the whole time they climb — the receiver cannot carry hands. note it anyway: half of every conversation here goes out on a channel this apparatus was not built for]

[whistle, high in the canopy, west side:]

♪ high — high — falling, twice
[call: FRUIT READY, west crowns. this one is known now. it took the receiver eleven days to learn it]

[answering whistle, farther:]

♪ two short, one held
[call: COMING]

[a monkey. no — four monkeys. shrieking of children, immediate, gleeful, warlike. a thrown stick knocks through leaves. a child's voice, top of the lungs:]

"HE'S GOT THE RACK END — SALT-FACE HAS THE RACK END — GO UNDER, GO UNDER—"

[thudding of small feet on wet wood, fast, sure, terrifying. an adult voice from below, not alarmed, not even loud by local standards:]

"If you fall in the tank again you dry your own clothes."

["dry" here meaning: near the fire. the receiver has stopped translating this. there is no other dry]

[the monkey called Salt-Face escapes with something. the children claim victory anyway. both sides appear satisfied. this engagement has been running, per the elders, for longer than anyone present has been alive, including possibly Salt-Face]

—

[and then:]

[nothing]

[the weavers stop. the pigeons stop. the children stop mid-word. four seconds. the receiver's meters drop so hard the operator checked the connections — but no: the south fall is still there, alone, the drone with nothing on top of it, and it is enormous in the gap, it is the loudest thing ever received, four seconds of only water falling off the edge of the world]

[then a shadow must have passed, because everything resumes at once, arguing about it, twice the volume, the weavers outraged, a child laughing shakily, and an old voice on the bridge says one word — the word for that silence, the hawk-word, said the way you'd say "there it goes" — and the receiver has the word now but will not spell it, because they have a word for the four seconds and no word at all for everything else, and the transcription is starting to think they are right]

—

[dusk comes up from below, the way everything comes up here. the light goes from watery to pewter to ember. the frogs arrive by sections — the moss on the low rails first, then the tank edges, then everywhere, the whole green pelt of the town ringing, ticking, belling, thousands of small wet voices in the upholstery]

[and then the old hollow tree opens]

[it is a sound like cloth tearing, if the cloth were a quarter mile long and the tearing took the better part of ten minutes. the bats pour out of the dead heart of the eldest tree and the sound goes up through the canopy and out over both valleys and down into the cloud, and all through the town, hearths get poked, pots go on, someone somewhere strikes nothing — no one here strikes fire, fire is only ever woken — someone somewhere breathes on coals, and forty threads of smoke go up into the mist and become it]

[a doorway. a hearth pit, clay-lined, ticking. an old woman's voice, and a younger one. the old one is a shaper. the receiver has sat outside this doorway many evenings now. tonight the cords are down off the wall — the transcription can hear them, a dry ropy shifting, the driest sound in the town]

"Third cord. Ninth knot from the hearth end. Read it."

[pause. fingers on cordage]

"A wrist-root. East tree. Started toward... the gap over the low tank. Weighted — two stones. The season knot is..."

"Is what."

"It's swollen. I thought wet got into it."

"Wet doesn't get into her cords. She waxed every one. No. That is how she tied grief."

[the fire ticks]

"Her son went down to the west valley that year and didn't come back up. Every root she started that season, she tied the season-knot fat like that, so her hands would find it in the dark and remember. There are six of them on this cord. Find the others later. Read the next."

"Tenth knot. The same root. She came back and... eased it? She slackened the line a finger."

"A finger and a half. Why."

"Because — the root was fighting?"

"Because the root was fighting, and she was not in a season for fighting anything. You'll do the same someday and you won't notice you're doing it, and forty years on some apprentice will read your cord aloud to some child and say: here, this is where her hands went gentle, something happened to her this year. That is what the cords are. Not the roots. The roots keep themselves, mostly. The cords keep us. Read the next."

"Eleventh. The root took the bend. She marks it... true?"

"She marks it true. Now put your hand on the doorway you came in through tonight."

[movement. a palm on wood — the receiver hears nothing, but hears the silence of a hand on wood, which in this town is a kind of sound]

"That's the root. That one. Ninth, tenth, eleventh knot, third cord. She bent your doorway the year her boy went down, and she eased it a finger and a half because she was tired, and that is why the lintel sits kind and low and no tall lowlander has ever come through it without bowing. Fifty years she's been in the east tree. You walked through her twice today."

[long pause. the frogs, the drip, the fall]

"Come dry. Eat. We'll read the fourth cord when the rain on the bridge slows."

[the rain on the bridge does not slow. they read anyway. the receiver stayed for all of it and transcribed none of it. some evenings the apparatus is only an excuse to sit outside a door]

—

[deep night. the wind arrives without ceremony, exactly as it always does: the drone of the fall wavers first, blown sideways, and then the whole canopy takes it up, and the sound of thirty enormous trees in a gale is the sound of surf, if surf were overhead, if you lived beneath the turning of a sea]

[the frog chorus rides it. frogs do not care about wind]

[the frog chorus near the east bridge stops]

[note the order of what happens next, because the order is the town:]

[first — before any crack, before anything a stranger would call a warning — a door-panel shifts. bare feet on wet wood, unhurried but immediate. an old woman was asleep and is now on the bridge, and the receiver knows it was the silence that woke her, the hole in the pelt of small voices, four spans of rail where nothing sings]

[second: her stick, tapping the great east limb. tock. tock. and the limb answers the tap soundly, it answers sound as a bell — ]

[third: her voice, low, to someone the receiver didn't hear arrive, because two more of them are already there, because the watchers move toward holes in the sound the way water moves toward low ground:]

"It taps true."

"It tapped true at midsummer too."

"Put your hand on the rail. Wait for the gust."

[the gust. the sea overhead heaves. and the receiver's own instruments feel it before the voices confirm it — a flat wrongness under the surf, a limb that makes no complaint at all where every limb around it is speaking, groaning, giving honest voice —]

"It's holding its breath."

"It's not holding its breath. It's lying."

[the worst word in the language, said quietly, on a bridge, in the dark, in the wind. no alarm follows. no bells, no running. instead:]

♪ low, held, then a hook upward at the end — three times, into the gale
[call: previously unknown to this receiver. logged fourteen nights ago from the west side, untranslated. translated now, by circumstance: LIMB LIES, EAST SIDE, CLEAR BENEATH. the receiver would trade a great deal to unlearn the translation the easy way]

[and beneath the whistle, along the root-streets, the sound of the town rolling over in its sleep and moving its children: pallets dragged, a baby complaining, a man's voice saying "up, up, we'll sleep at your uncle's, no, leave the pot, the pot has been through more storms than you" — and then the grove, incredibly, going back to sleep, minus one bridge, minus four households, everyone else trusting the watchers so completely that the snoring resumes within the hour]

[the limb did not fall that night. that is not the point. the point is that it could have, and no one would have been under it, and the watchers stood in the wind till the frogs came back to every rail but that one]

—

[morning. pewter, then watery gold. the gale gone as rudely as it came. one cane bridge-panel down into the moss, already being argued over by pigeons]

[the whistles go out at first light:]

♪ falling, falling, then a long level note, repeated across the canopy, relayed east to west
[call: LOWERING. TODAY. ALL HANDS]

[and the town turns out the way a wave turns over]

[what follows took the whole of the day and the receiver will not pretend to have kept up. transcribe instead what the meters kept:]

[rope. rope everywhere, wet fiber under load, a sound like slow strings — the whole canopy strung and singing in creaks as the great east limb, the liar, condemned at dawn by six old voices in under a minute, is taken in stages. the axe-work is almost the smallest sound of it. the large sound is the walking-down: a weight the size of an ordinary tree lowered through the levels of the town on a dozen lines, and the calls that manage it — not whistles now but the chant-count, forty voices keeping one time, HOLD — GIVE — GIVE — HOLD — with the children cleared back to the rails where they narrate everything at full shriek and the monkeys watch from the west crowns in what the receiver can only describe as professional respect]

[when the last section came down onto the root-floor there was a sound the receiver has no comparison for: an impact you hear in the wood of the whole grove at once, every rail and bridge and house-frame carrying it, a single soft boom in the body of the town — and then a beat of quiet — and then cheering, and someone already crying the way you cry at funerals that went well, and an old watcher saying, at a volume meant to be overheard:]

"Eighty years it stood over the tank stair and never dropped so much as a switch on anyone. It lied at the end. So do we all. Get the fires into it."

[and so: the only dry firewood this town ever has]

[the meal went on past the bats. transcribe: fat in fires, forty households cooking on one wood, the smell — even the receiver's housing smells of it, resin and smoke gone into the wet felt of everything — and grease and shouting and the hand-talk flying between mouths too full to whistle, and a man retelling the night on the bridge with himself somewhat nearer the center of events than the receiver's own record supports, and the old woman who woke first saying nothing, eating, her hand resting on the rail beside her the whole time, reading it, the way she reads it every night, the way she has read it for seventy years]

[a child, later, drowsy, at the edge of a fire:]

"Where does the limb go now?"

"Into us tonight. Into the ash pit tomorrow. Ash to the roots after that."

"And then?"

"And then it's in the tree again, only wiser. Go to sleep."

—

[night, after. the churn resettles, section by section:]

[the frogs are back on the east rail — a young line of them, thin-voiced, but singing, and the watchers walked past and heard it and said nothing, which is their way of saying good]

[a splice already bound over the snapped bridge-root: wet moss and cordage, and somewhere on a cord in a house near the tanks, tonight, a new knot — the receiver would bet the whole apparatus that the season-knot is tied fat]

[the coal-lending goes around: one hearth on the east side drowned in the gale, and the receiver has now heard, three separate times, three separate households offering fire before being asked, each with the elaborate offhandedness of people banking a debt too old to name. the drowned-hearth family took the third offer. the first two will remember being first. everyone will remember everything. that is what the town is instead of stone]

[the drip resumes its three rates. the gourd near the receiver takes up its eleven-second knock. static is climbing on this channel again — the wet gets into everything, the signal is growing moss, and the transcription can no longer reliably tell the apparatus's own hiss from the rain on the far bridges, and has decided this is not a fault]

[the south fall holds the drone]

[far off, a child's whistle, practicing, quiet, meant to be private:]

♪ high — high — falling... once. a pause. again: high — high — falling, twice, the second phrase flat by a hair

[call: FRUIT READY. there is no fruit ready. it is the middle of the night. an adult voice, warm, from inside a doorway:]

"Nearly. The second fall wants to drop farther. Again tomorrow. Sleep."

[the frogs. the drip. the fall. the vast held breath of the old hollow tree, full and warm and waiting for evening]

[the receiver will stay tuned. there is no end of this to reach. it was going on at full volume before this channel opened, and it will be going on after the moss takes the last of the signal, and the only thing this transmission has ever really carried, under all of it, is that the grove waits for no one, mends everything, and keeps every hole in the sound exactly as long as it must — and not one second longer]
