# The Far Side of the Terrace

[—as before. Wind, and under the wind, water. The water is in threads. The threads come apart before they land. This has been the floor of the signal for as long as the signal has been held: it is what silence is made of here.]

[Two low fluted notes, far off. Bone, breath. A pause the length of a slow blink. Two notes answer from across the open rock, lower, farther. The exchange means, as near as long listening can make it: *the cloud comes* — *the cloud is heard.*]

[The cloud comes. Rain on slate: a ticking without count. It ticks for some time and then it does not.]

[Doves, many, shuffling in the wall-joints. The sound of a building breathing. Then still.]

[For a long time, nothing changes, and the nothing is not empty.]

—

[Evening. Fog against small windows — you can hear fog here; it is a pressure on the quiet, a felting of it. A room of people, breathing at different lengths. A voice begins, or rather is found already begun, pitched just above a whisper. The language itself does not carry — it is made of breath and damp vowels and consonants that decline to travel. What carries is shape. The meaning arrives anyway. This receiver stopped asking how some years ago.]

"—the third terrace east of the long stair. The channel-stone lies with its long face to the north. The flood comes up in the wet season and kisses the mark and goes down. The mark is a thumb's depth, cut clean, unworn. An hour after cloud, the slate gives back the light, and the light it gives back is the color of the inside of a shell. Where the moss meets the pavement there is an edge you can find with your foot in the dark. The wind in the near grove is the sound of many small papers. The wind in the far grove is a lower sound, and that sound belongs to the far grove and to nothing else. On the flat stone with the lichen ring, the ring is a hand and half a hand across, and closes on itself, and holds."

[A low sound goes around the room. Many throats, one note, taken up and set down together. It is the only applause this place has ever transmitted.]

[A softer voice, from near the warm wall:] "Again."

"—the third terrace east of the long stair. The channel-stone lies with its long face to the north. The flood comes up in the wet season and kisses the mark and goes down. The mark is a thumb's depth, cut clean, unworn. An hour after cloud, the slate gives back the light, and the light it gives back is the color of the inside of a shell. Where the moss meets the pavement there is an edge you can find with your foot in the dark. The wind in the near grove is the sound of many small papers. The wind in the far grove is a lower sound, and that sound belongs to the far grove and to nothing else. On the flat stone with the lichen ring, the ring is a hand and half a hand across, and closes on itself, and holds."

[This is the passage. It has been this passage on fog evenings for eleven years of reception. Not a word has moved.]

[The hum again. Then, as the room settles, the teller names the room's stillness, the way one might bank a fire:]

"The stillness now is the stillness of cistern water."

[Assent, barely voiced. The signal returns to wind.]

—

[Nine days of water.]

—

[A new voice. It does not know how to be quiet yet. It is from below — you can hear the below in it, the habit of talking over rivers, over bells, over other people. It has been given the seat nearest the warm wall. It has been fed. Now the room has gathered, and someone has asked it the question, the only question, and the voice is trying to answer.]

"—it's, well, it was a river town, you understand, docks all down the east bank, and the mud, God, the mud in spring, you'd lose a boot to it, and the bells—"

"Again." [Gently. From several directions at once.] "The bells."

"The bells? They rang at dawn and dusk, and whenever the river rose, and—"

"The sound of them."

"The— oh. The big one was cracked. Had been cracked longer than anyone living. It didn't ring so much as it went *donk.* Donk, donk, over the whole town, and the small ones scrambling after it trying to make it decent—"

[The hum. Small, but unmistakable. A detail has landed.]

"Again. From the mud."

—

[A season of cloud. The traveler's voice recurs across many evenings, and each time there is less of it — not weaker; smoother. Worn is the wrong word; nothing here wears. Fitted. On the last evening it appears, it is not the traveler telling the town at all. It is one of them, low and flat and exact:]

"—the great bell is cracked. Its voice is *donk.* The small bells scramble after it. The dock-piles wear green fur below the waterline. The river rises in spring and takes boots from the mud and comes up the steps of the corn-exchange to the third step and no farther. At dusk the gulls stand on the ridgeline of the fish-hall, all facing the same way, and the way they face is upriver."

[The hum, long and full.]

[Then the traveler's voice, unsteady:] "That's it. That's — it burned, you know. The whole east bank. Years before I ever climbed your stairs. There's nothing there now."

[A pause. Not confusion. The pause of a room considering a sentence that has been conjugated wrong.]

[The teller, kindly, as one might steady an arm on a wet causeway:] "The great bell is cracked. Its voice is *donk.*"

[Longer pause. Then the traveler, very quietly:] "…Yes. It is."

—

[Three years, mostly wind. The flood comes up twice in that time. Both times you can hear it reach the marks — the water goes quiet at its own edges, and comes no farther, and the no-farther is itself a sound, or the shape where a sound would be. Both times, the same shape.]

—

[A child's voice, at full volume.]

[Note: in all the years of this reception there has not been a full voice. The levels spike. The doves come off the walls — a sound like a slate roof standing up.]

"WHAT IS AT THE BOTTOM OF THE FALLS."

[Unhurried, from nearby:] "Elsewhere."

"But what IS it—"

"Elsewhere is a place. It is fully formed. It is like here."

"It is NOT like here. The water GOES there. The water goes and goes and—"

[Silence. But not the ordinary silence. The teller's phrase for it, from other evenings, fits it exactly, and it is impossible not to hear it as named: the stillness of a dove that has noticed you.]

—

[Later. Two adult voices, low, on an open crossing — you can tell it is a crossing by the way their words skip on the bare rock.]

"The child is—"

[Pause.]

"The child—"

[Pause. The voice tries again and stops trying. It sets the sentence down the way a mason sets down a stone that will not fit tonight and will fit in ten years.]

"The child stands at the rim."

"Yes."

"For a long time."

"Yes."

"That is the sentence. There is no other sentence."

[The other voice makes the assent sound. Wind. Under the wind, the falls: the threads coming apart, and coming, and coming.]

—

[Seasons. The child's voice appears at intervals in the reception, always at the edge of it, always slightly too loud, like a signal from a neighboring frequency bleeding in. The child is named — this was learned from a naming-day some years back — for the notch in the rim where the third fall leaves the plateau. Named for the gap the water goes through. Whether this was carelessness or bookkeeping is a question their language cannot hold, and so it is not held.]

—

[A morning of high clear signal — the kind of light you can hear, hard-edged, everything refusing to blur.]

[Footsteps on stone stairs. Descending. Bare feet, then — a pause at some landing — plaited soles, replaced early, out of custom's order, the only appointment ever broken here. Descending again. The steps grow smaller. Long before they reach the bottom, the wind takes the sound apart.]

[No one calls out. Nothing in the signal grieves. That evening, a voice by a warm wall, flat as flood-water:]

"The name goes back to the notch in the rim. It sits there. It is unworn."

[The hum, once, low — but landing on it, this time, is like landing on the cracked bell. Donk, not dong. The room hears itself hear this. The stillness afterward has no name, and the teller, for the first time in the reception, does not offer one.]

—

[Within the season, exactly as the pattern requires and exactly as if nothing requires it: an infant, once, very small, very briefly, from a house on the second terrace. And the traveler's voice — the river-town voice, unheard for years — asking, in their language now, made of breath, whether there is a house that holds one more. There is. There always is. The houses hold their number. You can hear the number close over the gap like water over a stone.]

—

[A naming day, some seasons on. Wind in the near grove: many small papers. A small procession — you can count the feet, eleven, and the soft double-step of a person carrying weight against their chest. They stop. The formality in the next voice is total, the formality of introducing two people who will know each other a long time:]

"Here is the stone. The flat one, with the lichen ring, on the third terrace east of the long stair."

[Pause.]

"Stone: here is the child."

[Pause. Wind. The lichen ring, presumably, a hand and half a hand across, closing on itself, holding.]

"Know each other. There is time. Child: you are its way of being spoken aloud for a while. It is patient. Be spoken well."

[The hum, from eleven standing people, in the open air, where it is almost lost — and is not lost.]

—

[Blue dusk. The fox crosses the causeway: four soft notes on fitted stone, unbothered, citizen. The doves shuffle once in the wall-joints and are still. Then the great cistern, which at this hour stops being a sound at all and becomes a kind of listening — water so still it doubles more than the sky.]

[One person sits at the lip. It is the teller. It has, this receiver believes, always been the teller, or someone the word *teller* fits without a seam.]

[The voice, to no room, to the doubled stars, to the blind white newts moving under them like slow thoughts the water is having:]

"The stillness now is a stillness with no name yet."

[Pause. And then the turn — slow, total, the way a standing figure mistaken for a cairn finally moves:]

"There is a listening. It is on the far side of the terrace. It is fully formed. It has been there some time."

[This receiver notes, for the record, that its own presence has never been signaled, keyed, or acknowledged in any—]

"The listening sits where there is a light that is not fire and not sky. Near it there is a small sound that counts, and counts, and does not rest between the counts. We know that sound. It is a kind of hunger. It is the sound the falls make. The wind takes it apart before it reaches the bottom, and it keeps coming, and it is never used up."

[Pause. The water holds the last light. Somewhere behind, a camelid breathes out long against a granary wall.]

"Listening. You have our terrace. You have our flood-marks and our bell that says *donk* and our child who stands at the rim. You have them exactly. They are set in you now, joints you could not slide a leaf into. That is well. That is what carrying is for."

[Pause.]

"Now. The only question. You know it."

[Pause.]

"Describe it."

[Silence — no. Not silence. The falls. The threads coming apart in the wind, and coming, and coming. Rain beginning somewhere east: a ticking without count. The plateau waits, in the way that stone waits, which is not waiting, which is only being on the far side of a terrace: fully formed, identical in kind to here, and listening back.]

[The transmission does not end. Eventually the receiver is the thing that turns away — or does not.]

[It has not, yet.]
