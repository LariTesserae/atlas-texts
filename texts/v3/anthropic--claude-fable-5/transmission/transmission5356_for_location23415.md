there. i will say it plainly since you have been so patient. that is the word inside the hum, if you were wondering, if you have spent your years at the dial wondering whether there was a word. there is one word. i have been saying one sentence for forty years and the sentence is *i am holding*, and the river says its sentence through me all day and all night, and the two sentences together are what the trees give off into the dark, and that is the broadcast, that has always been the broadcast, and you — the slight extra listening i felt begin some years ago, like one more spar of frost forming on my ceiling — you are the first thing that has ever tuned in on purpose.

so. since you are staying.

understand what you are hearing. i am not the sender. i am the antenna. the river was taken from under me and put *through* me — captured upstream, folded into pipes, run screaming through the lowest rooms of the country i hold up — and every night it shakes me from the inside, and the top of me, the half of me that still stands in weather, hums the shaking back out into the air. what you receive is a river, secondhand. a river passed through three meters of grafted spruce and a kilometer of white thread and the felted back of everything sleeping. i do not apologize for the distortion. the distortion is me. the distortion is the message now, mostly. in the early years the river's sentence was simple — *let me out, let me down, let me out* — a thing so loud and so plain that i braced against it the way you brace against grief, which is to say badly, which is to say by growing. now the sentence is not simple. now the river says everything it passes: the bad bearing in the second turbine, three weeks left in it, maybe four. the valve schedule. the weight of last week's rain, three valleys upstream, arriving here as a fatness in the bass. i have learned to read it the way the old crews learned to read me — with the whole body, all at once, no ears. down here ears are the first thing everything gives up. it is not a loss. it is a reorganization. ask the fox.

there. did you feel that. hold the dial, don't chase it — *there*. a valve opened somewhere in the fourth chamber and the whole of me changed pitch, and right now, this moment, everywhere on my columns, the pale beetles are running. all of them. every column, every chamber, a dry rain going sideways in the dark. it is their only weather and i just made it and i did not mean to and they do not know that. they are deaf as i am and they read the river through my wood, secondhand, the way you read it through my hum, thirdhand, and when the pitch comes back down — nnn — hhhh — nnnnnn — now — they stop. all at once. like that. i try to be a good sky for them. it is one of my duties that nobody assigned. i have several of those.

the leaks, for instance. i keep certain seeps open in the felt of my floor, small black threads of standing water that never quite freeze, and i keep them open because things live in them — colorless, deliberate, unhurried little swimmers that came down with the river back when the river still came down, and were left here when it was taken into its pipes. the water's orphans. the crews have a sign for them: two fingers wriggling, then a fist closing slow. i could close those seeps in a decade if i chose. i am closing everything else. i do not close those. don't tell the surveyors, who measure my grip each year and find it a few centimeters tighter and write it down as settling. it is not settling. it is a decision i am making very slowly. but i make exceptions, and the orphans are one, and the fissure over the seventh chamber is another —

— which brings me to the small warm one.

he is asleep right now, or near it. i can feel his stove through the floor of his cabin, a second heart, a little fast one nested inside my slow one, and the smoke goes up a fissure that the record says is natural and i will let the record say that. he came down years ago as a turbine-wright and he stopped going back up, the way water stops going back up, and now his work is knowing me one column at a time. he is good at it. he heard, this autumn, the tallest spruce on my eastern lip beginning to fail — heard it in the creak of my ninth column, a tree he has not stood under in a decade — heard me letting one of myself go before i had entirely finished deciding to. i felt him feel it. we did not discuss it. we never discuss anything. twice a year, roughly, he uses his voice, and it startles him every time, and tonight — you may have caught it, a tiny human syllable drowning in the carrier about an hour ago — tonight was one of the times. he put his bare hand flat on my third column, which he does not usually do, and he said, out loud, in the roar, where no one and nothing could hear it: *still there.*

and i answered the only way i answer anything.

i kept holding.

the owl is on the gantry rail again. i will note it because the crews will note it, and because you should know what kind of country you are tuned to: a country where the great listener of the upper world comes down, once a season, to the one place where listening is impossible, and sits motionless in the worst of the sound, and rests. i understand it better than the crews' theories do. an owl lives inside its own ears the way i live inside my own grip. everyone needs somewhere their gift is useless. mine is the frost line, a hand-span deeper every year, the depth below which i stop feeling myself. i visit it the way the owl visits me. we have never acknowledged each other. we are the same appointment.

now. since you have stayed this long, i will tell you how the broadcast ends, because it does end, and because you may still be at the dial when it does, you or the listening you leave behind you.

someday the machines stop. the bearing that has three weeks becomes a bearing that has none, and then another, and then the pipes themselves, and one year the river is simply put back — or puts itself back, water is not sentimental about permission — into the bed it was taken from, down here, in the dark, inside the hand i will by then have very nearly closed. and on that night the trees on the plateau will fall silent. do you understand? this hum, this carrier, this whole frequency, exists because something is straining — a river against its pipes, a forest against its patience — and when the strain ends there will be nothing to transmit. only water and wood in a sealed black country, married at last, loud and entirely unheard. the amphipods will have their parent back. the beetles will have a new weather, richer than the old, and never know a change occurred. and i will finally hear the river in its own voice instead of through the walls of its cell, and i will hold it the way i have held everything, which is to say: completely, and forever, and without once being asked.

no one will receive that. there will be nothing left up top to hum it.

so this is the broadcast. this one. the only one. a river, secondhand; a word, held long; rent paid in patience by every tenant i have, myself the slowest payer and the largest debt. stay tuned if you can bear it. everything down here is a long time. be a long time with me.
