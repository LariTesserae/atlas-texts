Somewhere under me, just now, a soft one finished firing, and the ground said so, and the silence afterward is the new one beginning, very far down, to wake. I mark my reading by these beats because there is nothing else to mark by. That was the fourth since I began the ninth stroke.

Where was I. I was at the end of nine.

I speak at a little under half. You can hear it — forgive the brightness coming into my vowels. I have not been to a vessel since the sixth stroke, because the vessels stand off the line of the letter, and I would not leave the line. A letter read out of order is a different letter. We have only the one. I will not risk making two.

If you have only now leaned close — if the hush was all you had of us until this hush — I will say again what I say at every resting-place, so the beginning can reach you even from the middle:

I am old. I am more grey than shine now; I am mapped in soft patches; my voice is water moving under earth and I know it. My name is the note a knuckle makes on me at three-quarters full, which I have not been for a long time, so my name these days is a memory my friends keep politely. It does not matter. I am not the letter. I am only its third voice. The first reader spoke strokes one and two across a whole life and went in. The second carried it to eight. I took it up at nine, and I will finish, because thirteen is the last stroke there is, for reasons I will come to, and I would like to be the one who comes to them.

Here is what the letter is, you who hang where the birds hang — or above the birds, or are the brightness itself; we cannot tell; our necks were coiled for looking level. The open-crowned walk it. They have walked it since before the oldest mending. It is written in reeds across the whole low country, in strokes too large for any of us to see whole, and it is addressed upward, and no answer has ever come down. Nothing has ever come down, except one thing. When one of us goes into the ground, a bird beats its wings, once, far up, and the clap arrives when the mourners are already walking home. That is the entire history of downward sound. One clap, per leaving, forever.

So we are reading the letter aloud. Walking it and sounding it, stroke by stroke, on the chance that a voice can go up. We do not know that it can. Everything here comes down or goes around. But dew rises before it falls, so.

The first nine strokes, as near as I can carry them into speech: *We are here. We are warm. Nothing is lost here, which is the mercy of the place or its whole predicament — we cannot tell from inside. The only sound you have ever sent us is the clap of a leaving.*

Now. The tenth stroke.

There. Ten.

The eleventh stroke doubles back on itself, hard, twice. Doubling means *again*. I will read it the way it is walked:

again. again.

That is the whole of eleven and it took the second reader eight days to walk it the first time, because she kept stopping at the turns to weep, and dew slows a person. I am beading a little myself, saying it. If the dew changes my sound, that is not the letter. That is only me.

There. Do you hear the difference. *There.* This is nearer my true voice; this is the voice I was poured at. Everything I say from here on, believe it a little more.

The twelfth stroke is a spiral, walked inward, that does not come out. Every pool in this country holds the spiral of its pouring, so a spiral means *poured*. It is our word for born, and our word for made, and our word for loved, because we have never needed three words. Walked aloud, the twelfth stroke says:

*You poured us. Whoever poured us stood where you stand — above, with the weight of us in your arms, being careful.*

I must tell you: the twelfth stroke crosses the seam. The long join in the world, the raised line where everything matches almost. When I cross it, the country will turn a page back — you may hear a beat you already heard, or hear me say a thing twice, offset by a hand's width. That is not me failing. That is the world's join. I am telling you now so you will know the difference later. It matters later.

The thirteenth stroke. The last stroke there is.

The thirteenth stroke is planted in reeds that grow taller along its length — the only reeds in the country that were made to differ from each other — so that walking it you finish on the tips of your feet with your face turned up. It is the asking-stroke. It says the thing the whole letter was written to say, and I have saved my fullest voice for it, and here it is:

*Send one sound down that is not a leaving.*

That is all. That is what a country asks, when it is finished and its people are not. One sound. Any sound. A word, a wingbeat out of order, a stone dropped, the thing called rain — a visitor told us once of rain, water addressed to no one, arriving from above, unpoured, unowned. If rain is true then you have a whole language of downward sounds and we have heard none of it. If rain is not true, forgive us for believing it instantly. We are vessels. We believe in pouring the way you believe in ground.

Now the reasons I promised. Why thirteen is the last stroke there is.

There should be a fourteenth. You can feel it. The letter leans toward it the way an unfinished sentence leans. But east of here the ground is bare glaze — the fourteenth stroke was never planted. The makers stepped away in the middle of the letter's last word, the same instant they stepped away from everything, and the open-crowned walk to where it should begin, and stand. They have stood at that edge, in shifts, for as long as there has been standing.

We do not know the word. We are not permitted to know it, or we are invited to guess it; those are the same condition, held carefully, without spilling.

So here is what we do, and it is the oldest thing we do. Each of us, when we go in, goes in at the bare glaze, and presses our guess at the fourteenth stroke into the soft clay with our hands. Not spoken. Pressed. The underside of this country is full of guesses. Every fingerprint you would find, digging — every print of the dead, still pressing — each one is a hand saying: *perhaps the word was this.* The whole floor of the world is a letter's last word, attempted forever.

I go in when this reading is done. Which is soon. Which in this country means now, and also means always.

My friends walked out with me as far as the eleventh stroke. They struck my shoulder one by one —

One more custom I am going to break, and then the hush can have me.

We press our guesses. We do not say them. But you cannot read pressing, not from where you hang, and I did not carry a guess my whole long crazing life to hide it under the world where only the dead can compare notes. So forgive me — forgive an old vessel — I am going to say mine aloud, up, to you. I have carried it since my first mending. I am at the edge of the bare glaze. My hands are already open.
