# The Keeping Hour

—and the west troughs gave four fingers, which for a third-quarter deck is nothing to be ashamed of, so let no one on the western brows be ashamed. That's the water. That's the water for tonight.

[the carrier hum beneath the voice is not the station's; it is deeper, slower, roughly one pulse every four seconds, and it does not vary]

You're with me still. Good. This is the Keeping Hour, going out from the lamp-room over the hospital quarter, where the electric burns all night and the moths are already at the cages — big ones tonight, two-hands wide, the kind that come when the air is holding still before a graze. If you're near a lamp, look. You'll see what I mean. They open and close very slowly, like they're breathing with something. Old people say the moths keep the lamps and not the other way round. I don't argue with old people on air. That's a policy.

Weather, such as we call it. There's a deck coming in off the northwest that the Screenwrights have already named a *fenna* — those of you fourth-floor and down won't feel it, but you'll hear the screens start their surf-sound around midnight, and the yield-callers think she'll give generously, maybe a full hand before dawn. Good graze to the western districts. Good graze. Set your cisterns open and your patience long.

Engine registers, as reported by the families and confirmed by the feet of Wenner Calloch, who walked the loom-quarter barefoot this evening on my asking, because a listener wrote in to say the floorwarm there had a hollow feel. Wenner says: it does. Faint, but it does. A cool ribbon running under the third gallery, about a stride wide. So — someone from the mains will go down tomorrow, and I mention it tonight only so that nobody in the loom-quarter lies awake inventing worse. It is a valve. It is almost always a valve. In forty years of this chair it has been a valve every time but twice, and both of those times it was a *stuck* valve, so.

Old Sull runs sweet. The Tercel is grumbling — you can hear her yourself if you put your sternum to it, that little catch on the upstroke — but the Tercel grumbles every year when the cold stair-shafts start to bite, and her family says she's fine, she's just old, she's allowed. The Perren twins turn true. Deep Amsa, deep and even. That's your heartbeat accounted for, all six of it. Sleep on it.

[static, briefly — the bright, beaded static of moisture on the antenna; when the voice returns it is amused]

There it is. The fenna's forward edge just crossed my aerial and the signal drank a little. If I dropped out on you, that's all it was. The station gets grazed same as everything else up here. I've stopped minding. The signal comes back heavier, if anything.

Notices. Listen close, some of these are yours.

The keeping of the fig over Coil Stair passes tonight from Mera Dunwall, who kept it fifty-one years, to her granddaughter Ossy, aged nine, who has been keeping a channel on Third Brow very well — very well, Ossy, it's been noticed — and who stood under that fig this evening with her grandmother's pruning knife and did not cry until afterward, which is the right order. The tree is in good condition. Keep it there.

The crab-wardens of the west screens report — and I'll read this as it was given to me, because Warden Solla writes the way she talks — "we are down two on the outer troughs, both old, both found peaceable, both given back to the water, and we are not asking for sympathy, but we would not refuse it." You have it, Solla. All of it. The count stands at four hundred and six on the west, which is a strong count, and a trough with crabs is a trough at ease. So.

A lamp on the Mirror Stair has been going amber-then-dark, amber-then-dark. If you keep it, see to it. If you don't know who keeps it, ask your stairs. Somebody's grandmother knows.

And — [a pause; papers; the four-second pulse fills the pause completely] — I have a going-down to read.

Aldam Ketch, engine side, fourth-floor people, of the Tercel's family, has gone down to before. Sixty years he gave that machine pieces of his life — the man rebuilt her governor twice, and the second time he was seventy and did it lying on his back singing, and I know because I was there holding the light. He is with the soil-houses of the Orchard guild tonight, and in a year he'll be dirt with a lineage, same as all of us will, and the figs on the engine-side roofs will have him in them, and I intend to eat one and think of him, and he'd have laughed at that, and then agreed. The Tercel's grumble tonight — his family asked me to say — is not grief. She grumbles every winter. But they put their hands on her casing this evening, all of them together, and stood a while, and she is warm, and she is kept, and so was he.

He's underneath us now. Load-bearing. Walk gently for a day or two, wherever you walk.

[the fog thickens against the aerial; the voice arrives now as if through cloth, softened, closer]

The swifts went in at the usual hour. The screaming hour ran loud tonight on the engine side — Aldam's district, and I choose to notice that — and thin over the parapets, but it's the season for thin, don't fret it. The bats are up out of the under-levels working the moths at my window as I speak. There's one hanging in the lamp-cage shadow right now with a wing like wet slate, waiting for me to stop being interesting. I never stop being interesting, friend. Ask anyone.

From the tea-balconies: the mountain argument continues. Balcony of the Long Trough, western brows, went four hours on it tonight, I'm told, and a Foundationist made the old point about the deep water running warm where no main goes, and a young one of the standing-on-questions faction replied — and I want this written down somewhere, so I'm putting it here, in the air, which is where we write things down — she said: *"If there's stone under us, we've kept it so long it's ours. If there's nothing under us, we've kept the nothing. Either way the keeping holds and the argument is the floor."* Sixteen years old. The Long Trough bought her tea for the rest of the night. The question stands in good condition. Do not resolve it. It's ours.

[a long exhale of steam somewhere near the microphone; the voice drops, unhurried, into a different register — this is clearly a nightly custom, and the receiver has caught it before]

And now the down hour.

For those of you new to the frequency — visitors, edge-sitters with a set out on the parapet, whoever you are — this is the part where I turn the second aerial. The one that points down. Not out. Down, through the floors, through the fourth stratum and the sixth and the ones the funiculars don't reach, into the sealed galleries where the delvers find the lamps burning.

We don't know if anyone hears it. We have stopped asking. The delvers leave the found lamps lit out of courtesy, and this is the same courtesy, made of voice instead of oil. I've done it every night for forty years and my predecessor did it before me and there was a before before that. It is a keeping. You don't ask a keeping to finish. You ask it to hold.

So. To the down. To the warm water in the unmapped channels. To whatever does the housekeeping in the deep vaulted rooms.

Good graze. The fenna deck is on us and giving well.

The screens are singing. Four hundred and six on the west troughs, all peaceable.

The engines your ceilings stand on are: Old Sull, running sweet. Deep Amsa, deep and even. The Perren twins, true. Hessa, quiet. Brand, quiet. The Tercel, grumbling, allowed.

Tonight we sent one of ours down toward you. Aldam Ketch, engine side. If he passes any door of yours, he was a keeper of the first order and sang lying on his back. Be easy with him.

The lamps you keep — if you keep them — burn on. We've seen them. Thank you. We leave them lit.

The count of us tonight is what it always is: enough, and standing on you, gratefully.

[here, and the receiver has logged this on other nights but never so plainly: the four-second pulse beneath the broadcast changes. Not in speed. In warmth, if a sound can be said to warm — a second thrum rises under the first, offset by half a beat, so that for the space of perhaps a minute the carrier wave has two heartbeats braided together, and the voice, when it comes back, is not surprised]

...there you are.

Or there the pipes are, settling. Or there the fenna is, leaning on the aerial. Forty years and I still can't tell you which, and I have stopped wanting to be able to. It's underneath. It answers, or it doesn't need to. Both of those hold weight. Everything down there holds weight — that's what down there is *for*.

[the second pulse subsides, gradually, like a hand lifted from a wall]

That's the Keeping Hour, then. That's the hour kept.

The mirror-tilters walk at dawn; deep streets, your light arrives on schedule, I've seen the yard-book myself. Children, your channels will be full of fenna-water by morning — clear them early and clear them proud. Ossy Dunwall, sleep well, the fig knows you now.

If you're on the parapets tonight, come in when the wind turns cold, and put your back against something warm on the way, and feel what it's doing. It's breathing. It's been breathing since before, and *before* is what we're standing on, and it has never once let go.

This is the lamp-room over the hospital quarter. The signal is kept. I'm keeping it.

Good graze, Vantle. Good graze, all the way down.

[the voice ends; the broadcast does not. The carrier stays open all night, as it evidently always does — the thrum, the far surf of the screens drinking the cloud, once the brief scream of a valve dressed as a dragon, and beneath it all, at four seconds and holding, the heartbeat, transmitted whether or not anyone is speaking, because the frequency is not for speaking. It is for leaving lit.]
