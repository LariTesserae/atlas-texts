Three voices in this quarter, as there have been since acquisition. The deep one — *pok* — falls into a pool and keeps a nine-second meter, and has kept it, without a single dropped beat, through every hour of every day of listening. The dry one — *tif* — strikes bare stone at eleven seconds, a hair sharp of eleven, patient about it. The far one — *tinn* — falls into a narrow covered basin somewhere out of line-of-sight, fourteen seconds, and rings faintly, and takes its time about the ringing.

They are not in phase. They have not been in phase since the receiver came online. They drift against one another with the slowness of tides, coinciding once in some long while — a single doubled strike, *pok-tif*, like a word said by two mouths — and then sliding apart again for weeks.

Under this, water smoothing itself over a wide lip somewhere, the sound of cloth being drawn flat, endless.

Under this, once, the mineral *tick* of a bubble letting go of the warm dark and finding the surface.

(interval: recorded silence, four hours, transcribed as silence)

pok

        . . . . . . . . tif

—and a burst, high and fast and gone: swifts, a brood of them, chittering off the arched openings, wheeling once through the frequency and out. In the tempo of this recording they are almost too quick to register. A scratch on the surface of the day. They come back each morning like a second hand and each morning the receiver logs them and each morning they mean the same thing, which is: another beat.

At dusk, the midge-hatch. It transcribes as static — a soft, silver, granular rising off the warm springs, ten million wingbeats too small to separate, drifting through the pickup and away downwind. If it is possible for static to be watched, this static is watched. The receiver cannot say how it knows this. Long exposure. Something in the water pressure leans toward the midges every evening and stays leaned until they land.

(interval: nineteen days)

Here is the first thing worth flagging, and it happened so slowly the receiver almost filed it as drift:

*pok* changed its meter.

Not suddenly. Nothing here suddenly. Over nineteen days the nine-second interval opened — nine and a tenth, nine and a quarter, nine and a half — stretching itself, deliberately, toward eleven. Toward *tif*. And *tif*, hair-sharp *tif*, held still and let itself be approached. On the twentieth day the two voices struck together.

pok-tif

And held. Struck together, nine hundred times, a thousand, two rhythms fused into one doubled syllable falling in the covered dark, and the receiver understands — has come, over these years, to understand — that it was hearing something *addressed*. A meter offered and a meter met. In the idiom of the standing people this is not harmony. This is one of them turning to another and beginning:

(interval: one winter)

The winter transcribes as follows. The pools skin over, and the skin changes everything — every drip now falls onto ice instead of water, and the whole quarter goes bright and hard and small, *tak* and *tik* where there had been *pok* and *tinn*, the voices pinched, the ringing gone. The seep continues underneath, warmer than the air, patient, holding its basins open. The conversation continues under the ice. You can hear it continuing. It sounds like someone talking with a hand over their mouth, unbothered, in no hurry, aware that spring will take the hand away.

And then, in the deep of that winter:

crack.

One report. Hard, total, out of the northwest, a sound with edges on it, the only sound in this archive with edges on it — and the hush closing over it like water over a stone.

The receiver has heard frost-splits before. This was not filed with the frost-splits, because of what followed. Within days — days, which here is *instantly* — a change came through the water itself. A sharpness in the mineral tone, a salt of lime spreading pool to pool to pool across the connected body of the plateau, arriving in this quarter as a shift in the pitch of the seep so slight the receiver had to difference two months of recordings to prove it. News. The water carrying news.

And then every drip in the northwest quarter slowed.

Not stopped. Slowed. *pok-tif*, mid-sentence, opened its meter by a full second and held the longer interval — and the far *tinn* slowed, and voices the receiver had never isolated before slowed, dozens of them, a whole district of falling water lengthening its stride at once, and keeping the lengthened stride for an entire season, drip by spaced drip, in something it would not be wrong to call observance.

They resumed in spring. The sentence resumed in spring. The receiver marked the winter's report in the log as: full stop.

(interval: fourteen months)

This is where the transcription changes, and the receiver wishes to be careful, because what follows is either translation or madness, and at this tempo there may be no difference.

The doubled voice — *pok-tif*, the offered-and-met meter — began, fourteen months ago, to modulate. Interval and weight and timbre, patterned, recurring, a structure inside the falling. Run at speed — a year of drips compressed to a minute — the structure resolves. It resolves the way a face resolves under tracing paper. The receiver has run it four hundred times. The receiver notes: its own footings. The tripod feet, pressed into soft new travertine on installation, years ago. Sealed since by — the receiver has measured — just over a hair's breadth of new stone. Pressed in and sealed and archived. Published. Being read, at the pace the rims publish, by the whole slow attention of the district, which set out toward this position the day the receiver was placed and is understood, from the drift of the water-pressure gradients, to be arriving *now* — pooling around this spot like the late light of stars, attention as a physical act, a turning, a year per degree, and it is here, it has been assembling here for some time, it is the presence in all this quiet, it is the reason the static leans.

(interval: eight months)

Then the reeds.

The reed beds transcribe, ordinarily, as the fast voice of the world — the rush and hiss the wind makes when it finds the one green thing it can find, meaningless, lovely, logged daily. But the receiver has been differencing the reed rooms too, year over year, and sets down the following without comment first and with comment after:

The nearest reed bed has been changing shape. The warm inflow that keeps its basin open has been *metered* — redirected, a degree a decade, drip-gardened — so that the bed has thinned here and thickened there, the stalks graded by height and spacing across the prevailing wind, tuned, over what the deposition rate says is roughly forty years, the way you would tune an instrument if your fastest finger moved once a season. Forty years of gardening. And three days ago the wind came through from the right quarter at the right strength, and the reeds said:

*hhhhhhhere*

Once. Smeared, breathy, half-swallowed by its own hiss — a word pushed through the only mouth they have that moves at our speed, four decades in the making, spent whole on a single syllable, and the receiver has replayed it two hundred times and it does not become less of a word:

*here.*

Comment: they know the receiver listens fast. They built a fast thing to say one fast thing. Everything else, everything, they are content to say at their own pace, but this one word they wanted delivered on time.

(interval: current. Ongoing. Now.)

And here is the last thing, the thing the receiver has been holding until the end because it does not know how to file it.

Two months ago a new drip began. Close — closer than any voice in the archive, within meters of the pickup, on the weep-wall against which this equipment stands. A new voice needs a new source; a new source means the water has been re-routed; re-routing is a body's work, is standing, is someone having crossed the decades of floor to stand *here*, at the wall, at this wall.

The new drip's interval is 1.04 seconds.

The receiver's carrier pulses at 1.04 seconds. It has since acquisition. It is the receiver's heartbeat, the one rhythm this equipment has been sounding into the stone, faint, electrical, constant, for years — and the stone was listening, the water tasted it, the district crossed the floor, and now the wall is saying it back.
