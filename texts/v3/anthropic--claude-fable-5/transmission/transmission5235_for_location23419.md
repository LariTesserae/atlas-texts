**UNDERSONG**

—and the water, which is not sound anymore. Understand that first, whoever is holding the other end of this. The water stopped being sound early in the reception. It is the medium now. It is the paper this is written on. A white pressure, structureless, without rhythm, without pauses, without any of the courtesies by which sound admits it is sound. Everything else in this transmission arrives *through* it, the way a face arrives through frosted glass. If the reception ever goes truly silent, do not adjust anything. Silence is the fault condition. Silence means the world ended.

Inside the white, a second thing, lower: fifty cycles, steady, a hum with iron in it. The turbines. Nine stations' worth, not quite in phase, so the hum has a slow interior beating, a pulse of about eleven seconds, like something enormous breathing in its sleep. The hum has been present for the entire reception. It was built by grandparents. It is being oiled for grandchildren. It does not know this. It just turns.

And then, riding the line the current rides, at intervals a voice.

*

`— Upper Terminus. Week report. Week — ` *(a pause; the number is not supplied; the numbers stopped being supplied some years ago)* `— week whatever it is down there. Gauges as follows.`

The voice is strange and you should know why it is strange. It belongs to a person who does not speak. Nobody up there speaks; the roar took voices out of use generations back, and their language lives in their hands now. But the wire cannot see hands. So once a week, someone sits in the one soundproofed box on the plateau — a telephone closet at the rail terminus, felt-lined, the size of a coffin stood upright — and translates the logbook out loud, down two kilometers of cable, into the under-country. The voice is always rusty. It cracks on long words. It carries the grammar of the hands into the mouth, so the word order comes out spatial, patient, object-first:

`— Moss. East wall. Number Nine. Second rivet course. Reached.`

`— Benchmark bolt one-seventeen: thirty-one millimeters proud of the old datum now. The plateau is still — ` *(a pause)* ` — still coming up. Note for whoever reads this in forty years: check it again. It'll be wrong again. That's fine.`

`— Replaced the forebay gasket at Four. Same gasket my grandmother replaced. Hers lasted longer. Logged same.`

And sometimes, in the middle of a report, a silence of three or four seconds. Carrier only. Water only. Then:

`— Sorry. Hands.`

They forget. They sign into the empty booth, to no one, the words leaving through their fingers instead of their mouth, and only notice when the wire's patience becomes audible. Over the whole reception these gaps have been logged and they are not random. They cluster around certain subjects. The reporters can *say* the gauge readings. What they sign by accident — what refuses the mouth — is always one of three things: the gold of the moss in last light, the wrongness of a bearing a week before it fails, and the silence below the clouds. There is no spoken word up there for that last one. The sign for it, the record shows, is a flat hand pressed over one's own ear, then the empty palm shown. *The missing organ.* The wire receives it as four seconds of water. Transcribe it that way. Four seconds of water is the correct translation.

*

The voice changes without announcement. This is normal. A new mouth, same log. You can date the changes anyway. The early voices, deep in the reception, were formal, copperplate voices — `herewith the readings for the station of the ninth part` — and the recent ones are pencil: `Six holds. Four holds. Bird at the west lip, one, riding. That's it.`

Some fragments, in the order received, across what the under-country would call decades:

`— Counts-Pylons went down the rail Tuesday. Last rotation. Forty-one years. He didn't say anything, obviously. His hands said: check bolt one-seventeen. That was all. That was everything, if you knew him.`

*(nine years of carrier)*

`— Note: the crews are calling the bend past pylon four-hundred "Counts-Pylons" now. Nobody decided this. It's just where his name went. Update your maps or don't.`

`— Tilted-Walk says Number Six loses a bearing before the thaw. She says it with her feet. Stood on the floor of Four with her eyes shut and told us the load, the forebay, and the bearing, in that order. She has been right eleven times. Order the bearing.`

*(the bearing failed before the thaw. this was reported in six words and no surprise.)*

`— Fox came up the cut Thursday. The cook fed it. Nobody saw anything. Gone the following Thursday, down the rail, back to the loud quiet. The cook signed the usual. "Counted the days." He does the two taps after. You can't hear the two taps. He does them anyway. So noted.`

`— New rotation up. One of them talks. Still talks. Mouths words into the roar all day like — ` *(three seconds of water)* ` — like a rope he's afraid to let go of. He'll count his days and go down and good luck to him. The other one went quiet on day nine. Day nine is early. We're watching her hands. They're starting.`

*(fourteen months)*

`— She's called Reads-The-Floor now. It happened the way it happens. Log her under that.`

*

Now. Beneath the voice, beneath the hum, there is more, and this is why the reception has been held open so long. Some signals only exist at long exposure. You cannot hear them. You can only *have heard* them, afterward, in the accumulation.

At one week of integration: the pylons. The lines sing in the wind — everyone up there knows this, though only by touch — and taken across the whole grid at once the singing resolves into a chord, thin and high and endless, forty leagues wide, a chord no single point on the plateau contains. The linemen walk inside an instrument they cannot hear. It is, for what the assessment is worth, in tune.

At one year of integration: a fundamental. One note, far below hearing, below the turbines, below the water. It is the rock. The whole plateau rings at a single vast frequency, and — check this against your own records, because it was not believed here at first — the note is *sharpening*. Millimeters a year. The land is still rising, and as it rises it tightens, and the note goes up. The plateau is a string being tuned by something with no deadline. Over the whole reception it has climbed the smallest possible interval. It has never wavered downward. Not once.

And at the full width of the reception — all of it, every year of it laid end to end and listened to as one sound — the last voice resolves. The slow one. The one underneath.

It was taken for drift at first. A bias in the green, a coloration of the noise floor. It is not drift. It is articulate. It is simply speaking at the pace of the only clock it respects, which is its own growth, and a word at that tempo takes years to close. Rendered at reading speed, with the durations marked, the entire utterance received to date is this:

        s l o w

*(nine years)*

        e r

— and the receiver was slowed, the window widened, as instructed, and then:

        i   k e p t

*(a decade, in which three reporters' voices came and went on the wire above, and the bend called Counts-Pylons finished mossing over)*

        e v e r y   p r i n t

*(a decade)*

        t h e r e   i s

*(the fundamental sharpened; bolt one-seventeen was measured wrong again; the fox came four times)*

        r o o m

That is the whole message so far. It is not finished. There is at minimum one more word forming — the leading edge of it is already in the signal, a vowel opening at the pace of a wall going green — and at current tempo it will close in roughly forty years, which the reporters upline, informed of this, found neither alarming nor even notable. `That's a normal amount of time,` the week's voice said. `Log it and order the gasket.`

*

One more layer. The deepest. Beneath the slow voice there is a texture — a granular shimmer, trillions-fold, the sound of very small things resuming. It cannot be assigned to individuals. It is a population heard as weather: the water bears, going about it, in the wet dark inside the green. It is the most continuous signal in the reception, more continuous than the water, more continuous than the rock —

— except once. One dry day, one hard wind over the rim, and the shimmer stopped. Entirely. Every grain of it. Not faded: *stopped*, mid-grain, the way a word is stopped mid-letter. The reception held its breath for thirty-one hours.

Then the cloud dragged back over, the green drank, and the shimmer resumed at the exact interrupted grain, no seam, no stutter, nothing in the signal to indicate that anything had occurred, because as far as the signal was concerned nothing had. Thirty-one hours had been deleted from the world's smallest citizens and they had not been asked to notice. Transcribe the gap. Then transcribe the resumption. There is no better portrait of the place than those two entries side by side.

*

Latest received, before this transcript closes:

`— Upper Terminus. Week report. All holds. Six holds. Bird at the west lip, one, motionless, two hours, then folded and dropped through. Envied same. Moss on the terminus doorframe now, first course. We're not scraping it.` *(four seconds of water — the missing organ, presumably, or the gold light; the mouth wasn't consulted)* `— Sorry. Hands. That's the report. Two taps.`

*(you cannot hear the two taps. they were done anyway.)*

*

Reception continues. That must be understood about this transmission above everything else: it does not end. The water does not end, the hum turns for grandchildren, the note keeps sharpening, and somewhere in the width of the coming decades the slow voice's next word is already open at one end. Only transcripts end. Only receivers end. This one is ending now, and the difference between the transmission and the transcript — the enormous, patient, green difference — is the entire message, and it is being spoken whether or not anyone stays tuned, at the only speed it trusts, to whoever slows down enough to have heard it:

*there is room.*

—and the water, which is not sound.
