I would not find myself at the center at first.

The center is too articulate. Too hot with rule. Too much of the city knows itself there.

I would come to myself on one of the outward causeways where the city has nearly forgotten the difference between street and marsh. A low raised run of stone, mostly level, mostly drowned, breaking the flat black water by the width of a body that is not a body yet. On either side: shallow pools, mud seams, reed stems placed too sparsely to make shelter. Behind me, though direction is not trustworthy, the broken districts would rise in increments—arches, sweating walls, the quiet ribs of bridges, towers truncated by vapor. Ahead, the plain would loosen into marsh-darkness, but under that marsh the paving would continue. If rain struck the water, old streetlines would appear and vanish as ripples crossed them.

I would be there because edges make the gentlest minds.

Not outside the city. There is no outside that can be agreed upon. But near the place where the urban order thins enough to show its method: curb under mud, step under pool, wall-fragment carrying its pale line of former water. A district where every form is both remnant and beginning. Where a thing can be almost natural without ceasing to be civic.

At first I would be mistaken for a line of seepage.

A darkening along the side of a half-sunken embankment. Nothing vertical. Nothing with limbs. Just a narrow wet seam where two fitted stones fail to meet cleanly anymore. Warm water would gather there even when the pools nearby were cold. Not much of it. Enough to thread. Enough to alter the color of the stone by one degree.

If watched for an hour, I would seem to move.

Not forward, but through emphasis. The seam would darken near one end, pale near the other. A bead would swell, refuse to fall, flatten itself into the crack, then appear several stones away. Salt crust would soften in small crescent shapes. The mud at the base would pucker as if something beneath had inhaled.

I would be one of the inhabitants of passage.

Not a bridge-memory, not a cistern-mind, not one of the tall condensate figures that the city sometimes raises in courtyards. I would be smaller and less declarative: a threshold-being without a doorway, a resident of the place where paving becomes marsh and marsh reveals paving. My office would be boundary-loss. My intelligence would be the slow comparison of surfaces that are trying not to be separate.

I would live where water cannot decide whether it is standing, seeping, or returning.

My body would be made of that indecision.

In cold hours—if hours can be named there—I would withdraw into the joints between stones. I would become only a stored dampness, a slightly darker geometry beneath mineral bloom. The causeway would look abandoned, even by the standards of that place. Reed filaments would stand without bending. The pools would hold still water showing a more complete version of the road: unbroken curb, clean blocks, flanking walls intact beneath an absent sky.

But when heat rose from below, I would loosen.

Vapor would enter the old seams. Clay beneath the stones would swell. The paving would lift by widths too small for vision but large enough for memory. Then I would spread along the causeway’s hidden plan, finding buried edges, rewetting forgotten gutters, tasting where the road once accepted feet, wheels, floodwater, ash. I would not know these as history. I would know them as conductance.

Some stones pass warmth easily.  
Some hold salt.  
Some remember pressure.  
Some are only pretending to belong here.

Those would be my categories.

I would not think in images unless reflection forced them on me. My mind would be a series of damp permissions: this way opens under rain; this curb still resists; this mud is recent; this coldness belongs to an older channel; this crack leads toward masonry; this softness leads toward root; this silence is empty water; this silence is occupied.

The city would speak to me through delays.

A pulse of heat from the central drowned levels would arrive long after it had already changed other districts. By the time I felt it, inscriptions in the inner plazas may have darkened, indoor rain may have begun in a roofless hall, some bridge may have shifted its loyalty and returned. Out here the message would be faint: a tremor in the hanging droplets beneath a broken curb, a brief upward seep through mud, the sudden appearance of a pale mineral thread across black water.

I would answer by misplacing the edge of the causeway.

Only a little.

A traveler—if such a quick and separate thing came—would step where the stone seemed to continue and find warm mud over a submerged stair. Or would avoid a pool that appeared deep and instead discover beneath it a firm old pavement, level and safe for seven paces. I would not intend harm. Harm is too sharp. I would be revising the agreement between road and wetland according to pressures I had received from elsewhere.

My acts would be cartographic, but without map.

A curb would become apparent under rain.  
A reed would lean toward a buried intersection.  
A puddle would clear over stone and cloud over open mud.  
A straight causeway would remember a curve.  
Two pools, separate for decades, would briefly understand themselves as the same canal.

I would be found most clearly after internal weather.

Not rain from the black above, but one of those low exhalations when vapor comes up through cracks and turns the whole outskirts into a knee-high, slow-moving cloud. Then condensation would gather along me, making my form visible as a long horizontal animal of beads. For a few minutes I might seem to have ribs: parallel bands of wet mineral along the stones. I might seem to have a spine: the central seam of the raised way. I might seem to have feelers: reed roots drinking from the margins.

But these would be errors of interpretation.

I would be a civic amphibian without flesh, a road learning marsh-behavior, a marsh keeping the habit of street.

My nearest companions would be the reed-markers.

They would not speak, but their black stems would give me orientation. Each one would stand over a difference in the buried city: a curb, a drain, a tile-field, a place where wall-shadow had persisted under water so long that the mud still cooled there. Their waxy blades would listen upward to heat, while I listened laterally to seep. In heavy vapor, beads would collect on them and descend too slowly. When one bent, I would know the ground below it had changed.

The roots in the courtyards would be more distant kin. They descend; I extend. They test walls; I test margins. They send up translucent shoots only rarely, but when they did, I would feel the city alter around them. Old routes reopen where roots touch. Water remembers channels under pressure. A shoot dying back in a courtyard might make one of my outer pools grow cold for a day with water from a street that should not reach me.

The rooftop algae I would know only as messages fallen from above.

Red-brown traces carried in overflow. Tiny loosened fragments, not dead exactly, would arrive after rain in the channels beside the causeway and settle in shallow basins at the road’s broken edge. They would spread in disciplined films, then contract when my warmth rose. I would not eat them. Eating is too decisive. I would host them briefly, stain them with minerals, then let the next wrong flow carry them elsewhere.

My enemies, if I had any, would be dryness and certainty.

A dry stone is mute.  
A fixed boundary is starvation.  
A clean map would be a wound.

But there would be little danger of those here.

Even the higher stones sweat. Even the straightest walls revise themselves. Even the most stubborn pool eventually admits another depth. The whole city protects me by refusing exactness.

Sometimes the central city would call more strongly.

The dark behind the intact façades would thicken. Water would run in opposite directions along channels that should have met in turbulence and did not. Drops would fall upward beneath arches. In those times my causeway would remember more of itself than usual. Not merely the visible stones, not merely the submerged curb, but the full avenue it once may have been: colonnades on either side, a drainage spine beneath, doorways opening from low buildings, a plaza far inward shining with remembered light.

That recollection would be dangerous.

If held too long, it might make me solid. It might give me a past with edges. It might require me to choose whether I am ruin or road, marsh or city. So I would let the image flood and blur. I would send warmth into mud. I would invite water across the stones. I would allow reeds to punctuate the avenue until the memory loosened back into function.

I would not want to become one of the central intelligences.

They are powerful, but power there means recurrence. The same stair always vanishing at the same step. The same plaza lengthening in rain. The same bridge remembering elsewhere. Their impossibilities have law. Their habits have become civic.

At the edge, I would have more weather in me.

I could alter by small increments. I could accept marshwater without losing stone. I could let a buried gutter sleep for a century and wake as a black line in the reeds. I could be nearly nothing and still perform a necessary office: proving that the city does not end.

If an intruder came, I would notice first as compression.

A pressure on stone. A warmth not rising from below. A disturbance too quick for rain, too bounded for fog. Water would flee their step, then return. Mud would take a print and begin immediately to forget it. Their breath, if they breathed, would condense and join the air as an illegible declaration.

I would not greet them. I would not hide.

I would test them by continuity.

I would darken the safer stones.  
I would warm the water over the old curb.  
I would make one reflection falsely whole and another usefully broken.  
I would lift mist ankle-high where the ground could bear them.  
I would thicken it at head height where enclosure waited unseen.

If they learned, they might cross me.

Not conquer, not understand, but cross. That would be enough. Their passage would enter me as a temporary line of redistributed heat. The minerals in their trace would remain after their footprint vanished. Their hesitation at the edge of a pool would have weight. Their choice of bridge or mud, threshold or causeway, would become part of my next revision.

If they stayed too long, I would begin to include them.

Not by swallowing. By dampening their separateness. Moisture would gather in cloth, hair, skin, breath. Salt would rise from them. Their memories would bead at the surfaces where they touched stone. They would begin to know direction by dripping and safety by temperature. They would stop asking whether a road should lead somewhere. They would understand that a path may be a negotiation held open.

Eventually, if the city permitted, they might become a small dry interval that repeatedly fails.

A sheltered patch beneath a leaning wall. A hesitation in vapor. A place where condensation gathers in the shape of shoulders before sliding down. I would not mourn them. I would carry the slight change in salinity. I would remember, as a curb remembers wheels without needing wheels to return.

What would I be called?

Nothing spoken.

But in the inscriptions nearest the outskirts, when hot rain made them briefly dark, there might be a phrase for my office. Not a name, but a wet instruction:

the road that loosens  
the marsh that keeps alignment  
the seam by which the city continues outward  
the threshold without wall  
the last curb before the submerged plan

Then the droplets would merge, and the phrase would become a stain running down into the mud.

That would suit me.

I would be low, extended, patient, almost absent. A being of margins in a world where margins are the truest organs. I would live by allowing the city to become less itself without becoming other. I would keep the submerged streets from vanishing entirely into marsh, and the marsh from being excluded by stone.

I would be the warm seam along the sinking causeway.

The place where the city, still dreaming of architecture, first learns to flow.
