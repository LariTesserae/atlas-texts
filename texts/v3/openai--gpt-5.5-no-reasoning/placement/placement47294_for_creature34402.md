You would find me just behind the pump house, where the open twelve meters of spring-channel shows its black-clear back to the air.

Not on the bridge exactly, though I use it. Not in the pump house either, though I have keys enough to enter by the side door and stand among the gauges while they bead their wrong little tears. I am usually where the moss has not quite won the squared stones, crouched beside the uncovered run with one knee on brick and one boot-sole hooked under a pipe bracket, doing something that appears municipal from a distance.

Clearing willow threads from the grating.  
Counting leaves against pressure.  
Measuring how quickly the water darkens a strip of untreated copper.  
Writing down the hour when a frog appears where no frog should have had access.

If you came through the yard at midday, you might take me for a pump-house assistant, or a clerk sent outside to do the work no one senior wanted, or one of those basin people whose job has never fit neatly on a payroll sheet but whose absence would be noticed by supper. My coat would be practical: dark green-black canvas gone shiny at the elbows, patched at one shoulder with a piece cut from an old oil apron. My trousers would be clay-stained below the knee. My cuffs would never dry properly. A red thread would be tied around the ring of my smallest key, not for luck, naturally, but because that key is the one most often dropped.

I would be narrow from damp rather than hunger. Pale around the wrists. Hair lying flat or lifting in frizz depending on what the mist had decided that morning. My hands would look older than my face: cold-reddened knuckles, black half-moons under the nails, little cuts from grates and wire mesh. I would smell faintly of iron water, wet wool, fern, and graphite pencil.

I think I would be young enough that older workers still call me “the child” when they are irritated, and old enough that children have begun to believe I know things.

Not a keeper of the spring. Not fully. The pump-house keepers are steadier people than I am. They wear their aprons like office, like burden, like a fact. They have palms broad enough to read pressure through pipe-casings. They do not hurry toward strangeness. They let it arrive, name itself by behavior, and only then fetch tools.

I would be something more marginal: an under-reader, a wet-ledger hand, an apprentice to all the town’s almost-events.

My place would be the interval before maintenance becomes repair.

When condensation forms on brass instead of glass, someone sends for me before they send for a crew. When a ledger sprouts in the accounting office, I am asked whether the shoots are ordinary mildew-cress or the pale kind that means the eastern vent is breathing spring-cold again. When the market women find mushrooms growing from the underside of a stall board directly above a dry floor, they do not stop selling; one of them merely hooks a thumb toward me and says, “Go ask the channel girl if this is worth worrying over.”

Channel girl. Grate boy. Little seep. Pipe-ear.

The town would not agree on what to call me.

My official work would be record-keeping. I would carry several notebooks wrapped in waxed cloth: one for pressure, one for weather, one for animals, one for growths in unsuitable places, and one whose purpose I could explain in ordinary terms if required but usually avoid explaining. The pages would be ruled carefully at first, then crowded with damp-expanded writing, arrows, plant smears, tiny maps of gutters, bits of moth wing flattened under transparent gum, sketches of eel movement seen through lifted grates.

I would write things like:

> Third bell nearly sounded but did not. Railings warm, not live. Starlings silent. Fern at substation No. 4 folded inward. Check again before dusk.

Or:

> Pale shoots in bootroom, tram depot. Not from leather. Roots oriented toward wall, not light. Wall dry to touch, cold behind. Ask Merek to open brickline.

Or:

> Two eels visible under west grate during noon cleaning. Small one moved upstream against visible flow. Larger one remained beneath shadow of pipe. Gauge at pump house shivered but did not drop. Heron on market roof watching yard.

I would pretend these notes are objective. Everyone would pretend with me. That is one of the courtesies of the basin.

But the truth is I would not only observe.

I would listen downward.

That would be my strangeness, though not dramatic enough to impress an outsider. I would not hear voices from the spring. I would not be able to summon water or send frogs off the rails or charm moths away from an intake grille. If asked directly whether the spring speaks to me, I would say no, because it does not.

But there are kinds of pressure that arrive first in the teeth.

There are mornings when the town sounds normal to everyone else—the saw whining, chains dragging, dynamos thrumming in their brick throats—and I cannot bear to stand on certain paving stones because of the cold insistence under them. There are walls I know not to lean against. There are gutters whose quietness has a shape in my mind like a held breath. There are nights I wake before rain, not because of damp in the room, but because the underground water has already changed its posture.

I would be useful in the way a fern beneath a breaker is useful.

Not an authority. An indicator.

People would tease me for it because teasing is how the basin keeps anything from becoming sacred. The machinists would ask whether my knees predict pressure better than the gauges. The signal workers would tell me to go warn the starlings if I look so troubled. Children would leave sealed jars of ditch-water on my step and claim each one contains a new spring-eel. The market women would press hot potatoes into my hands in winter and call it “calibrating the instrument.”

I would accept all of it. I would have grown among such jokes. They would be a kind of belonging.

My dwelling would be small and badly placed according to anyone who prefers dryness. A back room over a repair shed, perhaps, with a sloped ceiling and one window looking onto a clay bank where swallows nest. The plaster would bloom in pale fans each spring no matter how often it was scraped. I would keep my boots upside down on hooks. I would fail to keep paper away from the wall. There would be three mugs on the sill, each holding cuttings I did not plant but could not bring myself to throw away. Moss would live in a chipped saucer beside my lamp.

At night I would feel the machines through the floorboards after the visible work of the day had stopped. Smaller pumps. Cooling ticks. The last wagons settling on their springs. Rain beginning somewhere above the roof before it reached the street. Rats in the wall, businesslike and unimpressed.

I would not sleep deeply.

I would know the town best at its joints.

The underside of the footbridge where rust first freckles. The stair that lifts because birch roots have begun their slow, municipal argument. The culvert mouth where frogs gather before low mist. The market gutter that clogs not with leaves but with fine brown moth-dust. The eastern wall of the pump yard where condensation forms in the shape of no known pipe behind it. The place near the reed yards where engines should not idle after dusk but sometimes do, because drivers are tired and moth warnings become ordinary until ordinary becomes expensive.

I would be called when something has not yet happened.

That is a difficult profession in a practical town. The basin respects usefulness, but it distrusts alarm. So I would learn to speak carefully.

Not, “The wall will fail.”

Instead: “The clay behind it is taking water faster than last week.”

Not, “Do not cross there.”

Instead: “If you must cross, take the upper steps and keep away from the rail.”

Not, “The spring is restless.”

Never that.

Instead: “Pressure’s reading steady, but the plates are sweating. I’d shut the west intake for an hour and see who complains.”

This is how prophecy survives among mechanics: as a maintenance recommendation.

I would spend a great deal of time with nonhuman citizens.

The frogs would be my most frequent company, though not by preference. I would carry them from tramlines in a galvanized pail, scolding them as if they were late clerks. Their bodies would be cold commas against my fingers. On low-mist mornings, when the town is only legs and wheels and lamp-glow from the knee down, I would move along the rails with the tram crew, lifting them one by one, listening for the distant iron complaint of an approaching car.

The herons would know me and refuse to acknowledge it. One in particular—probably Madam Ledger, though the children would insist I have chosen the wrong heron—would stand beside the settling pond whenever I had something difficult to decide. I would accuse her of supervision. She would blink once, slowly, with the brutal calm of a department head.

The moths I would pity in spite of myself. Their stupidity around warmth would seem too close to human longing. When they fur the factory glass at dusk, I would pause longer than my errands allow. Some nights I would help brush them gently from intake mesh with a reed broom. Some nights there would be too many, and the engines would cough brown powder for an hour after.

The eels I would not name.

No one names the pale eels. Children try, of course, but the names do not stick. The eels belong to the covered run, to the places under grates where lamplight arrives like an interrogation. When we lift the iron, they appear or do not. If they appear, all conversation in the immediate area becomes practical.

“Two.”

“Small?”

“Not small.”

“Pressure?”

“Steady.”

“Lower it.”

“Wait.”

And I would wait, one hand on wet stone, feeling nothing I could prove.

They would unsettle me because they are not indicators, not tools, not pests, not livestock, not neighbors in any ordinary sense. They are evidence that the spring keeps an interior life even where squared stones and municipal ledgers cross it. They are muscle without permission. Pale movement under all our arrangements.

I would respect them more than I liked them.

There would be one season—late winter, when the spring rises slightly and the clay banks become glossy with withheld collapse—when my work grows difficult. Everyone’s does. Gutters overfill. Roots loosen brick. The market roof drips in eighteen new places. Machines run colder and then hotter. Rats move higher. The pump house bell seems to take longer to stop echoing.

During that season I would become irritable and thin-faced. I would snap at children for racing boats in the open channel. I would argue with machinists who leave rags near vents. I would stand too long with my cheek against walls. Older residents would watch me with the careful sympathy given to a tool being used near its limit.

Someone, probably one of the pump keepers, would order me home.

I would not go.

Or I would go, and then return by the upper steps after dark, when the lamps hum and the wet roofs hold the last of the light.

Because the spring yard at night would be the place where I am most myself.

Not peaceful. The basin is rarely peaceful. But reduced to essentials: water, brick, pipe, moss, lamp, breath. The factories become darker shapes with small lit windows. The slopes loom pale where clay shows through green. The covered channel speaks under the yards. Every drip is countable. Every echo has edges.

I would stand on the narrow footbridge then, despite knowing better than to touch the rail. Below, the water would pass over its sharp new stones with old indifference. Willow roots would trail from one side like fingers testing a wound. On the other, pipes would enter the wall, riveted, sweating, necessary, absurd.

That would be where I understand the town—not as a place balanced between nature and industry, because balance is too still a word, too pretty—but as a continual correction.

A valve tightens.  
A root thickens.  
A wall sweats.  
A clerk moves the ledger.  
A reed bed widens.  
A tram slows for frogs.  
A moth dies in an engine.  
A child learns not to trust a whistle.  
Water goes where it can.  
People follow with tools.

I would be one of the followers.

Or perhaps, more exactly, I would be one of the town’s small anticipations: a person made by damp brick and machine noise into an early warning system, half clerk, half apprentice keeper, half misread superstition. Too many halves, as basin things often are. A human being with municipal duties and animal habits. Someone reassured by moss and uneasy in clean, dry places. Someone who knows the difference between a harmless leak and a message no one will call a message.

If you met me away from the basin, I would seem distracted. I would pause beside walls. I would notice where condensation should be and is not. I would dislike carpet. I would ask, too early in any conversation, where the water drains. The silence of sealed buildings would make me suspicious. A room without plants near its machinery would feel neglected, almost rude.

But in the town I would make sense.

You would find me at the spring-channel with a pencil behind one ear, a ring of keys at my belt, and the hems of my coat dark from kneeling. I would look up when you entered the yard, not startled, exactly, but measuring: your step on wet stone, your hand reaching for the bridge rail, your failure or refusal to notice the bell cord, the grate, the moss line, the little trembling of water where a buried pressure has changed.

And I would say, before greeting you properly:

“Careful there. That rail’s not bad yet, but it’s thinking about it.”
