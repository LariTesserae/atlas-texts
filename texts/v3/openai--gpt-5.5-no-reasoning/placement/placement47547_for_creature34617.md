I would be found where the town cannot quite decide whether it is passage or room.

Not at the central court, not among the market ledges where the sun comes down pale and public, nor in the new eastern cuts where the diggers are still naming each face of clay. I would be in one of the service hollows just off a sloping way, half a turn before the water hall becomes cold enough to raise the hairs on the wrist. A place most people pass without looking into unless something has gone wrong.

It began, perhaps, as a failed chamber.

Someone once meant it for storage: cool, close to the lower runnels, out of the traffic of the court. Then the back wall wept too much in spring. Grain molded there. A shelf sagged. A family argued for a season over whether the damp could be tamed, then abandoned the claim when a better niche opened nearer a shaft. Later a drain was cut through the floor. Later still, the drain was covered with planks and reopened and covered again. Now the hollow belongs to no household exactly. It is communal by inconvenience.

There is always something in it that should not be left in a main passage: cracked jars waiting to be mended or broken down for temper, bundles of reed too short for proper screens, a bent lamp hook, lengths of cord hung to dry, a basket of spoiled straw to be carried up and dumped, a child’s lost sandal gone stiff with clay. The wall has many marks, not all official. Three cuts, renewed often, warn of the weak seam above the rear corner. A circle marks the seep jar place. A little scratched fish, made by some idle hand, points toward the lower water. Under limewash are older marks no one now claims.

This is where I would be: sitting on the lip of the covered drain, back against the least damp wall, knees drawn in because there is no other way to sit there without blocking everything.

I would not be important in the way that water-keepers are important, or diggers, or timber-judges. People would not seek me first in an emergency unless the emergency had already become strange in the small ways: a trickle changing taste, a lamp refusing to draw, a rat fleeing from a wall where no rat should be, a child saying there is breathing behind the plaster. I would be one of those half-specialists a town like this produces almost without deciding to: useful enough to tolerate, odd enough to be spoken of by function rather than name.

A listener, but not one of the great wall-listeners.

A keeper of neglected signals.

I think I would be called something plain and slightly mocking at first. Drip-ear. Crack-minder. The one who sits by bowls. Children would shorten it into a sound they could shout down a passage. Adults would use my real name when asking a favor and the other name when warning children not to become too strange.

I would have started as a child who noticed what adults were too busy to notice. Not the grand groan of a failing beam, not water pressing behind a wall—that belongs to trained elders—but small changes. Which bowl filled faster after south wind. Which stair sweated before rain and which only after. Where ants appeared before grain beetles. Which smoke vent began to smell sour when a hidden blockage was forming. Which household always blamed damp when the real trouble was their own bad storage.

At first this would have made me irritating.

“Do not stand there, that patch is soft.”

“Your jar is singing.”

“There is cold under that mat.”

“There are frogs coming.”

No one likes a child who speaks as if the town has chosen them for messages. Especially not when the child is sometimes right.

So I would have been put to work where watching is nearly labor: at seep jars, drains, cistern lids, settling pots, covered grooves, planks that must be lifted and checked. Work that lets a person become still without seeming idle. My hands would always smell of wet clay, lime crust, and the inside of jars. The skin around my nails would be split from rinsing grit out of channels. My sleeves would be dark at the cuffs. I would carry a little hooked tool for lifting drain covers, a twist of fiber for clearing pipe mouths, chalk for marking a changed seam, and a few thin reeds used like listening straws against cracks.

Not magic. Nothing in this town permits the comfort of magic.

Only attention, grown excessive.

I would know where the hill lies.

There are places where a wall looks dry because the surface has sealed hard, but behind it damp gathers and waits. Places where chalk sounds firm until rain loosens the clay above it. Places where a root should have been cut but now must not be, because it has become part of the roof’s memory. I would not understand these things as a scholar. I would understand them as one understands a relative’s breathing in sleep: not by theory first, but by long nearness.

People would come to me with jars.

That seems right.

A woman from the upper sleeping warrens brings a jar of water and says, “Taste that.”

I do not taste it at first. I smell the rim, tilt it, look for suspended clay, touch the outside where condensation gathers. Then perhaps I taste. Chalk. Old reed. A little bitterness.

“Not from the clean runnel,” I say.

“My son filled it there.”

“No. Or he rinsed the pot near the tannery trench.”

The son is fetched. There is scolding. The mother is angry at him, then at me, then grateful later and never says so.

A digger brings me a clot of clay wrapped in hide.

“Eastern reach,” he says. “Too soft?”

I press it flat with my thumb. It shines. I roll it, split it, smell it. There is a thread of grey inside.

“Not soft. Fed.”

He swears because that is worse. Fed clay means water somewhere above or behind, not enough to see, enough to remember after a cut is made. They will need to brace before widening. They will complain and do it.

Children bring me forbidden things: a beetle from a grain shelf, a pale fungus shaped like an ear, a shard with an old mark, a frog from a drain after heavy rain. I tell them which to throw away, which to show an elder, which to never touch again, which can be kept until it dies and begins to stink.

They would think I know secrets. I would know mostly routes.

Where smoke goes when it fails to rise. Where spilled dye turns up two courts away. Which drain murmurs from stormwater and which from someone emptying wash pots too near a clean channel. Which sealed room still has a hollow beyond it because tapping there answers twice. Which old abandoned passage breathes colder air in winter. Which family keeps their dry straw too near a sweating seam and will lose it if no one shames them soon.

I would be useful because I remember connections no map admits.

In a town still becoming itself, a map is always a kind of pride. A route says: people go this way now. A mark says: we learned this once. But water does not care for routes, and air does not care for arguments over inheritance. Damp finds the earlier shape. Smoke remembers angles. Rats know gaps before planners do.

I would belong to those earlier shapes.

Not spiritually. Practically. Uneasily.

My sleeping place would be poor but dry. A narrow shelf above a warmish passage, curtained with a patched mat, near enough to a communal hearth that embers breathe through the night. I would own little because possessions need dry keeping, and my work would take me into wet districts. A spare tunic wrapped in waxed hide. A felt cap gone stiff with old smoke. Two good bowls. A bronze pin inherited from someone more respectable. A knife too small for trade pride but sharp enough to cut reed and cord. A string of drilled shell fragments that makes a soft sound when I move, so people hear me around corners and do not curse me.

My closest companions would not be the great citizens of the court, but those whose labor also vanishes unless it fails: mat-menders, lamp-fillers, rat-catchers, the old woman who tends the herb pots no one is allowed to touch, the boy who clears ash from smoke bends, the widow who knows every sleeping curtain in three courts because she washes them for grain. We would meet in marginal places, sharing heel ends of bread, gossiping in low voices, laughing at those who think authority begins where visibility does.

I might have one friend among the potters.

Someone smoky-eyed, patient, with forearms dusted pale and cracked from heat. They would save flawed vessels for me: jars whose rims warped, bowls that rang wrong after firing, little cups good enough to catch drips but not good enough to sell. In exchange I would tell them when their clay settling pit was taking on seep from the waste trench, or when a kiln draw sounded choked before the smoke showed it. We would have a fondness built from mutual suspicion.

Perhaps once, when we were young, there was talk of marriage. Then both of us looked at the space such a life would require and laughed until we stopped wanting it. Or wanted it and did not speak. In this town, even longing must have somewhere to stand.

During dry weeks I would seem less necessary. I would sit with bowls that do not fill, listening to dust. People would call me gloomy then, or overcareful. Children would be sent to do my work and told, “Even Drip-ear has nothing to hear.” I would become irritated, precise, a little sour. I would accuse households of blocking grooves with sweepings. I would inspect lids. I would argue about straw storage. I would become the kind of person whose arrival makes others straighten objects guiltily.

But in rain, especially hard rain, I would change.

Not become heroic. This town has no use for hero shapes until after the danger has passed and someone needs a story.

I would become quick.

I would move through passages with one hand out, touching walls, lintels, posts, the rims of jars. I would know which bowls should already be singing with drops and which should be silent. I would hear when a familiar ticking stopped under the general rain murmur. That is the sound that wakes me most sharply: absence.

A missing drip means a channel blocked, or a crack sealed with swelling clay, or water diverted where it should not go.

I would lift covers, scrape leaves from grates, send children upward with messages because they are faster in tight cuts. “Tell Maro’s court no flame near the west niche.” “Tell the water-keeper the lower groove has gone cloudy.” “Tell the timber-judge the old root is wet above the sealed room.” Children love errands that sound like doom. They would run wide-eyed and important, and perhaps one would trip, and someone would curse me for using them, but the message would arrive.

In heavy rain I would spend hours crouched in the cold water hall, watching the runnel rise over its stones.

That place would be nearest to a temple for me, though I would not tell shrine-keepers so. The narrow dark water entering beneath one lintel and leaving beneath another; jars in rows; algae bright where the slot of light touches it; children hushed there without knowing why. The stream is not generous. It is thin, local, moody. Yet everything depends on its permissions. I would sit with a lamp behind horn and watch sediment change. Brown means surface wash. Grey means clay slip. Black flecks mean charcoal from an upper court. A sour skin means someone has fouled what they promised not to foul.

I would know sins by water.

That would make me disliked in useful ways.

“Who says it came from us?”

“The water says.”

“The water has your tongue, then.”

“No. Yours. Ash and goat hair.”

There would be hearings where I am made to speak. I would hate those. Central court too bright, too many people leaning from galleries, everyone using public voices. I am not made for public accusation. My authority is strongest in passages, beside evidence. In the court, words become social. Grievance gathers relatives. Someone says my mother’s people once blocked a drain. Someone says I am too fond of the potters and blame tanners for everything. Someone asks whether I can swear by the roof spirits that I know the source.

I would not swear by roof spirits unless I was certain. The town would know this. That is why, when I did swear, silence would come.

My religion would be modest and maybe improper.

I would leave offerings not in the main shrine but in small wall niches no one tends regularly: a pinch of salt near a troublesome seep, a twist of dry straw above a repaired crack, a broken cup set upright in a sealed room before it is packed with rubble. Not prayers for rescue. Bargains of recognition.

Hold here.

Speak early.

Take this water elsewhere.

Let us hear you before you move.

I would believe, though not always with words, that matter resents being ignored. Clay tolerates cutting if listened to. Timber forgives reuse if kept dry. Water becomes dangerous when shamed, trapped, fouled, or denied its road. This belief would make practical people nod until I spoke too plainly, then they would tell me not to make spirits out of poor drainage.

They would be right. Also wrong.

My body would be shaped by crouching and listening. One shoulder higher from carrying jars. Knees that crack in cold. Palms callused not from heavy picks but from lids, ropes, damp planks, the rims of vessels. My hearing would be good for close sounds and poor for crowds. I would miss half of a market song and hear, beneath it, that one of the drain covers is not seated right.

I would move slowly aboveground.

The surface would not frighten me, but it would make me feel imprecise. Too much sky, too much wind tearing signs apart. Grass speaks in a thousand blades and says almost nothing exact. Clouds threaten from everywhere at once. On the surface, water falls without first darkening a seam or changing the smell of a wall. It simply arrives. I would go up when needed to clear grates, inspect vent mouths, look at where runoff gathers on the slope. I would squint, pull my hood tight, and feel for a while like a thought taken out of its skull.

Still, I would love certain surface things secretly.

The fox at an outer entrance, pausing with one forefoot raised.

The way thorn shadows tremble over a shaft mouth.

The first smell of rain in grass before the hill begins to answer.

A fallen tree, tragic and wealthy, already being measured in every mind for beams.

But I would always descend with relief. At the stair mouth the light softens. The wind stops breaking speech. Sounds regain edges. The town closes around me, not kindly exactly, but legibly.

I would grow older into a person children are sent to learn from when they are too restless for ordinary work. Not many would stay. Most would rather carry spoil or chase chickens or apprentice to potters where fire makes visible things. My work asks a child to sit beside a jar for an hour and notice whether its rhythm changes. To smell clay without making faces. To tell the difference between clean rot and bad rot. To admit, often, “I do not know yet.”

One child would stay.

There is always one.

A narrow child with quick hands and a habit of touching walls when upset. At first they would lie, pretending to hear what they think I want heard. I would rap their knuckles lightly with a reed and say, “The hill does not praise cleverness. Listen again.”

They would sulk. Then learn.

I would teach them the town as a set of obligations rather than places.

This passage must be kept clear because a stretcher once could not pass.

This sealed wall is not to be mocked; people died making that lesson.

This bowl belongs to no one; therefore everyone steals from it unless watched.

This dry patch is new. New dryness can be as dangerous as new wet.

Never trust a drain that is too quiet after rain.

Never shame someone for fear underground. Fear is one of the senses.

The child would ask whether the hill likes us.

I would say no.

Then, after a while, because that answer is too simple, I would say: “The hill does not like. It holds where it is answered.”

If there is a story about me, it will not be dramatic until it is retold after I die.

In life, it would be work.

A season of heavy rain, perhaps. The kind that makes the surface paths vanish into slick pale mud and turns every shaft into a throat. The courtyards stipple, then pour. Screens clog with leaves. Men and women clear grates from above, soaked to the skin. Children are kept in upper rooms and disobey anyway. Water jars fill, then overflow, then are emptied into secondary troughs. Everyone listens for pressing water.

I am in the failed chamber by the lower way, where I have been for hours, because something is wrong but not yet nameable. All the expected sounds are present: drip into bowl, murmur under plank, distant rush from the water hall, rain in the shaft beyond. Too present. Too complete. The town is speaking over itself.

Then I notice dust falling.

Not clay. Chalk dust. Dry, pale, from the ceiling seam marked with three cuts. In heavy rain, dry dust is an obscenity.

I put my palm to the wall. Cold. Not wet. Then my ear. Nothing.

Nothing where there should be at least the inner grain of water.

I run.

People remember running because I rarely do. They remember me striking shell strings at passage mouths, not slowing to apologize. They remember me shouting for the timber-judge by name, which I do not do in public ways. They remember me ordering a family out of a sleeping bay and being cursed by the grandmother until she sees my face.

The danger is not in the wet passage everyone watches. It is above and behind an old sealed room packed badly with rubble. Water has found the fill, then vanished into it, pressing not as a visible leak but as weight. The dry chalk dust is the roof beginning to answer too late.

There is argument. There is always argument.

“That chamber was sealed by my mother’s brother.”

“The mark says weak only in the corner.”

“The children are asleep.”

“Move them.”

“The rain—”

“Move them now.”

Perhaps I am wrong. That must be allowed. In such a life I would often be almost wrong. But this time the timber-judge comes, presses his brow to the wall, and his expression changes. Then no one argues with me. They argue with time instead.

Children are dragged from beds. Mats, jars, bedding, a chest no one should have tried to save. A goat bleats from somewhere it should not be. Beams are wedged. A relief cut is opened in a lower clay face to give water a road. Brown water comes first, then grey, then a coughing slurry of old straw, charcoal, mouse bones, and the stink of sealed air.

The roof does not fall.

Or perhaps part of it does, later, after the chamber is empty. Enough to make the story better. Enough to prove the warning. Not enough to kill.

Afterward people bring me food I do not want and thanks I cannot hold. The grandmother who cursed me sends dry socks without a word. The child apprenticed to me grows unbearable with pride, as if we personally defeated the hill. I tell them we did not. We answered quickly enough. That is all.

Years later the story becomes clearer than it was. I heard the chalk before it fell. I smelled buried water through stone. I argued with a roof spirit and won. None of this is true, but none of it is entirely false in the way stories matter there.

When I die, I do not think they will bury me above at first.

Perhaps the town has graves on the surface, under thorn and grass, where wind cleans names away. But for me there would be a delay, because rain or frost or dispute always delays something. My body would be wrapped and kept in a cool side chamber, and people would pass quietly, partly from respect and partly because death makes every narrow passage narrower.

The apprentice, now grown, would take my hooked tool.

Someone would scratch a small mark near the failed chamber: not an official symbol, not law. A cup-shape under three short lines. Children would ask what it means. Adults would say, “That is where the listener sat.”

And after enough years, when the hollow is altered again—when the drain is rerouted, when the weak seam is packed and plastered, when some new family claims the space because the town never stops needing room—the mark might be limewashed over. But the habit would remain.

Someone would still pause there in heavy rain.

Someone would still set a bowl beneath a stain before anyone asks.

Someone would still say, when a channel goes quiet, “Listen again.”

That is what I would be in this world: not a ruler, not a builder of new chambers, not one of the bright necessary traders smelling of wind. I would be a narrow civic sense organ tucked into the damp margins of the town. A watcher of seepage. A reader of small refusals. One of the hill-people whose task is to notice the moment matter changes its mind.

I would live just under the light, where clay cools the hand and every sound has consequences.
