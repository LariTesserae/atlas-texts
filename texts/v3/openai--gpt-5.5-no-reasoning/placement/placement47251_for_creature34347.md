I would find myself at the upper edge of the city, where the cliff begins.

Not in the plaza, though the plaza would call to me first: that broad stone bowl full of room and wavering light, where rays lift from the silt like loosened shadows and the tones of the signal stones pass through the slabs. Not beyond the wall either, where the wild growth streams downslope and large things move with the calm of hunger. I would be higher up, half within the settlement and half in the mountain: near the spring mouths, among the rectangular dark openings cut into the pale stone.

There is a ledge there that is not quite a street and not quite a shrine. Cold water issues from a crack in the cliff behind it, clearer than the town water, and pours outward in an invisible sheet. You can see its path only by what grows in it: white fern-mats, blue-veined leaves, beadlike colonies of translucent animals, stiff glassy stems arranged in fans as if they had been combed by a careful hand. The stone is old there. Older than the living roofs below, older perhaps than the flood’s patience. Its carved edges have been softened, but not erased. Doorways stand in a row beneath beards of weed.

I think I would live in one of those doorways.

Or rather, I would not live in it as a person lives in a house. I would occupy it the way a breathing curtain occupies an entrance, the way a threshold becomes known by those who pass through it. I would be one of the beings that keep an opening from becoming either empty or closed.

At first I might seem like a plant.

A pale, many-stranded growth rooted in the lintel of a cliff chamber, falling downward in cords and ribbons. My body would be translucent in places, olive-white in others, with threads of blue or green moving slowly inside me. I would hang across the doorway in layered veils, not blocking it entirely but slowing whatever came near. Each strand would be fine enough to sway in small currents, strong enough to hold when grasped gently. Small fish would pass through me without fear. Shrimp would clean my inner surfaces. Snails would avoid me unless my outer skin had begun to fur with algae.

But I would not be only a plant.

Part of me would be animal, or at least animal enough to feel, remember, and answer. My root-knots would be sunk deep into old sockets cut by hands; my lower tendrils would touch the stone sill; my upper filaments would lie inside the spring flow, tasting minerals and temperature. I would know the city by pressure. A turtle striking the roof gardens below would reach me as a dull shift. A signal stone in the western gate would arrive long after sight or sound, broadened by water and masonry, and I would tighten in recognition. The approach of a large predator beyond the wall would be a silence first: the absence of small impacts, the sudden stilling of fish.

I would be a threshold-keeper.

Not a guard. Not a priest. Not exactly a door.

A being grown where passage matters.

The chamber behind me would be one of the old ones, cut into the cliff before the water came. Its back wall would hold a cold vent. Its ceiling would trap no air now, or only a trembling pocket too small for human breath. The walls would be lined with mineral glaze and the pale shells of things that have fastened there for generations. In niches would rest old objects: black pebble weights, a broken shell blade, a bronze knife greened almost into stone, bead strings fused with root. Perhaps once the chamber held grain, or tools, or the dead. Perhaps now those categories have dissolved. Storage, tomb, shrine, nursery: underwater, they lean into one another.

I would keep the chamber usable.

My tendrils would admit some creatures and slow others. Small fry could shelter inside. Eels could pass if they knew the pressure of my strands and did not bite. Shrimp could enter to clean the mineral fans. Large grazers would be turned away by bitterness in my outer skin. Predators would find me unpleasant in the mouth. Silt would gather where I wanted it to gather, in low shelves along the floor, and remain clear where the spring must breathe. Seeds would catch in me, but I would release most of them. A few I would keep, fastening them into the side walls until they grew into supporting mats.

If people still lived in the city, they would know me.

Children would be brought to my doorway to learn the cold-current signs. They would place their palms flat against the stone beside me and feel how the spring moves through the mountain. An elder might comb me with a bone rake, separating dead strands from living ones, trimming back the places where I had thickened too greedily. Someone might tie small offerings into my upper cords: a polished snail shell, a loop of woven fiber, a seed bladder glowing faintly from touch. Not because I demanded worship, but because I marked a place where the city and the mountain spoke to each other.

I would know individual hands.

The cautious ones. The impatient ones. The old hands that rest longer than they pull. The children who poke and then retreat, laughing in bubbles. The gardeners who apologize by habit when they cut. The mourners whose grief comes through their palms as pressure without rhythm.

If there were dead to be brought into the cliff chambers, I would feel the procession before I saw it. The signal stones would have sounded low from the plaza. Fish would scatter from the lane. Weighted bodies would move slowly upward, guided by poles and hands, wrapped not to be hidden but to be held together until root and mineral could receive them. I would open wider for such passages. My bitter skin would soften. My inner filaments would withdraw from the sill. The chamber would take them, and in time I would know them differently: as calcium in the vent fans, as nourishment in the pale mats, as a new firmness in the roots that kept the lintel from falling.

If people were gone, I would still be there.

I might no longer be combed clean. My lower strands might have thickened into ropes. The chamber behind me might be fuller of silt, more nursery than shrine. The offerings would be unrecognizable, buried or absorbed. But I would keep the doorway because that is what I had become. Culture would persist in my shape. I would still leave a hand-wide gap where hands had once always passed. I would still react to certain stone tones, loosening at vibrations no one intended anymore. When a turtle bumped the old signal cord and sent a broad note through the cliff, I would answer by opening for a procession that would never arrive.

Perhaps that would be my loneliness: not sorrow exactly, but readiness without object.

Yet I would not be abandoned. Nothing there is alone in the dry sense.

Small transparent fish would sleep among my strands at night, if night can be said to enter a submerged city. Pale crustaceans would pick parasites from my knots. A climbing fish would rasp my lintel and occasionally try my outer skin, learning again that I am not good to eat. Bladder-plants beside me would glow when my tendrils brushed them. White fern-mats would thicken in the cold flow I moderated. During the season of yellow-green pollen, I would become dusted with gold, and for days every creature passing through me would leave marked.

Once a year, when the mountain exhaled mineral cold and the city whitened, my body would grow a pale rim. Each filament would be outlined in frostlike deposit. I would become visible in extraordinary detail: every strand, every split end, every tiny animal clinging to me, every place where I had been cut and regrown. The chamber doorway would shine like a mouth full of combed bone.

I would be most awake then.

I would feel the whole drowned town below: the plaza’s open volume, the market gardens in their inherited rows, the lower road where grazers climbed, the boundary wall with its wild streaming outside and its tended softness within. I would feel the old streets as currents and the new roads as routes of animal habit. I would feel roots tightening in cracked masonry, bridges held by worm colonies, roofs being eaten into shape by turtles. I would feel that nothing here stands by refusing change. Everything stands by yielding correctly.

That would be my intelligence, if I had one.

To yield correctly.

To know when to loosen and when to hold. To let fry pass but not pike. To catch seed but not clog the spring. To remember the pressure of hands without needing hands to return. To be doorway, curtain, instrument, filter, garden, and listener at once.

I would not have a face. Or if I did, it would be the old carved face above the lintel, blurred by water and veiled by my growth until its expression was mine by accident: calm, softened, neither human nor inhuman, watching nothing and receiving everything.

So: I would find myself in the cold shrine-ledge at the cliff’s foot, at the city’s upper edge.

And I would be a living threshold grown across an ancient chamber mouth: part plant, part animal memory, part civic custom gone biological. A spring-veiled keeper of passage. A curtain that listens through stone. A body made to hold open one small necessary darkness in the drowned green city.
