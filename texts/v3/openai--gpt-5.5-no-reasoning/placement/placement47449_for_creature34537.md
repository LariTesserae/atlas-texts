I would find myself not at the center of this place, though I would be drawn there.

Not one of the measurers seated in the dripping court with cords darkening in my hands; not an elder whose ear has grown trustworthy against stone; not a turner with a genius for persuasion. Those are people already too named by use. They belong to their tasks the way a channel belongs to its fall.

I would be found nearer the edge.

Not outside the settlement, exactly, but where the planned city thins into raw mountain: the upper rim of the hollow, where the last roof-garden gives way to blue clay shelves, wet scree, and stone that has not yet agreed to paths. A place where someone has set three trial stakes and then abandoned them for a season because the water would not answer plainly. There is a half-dug groove there, no longer than an arm, filled with clear standing water and the drowned husks of pale insects. Beside it are flat stones sorted by size. Not enough stones for a wall. Enough for an intention.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be young, or young in the terms of that people: old enough to be trusted alone with a basin, not yet old enough to be trusted with a threshold.

A child almost finished being a child.

One of those who has begun to receive work-names but whose birth-name is still used when someone calls from a doorway in alarm. Perhaps I was born during a weather hesitation, when mist lifted and lowered three times before dawn. My first name might have been Cloud Comes Back, or The Drop That Waited. Something mildly embarrassing, fondly kept by elders, too soft for the work I want.

I would want to be a turner.

Not because turners are honored, though they are. Not because they make the visible parts of the city’s ethics, though they do. I would want it because I would be impatient in the particular way this place produces impatience: not fast-footed, not loud, but inwardly full of imagined routes. I would see water spread and immediately wonder what angle might teach it grace. I would carry possible channels in my mind the way other children carry songs. While others played at miniature cities, I would not be content merely to watch a leaf drift. I would alter the banks. I would pinch clay. I would make the leaf pause, spin, release, return.

This would make the adults wary of me.

Not displeased. Wary.

A child too eager to guide water is like a child too eager to speak for an elder. One listens first. Everyone knows this. I would know it too, and still my fingers would itch toward the mud.

So I would be given small tasks that are almost punishments and almost blessings. Scraping algae from the underside of spill-stones. Carrying grit from household basins to path repairs. Sitting beside a covered underpath with my palm on the paving until I could say whether the flow beneath was clear or crowded. Not allowed to change anything. Only to report.

I would be bad at this at first.

I would report not only what was there, but what I thought should be done. An elder would say, “The channel did not ask you to improve it.” A basin-keeper would say, “You have brought back advice when you were sent for silt.” Someone kinder would say, “Your hands are older than your listening.”

That would hurt more than a scolding.

I imagine I would live in one of the expected houses.

Not wholly empty, not wholly occupied. Perhaps my birth-parents died, or perhaps they belong to another flow and I was claimed early by a path that saved itself through me. I heard something one night: a change beneath the northern underpath, a hollowing, a thin wrong knock under the stones. I woke a roof-family before the paving collapsed. After that, the north underpath had some claim on me. Adoption by infrastructure: a thing outsiders would misunderstand entirely, and I would both resent and cherish.

The house where I sleep would have two unused sleeping platforms, rolled mats aired and returned, cups inverted beside a hearth lit only in the coldest weeks. Its inner basin would be lined with green pebbles in a spiral that someone else arranged before I was born. I would maintain it carefully, partly out of duty and partly because the basin is the nearest thing I have to an older sibling. It receives what the room cannot say.

At dawn I would crouch beside it and sort what the night brought.

A little grit.
A fern seed.
Once, a white thread of root entering through the clay seam.
Once, a drowned translucent snail, so small I would almost miss it.

I would not throw the snail away. No one would. I would carry it to the moss below the eastern wall and press it in with one wet thumb.

My hands would always be cold.

Broad-palmed, perhaps, chapped at the knuckles, the nails dark with blue clay. My clothes would never dry completely. I would have a hood too large for me because it had belonged to someone expected to grow into another name. Around one wrist I might wear three cords: one for my birth-house, one for the north underpath, one for apprenticeship not yet granted. The third would be unknotted. That would bother me. I would touch it too often.

My closest companion might not be another child, though there are a few.

It might be a roof-gardener with bad knees who lets me climb where I am not yet supposed to climb. Or one of the central measurers, nearly blind, who finds my impatience amusing because she has outlived several generations of impatience and knows which kinds become useful. She would call me Little Straight-Cut when I annoyed her. This would be a sharp insult in that place and therefore a joke.

“Little Straight-Cut,” she would say, “come listen to the southern stone and tell me what mistake you hear.”

“I hear no mistake.”

“That is yours.”

She would teach by refusing to explain too soon.

From her I would learn that rhythm is not counting. That two drops equally spaced may not be saying the same thing. That a hurried drip may mean abundance, blockage, thaw, root pressure, or the vanity of whoever last cleaned the crown groove. That the city’s heart is not one sound but disagreement held together.

I would love the central court but not feel easy in it.

Too public, though often empty. Too much listening layered on listening. The upright stones would seem almost alive in the wrong way: not like persons, but like obligations. I would cross the court quietly, as everyone does, but my attention would always be stolen by the incomplete gap in the ring. The missing stone. Or perhaps there is no missing stone yet, only a place where one will be raised when the settlement has enough names.

I would think about that gap too much.

Children are not supposed to speculate loudly about future civic organs. Still, I would. I would imagine the sound the next stone might make. I would wonder whether its drip would be slow, double, feathered, blind. I would dream of finding the seep that feeds it before anyone else does.

And that, I think, is where the place would begin to change me.

Because I would go looking.

Up beyond the last settled channels, past the trial stakes, over damp shelves where the clay shines like bruised skin. Not far enough to be truly lost, but far enough that voices from the hollow arrive broken. I would kneel among raw stones and press my ear to surfaces not yet named. Most would say nothing I could understand. Some would be full only of cold. Some would make my own pulse too loud.

Then one day I would find a dry-looking stone that wets my cheek.

Not dramatic. No vision. No voice.

Only this: my ear against pale green-grey rock, and after a long while a bead of water forming where there had been none, touching the edge of my jaw, running down into my collar.

I would stay very still.

The first lesson of the hollow: do not seize the occurrence.

The second: do not pretend it did not choose a place to happen.

I would mark nothing at first. I would not scratch the rock, not set a pebble, not tie a cord. I would return the next day, and the next. I would bring no one until I knew whether it was a seep or only weather, whether the stone repeated itself or had merely spoken once in sleep.

For three mornings it would bead.

On the fourth it would not.

On the fifth it would bead twice, from two points no wider apart than a thumb.

Then I would tell the blind measurer.

She would not ask me what it means. She would ask how long I waited before wanting it to mean something.

If I answered honestly, she might take me seriously.

This would become my apprenticeship: not yet a turner of household channels, but a watcher of uncommitted water. A listener at the edge of the plan. Someone sent to places where the mountain is considering an addition to itself.

My work-name might become Rim-Waits.

Or First Ear on the Upper Stone.

Or, if the people are teasing and accurate, Not-Yet-Turner.

I would hate that one until I loved it.

I would occupy the threshold between the city as drawn and the city as emerging. My task would be to notice where the land begins rehearsing a future street, where condensation gathers along a line that no tool has cut, where moss pales after footsteps though no path exists, where a trickle refuses all obvious descents and lingers as if awaiting manners.

I would not build. Not at first.

I would bring reports to the roofless chamber, where the exposed bedrock sweats its faint map. I would kneel with elders and compare the ridge I found outside to the ridges in the chamber floor. I would watch for three days to see whether condensation answers. I would learn the terrible restraint of not acting on a beautiful possibility.

And I would fail sometimes.

I would make a little groove when no one had permitted me. A tiny one. Barely a finger’s width. Just to see.

The water would take it too eagerly.

That would be the frightening part. Not refusal, but obedience. It would run where I suggested, and for a moment I would feel the joy of influence. Then, downslope, a household basin would cloud. A moss bed would yellow at the edge. Some small consequence would reveal itself, not catastrophic, but undeniable.

No one would shout.

That would make it worse.

I would be taken to undo it. Not symbolically. Actually. Clay packed back. Stones lifted. Flow coaxed away. Apology performed as repair. For days I would carry silt from the affected basin and restore the moss with water from the least-used channel.

The elder would say, “A wrong thread may become a habit.”

I would answer, because I am still young, “It was only small.”

And she would say, “So are beginnings.”

After that I would become more useful.

Not less imaginative. More afraid in the proper way. Fear here is not terror; it is respect for delayed consequence. I would begin to understand that the city is not waiting for someone bold enough to complete it. It is waiting for enough people patient enough not to complete the wrong thing.

I would still dream ahead.

At night, in the expected house, listening to the basin receive roof-sweat and wall-sweat and the faint underpath tremor, I would see streets not yet opened. A second ring above the first. A covered stair. A court where children not born yet argue over miniature channels. Smoke from western roofs. The missing stone raised and dripping in a rhythm that makes the others alter themselves.

I would wake with these plans bright in me.

But I would not run to build them.

I would carry them to the roofless chamber and wait to see if the mountain was also dreaming.

So who or what would I be?

I would be one of the first generation born after the plan became too large for its people.

A damp, narrow-shouldered apprentice at the settlement’s upper edge, claimed by an underpath, tempted by channels, chastened by one early mistake. A child of expected houses. A listener not yet trusted with turns. Someone whose gift is not mastery of water, but noticing where water has begun to imagine civic life.

I would be found kneeling beside the half-dug groove on the rim, hood dark with mist, cheek against the young stone, trying to tell the difference between a seep, a summons, and my own wanting.

And if I became anyone important there, it would not be because I made the city grow.

It would be because, once or twice in the long wet patience of that unfinished mountain, I knew where not to cut.
