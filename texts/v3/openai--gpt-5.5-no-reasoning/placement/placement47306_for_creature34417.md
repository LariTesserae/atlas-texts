I would be found near the basin, though not in it.

At first I think I am one of the keepers: a narrow person with a lamp on my brow, a ring of tags against my ribs, a hand already blackened from touching what should not have been touched. I can imagine the stoop in my neck, the permanent caution of my shoulders. I would know which steps sound hollow and which pipe to lean against in the cold hour. I would have a private map made of burns and spared knuckles.

But the longer I stay, the less I seem like someone who enters and leaves.

I would belong to one of the repeated places. Not the whole district, not the grand hidden system, but a small dependency within it: the north edge of the maintenance bay, where the cooler drip falls and the moss has managed to remain almost green. I would be the thing that tends that edge without quite meaning to. A little custodian of a fault. Not appointed. Not seen often. Necessary only in the way a shim is necessary, or a rag tied to a rail, or a chalk mark renewed because hands still need warning.

I would live in the low fog when the bellows exhales.

Most of the time I would be mistaken for conditions: condensation on a pipe, a shoulder-shaped darkness between two tanks, a dark bundle of cloth left on a grating. When the fog lifts, I would not so much appear as fail to disappear completely. A crouched, jointed, heat-polished being; human-sized only if unfolded, and I would seldom unfold. My back would have learned the curve of the duct above the basin. My knees would know hexagonal tile. My palms would be tough in different ways: one scarred by valve wheels, one softened by wiping water from gauge glass.

I would have a face, but not a very available one. The dark would keep it. Perhaps it would be long and damp and pale where the lamp never reaches, with eyes that return green pinpoints when they catch a pilot bulb. Perhaps I once had ordinary eyes and they have become better at noticing shine than shape: the swelling bead under a flange, the new brightness where a lever has shifted, the oily rainbow that means a seal has begun to fail.

I would wear remnants of keepers’ things, but wrongly, as if inherited from hands larger or more decisive than mine. A rubber apron cut down and retied. Canvas sleeves stiff with mineral. Copper wire around one wrist. Tags with numbers whose leaks are gone. A fuse puller worn as a charm, though I know better than charms. My clothing would be less an outfit than insulation: against heat, against edges, against being too separate from the machinery.

I think I would have a task, but it would be difficult to describe as work.

I would collect the almost-water.

Not water enough to drink freely, not water permitted to become a stream, but the beads before they fall, the threads on the inside of gauge-glass, the sweat beneath cloth wrappings, the three interrupted drips that almost become a rhythm. I would move with a shallow cup, a strip of rag, a bent spoon of dark metal. I would draw moisture from where it gathers wrongly and give it to where a small life is still negotiating.

A drop to the moss at the north edge.

A wiped palmful to the fern behind the return line.

One careful touch to the translucent vine with three leaves, not too much, because abundance would shock it.

I would not be kind, exactly. Kindness is too large and airy a word for that place. I would be exact. I would know the smallest ration that keeps a thing from ending.

When the human keepers come through, I would avoid them unless they are making a mistake. Then I might tap.

Not speech. Speech would feel extravagant. I would use the code the district already accepts: two fingernails against pipe, a wrench lightly against rail, a small click from under the stair. If they reach for a valve that has been waiting too long to be touched, I would tap once from behind them. If they pause and listen, I would tap again, farther left. If they ignore me, the room would instruct them in its rougher way.

Some of them would know me, though not by name. They might say, “Leave the cup there.” Or, “Don’t clear that moss.” Or, “If the north side is wet, wait before bleeding the line.” They would pretend these are habits, not agreements.

Maybe an older keeper once fed me crumbs of hard bread and let me sleep in a dead switch cupboard. Maybe I was a child of the district, misplaced during a shift change and never fully reclaimed. Maybe I was never human at all, only one of the cable-creatures made larger by warmth and attention, taught to imitate maintenance because maintenance is the safest form of movement here.

Or perhaps I am what becomes of a keeper who stays too long and is gradually accepted into the room’s grammar.

A person may begin by repairing leaks and end by belonging to them.

My den would be behind the clustered pipes over the basin, where cloth bandages hang in stained loops and the air is thickest. There is a recess there between a tank and an old ceramic conduit, too small for standing, large enough for waiting. I would line it with shed labels, softened gasket rubber, a square of felt, moth-eaten wool, brittle insulation, and a laminated schedule curled by heat. I would keep found things sorted by usefulness: copper strips, washers, chalk nubs, valve pins, dead bulbs, white mineral flakes, lengths of string, fragments of porcelain fuse, wing husks. Nothing would be treasure. Everything would be potential repair.

I would eat what the district allows: fungus scraped from safe seams, crumbs left by keepers, blind insects caught under curled paint, broth thickened with mineral taste if someone above has poured it down in pity or forgetfulness. I would drink at the dawn pipe before it turns medicinal. I would know, with bodily conviction, that the same source can be nourishment at one hour and poison at another.

My calendar would be made of pressure.

Warm afternoon would press me lower to the tile. Storms above would announce themselves as cold metallic tapping long before water arrived. Maintenance days would taste of disturbed dust and fresh fear. A new seal would be famine for something. A failed gasket would be spring.

I would dislike new clean surfaces. I would distrust white light. If someone came down with bright lamps and a plan to rationalize the district, I would withdraw into the cable shaft and wait for the room to resist them. It would. Not violently at first. A stripped bolt. A mislabeled conduit. A pipe whose origin cannot be found. A panel sweating root-shapes over their diagrams. The place has survived many intentions.

I would not be the spirit of the district. That would be too large for me.

But I might be one of the district’s smaller memories given a body.

The memory of hands wiping condensation from glass.

The memory of someone tying a rag at head height after striking their skull.

The memory of numbers painted under leaks.

The memory of sitting beside the basin long enough to learn that three drops can nearly speak and still refuse pattern.

If I have a name, it is probably not a name in the open-air sense. It would be a practical designation, half joke and half warning. North-Edge. Driphand. The Small Attendant. Basin Thing. Someone might chalk a mark near my recess—not my portrait, only an arrow that means leave this passage narrow, leave the cup, do not strip the moss.

Where would I find myself?

Crouched on the black hexagonal tiles at the north edge of the grating, one hand on the warm iron, one hand cupped beneath a forming drop. Above me the bellows slowly remembers to breathe. Around me the pipes lean close, wrapped and stained like old injuries. The pilot bulbs burn their minimal stars. The basin steams without hurry. Somewhere a keeper pauses, hearing me before seeing me.

I wait for the drop to grow heavy enough.

I do not hurry it.

Nothing here is improved by hurry.
