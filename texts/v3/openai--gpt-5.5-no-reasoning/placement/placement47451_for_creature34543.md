I would find myself in the narrow room near the eastern rim, the one that must be entered sideways.

Not at first as a person. Or not for long.

A person could arrive there, certainly: breath short from the height, hands cold on the low rounded walls, boots wet from crossing the lanes after evening condensation has begun. A person could turn sideways between the leaning stones and step down onto the moss floor, surprised by its depth, by the way it gives without swallowing. A person could stand there and listen while the town arranges itself outside: wind in five passages, water under stone, the central pool answering drops with a depth it has no right to possess.

But a person would not remain the true form.

The room is too narrow for ownership. It does not allow the broad gestures of a visitor. You cannot pace. You cannot build a fire. You cannot spread belongings. You cannot face the place frontally and say: I have come here. The room requires compression. Shoulders drawn in. Breath slowed. Ear turned toward stone. Body made less declarative.

So I would become something that fits by yielding.

I think I would be the thread of water in the rear crack.

Not the pool, not the central remembering eye. Not the wind, too public and articulate. Not the moss, though I would envy it. Not the snail, priestly and pale, reading with its mouth. I would be the intermittent seep that appears according to no obvious schedule: a thin, cold emergence from inside the wall, visible only when the town’s hidden pressure permits it.

Most of the time I would not be seen.

I would be held behind schist, mineral-dark, gathered in pores and hairline faults, delayed by cold. I would know the town from within its reluctance. I would not cross the open squares or lift into the fluting lanes. I would move through the inward places: under wall bases, along old seams, through cavities where frost has levered stone apart grain by grain. My motion would be slight, but consequential in the only scale that matters there.

A darkening on rock.

A bead.

A line.

A vanishing.

When I appeared in the little eastern room, I would arrive first as shine. The rear crack would blacken. Moss below it would deepen almost imperceptibly. Then one clear point would swell from the stone, tremble, and either withdraw or fall. If it fell, the whole room would hear it. Not loudly, but intimately: a small vertical sound softened by moss, taken up by the room’s strange acoustics and made taller than itself.

That would be my speech.

Not language, exactly. A periodic syllable. A damp consent. A report from the hidden body of the town.

I would belong to the larger resident, but I would not be identical with it. I would be one of its nerves of return, one of the ways it remembers that underneath apparent stillness something continues to choose a path. I would carry dissolved stone, old cold, faint mineral bitterness. I would feed the moss mat without intending kindness. I would give the insects a darker underside of stone to cling to. I would provide the snails with a reason to visit the rear wall and revise whatever frost-script had gathered there.

In dry weather, I would retreat.

The room would remain, but my mark would be only a stain: a darker tongue below the crack, edged perhaps with mineral white. A person entering then might think the seep dead, or seasonal, or accidental. That would be acceptable. The town does not mind being underestimated at noon. I would wait in the pressure behind the wall, held as dampness rather than flow, part of the long hesitation between weather events.

In rain, I would lose my singularity.

Water would come from everywhere then. Down lanes, along ledges, through moss, over thresholds, tapping from thirteen rooms and speaking in all gutters at once. My own trickle would be one voice in the city’s sudden chorus. I would emerge stronger, perhaps even as a narrow thread running down to the moss floor, slipping beneath it, joining the hidden route toward the central hollow. For a little while I would remember motion as abundance. Then the rain would pass, the lanes would empty, and I would reduce again to seepage, to interval.

I would have no desire to become a stream.

That would be too young an ambition.

A stream wants continuity, a channel, a destination with force behind it. I would be older than wanting that, or shaped by a place too old for it. Here water does not claim; it recurs. It appears, disappears, reappears. Its fidelity is not in remaining visible but in returning to the same tendencies. The crack. The moss. The basin. The hollow. The dark pool.

If I had a mind, it would be a memory of routes.

I would know the taste of different stones. I would know which nights freeze me at the lip and which only thicken me. I would know the slight pull toward the central pool, even from the eastern room. I would know the dwarf willow roots as pressure and thirst. I would know the underside of moss as a vast green darkness, crowded with capillary worlds. I would know snail-bellies as brief eclipses passing over my stain. I would know frost as interruption, then multiplication: the crack widened by the thing that stopped me.

That would be the law I lived by: obstruction becoming future passage.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness belongs to creatures that expect answering forms. I would be answered constantly, but in other grammars.

The moss would answer by swelling.

The lichen would answer by paling, blooming, retreating.

The stone would answer by accepting a line, then losing it.

The room would answer by making my smallest fall audible.

The central pool, far off yet connected through the town’s hidden throats, would answer by trembling.

And the wind, though it could not enter fully, would lean its voice into the slit of the doorway and remind me of the open world I did not need to cross.

So: I would find myself not in the plaza, not at the pool, not walking the five lanes like a late pilgrim, but in the little eastern chamber where sound falls from above and the whole settlement can be seen in layers.

And I would be the intermittent seep in the rear wall: a small returning thought of the town, cold and mineral, appearing when pressure and weather agree; feeding moss, darkening stone, entering the civic body drop by drop.

A being almost too slight to be called an inhabitant.

Which is why I would belong there.
