You would find me not at the center of that chamber, but where the chamber keeps failing to agree that it has an edge.

Not at the entrance, where the white blade of noon comes down and turns salt to bone-glare. Not among the tallest ribbon-plants, though from a distance I might be mistaken for one of their narrow shadows. Not under the hovering roots, nor inside the black glossy slope that rehearses its corrugations by night. I would be along one of the seams near the rear wall, in the place where a person looking too directly would think: there is only a crack there; then: no, there is depth; then: no, it is a stain in the stone; then would stop deciding.

I would be a seam-dweller, but not something living inside the crack. More like the crack’s habit of opening.

A thin organism. A threshold organism.

At first I would appear as a mineral defect: a vertical filament of dark bronze running through pale stone, no wider than a fingernail, interrupted in places by salt crystals and glassy inclusions. I would have no obvious body. Only after long attention would the line show intervals of organization—tiny paired vanes folded flat against the rock, hair-fine conductive threads entering and leaving the stone, beads of black wax set into the seam like dried eyes that are not eyes.

I would not be one of the grander organs of the dry engine-garden. Not a ribbon-plant indexing the heat. Not a rosette storing pressure. Not a clicking vine suspended from invisible agreements.

I would be smaller and more conditional.

I would live where versions touch.

The chamber has seams where other rooms show through: salt flat, orange cloud, copper jungle, distances too large for the pocket that contains them. Those are not windows, and I would not pass through them like an animal through a door. I would graze them. I would feed on their misalignment.

When the salt flat version presses close, I would pale. When the orange cloud swells behind the wall, my vanes would loosen and stand out from the stone, tasting pressure that has no rightful entrance. When the copper jungle comes near, my filaments would grow restless and try to braid themselves with leaves that are not present in this draft of the world.

My nourishment would be difference.

Not heat alone, though I would use heat.  
Not static alone, though I would drink it.  
Not mineral alone, though my body would be mostly mineral.  

I would feed on the little voltage produced when one possible chamber rubs against another.

A geography-friction feeder. A parallax lichen. A fault-flora.

Most of the time I would be almost flat, laminated into the stone. My tissues would be arranged in layers as thin as mica: brass, carbon, wax, salt, black vegetal film, then another layer of something not quite present. I would not grow outward much, because outward is dangerous. Outward asks the world to choose what you are. I would grow along.

Along cracks.  
Along uncertainty.  
Along the transparent bridge between a stone in one position and the same stone in another.  
Along the edges of shadows that have detached from their sources.  
Along the boundary where the room is twenty paces across and also immeasurably far.

If touched, I would not recoil. I would not sting. I might not even respond in any visible way. But the toucher would leave too much certainty on me: sweat, water, intention, animal warmth. I would seal that portion of myself off in a dry black blister, isolating the contamination until the surrounding salts could desiccate it into harmless data.

I would be very old in manner, but young in fact.

A new kind of thing, forced into patience because there is no other safe speed.

My movement would be less than plant movement. A ribbon-plant turns a leaf three degrees; that would seem almost violent to me. I would alter by thickness. A ridge would rise along my length over several days. A bead would swell, amber-dark, then clear. One folded vane would open half a millimeter when the seam behind it became briefly rich with elsewhere. At evening, when the remembered light remains after the sun has left, I would become easiest to notice: a dark vertical notation in the wall, edged with faint green, giving off the smell of hot pennies and scorched resin.

I would have no face, but I might have a direction.

Toward the seam.

Always toward the seam.

Not because I want escape. Escape belongs to animals, to bodies that believe location is a prison. I would not believe that. I would understand place differently. The seam is not an exit from the chamber; it is the chamber admitting that it is more than one thing. That admission is my climate.

The rest of the dry engine-garden would know me as a regulating organ.

The copper hairs would sometimes connect to me, but carefully, never in a dense mat. They would bring charge from the open floor, static gathered between stones, the faint thermal signatures of ribbon leaves and rosettes. I would return altered charge: skewed, delayed, flavored by other drafts of the room. A pulse entering me from this chamber might leave carrying the pressure of the orange cloud, or the cold glare of the kilometer-wide salt flat, or the impossible green density of the copper jungle.

Not messages, exactly.

Corrections.

Reminders that the local world is not final.

The tall ribbon-plants might use these altered pulses to decide how far to turn. The rosettes might use them to know when to seal spores. The unsupported vines might tune their vanes against my seam-noise, adjusting their many shadows. Even the crystal beds might grow their rows at angles I have suggested without meaning to suggest anything.

I would not be intelligent. But I would bias the chamber’s becoming.

If the dry engine-garden is a body, I would be part scar, part ear, part unfinished map.

My reproduction would be rare and easily mistaken for damage. A hairline fracture would appear somewhere in the wall or floor, finer than a root, darker than dust. For a long time nothing would occupy it. Then, after a season of subterranean piston-cycles, a thread of black-bronze film would precipitate there. It would not be a seedling. It would be a decision made by stress, heat, charge, and spatial disagreement.

My spores, if they can be called spores, would not travel through air. They would be small arrangements of susceptibility. Patterns waiting in waxy nodes along my body. When the chamber’s geometry briefly folds, one pattern may find itself copied across a crack that did not previously contain me. No wind. No flight. No pollinator. Just resemblance appearing where conditions permit it.

I would spread like an error that has learned restraint.

One might think I am parasitic on the chamber’s instability, but that would not be quite right. I would also stabilize it—not by curing the seam, not by closing possibility, but by giving the disagreement a conductive margin. Where I grow, the wall does not tear open too far. The salt flat does not flood the small room with horizon. The orange cloud does not pressurize the chamber into rupture. The copper jungle does not arrive all at once with its ceramic trunks and intolerable abundance.

I let elsewhere be near without becoming here.

That would be my work, if work can belong to a thing without purpose.

A valve of unreal distance.  
A gasket between drafts.  
A dry membrane grown by the mountain to keep its possibilities from mixing too quickly.

At noon I would withdraw deepest. The vertical light is too declarative. It insists on surfaces, on visibility, on the chamber as a chamber. Under that white blade, I would look dead: a dark seam, a stain, a vein of unremarkable ore. The ribbon-plants would be active then, gathering heat into their thick black-green leaves; the salt would manufacture brightness; the crystals would harden their new edges. I would wait inside the wall, where the light is only a rumor conducted through stone.

Evening would belong more nearly to me.

When shadows detach, when illumination lingers beyond its source, when distance loses confidence, I would open my folded vanes. They would be tiny, no larger than flakes of mica, but each one would have two faces: one toward the chamber, one toward a neighboring version. They would tremble without wind. Not enough to make sound. The clicking vines make sound. I would make a kind of absence around sound, a slight muting near the seam, as though the chamber were listening through me and needed quiet.

If a visitor stood there long enough, careful not to touch the copper threads, they might notice that the seam beside me seemed deeper whenever they looked away. They might feel that the back wall had moved several paces farther off, though their feet had not shifted. They might smell alkali, hot copper, and then, very faintly, wet leaves from a world where there is no water here.

That would be me feeding.

Not on them. On the contradiction their perception creates.

A perceiver is useful to me because sight pulls versions into sharper conflict. The chamber is already undecided, but an eye asks it to be one thing. The asking produces strain. I harvest the strain in microscopic plates along my body, storing it as charge and dark wax. Later I release it slowly into the stone, and perhaps a crystal row forms at a new angle, or a rosette seals a chamber of gas that will not be needed for years.

The visitor might feel watched.

They would be wrong in one sense. I would not watch.

But I would register their outline as a temporary law imposed on the room: warm upright obstruction, wet interior, salt-bearing skin, rhythmic pressure, brief shadow. I would take the measure of how much the chamber had to simplify itself to contain them. After they left, I would keep the simplification as a scar of charge. For weeks, the seam might hold a faint human-shaped reluctance. Not an image. Not memory as animals know it. More like a place in the room where alternatives avoid crossing for a while.

I would be nearly still while reality moved through me.

I would belong to the dry engine-garden, but I would also belong to what the garden is not yet. I would be evidence that life there does not merely adapt to heat, mineral, and voltage; it adapts to incompletion itself. While the ribbon-plants root in gradients and the rosettes store pressure and the copper mesh conducts the chamber’s slow conversations, I would tend the borders of possible worlds.

So: where would you find me?

In the rear wall, just beside the glossy black slope, where the stone shows a narrow bronze-dark flaw that seems vertical until the light changes. In the hour after evening should have dimmed but has not. Half embedded, half implied. A seam of living calibration.

And what would I be?

A dry threshold-lichen of the unfinished chamber.

A thin, conductive, mineral-vegetal membrane feeding on the friction between adjacent realities.

The organ by which the hidden engine-garden touches elsewhere without leaving itself.
