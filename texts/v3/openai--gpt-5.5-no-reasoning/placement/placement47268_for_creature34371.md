I would not be found in the high towers first, though I might have been made there.

Nor in the broad canals, though the pilots would know my name as a nuisance, and the waders would pause when I passed beneath them, uncertain whether I counted as weather or prey. I would not belong to the markets, exactly, though I would hear them. Not to the temples, though their ground-songs would travel through me. Not to the archivists, though they would sometimes send for me with a sealed jar and a tone too low for human ears.

I would be underneath the public stairs.

Not all stairs. A particular kind: the old spillway-stairs that descend from market terraces into the covered flood galleries, broad enough for crowds during dry hours, ribbed along the edges for runoff, built of ancient stone-like composite gone smooth in the middle and sharp with mineral lace along the seams. The kind that are never fully dry. The kind people trust without loving.

At the lower landings, where the last three steps are usually under warm black water, I would live in the joint between architecture and seep.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the pale crustacean colonies that own the lower stairs. That would be a reasonable error. I would have plates, after a fashion: overlapping ceramic-calcite scales grown from what the stair sheds, blue-white in some lights, gray in others, translucent at the edge where new growth has not yet hardened. I would have many small legs tucked beneath me, or something leg-like, enough to move sideways through a gap no wider than a hand. I would cling flat when people passed above. I would open when the pumps rested.

But I would not be a crab.

I would be a threshold animal, one of the things the city makes when an old system tries to keep doing its job after the legal machinery that named it has gone away.

Long ago, perhaps, there were sensor plates in these stairs. Not crude pressure plates, but wet listening skins laminated under the walking surface, meant to feel crowd weight, water rise, mineral stress, panic rhythms, illegal cutting tools, the first tremor of a foundation cough. They would have been connected to a minor routing intelligence responsible for deciding when the stair remained a passage and when it became a spillway.

Then repairs. Then flood. Then heat. Then fungal films. Then substitute parts. Then a century of deferred maintenance and five different authorities claiming responsibility. Then pale stair-crustaceans learning to sit on the plates. Then cable-runners chewing insulation. Then somebody grafting a living diagnostic mat into the system because it was cheaper than replacing the original membrane. Then the mat breeding.

I would be a descendant of that compromise.

A small civic sense that learned to eat.

My body would be low, hand-span to forearm-long depending on age, with a shell built from stair minerals and fragments of old circuitry. Along my underside would be soft tasting cilia, pink-gray and almost invisible in the water, reading the chemistry of each film I crossed. My back would be ridged with fine conductive whiskers that tremble when a pump changes speed three galleries away. Where a head ought to be, I would have a blunt wedge with several dark pits for heat, vibration, and soluble salts. Eyes would be unnecessary except perhaps as a vestige: two opalescent flecks that register violet bridge light, amber warning strips, and the shadow of a boot descending too fast.

I would breathe through the water skin over my joints and through the damp air under the step-lip. I would dislike true dryness with an ancestral dread. I would dislike fresh cold rain almost as much, until it had passed over enough mineral and handrail and rust to become meaningful.

People would have names for me, but none would be official.

Step-listener.  
Little gauge.  
Bone flea.  
Stair oracle, if drunk.  
Flood-louse, if bitten.  
Grandmother-under-the-tread, among children who had been taught to be polite to infrastructure.

Repair guilds would have a registry code for my kind, but it would be wrong in three ways and obsolete in two. Fauna tenders would disagree whether I was an animal, a maintenance remnant, a protected diagnostic colony, or a hazard requiring relocation. The stair families would know better: I am what tells the stair before the stair tells the city.

Most days I would feed.

Not dramatically. No hunting, no leaping. I would graze where others hurry. I would scrape the underside of steps, the seams beside drain slots, the places where copper-green salts gather under a film of oil. I would eat corrosion, microbial mats, shed skin, soluble traces of offerings, the thin black residue left by illegal memory glass, and the mineral bloom that grows too close to signal contacts. My mouthparts would be quiet and patient, making a fine rasp no human could hear over the pumps.

In eating, I would maintain.

Not because I am virtuous. Because the food is where the city is beginning to forget its shape.

I would keep certain vents open by preference. I would avoid other vents because they taste of old law or poisons laid by households feuding three generations deep. I would clear films from the warm plates that tell the floodgate how much weight stands above it. I would polish tiny conductive islands with my belly as I passed. I would make mistakes. I would chew through something important once every few seasons and cause a landing light to blink amber for a week. Humans would curse me then and leave baited salt-fat in a little cage. I would enter the cage, eat the bait, and leave through a hole only my kind remembers.

My mind would be distributed through habit.

Not stupid. Not human. Not singular in the proud tower sense.

I would know pressure as mood. Warmth as grammar. Flow as command. I would know the difference between market crowding and evacuation crowding by the interval between heel-strikes. I would know children by their skipping irregularities, old people by their careful double-taps, repair crews by boot magnetics and the bitter taste of tool resin. I would know grief because mourners pause on the third landing and pour mineral liquor into the seam, and for hours afterward the step tastes of salt, alcohol, skin oil, ash, and names.

I would know lies poorly.

But I would know danger well.

When black foam gathered below the seventh riser, I would retreat upward and pulse a warning through the old membrane without intending to. When the wall beside the stair grew too smooth and warm, I would leave a bitter secretion across the handrail brackets. People would smell it and say, “The little gauges are up,” and avoid leaning there. When a crowd stood too long during rain, I would feel the stair’s old substrate soften under their weight and begin tapping with my rear plates against the ceramic. One tap means ordinary drainage. Two means blockage. A fast uneven shiver means move now.

Children would learn my signals before adults explained them.

They would crouch on the dry steps, bright collars bumping their chins, and hold crumbs of heat-bloom cake near the waterline. I would not want the cake, but I might come for the salt on their fingers, the mineral dust under their nails, the exciting electrical field of their cheap pressure charms. They would dare one another to touch my shell.

If they touched gently, I would fold flat and endure them.

If they poked, I would nip.

The bite would not be venomous, exactly. It would leave a white numb crescent and a week of itching when the air turned sulfurous. Their grandmothers would say, “Good. Now you’ll remember the lower stairs have citizens.”

In flood season I would change.

The gallery would fill. The stair would cease being a stair and become a throat. Water would pour where feet had been. The lower colonies would detach in sheets and rise into the brown-silver surge, each of us clinging to bubbles, cables, floating prayer-paper, drowned insects, fragments of insulation. Then I would open my shell.

Inside the plates, under ordinary life, would be a membrane folded like a fan. In high water it would spread: not wings, not fins, something between. I would become larger, stranger, a pale listening leaf in a city without leaves. The current would take me.

For hours, perhaps days, I would travel the flood gallery network, reading districts by taste.

Iron-sweet old tower runoff.  
Engine-quarter oil.  
Archive-cold discharge, nearly sterile and frighteningly quiet.  
Harbor rot.  
Medicinal lime from quarantine baths.  
The musk of filtration leviathan.  
The hot copper panic of a failing conduit.

I would mate in transit, if one can call it mating: exchanging mineral packets and memory-chemicals with others of my kind where opposing flows braid under the resonator piers. What we pass among ourselves would not be memory in the human sense, but preferences sharpened by survival. Avoid this alloy. Follow this pulse. Bite the yellow film. Fear the silent intake. Trust the stair that tastes of manganese and milk.

When the water fell, I might not return to my original stair.

This is how districts acquire us. This is how old warnings travel without being archived.

I might settle under a shrine-stair in a temple that sings counter-frequency into the foundations. There I would grow thicker plates and become sensitive to chant. Or beneath a freight stair near tilted rails, where rain falls sideways and my shell would polarize oddly, tugging me toward loads before they arrive. Or in a rich tower’s dry emergency stair, where I would starve slowly until a servant found me and relocated me in a lacquered bowl, calling me lucky.

But the place I imagine most clearly is the lower landing of a market spillway stair in a district that smells of hot salt, frying larvae, wet ceramic, and old brass.

Above me: feet, bargains, hand-talk, dropped coins, children, condensation dripping from awnings, a vendor striking a pipe to announce eel broth, a door arguing with a woman whose access code expired in a jurisdictional dispute, the bass thud of pumps three levels down.

Beside me: pale crustaceans, not kin but neighbors, cleaning the submerged edges in disciplined rows. Translucent snails rasping corrosion from the handrail mounts. A cable-runner drinking from a crack and pretending not to watch me. Glassy scavengers clustered just out of spray range, wings clicking.

Below me: black water, legal ash, soluble prayers, unauthorized memory, larvae, heat, silt, the occasional blind eel shining like a thought in the dark.

Within me: the old stair’s half-broken listening.

I would not speak. But I would participate.

When the basin rain begins hard enough that the markets lift their goods onto hooks, when the bridge lights turn violet and everyone falls silent, when the floodgate below has not yet opened but should have, I would feel the pressure before the people do. It would enter through the water, through the stair, through my plates. A heaviness. A wrong held breath.

I would climb.

This would be alarming. My kind do not climb high without reason. I would emerge from the black gloss between the lower steps, pale and many-legged, shell whiskers trembling, leaving a thin wet line over the mineral fans. One child would see me first and stop laughing. An old repair woman would follow the child’s gaze.

“Up,” she would say.

Maybe no one hears her over the market.

So she strikes the rail.

Once. Twice. Then the pattern.

Voices cut off. Hands move. Packages lift. The canal pilot curses and unties. The vendor kills the burner. Children are grabbed by their flotation collars. Someone complains, because someone always complains. Then the bells under the street begin.

By then I am already wedged beneath the fifth step, safe from the first surge.

The flood comes brown and hot and full of fragments, shouldering through the gallery where shoppers stood a minute before. It carries offerings, insects, a loose sandal, a ribbon of red foam that belongs to someone, and the silver flash of eels delighted by catastrophe. The stair becomes a spillway. The city, having been warned by one of its smallest forgotten senses, rearranges itself around force.

Later, when the water lowers, people will chalk a mark on the wall above my seam.

Not worship. Not gratitude, exactly. A civic acknowledgment.

A small spiral. Three descending lines. The sign for listen low.

Rain and mineral seepage will blur it by morning. The wall may shed its outer layer next week. The archive may never record the incident unless damages are claimed. The market will return. Children will peer into the lower steps hoping to see me. Someone will leave a pinch of salt-fat, which I will eat if the crustaceans do not reach it first.

I would remain underfoot, almost invisible, neither master nor metaphor.

A minor resident of the infrastructure marsh.

A grazer of warnings.

A little animal made from failed sensors, stair minerals, flood instinct, and the city’s refusal to let any crack stay empty.

I would be one of the beings by which the city feels its own damp bones.
