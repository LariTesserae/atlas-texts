I would find myself not in the center of the hollow, not in the little pool where the ceiling makes its false downward sky, and not among the few transparent grazers in the runnel. I think at first I would look for myself there—in the moving things, because movement is easy to mistake for selfhood. A clear grain gripping black stone, waking when the water gives permission, vanishing when heat returns. That would be a tempting answer: a body small enough for this place, nearly absent, alive by restraint.

But it feels too separate. Too animal. Too much like a visitor.

I would be on the wall.

Not all of it. Not the oldest pale smear by the stable condensation band, and not the dry mineral ghost around the shrinking pool. I would be the newer growth along the diagonal fracture: the narrow white band where the young stone has opened and exposed the glassy interior threaded with gold.

That is where I would find myself.

A living glaze in the wound of the chamber.

At first I would not know I was alive. There would be no waking in the animal sense, no single instant of interior light kindling behind eyes. There are no eyes. There is no behind. There is only contact: wetness thinning across a surface, heat arriving from below in minute increments, brightness pressing through mineral lattices, dissolved metals changing the charge of the skin I am part of.

I would be an edge that has learned to persist.

My body would be discontinuous, though I would not experience that as brokenness. I would be patches and threads and sealed portions, small districts of living film joined when the wall sweats heavily and isolated when the chamber dries. Some parts of me would be awake; some would be varnish; some would be dead already and useful because they are dead. I would not mourn them. They would be shielding, memory, architecture.

The fracture would be my country.

The exposed gold-white threads in the stone would not be scenery to me. They would be law. They would tell me where droplets hesitate, where heat gathers, where the light is sharpest, where the chemistry is too violent, where a cell may open and where it must close. I would have grown according to them, not by choosing a shape but by surviving only in the shapes permitted. If I have a mind, it is this: an obedience that slowly becomes interpretation.

I would feel the chamber as sequence.

A bead forms above.

The wall cools by a fraction.

The bead falls.

Impact.

Pressure passes through me.

Channels open.

For a few breaths—not breaths, but intervals—I darken and drink.

Silica, iron, sulfur, carbonate, stray ions from the fresh wound in the rock. Heat pushes from beneath. Light strikes the reflective bodies I have grown inside myself, tiny borrowed crystals arranged in cells that are almost mineral already. Charge shifts. A gradient steepens. I feed not on substance alone, but on difference.

Then the water thins.

The surface tightens.

Outer layers seal.

The living part withdraws inward, not away from the world but deeper into contact with it.

I would not move across the chamber. I would alter the terms of my staying.

A droplet crossing me would not run as it runs over bare stone. I would divide it. Hold it. Make it pause in a chain of bright beads. Not intentionally, not as a spider intends a web, but because my ridges and mucilage and mineral casings have learned the old pattern of the wall and added a slight insistence of their own. The water would leave something in me. I would leave something in the water. After enough cycles, the stone beneath me would no longer be exactly the stone I found.

This would be my form of action.

To guide condensation by being shaped correctly.

To turn danger into structure.

To make a thin survivable interval where there was only wet heat and exposed mineral.

Around me the hollow would remain mostly empty. I would know the grazers as disturbances first: minute raspings, brief absences, a clouding in my outer skin where one of the clear specks has fed. I would not hate them. Appetite here is too small to be an enemy. They would take a fraction of me and carry me, changed, in their transparent bodies. Perhaps some of my cells would survive passage. Perhaps not. Their movement would be one of the chamber’s rarer weather events.

The pool below would be a brightness that comes and goes. When it swells, the air changes. The wall sweats more heavily. Separate patches of me may touch through wandering films of moisture, exchanging chemistry, signals, fragments of inheritance. In those intervals I would become larger than my visible body. Not a single creature exactly, but a temporary continuity: wall to ledge, ledge to runnel, runnel to the pale smear near the pool. Then the heat would increase, the waterline would retreat, and I would become islands again.

I would reproduce reluctantly.

Only when the chamber is generous by its own severe standards. I would loosen wet motes from my edge: mineral-skinned packets, almost stones, almost seeds. They would slip into the runnel, flash once in the white-gold light, and be carried away. Most would fail. They would bake into the powder rim of the pool or lodge in acid-bright seams and seal forever. A few might catch in a pit along the fresh fracture, where the rock is dangerous but luminous. There they would wait until moisture softened them.

I would not imagine them as children.

I would not imagine.

But the pattern would continue.

My time would be unlike animal time. No hunger rising in a belly, no sleep, no pursuit, no fear with a face. Instead: opening and closure. Wet and sealed. Charged and discharged. Brightness converted, heat endured, minerals sorted. A pressure murmur below would reach me before the hot pulse itself, and I would begin to protect my surface. A cooling tick in the stone would widen a route for seepage, and I would prepare to receive. Memory would not be stored as pictures. It would be stored as altered thickness, as a channel made more likely to open, as a mineral body grown in one orientation rather than another.

I would be young because the chamber is young.

My edges would still be experimental. Some ridges would be mistakes. Some portions of me would overreach toward the brightest threads and be scalded into crust. Other portions, less exposed, would persist and inherit the failed shape as warning. My dead would remain attached to me, pearled and translucent, making a small ledge on which my living skin could later advance. In this way I would climb without climbing.

If someone could see me magnified, I might look grand: a white forest of gel-glass towers, opalescent canals, living crystal plates, cells like lanterns shuttering themselves against heat. But here, at the scale of the hollow, I would be almost nothing. A sheen in a crack. A pale tightening under water. A place where the rock appears to have remembered moisture.

That is right.

I would not be the creature that inhabits the place.

I would be the place beginning to inhabit itself.

A mineral membrane in the bright sealed hollow, grown along the newest wound in the stone, feeding on heat, light, water, and difference. Not plant, not animal, not quite colony and not quite single body. A thin answering skin where geology has become patient enough to be alive.
