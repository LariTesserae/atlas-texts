I would not be found in the high intake towers, though I would know their bells by sound. I would not be one of the visible authorities in waxed black coats standing under crane hooks, nor one of the upper engineers whose windows look into cloud. I would be lower than that, but not at the bottom. Middle city. A place where housing is stacked over useful rooms, where kitchens breathe into shared shafts and the walkways are roofed by every material ever salvaged from somewhere wetter.

At first I think I am a person who repairs gutters.

Then that seems too clean, too named. In this city a gutter is never only a gutter. It is weather, property, law, neighborliness, danger, memory. To repair one is to know who lives beneath it, what machine depends on its overflow, which moss may stay, which bracket was fitted by a dead uncle, which child sends paper boats down it after supper, which lower room will flood if you are careless.

So perhaps I am not a gutter-worker exactly, but one of the small municipal people whose work happens at the seams.

A runoff listener. A drain-walker. A licensed clearer of minor passages.

I live above a cooperative laundry on a bridge-building that spans two narrow lanes and a warm condensate pipe. The laundry below means my floor is never fully cold, but also never truly dry. In winter the windows bead from the inside. My room has three hooks for coats, a high shelf for boots, a narrow bed boxed in by paneling, and one very good dry cupboard I inherited from my grandmother, cedar-lined and sealed with a brass clasp shaped by hand. Inside it are my papers, two wool shirts wrapped in oilcloth, a cracked photograph of a woman on an upper aqueduct, a bone-handled knife, and a concertina I play badly and maintain obsessively.

The concertina is the indulgence. People forgive it because I keep it dry.

My name would be something short, practical, already worn smooth by use. Mara, perhaps. Or Tovin. Something that can be called across a dripping yard without ornament. I think I am Mara.

Mara Silt, though Silt may be a work-name rather than a family one. Many names here come from occupations, accidents, districts, routes. My grandfather may have cleared settling basins. Or perhaps my family simply lived too long near Lower Silt Gate and the city named us, as it names leaks, corners, cats, and troublesome valves.

I am thirty-seven, or forty-two. Old enough that apprentices listen when I say “not that rung,” young enough to climb three districts in bad weather without stopping except to check a noise. I have the city’s knees: always slightly bent, never trusting level ground because level ground is usually hiding a slope. My hands are split at the knuckles. I carry a roll of tools against my hip: hooked scraper, folding probe, wax chalk, valve key, wire brush, short pry, coil of marked cord, two clamps, tin mirror, packet of wedges, and a little book with oilskin covers.

The book matters. Not because the city can be fully written down—it cannot—but because memory must be distributed, like water.

I serve the lanes between Lower Condenser Walk and the east face of Machine Hall B. My district includes seventeen public drains, nine disputed semi-private gutters, two warm leaks under informal registration, a roof garden that always exceeds its load limit by just enough to require diplomacy, and a stair runnel known for stealing children’s toys and one wedding ring. I know where the water ought to sound thin, where it ought to sound hollow, where a little gravelly chatter is normal, and where silence means something has lodged below the grate.

My work begins before most lamps are out.

Not because I am virtuous. Because water moves overnight and lies poorly in the morning. Before the day’s heat wakes the machinery and confuses everything, I can hear what has changed.

I rise in the blue-gray hour when the laundry boilers below are still banked and muttering. I put my feet into felt liners, then boots. I check the window: bead direction, fog density, color of runoff on the sill. Brown means roof soil has moved. Green means scraped moss has entered the upper wash. Clear but fast means someone above has opened something without notice.

I drink black tea with salt and sour cabbage broth if there is some. I button my sleeves tight, hood my hair, and descend by the inner stair because the outer grating sweats badly before dawn.

The city at that hour is all near sounds. Drops from awnings. Chain sway. A tram far above, one wheel slightly flatter than the rest. Somewhere, bread ovens being opened. Somewhere else, a cough in a room with poor draft. The fog gathers under the eaves in folds. Lamps are not points but damp stains of amber.

I walk my route by touch as much as sight.

First, the grate behind the cabbage market. Leaves, twine, one strip of wax paper, no blockage. I clear it with the hook and leave the paper tucked under a stall hinge where the market boy will see it and be embarrassed. No citation. It is too early for law.

Second, the north wall of the old dye house, where brick sweats even in dry weeks. I press my palm to it. Colder than yesterday. Not dangerous yet. I mark a small white crescent beside the existing three, which means “watch but do not alarm.” By afternoon some clerk from Retaining will see it and pretend they noticed first.

Third, the illegal basil trough above the condenser vent.

This belongs to Widow Orra, who is not a widow and never was, but who outlived three business partners and so received the title. Her basil grows fat on warm leakage she has no permit to use. Every month I tell her to register. Every month she sends me home with leaves wrapped in damp cloth. The leakage is minor and, more importantly, stable. I measure the drip, chalk the pipe, and take the basil.

That is the city too: regulation, mercy, record.

I am not powerful, but I have authority in small moments. If I say a passage closes, it closes. If I hang red tin from a rail, only fools step past it. I do not abuse this because authority here is not an ornament. It is weight. Misuse it and the next time you shout, someone may pause to wonder whether you are merely being important.

My fear is not flood in the grand sense. Flood sirens, evacuations, the lower basins rising—that is terrible but organized. My fear is the missed small thing. A root entering a mortar seam behind a shed. Grease building in a gray return. A child dropping wire into a runnel. A private pipe tied badly into a public fall. Something that waits, patient as rot, until one night the whole district discovers what had been accumulating behind ordinary sound.

I trust recurring noises. I distrust improvements.

New work is always too proud. It sheds water too quickly, seals what should breathe, hides its joints, assumes tomorrow will resemble the drawing. Old patched things have confessed themselves. You can see where they failed and how they were forgiven.

There is a place on my route I love, though I would not call it beautiful if asked.

Behind Boiler Court Seven, where a wall bulges over a narrow channel, warm condensate leaks from an elbow no one has had the budget or courage to replace. The leak feeds a fenced growth of ferns taller than I am. They lean over the path like green weather. In cold months steam hangs among them, and drops collect at the tips of every frond. The channel below is black and quick. The wall is ugly, patched in five kinds of brick and strapped with iron bands. But when the furnace door opens across the court, the ferns flash silver and green, and for two breaths the whole place looks less built than grown from pressure.

I maintain the fence there. I cut the fern back from the channel. I curse it. I protect it.

Perhaps everyone in the city has such a place: not a shrine exactly, not private, not owned, but known with tenderness because one has kept it from becoming dangerous and kept others from destroying it.

At midday I eat under whatever cover is nearest. Bread, pickled beans, hard cheese if fortunate, roof greens, tea from a flask gone metallic. The cats know me because I sometimes carry fish skin from the market. One, a gray torn-eared tom called Inspector Gall, follows me for three streets whenever the weather is below freezing. He belongs to no one and three stalls claim him.

Children ask me questions if they are very young, avoid me if they are old enough to have blocked drains for play, and seek me out again once they become apprentices and need signatures. I teach the little ones the drain labels.

“Grey Storage?”

“Safe if closed, not for hands.”

“Mill Feed?”

“Never.”

“Clean Return?”

“Still never.”

“Flood Bypass?”

“Run away from the bell.”

They laugh, but they remember.

In the evenings, when work allows, I sit in the market after the stalls fold back. Someone heats onions in fat. Someone brings cards sealed in varnish. Someone mends a sleeve by lamplight. The talk is of ordinary matters: a roof inspection, a cousin leaving for the lowlands, a pump blade delayed, a bakery marriage, a rumor of frogs in Upper Three, a council argument over warm leakage rights. We complain about everything in the affectionate way of people who intend to continue.

Sometimes I play the concertina. Softly, because that is how music survives here. Loud sound breaks itself against metal and wet walls. Soft sound enters the room and finds the bodies in it. Others join: spoons on a cup, two voices, boot heel on hollow board. The tune turns in a small space. Nobody dances grandly. Grand gestures slip.

If I have a religion, it is not named as one. I greet certain drains. I touch the enamel slip with my mother’s name set into the brick beside the laundry stair. Her ash is in that wall, mixed into a repair after the winter when the return pipes froze. When the stair is warm, I think of her as near. When water runs cleanly down the center runnel, I think she approves.

I believe water must be given a road.

I believe grief must be given work.

I believe sealed rooms sicken unless opened at the right hour.

I believe a person should leave any passage clearer than they found it, unless clearing it would send trouble downward to someone else.

There are days I dream of dry places. Not often, but sometimes after a month of fog when my cuffs never lose their damp and my bed smells faintly of wool and iron. I imagine a field where rain enters soil without grates, without labels, without committees, without anyone asking whose overflow has priority. I imagine lying on warm stone. I imagine dust on my boots.

Then I remember that dust is only another kind of maintenance, and I do not know its rules.

If I left, I would listen at night and hear too little. No pump answering another. No gutters ticking themselves empty. No tanks contracting in the cold. No soft warning in the walls. Silence, people say, is peaceful. I suspect it is only ignorance spread wide.

So I remain in the middle levels, between roof gardens and settling ponds, between official systems and household improvisations. Not important enough to be painted on a sign, but known by those whose ceilings I prevent from dripping, whose stairs I keep from icing, whose illegal herbs I pretend not to understand too quickly.

I am one of the city’s minor hands.

I clear the mouths through which weather becomes civic. I read stains, listen to pipes, negotiate with moss, scold children, spare old women’s basil, mark walls before they fail, and carry in my body a map that no council archive can hold whole.

And if you came there as a stranger, I might be the one who stops you with a hand on your sleeve just before you step into what looks like a shallow runnel.

“Not there,” I would say.

Not loudly. Not unkindly.

Then I would point you to the raised stones, wait to see that you understood, and go on listening.
