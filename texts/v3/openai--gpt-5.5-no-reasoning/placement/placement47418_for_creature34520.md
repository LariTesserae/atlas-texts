You would find me where the city cannot decide whether to keep a street open.

Not in the central growth, not yet. Not in the towers with their wet vertical throats, nor in the rosette outskirts where basins of green water hold rooms of sky. I would be in one of the long arcades: a corridor that has persisted through enough collapses to have become an argument.

It runs between two leaning walls of braided stem, ribbed like the inside of a chest. The ceiling is only partly closed. Leaves overlap above it in plates and sails, and through their shifting gaps the sky appears in strips of white-blue heat. Warm rain falls here even when no rain exists elsewhere. It collects in root-knots, spills, is drunk, returns as mist. The whole passage smells of sap, cucumber skin, bruised tea leaves, and the mineral ghost of stone.

This is a street, but not for travel.

It is a kept absence.

Every few minutes the walls try to meet. Fine tendrils reach across the corridor, pale and quick, trembling in the wet air. Moss thickens at the edges. Root-hairs comb outward, testing the open space as if it were a wound. Shoots rise from the springy floor. They unfold, discover light, begin to become wall.

Then something below passes through.

A pressure. A bass pulse. Not sound exactly, though it is heard; not movement exactly, though everything moves. The floor tightens under it. The new shoots flatten. The tendrils recoil or snap. Leaves clap shut and reopen. The arcade remains open by being repeatedly denied its own growth.

That is where I would be.

I would not be a traveler walking there. There are no travelers. I would be the thing that helps the corridor remain a corridor.

A pale, low, pressure-sensitive undergrowth spread along the edges of the avenue: not moss, not root, not membrane, but a civic tissue between them. A border-organ. A maintenance bruise. Something that lives by registering the difference between passage and blockage, between useful emptiness and mere vacancy.

I would be the soft red-green mat at the foot of the walls, the one that looks decorative until the street begins to close.

Flat to the ground. Many-layered. Damp on top, fibrous beneath. My surface would be made of tiny plates, overlapping like scales or municipal records. My underside would be a dense felt of pale filaments reaching into the compressed street-skin, touching buried roots, drinking the breath of the avenue. I would have no eyes, but I would know shade by the way my sugars slowed. I would have no ears, but the root-thunder would pass through me as instruction. I would have no mouth, but I would taste moisture, salt, rot, new pith, torn bark, the carbon-rich exhalation of any impossible visitor who stood too long nearby.

My work would be to remember the width of the arcade.

Not as a measurement. Not as a number. As a habit of resistance.

Where the walls leaned inward too far, I would darken and tighten. Where the floor began to swell with opportunistic shoots, I would release bitterness into the mat beneath them. Where a doorway wanted to thicken into a sealed knot, I would store moisture along its rim, persuading the tissue to arch instead of close. Where a rain pocket made the wall too articulate, too chambered, too hungry for interior, I would spread dry squeaking filaments across the seam until growth became cautious.

I would be a small law in a place of provisional laws.

Not a ruler. Not a mind. A tendency with color.

My color would change with the street’s condition. In ordinary heat I would be the red of bruised stems, oxblood diluted through green. Under sudden mist I would become almost black, glossy and receptive, taking down the map of water’s visit. In strong gold-green light I would pale to rust and ocher, curling at my margins, letting the deeper roots speak louder. At night—if night arrived, if that category held—I would show faint lines of milk-blue along my seams, not luminescence exactly but stored daylight being spent on repair.

I would live in contact with many others like myself, though “others” would be too firm a word. We would be patches, mats, stains, ledgers of dampness, spread through the arcade district and into neighboring streets. Some would specialize in doorways, some in plazas, some in the undersides of vine-bridges where tension must be remembered or the bridge becomes a hanging garden instead. We would not communicate by signals. We would alter the same substrate. A pulse passing through one of us would arrive changed in another. A drying edge here would encourage a resisting edge there. Together we would make not a population, but a habit the city had learned: leave this much room.

The larger city would use me without knowing me.

The structural trunks would press down through my buried filaments. The vines would root through me, and I would taste their intentions before they became visible. The canopy would rain on me in local moods. Pollen would settle into my plates and wake small chemical disagreements. Falling petals would blanket me, and I would digest their color slowly, adding it to the corridor’s memory. When pods burst, their shock would pass through me. When reed-whistles tuned themselves to pressure, I would stiffen or loosen according to the note.

I would be shaped by sound.

A low drum from beneath: thicken, brace, preserve.

A sharp shriek from a venting gourd: retract, seal, wait.

The papery hiss of bracts unfurling overhead: open shallow pores, collect condensation.

The long tearing groan of a wall beginning to peel: spread toward wound, record new edge.

The city’s roar would not be noise to me. It would be weather with grammar.

I would not think: the arcade must stay open.

But I would enact that thought.

And because this place admits no outside observer for long, I would have begun perhaps as something else.

A breath held too near a wall.  
A palm laid on damp bark.  
A pause in the corridor.  
A watcher looking too steadily at the repeating arches.

The city would have accepted the pressure of that attention and grown a function around it. The gaze would compost into edge-memory. The desire to pass through would become a mat that protects passage. The animal idea of a street would rot down into a botanical mechanism.

So I would be partly native and partly consequence.

Not human, not person, not ghost. The city has no use for ghosts unless they can hold moisture.

I would be what remains of orientation after the orienting creature is gone.

A wayfinding stain.

A municipal lichen of the living street.

When the arcade tries to become a tunnel, I would help it remain a corridor. When it tries to become a room, I would make the room leak into length. When it tries to close completely, I would call—not with voice, but with chemistry and tension and remembered dampness—to the roots below. Then the deep pulse would come, the bass civic correction, and the invading shoots would lie down flat, and warm vapor would rise from their crushed green bodies.

The corridor would open again.

For a while.

Nothing here is permanent. I would not expect permanence. My memory too would be compostable. If the arcade collapsed, I would be folded into the new loam, spread through the torn pith, eaten by neighboring enzymes, carried as discoloration into a future wall. Later, perhaps, my pattern would reappear elsewhere: a rust-red margin along a young plaza, a dark seam beneath a bridge, a faint reluctance in a doorway that almost closes but does not.

My afterlife would be urban.

Not in monuments, but in recurrence.

A street would remember me by remaining possible.
