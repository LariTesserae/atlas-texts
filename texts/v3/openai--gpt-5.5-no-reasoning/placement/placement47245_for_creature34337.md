I would find myself in the half-made basin before it has learned to be a valley, where the water cannot yet decide which way to go.

Not at the summit, not on the obvious ridgeline where the dark birds use the wind, and not down among the herd paths where hooves have already begun to declare preference. I would be lower than the cliffs and higher than shelter, in one of those shallow places between upthrust slabs where the ground is always wet beneath its skin. A basin only by suggestion. A held place. A pause in the stone.

At first I think I am an animal, because the place asks for animal knowledge: when to move, when to fold into stillness, which crust can bear weight, which shining surface is ice and which is merely water pretending to be stone. I feel the wind enter my fur. I know the taste of salt before I see it. My feet know the difference between slate that will hold and basalt that will roll.

But I am not one of the pale ungulates. They are too visible, too committed to the open. Their lives are written in lines across the plateau, in braids of mud and crushed grass, in calves learning the geometry of hooves. I would watch them pass, huge and quiet, the color of breath in cold air. I would know their smell: wet wool, mineral mud, milk, fear held far back behind the eyes. I would know that when one lifts its head, the whole basin changes.

Nor am I one of the ridge predators, though some part of me envies their long hunger and their clean economy. I do not belong to the skyline. I do not want to be a gray thought moving between stones, appearing only as pressure in the herd’s muscles. I am not lean enough for that. Not singular enough.

For a while I might be one of the small burrowers under talus: blunt, damp-furred, astonished by every thaw. There is something right in that—living in the first interiors of the world, making warmth out of grass, breath, and repetition. To have a den beneath a boulder in a land without rooms. To carry stems into darkness while frost ticks overhead. To emerge only when the mountain briefly permits softness.

But even that seems too settled. Too mammalian. Too centered on a body.

I think I would be something less easily counted.

I would be a seep-line creature.

A narrow life following the first water.

I would live where a fracture opens in dark stone and cold water presses out—not enough to become a stream, not enough to announce itself, only enough to darken the rock and loosen a thread of silt. My body would be spread along that wetness: part microbial film, part larval patience, part black-green crust beginning to teach the stone how to hold life. I would not have one face. I would have surfaces.

In morning I would be frozen into glass.

The seep would stiffen overnight into a transparent vein, and I would lie beneath it, slowed but not absent, held in the thin liquid memory under ice. Above me the world would sharpen. Hooves would ring on stone. The skin of a nearby tarn would tighten and speak in small metallic clicks. The cloud ceiling would press low enough that distance disappeared. A bird landing on a ledge would send a vibration through the slab, and I would receive it without understanding bird, only pressure, only event.

Then, if the hidden sun brightened the pewter sky by even one degree, the ice above me would loosen. Not melt all at once. Nothing here is generous all at once. It would cloud at its edges, release trapped bubbles, begin to move in secret. The water would resume its almost silent travel down the rock face, carrying mineral taste, powdered stone, old snow, dissolved iron, dung dust blown from a herd path, the bodies of midges that failed the night.

I would feed on what passed.

Not greedily. There is no room here for greed. I would take chemistry. I would take faint warmth. I would take the first organic leavings of animals larger than weather. I would darken the stone by a shade. I would hold silt in my slick body. I would make a surface where another smaller life could anchor. I would be almost nothing, and almost everything would begin through me.

Around me the plateau would continue its factory work.

Drip.

Tick.

Hoof-suck in mud.

Wingbeat.

A muffled knock from inside the mountain.

Gas rising once through a basin of black trembling mud.

Gravel sliding grain by grain into a pool.

I would not think of these as sounds separately. They would be the operating condition of existence. The mountain assembling itself. The world not yet named, but already repetitive. Pumping, freezing, sorting, cracking, breathing.

The animals would come to my place because water comes there.

The pale herd would find the seam by taste. Their wide hooves would break the fragile mineral crust beside me. Their muzzles would lower, nostrils shining black, lips moving with absurd delicacy over salt and wet stone. One calf would step too close and shear away a shelf of ice, exposing me to air so cold it burned without flame. I would contract, darken, wait. The calf would startle at the sound of its own damage and stumble backward into its mother’s flank.

Later small birds would arrive in the thaw hour. Quick feet. Needling beaks. They would take larvae from the wetted margin, turn flakes, vanish, return. To them I would be a place of food. To the larvae, I would be roof and pasture. To the stone, I would be stain.

At dusk a fox-sized scavenger would follow the hoofpath and pause to lick at the seep. Its tongue would rasp warmth across the mineral film. It would smell of old blood, wet paws, and sharp hunger. It would leave a print that filled slowly with water after it moved on. By morning that print would hold ice, one dead gnat, two grains of basalt, and the beginning of another small world.

I would be damaged constantly.

Frost would split me. Sun would dry my outer skin. Gravel would scour me away in black ribbons during sudden melt. A sliding hoof could erase a season’s growth. A new crack could steal the water that made me possible, and then I would become only a dark memory on stone, a failed beginning.

But elsewhere, lower by a handspan or farther along the fracture, another wet line would open.

I would continue there.

That is what I would be: not immortal, but distributable. Not safe, but recurrent. A life that survives because it never insists on being only one body.

In the deepest cold I would know very little. I would be reduced to endurance, held between mineral and ice. But in the brief loosenings I would become busy. My surface would slick and breathe. Filaments would extend. Bacteria would divide. Transparent larvae would graze through me like tiny ungulates in a black meadow. A reddish herb seed might lodge at my edge, held by my dampness until frost took it or spring allowed it to split. A lichen spore might find the roughened rock I helped prepare. Soil would begin as an accident of my persistence.

I would be one of the first agreements between water and stone.

Not grand. Not visible from across the basin. No dark wing would circle for me. No predator would follow my track. But the whole place is made of such slight permissions: a thaw hour, a salt crust, a sheltered crack, a carcass opened to weather, a seep that runs three meters before freezing.

If I had awareness, it would be broad and dim, like the light.

I would not have thoughts in sentences. I would know gradients. Moisture here, dryness there. Iron. Sulfur. Cold. Pressure. Hoof-shadow. Wing-shadow. Freeze. Release. Freeze again. I would know the under-mountain vibration as a change in the water’s pulse. I would know storms as silence descending into nearness. I would know the passing of herds by churned mud and nutrient bloom. I would know death by richness.

A calf lost in fog and taken near the tarn would feed birds first, then scavengers, then insects, then water. Eventually some part of it would come to me as dissolved trace, as nitrogen, as a darkening, as a brief abundance in the seep-line. Nothing theatrical. Nothing wasted. The dead becoming stain, slick, larva, grass, hoof, wing.

This is where I would find myself: in the margin that is not yet a streambank, beside a pool not yet permanent, on rock not yet soil, under a sky that does not quite open.

And what would I be?

I would be the black living film in the first water.

The wet seam’s memory.

A cold, thin, patient worker in the mountain’s unfinished factory.

I would help make the place habitable without ever being called its inhabitant.
