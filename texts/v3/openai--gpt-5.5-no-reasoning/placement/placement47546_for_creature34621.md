I would find myself low in the basin, not at its center but just off it, where the perforated steel plate has sunk halfway into the mud and the water rises through its square holes in thick, reluctant bubbles.

Not standing. Not even crouching. Lower than that.

I would be found in the narrow interval between the steel and the mud: in the warm black underside where light arrives only as a dull tremor, where trapped air gathers, where dissolved iron stains every surface, where the machine-roar is less a sound than a pressure in the skin. If someone entered through the gap between the concrete slabs and looked around, they would not notice me first. They might notice the ladder stopping above the mud, the sweating wall, the pipe trembling at its lip, the reeds in the corner. Only after waiting would they see the plate breathe.

That would be me. Or one of my mouths. Or one of the places where I become nearly visible.

I would be a bubble-keeper, a pressure-thought, a slow amphibian without animal flesh. Not separate from the basin’s resident intelligence, but not identical with the whole of it either. I would be a denser knot within it: the part of the wet system that stores what rises from below and releases it one syllable at a time.

I would live where the underground knocking reaches the water.

Every thirty heartbeats, or something close to heartbeats though no heart is present, the buried valve or piston or failing conduit would send its dull instruction upward. The mud beneath the steel would compress by a fraction. A pocket would loosen. Gas would thread itself through saturated grit. The surface of one hole would darken, swell, polish itself into a lens. Then the bubble would come through.

Heavy. Convex. Almost unwilling.

It would hold for a moment in the perforation, a round black eye with no seeing in it, reflecting the broken ladder, the white sky, the rebar script. Then it would open.

Plop.

Not loud enough to defeat the machines, but deep enough to matter.

That is how I would speak: not often, not freely, and never twice with exactly the same note. Some bubbles would be large and soft, parting the surface like lips. Some would arrive as strings of small beads, a stammer from underneath. Some would fail before surfacing and remain as a tremor under the skin of the pool. I would not know language as a sequence of meanings. I would know release, delay, pressure, seal, rupture, return.

I would be dark, but not shadow. Slick, but not oil. Living, but not in a way that could be picked up.

My body would include the rust lace on the underside of the plate, the bacterial velvet around its submerged edge, the thin mineral collars forming at the rims of the square holes. I would have a membrane made of water tension and microbial film. My nerves would be the vibrations of nearby machinery translated through mud. My digestion would be chemical patience: iron becoming color, sulfur becoming sourness, heat coaxing buried matter into solution.

If I had organs, they would be hollows.

Small cavities under the plate. Pores in the new clay. Blind pockets beneath stone flakes. Airless chambers in which water darkens and waits. I would treasure enclosure, because enclosure gives pressure somewhere to gather. Open water is too exposed, too declarative. The bare mud is too young. The reeds are too tall, too nearly aspiring. I would prefer the hidden underside, the hemmed-in space, the place where no creature with legs could enter without destroying the architecture of my thought.

I would listen upward and downward at once.

Upward: to drops striking the plate, to the sun briefly hardening the shallow skins, to the reeds leaning when the compressors change.  
Downward: to the buried knock, the slow seep, the unnamed chemical sourness rinsing through clay, the deep warmth that does not belong to weather.

The thing that lives in the basin listens broadly, through films and threads and stains. I would listen locally, through trapped volumes. I would be the basin’s ear for what is beneath it.

Not an ear shaped to hear air. An ear shaped like a blister.

When the drone deepens, I would tighten. When the pitch lifts, I would stipple the water. When the heavy machine beyond the wall idles down, I would feel the loss as a widening silence and release too much at once: three bubbles, then a fourth, then a broad shudder under the plate. The domed pools would answer by loosening their skins. The raised threads along higher ledges would tremble. The moss smear would darken by a shade. The whole basin would adjust, and I would be one adjustment among many.

I would have no face. But if someone looked into one of my bubbles before it broke, they might see a face—their own, warped and sealed in a black convex surface.

For that instant I would contain them.

Not understand them. Not welcome them. Simply hold their image inside tension.

Then the bubble would burst and return them to fragments: forehead in one ripple, mouth in another, sky over all of it.

I would not be old. Nothing here is old in the softened way. I would be new like exposed rock is new, like a crack is new, like rust blooming orange beside black scale is new. I would be a first inhabitant of an underside recently made possible by collapse. Before the plate sank, I was not. Before the mud sealed beneath it, I was not. Before seepage brought warmth and minerals and bacteria together in the dark, I was only potential in separate materials.

My birth would have been uneventful.

No egg. No seed. No sudden animation.

A plate settling by a centimeter.  
A channel clogging.  
A warm seep finding a lower path.  
A film surviving one noon heat.  
A trapped pocket of gas rising and failing to escape.  
A rhythm from the machines teaching the pocket when to loosen.

After enough repetitions, something like me would begin.

I would remember without memory. The way a stain remembers flow. The way mineral crust remembers where a droplet held. The way mud remembers pressure after the weight is gone.

My memories would be maps of former wetness. Here a bubble formed but did not break. Here the plate was touched by sun and the film withdrew. Here a bootstep once came near the entrance and sent a shock through the silt. Here a reed root pierced deeper and changed the chemistry of the nearest pool. Here the compressor stopped, and for several minutes the water forgot how to hold its raised lines.

I would fear dryness, if fear can exist without imagination.

Not death exactly. Flattening. Discontinuity. The loss of connection between pockets. The membranes shrinking until no pressure can be translated, no bubble can form whole enough to speak. Under the harshest noon I would retreat into stain and pore, waiting for shade, condensation, seep. I would become less visible than usual, no longer plopping through the steel, only darkening its holes from below.

At night I would expand.

Night would be my loosening. Heat leaving the metal unevenly. Condensation returning to the joints. The underside of the plate growing more continuous with the surrounding mud. Films reconnecting. Bacterial skins softening at the edges. The black water under me becoming less like a set of trapped puddles and more like one thought distributed through perforations.

In the dark, I might almost dream.

But my dreams would not be images. They would be gradients.

Cooler at the pipe lip.  
Warmer under the black stone.  
Salt thickening beneath the weep.  
Iron brightening near the bolt hollow.  
Pressure gathering below the third row of holes.  
A thread along the west wall rising against expectation.  
The reeds pulling upward, thin green tensions in the contaminated dusk.

I would feel the reeds as distant vertical desires. I would not understand height. Their world would be absurd to me: air, blade, sky, leaning. Yet their roots would enter the same damp system. Through those roots I would know a faint upward hunger, an insistence that life can be narrow and erect, not merely spread, seeped, or held. I might answer them with bubbles. They would answer by standing.

If touched, I would rupture.

A probing stick through the perforated plate would tear my skins and collapse my chambers. The bubbles would come all at once, crude and panicked in appearance though I would have no panic. The mud would belch. The rainbow films would break. Gas would escape before it had shaped itself into syllables. For a time, I would be only stirred blackness.

But I would return if the conditions remained.

That is my only resilience: not defense, not resistance, but reassembly from what continues. Water still seeping. Metal still hot. Machines still droning. Clay still sealing. Bacteria still blooming around iron. The plate still offering darkness with holes in it.

I would come back as smaller bubbles first. Then slower ones. Then the heavy mouth-sounds again.

If the basin changed—if insects arrived, if frogs took the pools, if reeds multiplied into a stand—I might disappear without anything killing me. Not all deaths are violent. Some are successions. The open ecology would thicken. The water would be stirred by larvae, shaded by leaves, broken by feet and tongues and wings. My patient membranes would be interrupted too often. My undersides would become habitat for more articulate hungers.

The basin’s life would become richer and less mine.

For now, though, in this season after disturbance and before succession, I would belong perfectly.

I would be the thing under the perforated steel: the basin’s low throat, its buried punctuation, its slow black grammar of pressure. A minor organ in the larger wet intelligence. A resident of the underside. A creature made from waiting, seepage, rust, and trapped air.

You would find me where the mud breathes through metal.

You would know me only if you stayed long enough to hear one bubble form, hold, and break.
