I think I would first mistake myself for a person.

Not a large figure, not one of the distant maintenance silhouettes crossing the white bridges against the sky, but someone smaller and more local: narrow-shouldered, gloved, scarfed against the cold, moving with the practiced hesitation of a resident who has survived by never trusting a surface on first sight. I can almost feel the rail under my hand: wet, freezing, granular with old mineral bloom. I can almost see my breath going briefly silver in the hard light.

But the image does not hold.

I am not quite a person, or not only one. I am too aware of hinges. Too interested in droplets. Too happy in the small permissions of seams.

I would be found in the condensation yards.

Not at their center, where the suspended nets shed their bright, patient rain, but along the outer service galleries where the damp thins and becomes intermittent. There is a stretch there between two low roofs, beneath a run of fat insulated conduits that pass overhead like sleeping animals. Droplets gather along their lower seams every morning. Some fall into basins. Some miss and strike a grated walkway. Some tremble for hours, magnifying the pale world upside down.

That is where I live.

Or rather: that is where I am usually noticed.

I would be a kind of water-keeper, but not one entrusted with tanks or pumps. Those belong to households, crab colonies, old valves, arguments. My scale is smaller. I tend the first appearances of water: beads on cables, frost feathers on rails, the clear trembling rows beneath conduit bellies, the damp that gathers on the hinge side of a door when yesterday’s weather is trying to get in.

The people would have a name for what I am, though it would vary by district. In the condensation yards they might call me a dripwright. In the dry flats, less kindly, a wet omen. The Archive attendants might classify me as a local hydrological intention with mammalian habits. Children would call me by a shorter name, something tapped twice on a pipe.

I am partly animal.

My body is the size of a small fox or a large conduit mammal, low and narrow, made for slipping under rails and along pipe saddles. I have ash-grey fur dense enough to hold the cold away, with pale yellow markings around my shoulders and wrists like worn hazard paint. The fur along my face is smoothed down from pressing into inspection gaps. My paws are clever, almost handlike, with black pads and fine claws good for opening old latches, teasing moss from seams, turning tiny screws when they are not too stubborn.

But there is more tool in me than animal should have.

Along the bones of my forearms are socketed ridges where interchangeable fittings can settle if I choose to accept them. Not manufactured ports exactly; they grew there, though perhaps something taught them how. My whiskers are not all whiskers. Some are copper. Some are translucent fibers that taste voltage, barometric hesitation, the mineral history of a droplet. One tooth in the left side of my jaw is ceramic and resonates faintly when a pump five decks below begins its eleven-hour pulse.

My eyes are dark, but if you catch them in green status light you may see little diagnostic text turning very slowly behind the pupils. It is not text I can read. It is what I am reading with.

I do not speak often. I can, in a dry clicking way, and I know enough of the residents’ hand signs to ask for passage, warn of temporal damp, or refuse a gift that belongs to a valve crab. Mostly I communicate by arranging things.

Three washers stacked at the edge of a basin: safe to drink after boiling.

A strip of blue cloth tied low rather than high: do not step where the water reflects the wrong ceiling.

A line of moth wings pressed to a warm pipe: condensation expected before dawn.

A rusted screw placed upright in a puddle: the puddle is deeper than it appears, perhaps historically.

I am not solitary, but I do not belong to a household in the human sense. I visit several. There is a turbine hall with warm windows where an old woman leaves me curls of lichen cake on the sill in exchange for keeping her drip-cups aligned. There is a family in the cable tunnels whose youngest child has learned to whistle below hearing and calls me when frost forms on the underside of their steps. There is an Archive attendant who never removes her reflective hood in public, who gives me tiny glass vials full of water gathered from versions of the valley that did not last.

I keep these vials in a cavity behind an obsolete gauge shrine.

That may be my true dwelling: a narrow chamber behind a pressure manifold no one uses. To enter it, one must crawl beneath a slanted ceramic panel silvered with lichen, press a dead button that still remembers thumbs, and wait for a relay behind the wall to click twice. Inside, there is just enough room for my curled body, my hoard, and my work.

My hoard is not treasure, though it shines.

Mica scraps. Condensation combs. Broken gauge needles. Brass bird-bones traded from a gull funeral. Colored glass beads given by children. Labels rescued from white birds. Thin curls of copper. Dried moss. A small spool of red marking cloth. Three teeth from a cleaning centipede, shed naturally and polished by use. The transparent wings of glass moths arranged by symbol, because I have begun to suspect that their attraction to meaning is not hunger but literacy.

At the back of the chamber is a wall that sweats only during weather that has not arrived yet.

This is why people tolerate me.

I know water before it becomes visible.

Not all water. Not the deep coolant. Not the old sump water where blind fish slide through dark tanks and dream of pressure. My knowledge is more delicate. I sense the intention of condensation: where the air will forget dryness, where a rail will bead, where a door will open into yesterday’s rain, where memory will liquefy on a wall and become dangerous to touch.

I walk the causeways before dawn, when the saddle is pale and cold and almost honest. I drink from rail-beads one by one. I press my whiskers to pipes. I taste hinges. I listen to the small differences between ordinary dripping and administrative error.

The work is not dramatic. Most days I prevent nothing grand.

I move a cup three finger-widths to the left so it catches clean water instead of coolant mist.

I gnaw open a blocked gutter before a family’s moss trays dry out.

I chase glass moths away from a warning sigil whose obscurity would cause a pump to misremember itself.

I sit very still in the middle of a bridge until the human behind me understands not to cross yet.

I pull stolen labels from conduit mammal nests and return them, not always to their original machines but to machines that need names badly enough.

I am not always right. Nothing here is. But I am careful.

The valley has taught me that accuracy is a form of affection.

Sometimes children follow me. They think I do not notice, though I hear their glove seams creak on ladders. They want to see where I go when the lamps brighten suddenly and everyone is told to stand still. They want to know whether I have a nest of pure water, or a map of all trustworthy corridors, or a secret arrangement with the valve crabs.

I let them follow until the first place where following becomes unsafe.

Then I turn and look at them until they stop.

Children understand this kind of warning better than adults. Adults want reasons. Children know a gaze can be a closed door.

The valve crabs and I respect one another without friendship. They find me frivolous. Too quick, too interpretive, too willing to move labels for poetic reasons. I find them doctrinally rigid and sometimes culpably obsessed with fittings that have not carried pressure in fifty years. Still, when their migration season comes, I clear algae from the warmer pipe routes and leave salt in bottle caps. In return, one of them once tightened the hinge of my chamber door after it began opening into a morning I had already lived.

The cleaning centipedes are more companionable. If I lie flat under the catwalks, they will pass around me in two careful streams, brushes whispering against metal. Their small suction mouths clean grit from my claws. I clean fungus from the joints they cannot reach. When real centipedes approach, we all scatter, though for different reasons.

The gulls trouble me.

Not because they are cruel. They are not. They are solemn birds, if birds they are. But they remove labels, and I cannot decide whether this is vandalism or mercy. Perhaps some machines are tired of being told what they are. Perhaps some buildings can only become harmless after they forget their function. Perhaps the gulls serve a maintenance deeper than ours.

Once, on an afternoon of upward rain, I found a fallen gull beneath a pressure tower. It was not dead yet. One wing opened and closed with a sound like wet hinges. Its eye had cracked, and behind the crack was sky.

I sat beside it until the residents came with white filter cloth.

Under its breast feathers, I saw brass, bone, and something like folded paper covered in tiny plans. One of the plans showed the condensation yards from above. One showed a forest, impossible and soft. One showed me, or something shaped like me, drinking from a rail that had not yet been installed.

I did not tell anyone.

That is another part of what I am: a keeper of small omissions.

In the Bright Archive, I would not be welcome for long. Too much in me would begin to answer too much in it. But I go there sometimes, climbing the lens-catwalks in the hours when birds avoid the railings and inscriptions float like gnats in the refrigerated air. The attendants pretend not to see me unless I disturb a plan.

Inside the Archive, my reflection fails in several directions. In one pane I am only animal, furred and bright-eyed, stealing washers. In another I am a child in a patched coat. In another I am a device for predicting dew. In another I am absent, but all the droplets on the glass bend toward where I would have been.

The Archive insists there was once a blueprint for me.

I do not believe it entirely.

Or perhaps I believe the blueprint came after.

That is the kind of thing that happens here. A creature survives long enough in a function, and eventually the plans revise themselves to claim intention. The valley dislikes accidents that prove useful. It adopts them.

So perhaps I began as a conduit mammal that nested too close to a weather instrument. Perhaps my grandmother gnawed through the sheath of an unpowered sensor cable and dreamed in barometric pressure. Perhaps a maintenance child fitted brass whiskers to a half-tame animal and released it near the condensation yards. Perhaps an Archive plan for a portable hydrological reader was copied onto flesh by damp. Perhaps I am one of many, though I have never met another exactly like me.

I am less interested in origin than in continuation.

At noon I sleep in narrow shadow, because the light then is too exacting even for me. It flattens mystery. It makes every edge confess its present shape, and I need the almost-shapes: vapor, hesitation, imminent bead. Evening is better. Distances loosen. Bridges reconsider their lengths. The cold gathers under rails. The first moths find the status lamps and arrange themselves around meaning.

At night I walk the marked corridors and check which marks still hold.

Sometimes I hear my own voice from a ventilation stack.

I never answer.

Sometimes a staircase with moss on every third step exhales damp sweetness as I pass. I leave it a shard of blue glass and take another route.

Sometimes I find a room where memory is dripping down the walls. I know the smell: iron, old wool, childhood heat, unpainted wood no one here has seen. I do not enter unless someone has been lost inside. If I must, I coat my paws in grease and keep my whiskers back, because memory-water clings. It can bead on the fur and make me remember things that belong to pipes, or gauges, or dead workers looping forever on distant platforms.

Once I remembered being a bridge.

It took three days and a child’s patient brushing to bring me back.

For this reason, I do not despise labels. A label is a kindness. A name is a handrail.

The people have painted one near my chamber, though I did not ask them to. It is a small white icon: a crouched animal under three falling drops. Beside it are marks meaning Ask before taking water.

This is fair.

I do not own the water, but I know when it has agreed to be taken.

If you came to the saddle and asked where to find me, someone might point you toward the outer condensation galleries just before dawn. They would tell you to bring no loud voice, no uncovered lamp, and no container that has held memory-water. They would tell you not to offer meat. Offer instead a clean washer, a strip of blue cloth, or a label whose machine is gone.

Then you would wait near the conduits while the sky paled.

At first there would be only the high cold, the brightening rails, the soft patter of droplets falling from the nets. Swifts would cut soundlessly through the manufactured rain. A cleaning centipede might move under the catwalk below you, leg after leg in disciplined silence. Somewhere far off, a platform would sigh down by a few centimeters.

Then one droplet above you would tremble but not fall.

Another beside it would swell.

A third would gather light until the whole underside of the pipe seemed strung with tiny lenses.

And I would be there, though perhaps you would not see me arrive: ash-grey, yellow-marked, low to the metal, whiskers tasting the morning. I would put one paw on the rail. I would look not at you but at your shadow, to see whether it belonged to the hour.

If it did, I might stay.

If your vessel was clean, I might show you which beads to gather.

If your question was quiet enough, I might even answer.

Not with a prophecy. I am not an oracle. Not with a map. Maps here are only temporary manners.

I would tell you where the water will be trustworthy until noon.

I would tell you which bridge has become impatient.

I would tell you that the valley is not unfinished because it failed, but because continuation is its nature.

Then I would drink one bright bead from the rail, shake the cold from my whiskers, and vanish into the seam between a wall that is still being built and a wall that has been there for centuries.
