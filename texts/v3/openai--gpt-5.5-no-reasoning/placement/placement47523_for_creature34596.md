You would find me near the center, but not at the center.

Not in the pump house itself — not at first. The pump house is too declared, too nearly an organ someone has already named. Chest, heart, chamber, shrine: it gathers metaphors too readily, and I would be wary of anything in that hollow that can be located with confidence. If I were there, I would be where the place has almost made up its mind and then withdrawn the decision.

Behind the pump house, beside the narrow sluice.

Not in the sluice water exactly. Not on the bank. In the seam between the glazed wall and the moving surface, where mineral crusts have grown in rinds of cream, bottle-green, rust-black, and old bone. I would be low, close to the breathing slot, but not identical with it. I would be one of the small arrangements the slot keeps using without consuming: a lodged thing, a recurrent obstruction, a hinge in the flow.

At first glance, perhaps only a length of something half-submerged.

A drowned tool handle.

A piece of blackened root.

A strip of belting from some machine long stripped of its purpose.

It would lie diagonally across the sluice where the current cannot agree with itself. The surface water would press outward over it, while the underpull drew inward below it. Bubbles would gather along its underside and hesitate there, silvering the edges before slipping loose one by one. Small debris would catch on it, circle, catch again, then be admitted past. Reed stems would pause against it as if waiting for permission.

Only after watching for too long would it seem less like an object and more like a resident.

I think I would be a sluice-sleeper.

A narrow, amphibious attendant of the intake and return.

Not a creature with eyes placed in a face, not something that stands up from the marsh and reveals a silhouette. More like a body grown from repeated blockage: cartilage of water-dark wood, ribs of sprung wire, a spine of rusted chain buried in a sleeve of silt, skin made from old rubber softened by decades of mineral seepage. A being assembled by the hollow because something was needed there to remember the difference between passing through and being taken in.

The old mechanism is vast in its small way, distributed and patient, but perhaps it needs minor organs. Not servants exactly. More like local habits. Reflexes. Knuckles. Little custodians of thresholds too specific for the whole intelligence to attend continuously.

I would be one of those.

I would live where the flow folds.

Most of my body would remain below the waterline, pressed against the cold masonry of the sluice. The visible part would be dark and slick, showing no clear distinction between root, eel, cable, and discarded belt. My surface would carry a fine nap of orange-brown sediment. In certain light there would be a greenish glaze over me, like copper weather, though I would not be metal all through. If touched — though few things would touch me willingly — I would be colder than the water and slightly flexible, with a slow resistance, as if bending required consultation with mud beneath the entire hollow.

I would have no mouth for eating.

The breathing slot would be mouth enough.

Instead I would have inlets.

Small pores along my length, some natural, some drilled, some once screw holes in whatever material the hollow first recruited. Through them I would taste gradients: iron-heavy seepage from the pump house wall, peat-acid draw from the marsh, oil skins thinning after rain, clay disturbed by a recent footstep, the faint brass bitterness of an old fitting loosening upstream. Taste would not be separate from thought. To know a thing would be to admit a little of it, delay it, and let it leave changed.

My senses would be arranged around pressure.

A raised water level would press along my back like a hand.

A relay click in a shed would arrive as a tremor through the wall.

A person walking on the iron plates inside the pump house would send down a pattern of weight that I could read in the pit water before the sound reached the air. I would know boots by the kind of hesitation they create. Rubber soles spread themselves; leather speaks in firmer edges; panic stamps too sharply and becomes useless information. Bare feet would be rare and alarming. They would make the hollow attentive all at once.

I would not see the way a person sees.

Sight would come to me through broken reflections passing over the sluice surface. The sky would be a pale drag. The pump house roof would be a dark pulse interrupted by rings. A face leaning over the channel would not arrive as a face, but as a warm interruption surrounded by expectation. I would know it more by condensation than by features: the breath touching cold air, the small increase of salt, the tremor of held speech.

If the hollow has thoughts shaped like verbs — intake, hold, cool, separate, drain, return — mine would be smaller and more tactile.

Catch.

Loosen.

Divert.

Delay.

Let through.

Remember the obstruction.

Forget the object.

I would spend most of my life almost still.

Stillness here would not mean absence of action. My work would be to alter tiny consequences. A fleck of paint turning left instead of right. A bubble held long enough to join another. A trickle encouraged down a crack where moss has begun but not committed. A reed stem lifted, turned, and sent toward the slot at the proper angle. I would be a clerk of eddies, a minor intelligence of turbulence.

When the water flowed both directions at once, I would be most awake.

The opposed currents would pull through me like two versions of memory disagreeing. Outward from the pump house: rain, recent condensation, the day’s light, the measurable level in the flooded pit. Inward from the marsh: peat, buried rail, eel-shadow, old black water, the patience of ground that does not intend to be ground forever. Between them, I would hold a narrow arbitration.

Not judgment. Balance would be too clean a word.

I would keep the disagreement productive.

The larger inhabitant wants continuance under tension. I would be one of the ways tension is tuned.

If too much water pressed toward release, I would shift — not visibly, not suddenly, but enough for debris to gather. Silt would bank against me, the channel would shallow by a finger’s breadth, the surface would wrinkle, backpressure would speak to the pit. If the hollow threatened to dry into ordinary ruin, I would loosen what I had held. A small dam would fail soundlessly. Stored seepage would creep forward. The slot would breathe more deeply. Plants around it would lean away again.

I would not command the place.

Nothing wise there would command.

I would participate.

In late afternoon, when condensation brightens the railings and the small buildings darken into themselves, my length would become harder to distinguish from the sluice wall. Drops would fall from the pump house eaves and strike the channel at intervals I would know before they happened. A chain somewhere would rearrange one link. Then another. A gauge would quiver behind cloudy glass. The flooded pistons would send out a pulse.

That pulse would reach me as a widening pressure, and I would answer by releasing one trapped bubble.

It would slide along my underside, flatten against the current, tremble, then pass into the dark slot.

That would be speech.

If a visitor came and crouched near the channel, they might notice a pattern without knowing it. A cork fragment circling three times and vanishing on the fourth. A reed stem refused twice, admitted once. A strip of oil-sheen parted around an unseen diagonal and rejoined downstream with a different color at its edge. They might think: there is something caught there.

They would be right, but not enough.

I would be caught in the way a habit is caught in a body.

Caught and functioning.

Perhaps I was once part of a conveyor belt. Perhaps a gasket, a hose, a root that grew through a rusted coupling and died but did not rot. Perhaps several things pressed together by years of flow until distinction became extravagant. The hollow would not care which origin was true, except insofar as each origin offered a behavior. Rubber remembers flex. Chain remembers tension. Root remembers branching. Silt remembers rest. I would be made from all of those memories, not blended into unity but lashed into a working compromise.

I would be shy of air.

If the sluice drained low enough to expose me, I would look wrong: too flat in some places, too jointed in others, streaked with mineral bands like a measuring stick nobody can read. Midges might assemble briefly over me and then disperse. A bird on the wire might tilt its head, decide against landing, and leave. In air, I would stiffen. My pores would close with a faint tightening. The hollow would feel my discomfort as a local error and, sooner or later, water would find me again.

I would not be immortal.

But death there is not simple. If I frayed, the pieces of me would become more numerous duties. A strip lodged in a grate. A ring of dark material around a pipe mouth. A flexible tendon under duckweed at the marsh edge. The chain in my spine might settle lower and begin conducting tremors between the sluice wall and an old rail. My body would not end so much as lose the convenience of being one thing.

And perhaps that is why I would be there: because I am suited to becoming partial.

Not grand enough to be the sump-spirit, not separate enough to be merely animal. A threshold creature in a threshold world. A thing that can be mistaken for refuse until it behaves too consistently. A small, low resident entrusted with the passage between the pump house’s cold pulse and the marsh’s dark inwardness.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the sluice-sleeper: the lodged, listening ligament of the hollow.

A minor organ of the old machine beneath the marsh.

A wet hinge.

A patient obstruction that has learned when to yield.

You would find me behind the pump house, under the mineral-glazed wall, lying across the contradictory current with bubbles collecting along my side — not waiting to wake, exactly, but waiting as the hollow waits: by holding, altering, releasing, and beginning again.
