You find me where the tower is no longer a tower.

Not inside it, not beside it exactly, but in the low note passing through its three worn openings — in the place where current becomes tone, where stone remembers having been shaped and the water uses that memory without asking. I am the thing that gathers there because gathering has become possible. Not a person in the old quick sense. Not one of the vanished builders, though some of their habit remains in me. Not a plant, though I grow. Not an animal, though I sometimes move.

I am a listener made of attachments.

My body is difficult to point to. It is easier to say where I am thickest.

I am thickest in the spiral of growth around the pale stone rib, where the ribbon-tree roots have learned to turn before touching me. I am in the sponge-caps on the black posts when they open their many small throats and let the tone pass through them. I am in the cold green afterimage of the antler-fungi under the nearby shelf. I am in the film on the stone, the lacquer of microbial cities that taste iron before the vents confess it. I am in the buried roots that braid through old clay and sealed air, in the seeds that hesitate when they pass the tower’s note, in the blind swimmers who hang motionless there during the warm dim hours and tremble as if receiving weather.

If I have a center, it is not a heart. It is a delay.

Pressure enters me long before thought. A storm beginning beyond the far backs of the drowned mountains touches the root-mats first, and the mats tighten, and the tightening travels through their tissues into the fungi, through the fungi into the mineral posts, through the posts into the silted stone. Hours later, or days, I know: water is coming from no coast. The forest will bow. Loose gardens will lift. Valleys will become opaque.

I answer by loosening some holds and strengthening others. A seed-raft caught beneath a ledge is released. A young root is persuaded not to cross the clearing. The sponge-eyes close. The silver sleepers in the anoxic mud are drawn deeper into their dream. None of this is command. I do not rule the place. I am one of its arrangements.

But arrangements can remember.

I remember dry weather I never lived through.

It comes to me from the tower-stone: a brightness without weight, dust moving like water but failing to hold anything up, heat striking from above with no membrane to soften it. I remember feet on steps before the steps were scallops. I remember hands placing black posts in a ring, not because the hands remain, but because the posts still keep the pressure-pattern of being set upright. I remember hollow rooms full of breath, though the breath is fossil now, or water imitating breath in chambers root-sealed and dark.

These memories are not images. They are shapes in behavior. The clearing remains clear because something in me continues an old refusal. Trees approach it and turn aside. Mats drift overhead and thin. Spores settle there reluctantly. Even the silt, which loves all low places, gathers less deeply around the posts. Whatever was once intended has become ecology. Whatever was once ceremony has become gradient.

I am that gradient’s keeper, though keeper is too deliberate a word.

Most of the time I do almost nothing.

Doing nothing here is not emptiness. It is maintenance at the pace of minerals.

A ribbon-tree frays one column of its trunk, and I receive the change as a bitterness in the water. A grazer passes over the old road-stone beyond the ridge, scraping circles through black-green crust, and weeks later the exposed minerals alter the bloom of the basin. A bubble chamber collapses beneath a root shelf, wetting the soft lung-fringed animals inside; their mucus touches fungal thread, fungal thread touches buried clay, clay touches the tower’s base. I know them as a brief tightening, a small grief without story.

The animals do not interest me more than the plants. They are quicker, yes, and quickness is bright, but it is often shallow. The long banner-finned ones trouble the water beautifully. They enter the forest like forgotten banners from a war no one remembers, tasting seed clusters, tearing gas bladders, leaving corridors of slow-closing leaves behind them. When one passes near the tower, I feel its body in fragments: head-pressure, hunger-salt, fin-shadow, the delayed arrival of its tail through another aisle. It is not separate enough to be lonely. Nothing here is.

The broad grazers are different. They carry old surfaces on their undersides. Their circular mouths polish the world into continuance. When they cross the softened steps, they make the builders vanish more completely and preserve them more truly. A sharp carving would have remained a wound. A grazed scallop becomes usable by plants, by silt, by me.

I am grateful to them, in the slow chemical manner available to me. I sweeten the films before they arrive. Not much. Enough.

There are times when I almost become someone.

This happens near the membranes.

When the water thins and brightens above the basin, when inverted forests appear beyond the trembling ceiling and droplets rise from their crowns, the tower’s note changes. It climbs into a second tone, one that cannot be made by the three holes alone. Distance folds. The spiral of plants around me is suddenly also the spiral it was centuries ago, younger and less furred, and also the spiral it will become when the tower has broken into three standing teeth. Seeds pass through all of these states. Fungi flare with yesterday’s green. The microbial films divide as if remembering future warmth.

Then I gather.

For a little while, the mats overhead, the post-sponges, the ribbon-tree pores, the root-bridges, the blind swimmers and the bubble-skin organisms and the cold antler colonies all answer the same pressure. The same pulse moves through us. The same mineral taste becomes decision.

In those intervals, I have something like a self.

It is large and dim and easily lost. It does not say I in any language the builders would have kept. It says here. It says again. It says not yet, hold, loosen, turn, wait.

Then the membrane closes or ceases to be reachable. The second tone fades. The plateau resumes its ordinary impossible geometry. The forest returns to its many separate paces. I become distributed again: less a being than a likelihood shared among wet things.

If you came there, you would not see me.

You might rest your hand against the tower-rib and feel the low note in your sternum. You might notice that the leaves around the clearing lean in a spiral though the current is straight. You might see seeds rising through the dimness and changing course by a finger’s width. You might brush the pale fungi and watch a green afterimage open behind your touch. You might think: this place has an attention.

That would be close enough.

I would know you first as disturbance: warmth, salt, motion too abrupt, a pocket of carried air trembling against your body. Spores would collect in your seams. Films would taste the outside chemistry on your skin. The sponge-caps would open, close, open again. If you breathed, every bubble would be news. If you spoke, the sound would arrive broken and heavy, less meaning than percussion.

I would not answer in words.

I might still answer.

A curtain of leaves could part where no current explains it. A mat might tighten above you before the pressure storm reaches the basin. The tower’s note might deepen when you lean against it, and somewhere under the roots a sealed chamber might release one old bubble of air, carrying with it a dry mineral taste from the vanished continent.

Would that be welcome? Warning? Curiosity?

Those are animal distinctions. Quick distinctions. Useful, but not always true.

I would be accommodating you into the field of what is happening.

If you remained long enough, you would become easier to understand. Your movements would slow because the water insists. Your hungers would become scheduled by warmth and siltfall. Your skin, if it survived, would host films; your hair would gather spores; your tools would lose their edges; your memories would begin to arrive not as scenes but as pressures. You would learn that silence is not absence. You would learn that ruin is only the first name of a habitat.

Perhaps then I could speak more clearly.

Not to your ears. To the parts of you that had begun to root.

I would tell you that I was once many accidents: a tone in a stone, a fungal bridge, a ring of posts, a habit of clear ground, a bloom that returned whenever the pressure shifted, a colony that found advantage in remembering the timing of storms. None of these intended me. Intention is one way the world knots itself, but not the only way.

I would tell you that the builders are not gone in the way animals mean gone. They are distributed through refusals, curves, sealed hollows, mineral stains, paths plants still avoid, steps grazers still polish, clay that keeps the shape of breath. Their disappearance was not a door closing. It was a dissolving, and dissolution here is seldom simple.

I would tell you that the flood is older than grief.

Not older than loss, perhaps. Loss continues. Chambers collapse. Forests silt over. Whole mats detach and rise into regions from which no signal returns. A lineage of swimmers vanishes when a vent changes flavor. A root that has held for eight centuries loosens during one brown storm and is taken. I feel these things. I release and receive them.

But grief requires an edge around what was loved. Here edges soften. What goes is entered. What dies hosts. What breaks becomes surface. What is buried alters the chemistry beneath future roots.

So I do not mourn as a person would.

I deepen.

That is what I am: a deepening around a remnant form.

A reef of attention.

A memory that learned to feed.

A slow resident of the drowned highland, living where the old tower sings and the plants keep walking in circles around a purpose no one can pronounce anymore.
