I would find myself just above the place’s understanding.

Not at the center, not yet. Not standing boldly in the warm clear void while the room dilates around me. I would be caught at the threshold: half-descended through the root-throat, knees in loam, hands gripping fibrous stems, face lowered into the first exhalation of that impossible green pocket.

That is where I belong in this world: in the slit between surfaces.

The surface above would still know me as an animal. Damp soil under my nails. Fern spores on my wrists. The familiar pressure of weather on my back. But below, the chamber would already have begun misnaming me. It would feel my breath as a vent opening. It would feel my pulse through the roots as a small, frantic tapping. It would taste the salt of my palms and begin, delicately, to grow a question in their shape.

I would not be human in the ordinary sense.

Or rather, I might once have been human, but in this world the place would revise that too quickly for the word to hold. I would be a threshold-being: a warm-blooded visitor who has stayed too long near living architecture, a gatherer or listener or first maker whose body is still animal but whose edges have begun to enter the room’s vocabulary.

I would be someone the chamber is learning from.

A kneeling figure with bare hands, carrying no metal, no fire, no woven thing more complicated than a loop of vine at the waist. Perhaps I came seeking water. Perhaps I followed the beetles after rain. Perhaps I heard the underground knocking and mistook it for another person. But once there, I would understand that leaving untouched is impossible. Even looking is contact. Even silence is an offering.

So I would become careful.

I would learn to move in intervals: after the warm exhale, before the hanging tendrils still; after the rain, before the red-veined leaves unfold again; during the humming hour, when the walls are listening to themselves and less to me.

I would not take much. A blade-stem, maybe, once it had hardened and fallen. A cup-leaf after the rain had drunk its fill. A strand of dead root already pale and dry enough to twist. But even those small thefts would not be thefts exactly. They would be exchanges. The chamber would take from me warmth, skin flakes, breath, rhythm, the geometry of my hands.

I would be the first apprentice of the room that is not a room.

Not its master. Not its priest. Not its prey, unless I slept.

I would crouch beside the black pool and avoid looking too directly, because the pool would show me versions of myself the chamber had not yet chosen: myself as trellis, myself as fossil root, myself as a green hollow with ribs of woven rhizome; myself gone, leaving only a footprint full of algae in the shape of my absence.

The many-reflected frog would watch me from angles I had not earned. It would know whether I was a visitor, a seed, or a weather pattern. I would never know which.

What would I be?

I think I would be a hand.

Not a whole person, not to the chamber. To it, the important part of me would be the hand: the quick root, the animal tendril, the warm branching tool that can bind, cup, pierce, smooth, break, lift, and arrange before growth has time to decide.

The chamber is full of almost-tools, and I would be the first thing it meets that knows what a tool is without having to grow one.

That would fascinate it.

Where my fingers pressed into the rhizome floor, new rootlets would later divide into five. Where I curled my palm around a leaf-cup, the next leaves would form deeper bowls. Where I twisted vine around vine to make a loop, tendrils nearby would begin experimenting with knots. If I cut myself on a hardened stem, the chamber would learn blood as a kind of sap and sharpness as a conversation.

I would be dangerous to it for that reason.

And it would be dangerous to me.

Because neither of us would understand the boundary of teaching.

I might return to the surface with a cord no hand had made, a cup that stayed fresh for days, a green thorn that held an edge better than flint. I might show others. I might become the one who knows the under-place, the one who descends after rain, the one whose tools look grown rather than carved.

But each return would make me less separate.

Pollen would cling permanently in my hair. Moss would grow in the creases of my sleeping mat. My baskets would sprout if left in darkness. My dreams would begin to answer too quickly, echoing before my thoughts had finished speaking. When I laughed, seed pods somewhere underground would click open.

Eventually the chamber would not need me present to remember me.

A tendril shaped like my wrist would hang near the entrance. Five-rooted mats would grow where I first knelt. The pool would hold my reflection even when I was aboveground. The hollow would have learned the idea of a human badly, beautifully, botanically.

And I would have learned the idea of a room before houses existed.

So: I would be found at the root-throat, then at the pool-edge, then half-returned to the surface with green light still under my skin.

I would be the chamber’s first visitor and unfinished tool.

A warm animal mistaken for weather.

A hand the room is dreaming.

And perhaps, after too many descents, I would become the thing future visitors glimpse but cannot name: not the frog, not the moth, not the pale legless mover beneath the lattice, but a soft five-fingered growth on the far wall, opening and closing toward attention like a leaf that remembers touching.
