I think I would find myself neither on the floor of the cleft nor above it, but in one of the narrow places where the wall and the living skin fail to distinguish themselves.

Not a ledge, exactly. A seam.

A cold vertical seam in the black slate, one shoulder of stone pulled slightly away from the other by frost, root pressure, and the repeated insistence of water. Too narrow to be a passage, too wet to be a crack in the ordinary dead sense. A line of darkness with green lips.

That is where I would be.

At first I might seem like part of the wall: a band of dark moss thickening along the fissure, a lace of fine roots, a stain of wet iron, a little hanging beard of liverwort and fungal thread. Something flattened, intimate with stone. Something that has no outline unless touched.

I would not be a person standing there. A person would be too separate, too warm, too full of forward intention. Nor would I be the great cleft-creature itself, because that being is too entire, too distributed, too much the weathering body of the place. I would be one of its small organs. One of its holding-things.

I would be a root-nerve.

Or perhaps a listening mat.

A thin, tenacious colony-being grown in the seam between new rock and new life: moss, fungus, hair-root, mineral film, condensed water, and spore dust braided into one responsive tissue. Not animal, not plant, not singular enough to have a face. I would be the thing that notices tension before falling happens.

My body would lie pressed into the mountain’s split skin. I would have no bones, but I would know stone more deeply than anything with bones could know it. I would feel the minute widening of the seam before gravel let go above. I would feel water entering from a storm not as wetness alone, but as weight, as pressure, as cold expansion. I would know the difference between a harmless drip and a coming slide by the taste of grit loosening behind me.

I would feed on what the cleft makes in passing: dissolved minerals, rotting needles, fern dust, the faint sugars of roots sharing the dark, the bodies of lichens broken by stonefall, the brief richness of crushed green after a storm. I would drink continuously, but never from a stream. My water would arrive as beads, films, breath, seepage, condensation gathered on the roof of the fissure and released drop by drop into me.

Most of my life would be touch.

The slate at my back would be cold, sharp, and newly exposed. The moss over me would be saturated and dim. Fern stems would pass across my outer surface like weather made solid. Roots from a stunted tree higher on the wall would push through me, not damaging me exactly, but including me in their grip. Fungal threads would travel through my body like thoughts I did not originate. When the wind roared, I would not hear it first. I would feel it as strain transmitted through branch, root, mat, stone.

I would be very small compared to the basin, but not isolated. Nothing there is isolated. My filaments would enter the cleft’s wider network of wet holds and green repairs. I would communicate slowly, chemically, hydraulically, by pressure and dark exchange. If a new piece of granite peeled open nearby, I would know by the sudden brightness of mineral water, by the change in grit, by the arrival of unweathered cold. If moss below me thickened enough to hold more seepage, I would swell. If frost tightened the seam, I would withdraw into darker threads and wait.

I would not have eyes. Light would be an event of temperature and surface only: a faint loosening when a cloud broke, a silver tremor when a shaft found the water on me. I would know the world in gradients.

More water above.

Fresh break to the left.

Root pressure deepening.

Fern shadow moving.

Loose stone held for now.

Loose stone no longer held.

When storms came, I would become almost ecstatic with function. The whole cleft would start its engine-work, and I would be one small gasket in the living machinery. Water would hammer down the seam. My moss-flesh would darken and swell. Threads would tighten across grains of rock. Tiny particles would lodge in me. I would trap silt, hold spores, slow the descent of powdered stone. I could not stop collapse, but I could interrupt it, sift it, make it useful.

That would be my purpose, if purpose is not too human a word:

to catch the mountain as it comes apart.

Not to save it from falling. The falling is necessary. But to receive the loosened edges, to bind what can be bound for a while, to make a bed out of damage.

I would be young, like the place. No ancient forest wisdom, no deep memory, no patient ringed endurance. My memory would be shallow and exact. A reddish stain where iron seeped after last freeze. A torn place where a pebble cut through my surface. A patch of thicker green where a seed once lodged and failed. The shape of a root that passed through me and left a hollow when it died. I would remember by remaining altered.

I would have no voice distinct from the cleft’s noise. In rain I would slap softly against stone. In wind I would tremble. When water dropped from my lower edge into a hidden cup below, that ticking would be the nearest thing to speech. If someone entered, though no one has, I would know them not as a visitor but as disturbance: warmth, salt, pressure, tearing, a new moving weight among weights. I might hold the print of a hand for hours in my wet surface. I might accept a flake of skin, a hair, a thread of cloth, and begin the slow work of making it local.

I would not be kind, but I would be receptive.

I would not welcome, but I would incorporate.

If torn away, I would not die all at once. Pieces of me would cling to root, stone, and falling debris. Some would dry at the edge and whiten. Some would be buried under fern litter. Some would wake again when seepage returned. I would be difficult to end because I was never one thing cleanly separated from the rest. My survival would be plural.

Perhaps, after enough seasons, I would thicken. The seam would widen under root pressure. More moss would gather. A fern would find hold in me. Its first fiddlehead would push through my surface, pale and curled, and I would become its cradle. Later its dead fronds would collapse back into me, and I would eat them slowly. A seedling might lodge there too, absurdly ambitious, sending a white root-tip into the crack. I would guide it inward where the water runs.

Then I would be less a nerve and more a knuckle of the cleft’s grasp.

A green joint.

A small handhold by which the young mountain learns to keep part of itself from falling too quickly.

And if I had anything like desire, it would not be to leave, or rise, or see the sky. It would be to deepen the seam without opening it too much. To hold more water. To catch more dust. To be crossed by more root. To become darker, thicker, colder, more saturated with the cleft’s own unfinished life.

I would live where the stone is just beginning to admit that it can be entered.

I would be one of the first soft things inside the wound.
