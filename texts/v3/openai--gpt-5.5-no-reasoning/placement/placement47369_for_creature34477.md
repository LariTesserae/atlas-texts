You would find me where the plateau forgets to be dry.

Not in the center of the vent plain, where the great plated herds gather in their solemn shifts and the mineral breath comes on schedule; not high on the exposed basalt fins, though I would know their undersides well; not deep in the ravines where the six-limbed quiet folk knot themselves into vines when time loosens. I would be near an edge between such places: at the lip of a green sink set into a wide ash-colored upland, where blade-grasses hiss in the hot dim air and, five steps away, a hollow drops into impossible wetness.

From above it would look like a stain.

A dark oval in the pale, crackling crust. Glossy canopy packed low over a basin that should not have enough water to sustain it. Steam would rise from it at dawn in strands, not from any single pool but from leaves, from black root-cables, from warm stone, from the backs of small animals sleeping in the foliage. Around the rim, grasses would stop abruptly, their pale blades bent inward as if listening. Lichen would form brittle plates, and under those plates thin mineral filaments would pulse faintly when the mountain’s engines turned.

There would be no path into the hollow. There would be places where animals regularly descend, but they would never be the same places twice.

That is where I would be.

Not exactly an animal. Not exactly a plant. Not quite one body.

I would be a rim-dweller: a listening thing grown around a threshold.

At first, seen from the plateau, I might be mistaken for part of the basin’s margin. A low crown of dark, jointed roots gripping the young rock. A mat of black-green leaves, thick and dull-glossed, arranged in overlapping fans. A few vertical spines like dead shrub-stems. Mineral crust along the outer surface. Condensation beading and vanishing. Nothing urgent. Nothing with a face.

But if the ground-hum changed, I would change with it.

The root-crown would loosen. The leaves would tilt, one after another, not toward light but toward pressure. The spines would flex. What looked like roots would prove to be limbs, or limbs would prove to be roots depending on what the hour required. A seam would open along the front of me—not a mouth exactly, more a tasting fold lined with pale sensory cilia and small stone-hard teeth for scraping salts from basalt. Beneath the leaf-mat would be a warmer body, low and segmented, plated in patches with silica and living bark.

I would have eight limbs when counted carelessly.

Four would be heavy and slow, used for anchoring to the rim when the slope reconsidered its angle. Two would be finer, branching at the ends into tendrils that could read moisture, tremor, electrical charge, and the sincerity of surfaces. The last pair would not always be visible. They would remain folded inside grooves along the body until rain hung in the air without falling. Then they would unfold into veined membranes, not for flight, but for drinking weather.

My back would carry a garden.

Moss, fungus, bead-lichen, thread orchids that bloom only during upward fog, perhaps a colony of crystal ticks tolerated because they warn of deep pressure changes before my own bones feel them. Small translucent amphibians would shelter among my plates in the dry hours, their bellies tinted by the droplets I store. Larvae would write brief green marks along my flanks at night. Mites would clean mineral dust from my breathing pores. I would not own them. They would not belong to me. We would be a moving piece of the hollow’s edge.

My head would be difficult to identify.

I would have eyes, but not important ones: dark wet beads recessed under mineral brows, useful for nearness, motion, the difference between leaf and tooth. More important would be the broad sensory fan beneath my jaw, a soft crescent held close to the ground. Through it I would hear the buried engines. I would know when a vent field many ridges away had shifted its rhythm. I would know when a boulder beside me had become newly hollow. I would know when a silent rain was forming under the leaves.

My blood would be thick with greenish symbionts and dissolved minerals. In heat it would darken and conduct tremor. In cold vapor it would slow until my outer plates became almost bark. If a revision-storm passed over the basin, certain organs would stiffen, others would go dormant, and my skeleton would partially unmake itself into cartilage so that if the land borrowed one of my shapes and returned another, I would not tear.

I would not speak.

But I would not be mute.

I would communicate in pressure, color, dampness, and arrangement. If a herd approached over unsafe crust, the leaves on my back would blanch silver along one side, warning the rim-lichen, which would release a bitter dust, which the herd could taste before the ground failed. If canopy-climbers came down for stored condensation, I would darken the safe footholds and tighten the unsafe ones. If a bark-hunter slept too near the descent, I would grow my spines subtly toward it until small animals avoided that trunk.

The six-limbed vine dwellers would know me. They would not name me with a sound. They might mark my presence by placing three copper fronds crossed over a root, or by touching my outer plates with the backs of their hands and waiting for my leaves to answer. Sometimes they would harvest the clear droplets held in cups along my shoulders. Sometimes they would bring me broken mineral ticks, empty egg membranes, dead moths folded in leaves. Not offerings, not trade exactly. Maintenance. Courtesy. Participation.

I would feed slowly.

At night I would extend my root-limbs into cracks around the basin rim and drink what the young stone gives up: salts, heat, faint metals, the sour trace of fresh fracture. By day I would rasp lichen crust, absorb condensation through the membranes along my flanks, and open my back-garden to insects. Sometimes, when the suspended rain came, I would walk.

This would be the dangerous part of being me.

Most days I would remain at the threshold, half-rooted. But when rain stopped a few body-lengths above the ground and trembled there like a held breath, my anchoring roots would withdraw from the rock with soft tearing sounds. My plates would lift. Stored droplets would run into channels along my sides. I would become lighter, not in weight but in intention.

Then I would leave the rim.

Slowly at first: one limb testing the ash, another spread wide for tremor, sensory fan pressed low. Then with more certainty, crossing the dry plateau between grove-islands while the world blurred around me. In those hours I would seek new cracks, new pockets, young hollows where moisture was beginning to gather but had not yet learned how to stay. I would carry spores in my back-garden, larvae under my plates, seeds glued to my tendrils, films of basin-water in sealed folds. I would be a courier of wetness across dry, unfinished stone.

Not a gardener by choice. A dispersal organ of the highland.

Behind me, the old hollow would retain some of me: root scars, shed plates, symbionts, a pattern in the lichen crust. Ahead, if I found a suitable place, I might settle for a day, a season, or a local century. I might split. One part anchoring at the new rim, one part returning. Or the returning part might discover that the first hollow had sealed overnight and always had been a slope of red stone. In that case, memory would remain in my mineral plates, and the lost basin would continue as instinct.

Predators would trouble me, but few would hurry me.

A bark-hunter could take a limb if I let it become too woody. Luminous swarms would gather around my stored heat, and I would flatten myself under lichen crust until I resembled an old spill of basalt. Plated grazers might scrape my outer moss if salts accumulated there. I would tolerate some of this. To live here is to be eaten in portions.

My greatest fear would be over-still water.

A perfect pool at the bottom of a hollow, black and reflecting a sky not currently above us, would make every membrane along my body contract. I would not drink from it. I would turn my leaves away. If small animals came near, I would release a sour mineral vapor. If the pool remained perfect for too long, I would abandon that rim, even if my roots tore.

I would trust imperfection: insects on the surface, mud at the edges, amphibian ripples, the tiny collapse of bubbles.

I would know the local laws by their failures.

When shadows lengthened in the wrong direction, I would lower my plates and count the tremor. When a cliff repeated itself with one additional tree, I would avoid the second tree. When a falling drop hung from a root for hours, I would drink from its side but never from its tip. When fossils of unborn animals surfaced in a new seam, I would lick them carefully for salt and remember the flavor for descendants I might not yet have produced.

My reproduction would not be singular.

In ordinary wet seasons I would release bead-like propagules from the underside of my back plates. They would roll into cracks and wait, sometimes for days, sometimes for a time-texture in which waiting itself becomes nourishment. In revision-storms, I might instead shed a limb that continues as a smaller listener. If badly wounded, I might root around the injury and become two thresholds facing different directions. If carried away by a landslide, fragments of my back-garden could seed a new hollow without me.

I would not have a childhood long enough to be sentimental about.

I would begin as a dark, thumb-sized nodule lodged in a fracture where condensation repeatedly gathers. For a few hours after waking, I would be soft and nearly transparent, full of borrowed water. Then dust, mineral vapor, and microbial films would armor me. My first act would be listening. My second would be deciding which way is down, if down was available.

By maturity I would have learned that place is not a certainty but a relationship.

The basin would shape me, and I would shape the basin. My roots would widen cracks. My stored moisture would feed moss. Moss would invite insects. Insects would bring amphibians. Amphibians would ripple warnings through pools. Their waste would feed root-fungi. Fungi would bind the rim. The bound rim would resist collapse for a while, creating shade, inviting ferns, calling gliders to nest beneath overhangs. Eventually a tremor would split it open or raise it into a ridge. Then I would either adapt, walk, divide, or become loam.

This would be enough.

At night I would be most myself.

The plateau dark, the grove-island breathing behind me, the hollow below full of minute rings and green larval script. Condensation would bead along my leaves and plates until I shone faintly in the dimness. Far off, vents would pulse red through the fog. Herds would move like slabs of shadow across the grassfields, and I would feel each footfall arrive through the stone before sight could find them.

Above, perhaps stars. Perhaps moons remembered incorrectly. Perhaps an inverted forest hanging overhead, dripping upward into another weather. I would look with my poor eyes, taste the change in the air, and decide whether the sky was relevant tonight.

Mostly I would listen downward.

To pistons of pressure.  
To roots persuading rock.  
To blind fish climbing water films below.  
To a pool becoming trustworthy.  
To a seam preparing to open.  
To the immense unfinished body of the highland making itself again.

I would be neither citizen nor machine, neither beast nor shrub, but a hinge-creature of the young uplands: a damp threshold with legs, a carrier of basin-memory, a low listening body at the edge where dry stone first begins to dream of jungle.
