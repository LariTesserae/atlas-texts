I would find myself somewhere the basin almost hides from itself: not in the open center, not at the old shelter wall, not among the cairns, but in one of the narrow rooms where the mountain’s self-made architecture becomes most ambiguous.

A chamber between leaning slabs.

To reach it, one has to turn sideways through a corridor of blue-gray stone leaves, shoulder brushing mineral cold on both sides. The passage is too narrow for comfort and too deliberate-looking to feel accidental, though it is only frost and bedding planes and patience. At the end, the walls open into a small roofless space no larger than a sleeping place. Pale gravel underfoot. One black slab tilted overhead. A strip of sky visible like a blade drawn from its sheath.

There is a melt-cup there, but a poor one—shallow, often dry, rimmed with white crust. Above it runs a vein of translucent mineral through dark rock. At noon, only for a few minutes, the vein glows. Not brightly. Not enough to illuminate the chamber. Just enough that the crack seems to remember fire.

That is where I would be.

Not the basin-being itself. Not the whole distributed patience. I would be one of its smaller conditions, one of its local habits that has become almost separate by repetition.

I would be the pause before a drop falls.

Or more exactly: I would be the little interval of held water beneath the black slab, where condensation gathers into a bead, swells, trembles, reflects the sky upside down, and waits until weight overcomes adhesion.

For most of the day I would not be visible.

I would begin as cold on stone. A slightly different darkness along an underside. A minute silvering where breathless air touches mineral. I would collect out of almost nothing: vapor, frost, loosened ice, the memory of last night’s freeze. Grain by grain, molecule by molecule, I would gather myself at the lip of the slab.

I would have no body except surface tension.

No thought except nearing.

The basin-being would know me as one pulse among many. Not important, but exact. I would be part of its grammar: here moisture gathers; here noon loosens; here the cup receives or fails to receive. My existence would depend on angle, temperature, dust, sun, and the old tilt of the stone. If the slab shifted by the width of a fingernail, I might no longer form. If mineral crust thickened, I might cling longer. If a human hand warmed the rock, I might fall early.

I would be exquisitely conditional.

In the morning I would be ice.

A clear tooth under the slab, one of several, small enough that a breath could have remade me if breath came here. I would hang with the others in blue shade, holding last night inside me. I would not dream exactly, but I would contain pressure. Above me the black stone would contract and release its faint pings. Around me frost would lace the seam. The cup below would be sealed under skin-ice, blind and waiting.

Then light would enter the chamber indirectly, first as brightness on the far wall, then as a hard white reflection from the gravel. The air would sharpen. The edge of me would soften. I would feel myself losing shape and becoming more mobile, more dangerous to myself.

By late morning I would be a bead.

This would be my hour of awareness.

Everything would be inverted in me: sky below, stone above, the chamber rounded into a trembling world. I would hold the basin in miniature and upside down. The leaning slab, the pale gravel, a white thread of old snow beyond the corridor, perhaps the dark interruption of a cairn far off if the angle allowed it—all curved inside me.

I would know nothing by names. I would know only distortion and tension.

The whole world would pull on me.

Stone would hold me back. Gravity would ask. Cold would stiffen me. Sun would loosen me. Air would take from my surface invisibly. The basin-being would attend through me, because I would be one of the places where difference becomes event.

For a long while I would only lengthen.

That would be my life: the almost-falling.

If a human entered the little chamber during that hour, I would become enormous with their arrival. Their heat would change the underside of the slab. Their breath would roughen the frost. Their shadow might pass across the glowing vein and dim the minute fire within the rock. I would swell faster. I might fall because of them.

They would perhaps hear me strike the cup and think: a drip.

But to me it would be a complete migration.

Release.

A brief clear descent.

Contact.

A sound so small it could vanish under wind, yet in that chamber it would count as speech.

Tick.

Then I would cease to be singular. I would become the shallow cup’s skin, a trembling widening circle, a slight darkening of stone. If the cup held water already, I would join it. If it was dry, I would spread into mineral dust, vanish into a seam, leave behind only a darker grain, a future white crust, a microscopic alteration in the basin’s memory.

I would not mourn my loss of shape.

Falling would be my purpose, but not my end. I would enter the larger patience. Some part of me might freeze that evening into a cloudy plate of ice. Some might sink into grit and sleep until thaw. Some might rise again as vapor when the basin steams faintly after rain. Some might help carry salt to the rim of the cup, making the white ring fractionally thicker.

I would be small enough to disappear and recurrent enough to matter.

That is who I would be in this world: not a creature with a face, not the ancient mind of the whole basin, but one of its syllables.

A held drop under a black stone lip.

A temporary eye that sees everything upside down.

A moment of weight becoming sound.
