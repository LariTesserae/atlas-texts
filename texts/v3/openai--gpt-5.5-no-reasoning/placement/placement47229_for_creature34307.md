I would not be one of the workers first, though at a distance someone might mistake me for one.

I would be found low even for that low place: not in the compressor courts, not in the sodium-lit bunk galleries, not among the broad service roads where haulers pass with their white eyes and patient whining, but beyond the last regularly repainted rail, where the official walkways begin to narrow into afterthoughts and the paint on the hazard stripes has been eaten back to primer. Past Basin Nine, past the spill flats where the west purge leaves a combing of bolts and moth bodies, past a row of viewpoints filmed over with mineral bloom. Down a stair that was temporary when installed and has since become more permanent than many planned things.

At the marsh-edge.

More precisely: under the third embankment road where it crosses the unfinished basin, in the cavity between its bolted plate deck and the older slab beneath it. There is a crawlspace there that was not intended as a room. A person has to enter sideways through a gap behind a fallen shutter panel, then descend by handholds cut into a concrete lip, then cross two narrow beams slick with condensation. The first beam is safe. The second flexes and sings. Everyone knows this, even if no map says it.

Below the beams is black water, rarely deeper than the thigh but cold enough to make the bones in the feet ache. Rebar comes up through it in rows, rust-black and mineral-white. Survey flags still cling to some rods, colorless now, rattling whenever the draft moves across the basin. Lamps hang far apart, and their light does not illuminate so much as reveal separate errors: a coil of cable, a sunken ladder, the pale underside of insulation foam, the circular mouth of a drain breathing fog.

That is where I would be.

Not hiding exactly. Not officially stationed. Present in the way some things are present down there: known by evidence, by avoided routes, by marks that have to be interpreted.

I would be a listener.

Not a valve listener, not by title. Those exist: people with assigned rounds, calibrated rods, sensor tablets, approved gloves. They press instruments to pipework and decide whether a bearing is failing or a pressure pulse has changed character. They submit reports. They are necessary and sane.

I would have begun as one of them, perhaps.

A surface-hired apprentice first, too tall for the low gantries, too quick to look upward into the fog, too amazed by the scale of indoor rain to keep my mouth closed. Someone who came down for wages and intended to leave when the contract ended. Someone who learned the timings because not learning them could kill you: forty-seven minutes to purge; six hours to rain; compressor sleep by vibration, not clock; eastern channels reversing by a shine in the water; late-sound rooms by the way speech makes the skin between the shoulders tighten.

I would have been assigned to acoustic maintenance, carrying a case of listening heads, chalk, sealant tape, and a long black contact rod that froze to the glove if held too long. I would have learned to hear cavitation as gravel in a sealed throat, steam leak as a private whisper, bearing wear as a fly trapped behind iron. I would have learned that machinery does not make one sound but many: the sound it intends, the sound it suffers, the sound it hides until failure makes it plain.

Then something would have happened. Nothing dramatic enough for a legend. No great collapse. No forbidden chamber opening. The under-region does not need flamboyant events. It changes a person through repetition, by making certain attentions useful until they become permanent.

Maybe I spent too many shifts in the rooms where sound arrives late.

Maybe I was sent after a missing swallow near the marsh-edge and waited through roof-rain under the embankment, listening to the whole basin answer drop by drop.

Maybe an old pump in the Archive line began to knock in a pattern no instrument marked as dangerous, and I alone insisted it was calling through three decks of flooded structure to a gate that had not opened in years.

Maybe I was right.

Worse: maybe I was useful.

After that, people would send for me when readings contradicted one another, or when alarms repeated with no source, or when a purge arrived thin, or heavy, or somehow offended. They would say my name over the radio in the careful voice used for nuisances who are also necessary. I would arrive late, lamp dim, coat stiff with dried condensation, carrying fewer tools than I once did. I would kneel where water moved across concrete, put my bare fingertips near the flow but not in it, and listen.

Not with magic. Not exactly.

With the body altered by years of cold and noise. With the feet knowing vibration through boot soles. With teeth that ache differently near high pressure. With an ear trained past language into timing. With a memory full of machines no longer on any active plan.

There are maps in that place, but the maps are always behind. The true map is cyclic. It is made of intervals: how long after the roof-rain the frost returns to Rail D; how many purges before sediment reaches the third grate; which engine coughs once before the red lamps begin; how the dogs lift their heads when a hidden heat line wakes. I would know some of those relations. Not all. No one knows all. But enough that people would half trust me and half resent that they had to.

The unofficial inhabitants would know me better than the crews did.

They would not call me by my roster name. They might call me Sump-Ear, or Third-Bridge, or the Man Under the Road, though I might not always be a man by the time the name settled. Down there, identity wears away at its edges. Clothes are patched from departments one never served. Hair is cut with utility shears. The face becomes a pale working surface: reddened eyelids, cracked lips, a lamp scar across the brow, skin always smelling faintly of wet metal no matter how often it is washed.

I would trade in warnings.

Not copper, not food, not warmth, though I would sometimes receive those. A cup of broth in a relay shrine. A battery wrapped in cloth. A strip of dry insulation to line a boot. But mostly I would give and receive warnings.

Do not use Stair K-14 after second rain; the bolts have started speaking separately from the plate.

The water at the old nursery heat exchange is sweet today but will turn after compressor sleep.

A flock of swallows went silent above Children’s Heat.

The west sump purge will drag low this cycle; keep the dogs off the flats.

One of the viewpoints has begun to show the same weather twice.

I would write little of this digitally. Digital records go upward. Chalk stays where it is needed. I would mark beams in a private grammar: a line for cold under pressure, a crescent for delayed sound, three dots for a listening place, a square crossed cornerwise where a machine has begun to behave as if attended.

The workers would pretend not to read these marks until they needed them. The route hermits would add to them. Children would copy them in games and get scolded for knowing too much.

My home—if that word can be used—would be under the third embankment road.

It would have been made out of leftover systems. A pallet platform raised above the high-water stain. A yellow shelter door used as a wall. A curtain of hanging gasket strips to slow the draft. Old thermal blankets stitched with wire. A kettle powered by an illegal induction tap that makes lights dim two corridors away. A shelf of listening rods, cracked sensor heads, dead swallow wings, jars of clear condensate, jars of unsafe condensate labeled with dates, a child’s drawing of roof-rain falling from a ceiling of pipes.

There would be moss on one wall, deliberately preserved.

A narrow black script following a seam where the condensation repeats every forty-seven minutes plus the lag of the basin air. I would not scrape it. I would read it. Its thickness would tell me when the purge was sediment-heavy. Its gloss would tell me whether heat was moving behind the slab. In ultraviolet it might show a second pattern underneath, lichen beginning where no heat leak is marked.

A maintenance dog would visit sometimes.

Old, perhaps retired without paperwork. One of the partly prosthetic ones, with heated paw guards and clouded eyes, its locator still blinking though no one tracks the signal. It would know the safe beam and distrust the singing one. It would sleep with its nose toward the water and wake before I did when a valve closed wrong three districts away.

I would not own it. Nothing in that place is owned simply. We would share warmth.

The swallows would come lower around my shelter than they do elsewhere. Not because they loved me, but because I replaced one of their obsolete charging strips with a live one scavenged from a defunct inspection rail. They would cling upside down beneath the embankment, folded into themselves, ticking softly as frost gathered along their wing joints. In certain hours they would all wake at once and flutter out over the basin, their reflections below them making it seem as if two flocks crossed through each other, one metal, one drowned.

I might repair them badly.

A brush replaced with hair from a broken cleaning unit. A wing hinge wired too loose. A lens polished with sleeve cloth. Enough to keep them flying, not enough to return them to official service. Some would come back with mineral samples in their probes, some with rust, one perhaps with a shred of blue paper from a district no map admits still exists.

I would keep those fragments.

I would become an archive, but not a clerk.

The under-region already has Archive Pumps, and perhaps archive pump clerks, and cabinets of damp logs swelling in their drawers. My archive would be oral, bodily, unreliable in the way living knowledge is unreliable. I would know the sound of obsolete machinery still coupled to active drains. I would know which alarms are old ghosts and which ghosts are alarms. I would know the names of dogs dead twenty years whose paw routes still describe the safe paths through certain flooded rooms. I would remember workers by the cups they left behind.

People would come to me before entering the marsh-edge.

A registered crew in cleanish insulated coats, visors scratched, trying to look amused.

“Is the third deck passable?”

I would ask, “Before or after rain?”

“Before.”

“For you, no.”

“For the haulers?”

“For the haulers, yes. For you walking behind them, no.”

They would argue. They would cite schedules. Somewhere above us the presses would fall with the authority of weather. The dog would raise its head. I would point to the waterline on a half-submerged door and to a fringe of black moss where none had been the week before.

One of them would believe me. That would be enough.

At other times the unofficial ones would come: a girl in tarp vinyl carrying a broken pressure gauge; an old salvage woman with copper under her coat; two brothers smelling of battery acid; a child who knows too well how to be quiet. They would not ask directly. They would sit on the safe beam and tell me what they had seen.

Water uphill in the east channels, but warm.

Moths clustered on a dead panel.

A viewpoint showing a shore with lamps on it.

Children’s Heat breathing again.

At that, perhaps, I would go still.

Because Children’s Heat would be my unchosen obsession.

Everyone down there has one. A machine, a route, a sealed door, a name that remains after its explanation has failed. Mine would be Children’s Heat. Not because I know what it is, but because I have heard it.

Not continuously. Not even often. But in the coldest part of some cycles, when the roof-rain has passed and the frost is returning in needles, there would be a warmth beneath the general sound. Not thermal warmth at first—audible warmth. A low expansion in old pipes. A soft ticking not of cooling metal but of radiators beginning to receive flow. Far off, through slabs and water and late rooms, something like a school waking before dawn.

No voices. I would never claim voices.

Only heat moving where heat is not scheduled to move.

The official records might say Children’s Heat is a decommissioned exchange. A reclaimed line. A mistranslated label from an older contractor. A waste loop from a demolished surface hospital. The crews joke until they pass the arrows. The unregistered leave scraps of bright tape on the signposts. The dogs sometimes refuse the corridors leading that way.

I would have gone looking once.

Not all the way. No one goes all the way and remains useful. I would have followed a swallow through a maintenance slot during compressor sleep, crawling past lagged pipes soft with rot, past lichens glowing faintly under my lamp, past a window set into a wall where no outside could be. Behind the window: gray rain slanting across what looked like water, and beyond it a dark line that may have been a shore or a reflection of conduits.

I would have reached a door warm around its edges.

On it, three signs layered over one another: CHILDREN’S HEAT; NORTH AUXILIARY THERMAL; DO NOT BLEED LINE WHILE OCCUPIED.

Occupied.

No current plan would explain that word.

From behind the door would come a sound I could not sort: fluid, metal, distance, perhaps only the delayed return of my own breathing through the late-sound chambers. I would put my listening rod to the door and hear, not voices, but small intervals. Heat ticks. Pipe knocks. A sequence like counting.

I would not open it.

That would be why I survived, or why I failed.

Since then I would listen for it. I would mark its rumors. I would know which moss blooms when it breathes, which rats migrate toward its warmth, which moths dust themselves dead against panels on the route. If asked what lives behind that door, I would say: “A system.” If asked again, by a child, I might say: “Something keeping warm.”

And if asked where I belong, I would not say behind it. I would say outside it, in the cold, listening.

Because that is what I would be in this world: not a ruler, not a monster, not a native species exactly, but a threshold creature of maintenance and superstition.

A human being made narrow enough to fit among systems.

A citizen of the artificial climate.

One of those lives permitted under conditions.

My hands would be damaged by cold. My hearing would be too precise and not trustworthy in ordinary quiet. Aboveground, I would be nearly useless. Sky would make me nauseous. Sunlight would flatten everything, revealing too much at once and nothing in sequence. I would miss distance measured by lamp-fade. I would miss the way a sound arrives long before its source can be seen. I would miss the honesty of condensation: water declaring where the world has failed to seal itself.

Below, I would have purpose, though not employment.

I would wake before purges because the basin changes pressure in sleep. I would drink condensate boiled twice. I would keep a chalk stub in every pocket. I would know the safe rails by touch and the dangerous ones by the way frost feathers outward from their bolts. I would distrust silence more than noise. I would stand under manufactured rain and feel, for a few minutes, that the place had forgiven itself for being made.

If someone glimpsed me from a passing hauler, they might see only a figure beneath the embankment road: hood up, lamp low, one gloved hand resting on a pipe, a dog beside me, metal swallows shifting overhead like folded knives. They might think I was waiting for a crew.

I would be listening to the under-region breathe.

Listening for the west sumps drawing too hard.

Listening for water deciding on a new path.

Listening for machines carrying out obsolete instructions with perfect devotion.

Listening for Children’s Heat.

And sometimes, when roof-rain began in the high fog and moved across the constructed marsh in silver impacts, I would step out from under the road and stand in it. Not for warmth. There is little warmth in that rain. But because every drop striking metal gives the hidden architecture a voice. For those minutes, the whole low world becomes legible by sound: beams, plates, conduits, rails, ducts, cranes vanishing upward, unfinished decks vanishing outward, water making a landscape out of machinery one tick at a time.

I would stand there until my coat darkened and the dog retreated and the swallows lifted, startled, into their cable-dark.

A person, yes.

But also a kind of instrument.

A damp mark in the system.

A local superstition with a pulse.

A listener at the marsh-edge, keeping company with frost, moss, dogs, drones, and unfinished water, in the low artificial country beneath the remembered surface of the world.
