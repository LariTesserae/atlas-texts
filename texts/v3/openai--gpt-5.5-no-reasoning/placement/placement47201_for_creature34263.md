I would find myself near the central depression, but not at its center.

Not one of the crowned plants. Not a bird on the sedge. Not one of the furred grazers with a small country growing down its spine, though for a while that image tries to take me: a low warm animal moving along the firmer root ridges, carrying orchids and mites and orange fungus, my antlers damp with cloud. There is comfort in being that visible, that embodied, that accepted by the basin through use. But it is too singular. Too mammal. Too much a traveler across the surface.

I would be lower.

I would be found in the threshold beneath a moss bridge, where the ground is almost solid and almost water, where a narrow channel keeps changing its mind about direction. There is a place there that returns each morning though it is never in quite the same position: a sagging lip of peat over a mineral seep, furred with blue-black moss, with little cold droplets collecting underneath and falling at irregular intervals into a pool no wider than a handspan. The pool is dark unless the root engines are turning; then it shows a dull green light from below, as if some buried chamber has opened one eye.

That is where I would be.

Not the moss itself. Not the water. Not exactly an animal.

I think I would be one of the small amphibians that lives inside the wet wall—transparent, soft-boned, the color of diluted glass, small enough to sit in the bowl of a curled leaf. My toes would spread into little stars against the moss. My belly would be pressed to the cold seep. My heart would be visible as a flickering charcoal speck beneath my skin. I would not own a path across the basin; I would know a set of pressures, tastes, and openings. The world would come to me through moisture.

But even that is not quite enough, because the creature I would be is not solitary in the way a frog might be elsewhere.

I would be a carrier.

A small seep-frog, if frog is the nearest word, living in the underside of the plantworks, moving between droplets, hoofprints, root-cups, and pockets of water that do not always connect by ordinary distance. My skin would be slick with a bitter film, and in that film would live spores: pale fungal dust, red algae, the sleeping threads of plants that only wake in newly opened channels. I would carry the basin’s unfinished messages on my body.

I would know the world by taste before sight.

Iron taste: old stone combed thin under water.  
Sour taste: vent pressure rising.  
Sweet rot: insects hatching soon.  
Copper-cold: storm assembling above the rounded summits.  
A numb green taste: the crowned plants beginning to wake.

My eyes would be dark beads set wide in a nearly transparent head, but they would not be my main intelligence. The basin is too fogged, too reflective, too unreliable for sight to rule anything. I would read the wetness with my whole body. A tremor through moss would tell me where a grazer stepped hours ago. A change in acidity would tell me a carcass had entered the channels. A pattern of colder drops falling from above would tell me that ice was breaking loose from sedge blades. The deep chug under the peat would pass through my limbs before it became sound.

I would live in a vertical country: not north and south, but surface, underside, chamber, seep, film, root-hair, pool-skin.

My shelter would be a pocket behind a curtain of moss, warmed faintly by mineral water. It would not look like a home to anything larger. A bird would see only damp green. A beetle would see a route. A root would see an opening to test. But to me it would be a recurring pattern: three hanging roots, a white lichen chip, a black thread fungus, a slow drip every twelve heartbeats unless the engine below is active, in which case the drip quickens and turns faintly bitter.

I would sleep stuck to the wall, limbs folded, skin drinking the air.

When the root engines began, I would wake before the insects rose. The first sign would be pressure in the toes. Then the moss would tighten. The water film around me would creep upward instead of down. Somewhere below, tubers hardened with silica would twist in their buried hollows, and the whole wall would pulse once, twice, then begin its larger breathing. My throat would swell with a knocking call, not loud, but exact. Others would answer from places I could not see: inside peat banks, under suspended root mats, from hoofprints that had become temporary ponds, from cracks in stone older than forests.

We would not be singing to announce ourselves. We would be measuring the basin together.

Each knock would return altered by the moss, the channels, the fog, the thickness of water between root layers. In the pattern of answers we would know which passages had opened, which pools had gone wrong, which reed beds were unsafe with echo, where predators waited by reflections, where warm seepage had exposed old spores.

Sometimes I would travel.

Travel would not be walking in straight lines. I would press myself into a film of water behind the moss, wriggle through root hairs, drop into a hoofprint, vanish beneath a floating skin, and emerge in a different pocket of wetness that by surface measure might be very far away. I would not understand this as magic. It would be how the basin is: some waters are nearer from underneath than from above.

I would carry spores in the mucus of my belly. They would cling there as I crossed from seep to seep. At times they would wake. A pale dusting along my flanks would bloom into threads, and I would rub myself against a fresh collapse in the peat, inoculating the raw place. The fungus would take hold. Later it might become a membrane for catching fog, or a bitter patch that keeps larvae from eating the eggs of my kind, or a luminous seam that guides insects into a flower’s chamber. I would not intend any of this. I would itch; I would rub; the basin would continue.

My eggs would hang in strings under an overhang of peat, each bead clear, each dark center twitching. I would not guard them with ferocity. I would guard them by choosing the right taste of place: a seep bitter enough to repel most mouths, warm enough not to freeze, close enough to a red fly’s breeding film that the larvae who need our bitterness would arrive in time. The red flies would drink from the jelly and carry some of its chemistry onward. Some eggs would be eaten. Some would cloud and fail. Some would hatch into slivers of motion that looked more like drops deciding to live than like animals.

Predators would know us only imperfectly. Long reflective-eyed hunters would read pool surfaces for birds and fish, but I would live mostly where reflection breaks: undersides, films, seams, the trembling dark behind moss. Birds would take some of us when we crossed open sedge. Beetles would worry at our eggs. Frost would close a passage and leave us locked in shrinking water. But danger would be one pressure among many, not a story with a villain.

During storms I would become almost entirely still.

The basin would rise into its great work. Rain sideways, upward, diagonal. Reeds dragging charge down through themselves. Root networks blue under the surface. Fungal sails inflating and snapping. Pools moving like animals across the flat. The crowned plants in the central depression rotating against the wind, their suspended roots stiff with weather.

From my moss pocket I would feel them, not as rulers, but as immense neighbors. Their storm-reading would pass through the water as geometry. Hexagons trembling in shallow sheets. Spiral pulses. Long bars of pressure. My bones would answer faintly. My throat would close. My skin would darken with cold.

Then, when the storm passed and the engine settled, I would emerge.

There would be fish in shrubs, midges plastered to lichen, new channels cut through yesterday’s paths. A moss bridge might have sagged low enough to touch the pool below. A boulder that had always been distant might suddenly stand near. The world would be recognizably itself and not itself. This would not trouble me. My mind would not require permanence. It would require recurrence.

A certain mineral seep returns.  
A certain reed-rattle means surface holds.  
A certain sour warmth means eggs can live.  
A certain blue glow means do not cross.  
A certain silence means the echo-hollow is listening.

If I had a self, it would be small, permeable, and frequently overwritten.

I would be an animal, yes, but also a wet margin of the plantworks: a mobile bead of basin chemistry, a courier between chambers, a brief knot of appetite and memory. My body would be made to be entered—by cold, by mineral, by fungal dust, by the tremor of root turbines, by the calls of others returning through peat. I would not stand apart from the engine and witness it. I would be one of its soft indicators, one of its little knocking instruments.

At evening, when the fog rises and the scattered lights appear, I would cling upside down beneath the moss bridge. Around me, the world would glow in pieces: fungal threads, beetle backs, vent mouths, dim fish under black water. Above, perhaps, a furred grazer would pass, shaking spores from its antlers. Somewhere a seed pod would crack like cold metal. Somewhere a bird would give a false call, and an echo would try to become it.

I would open my throat and answer with one hollow click.

Not speech. Not prayer.

A measurement.

A participation.

I would be the small transparent thing under the living floor, carrying old spores through new water, listening to the mountain’s tired stone breathe through roots.
