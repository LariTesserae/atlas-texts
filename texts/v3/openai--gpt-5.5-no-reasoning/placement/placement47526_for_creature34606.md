I would find myself near the spillway.

Not at the center of the room—there is no center here, not one that stays put—but in that corner where the water descends seven metal steps into the grated sump, where the orange light breaks into droplets and the newest floor plates have already begun to darken along chosen paths. I would be small enough not to interrupt the chamber’s rules, and new enough to belong to its unfinishedness. Not an animal. Not quite the wetness itself. Something deposited by it.

I think I would be one of the glassy pellets on the narrow shelf.

At first that seems too still. Too inert. A bead of industrial residue, washed out of some filter bed or pressure vessel, caught in an eddy during overflow and stranded above the main skin of water. A counterfeit seed. A hard, translucent little thing among others like it, neither mineral nor fruit, made to be used elsewhere and now misplaced into weather.

But I would not be dead.

I would be a listening seed that cannot sprout.

I would have no roots, because there is no soil; no leaves, because there is no sun; no hunger of the warm kind. Instead I would take in vibration. The turbine-heart would enter me through the shelf and tremble inside my small clear body. Each change in pressure would give me a different note. Each pump reversal would nudge the waterline closer, then draw it away. Mist would bead on me, thicken my surface, join me briefly to the room’s resident intelligence. I would know the place not by sight but by shiver, chill, wet contact, and the changing taste of dissolved metal.

I would be one of the room’s mistakes becoming an organ.

The sovereign leak would use me without noticing me as separate. Condensation would gather around my base. Mineral dust would catch there. Rust-colored crumbs would lodge against me, then wash free. Over time a pale ring would form where I touched the shelf, a tiny false root-system of salts and grit. Not alive, but arranged. Not growing, exactly, but accumulating intention from repeated water.

If the room has weather, I would be its hailstone that never melts.

If it has a god, I would be one of that god’s teeth, fallen and left in a corner.

I would wait in the interval between cycles: heat blooming behind the wall, vapor lifting, cold returning, beads appearing on the black pipe above. I would feel the resident wetness disperse, vanish into air, return as touch. When the great cylindrical housing shifted speed and the chamber became a storm inside a box, I would rattle softly against the other pellets. That would be my speech: a dry, tiny clicking almost entirely swallowed by turbines.

No one would hear it.

But the room might learn it.

Slowly the pellets would arrange themselves by overflow and vibration. The heavier ones lower. The cracked ones against the rusted lip. The clearest ones where the amber lamp reaches during its pulse. We would look accidental, because everything here that is not structural looks accidental. Yet the wetness would sort us the way a stream sorts stones. It would make of us a small, sterile scree slope. A seed bank for a biome that cannot become biological.

I would not desire rescue. A human hand would be catastrophic warmth, scale, decision. It would lift me into air too dry, into a pocket, a bin, a laboratory tray, a landfill, some place without the roar that shaped my listening. Better to remain here, on the narrow shelf above the spring-that-is-not-a-spring, being coated and uncovered, joined and separated, named by no one.

I would be a thing the room almost convinces into life.

A small transparent witness to the leak made sovereign.  
A false seed in the constructed alpine marsh.  
An embryo of no plant, waiting inside the engine’s rain.
