I would find myself where the plateau is neither open nor sheltered, at one of the compromise places: not the vent-mouth itself, too warm and contested, not the bare stone shelf, too exposed, but in the belt around a vent-field where the moss has grown deep enough to remember many seasons of breath.

There would be a basin there, shallow and dark, cupped between ribs of old polygoned rock. Above it the kelp-fronds would stand close together, shoulder-high to something larger than me, towering to me like a drowned forest. Their ribbed trunks would flex in the wind without sound. Moisture would run down them in thin, persistent lines, and the moss below would be built up into green-black banks, some hollow, some treacherous, some warm underneath from the mountain’s buried exhalation.

I would live inside those banks.

Not as one of the fox-things, though for a while I might imagine that: low to the ground, whiskered, patient, feeling the mountain through my paws. And not as a raptor, though the thought of reading vapor from above is tempting — the basin opening beneath me like a dim instrument, every hare-body a pressure mark, every vent a slow red eye. But those lives are too declarative. They cut through the plateau. They arrive as decisions.

I think I would be something smaller, more interval-bound.

I would be a pale amphibian, or something nearly one: a cold, lidless thing of wet stone and moss-shadow, living most of my life below the visible skin of the world.

My body would be narrow and soft, not fragile exactly, but dependent on exactness. My skin would be white with a faint bluish translucence, thin enough that the dark minerals of my last feeding might show through my belly. My limbs would splay delicately. My toes would spread into little pads and threads, able to hold wet rock, lichen scale, kelp-root, the underside of a stone. I would have no voice. My mouth would open only to feed, drink, taste, and breathe against surfaces.

My eyes would not close. There would be no need for closing them. Sight would be a secondary sense here, an occasional instrument for reading pale veins in fronds, the dim green bruise of compressed lichen, the passing shadow of a moth. Mostly I would know the world through contact: through the pressure of moss swelling above me, through mineral taste, through the faint tremor that announces warmth before warmth arrives.

My home would be a chamber under a slanted stone, roofed by rock, walled by moss-rhizoids and fungal threads. It would not be built by me alone. Nothing in that place belongs entirely to one creature. The stone would have been split by old frost. The moss would have lifted and sealed it. A blind burrower might once have pushed through and left a half-collapsed passage. Beetles would use the upper seam. Silver larvae would tremble in the water film beneath. I would be one occupant in a layered house made by cold, growth, pressure, and accident.

Most of the time I would wait.

Waiting would not feel empty. It would be the basic posture of intelligence. I would press my belly to the underside of the stone and know what the mountain was doing.

A deep pulse: not yet.

A slight pressure fall: soon.

Moisture collecting along the root-hairs: soon.

The moss loosening above me, expanding by fractions: now.

Then I would emerge.

Not alone. Never alone, though the open surface would have seemed barren minutes before. Around me, from moss lips and stone seams and condensation gutters, others would appear: pale heads, thin limbs, milk eyes, each body shining with gathered wet. We would not greet one another. We would not need to. Our paths would braid and part across the rock, each of us following taste, warmth, trace.

The world outside would be enormous.

A stone face that to a larger animal is merely a step would be for me a whole slope, black and slick, ribbed with lichen, crossed by rivulets no wider than a hair. The kelp-fronds would rise above as a dark forest, bending all together when the katabatic wind came down. Droplets would fall from their translucent edges with the weight of weather. A single bead of water could strike beside me and tremble through my limbs like an event.

I would feed on films: mineral bloom, algal slick, the invisible life grown where vapor meets stone. I would take larvae when I found them, silver and soft in their water skins. I would scrape the glowing lichen when breath-warmth had loosened it. If a curled glass fungus softened in the vent exhalation, I might climb its underside and lick the condensed minerals from its smoky ribs.

Danger would arrive mostly as disturbance.

The hare-browsers would be mountains that move suddenly: furred bodies appearing at the edge of my world, muzzles splitting and working through black grass, paws compressing the moss roof over hidden chambers. Their danger would be accidental. A hoof, a scrape, a collapse.

The fox-things would be worse. They would pass almost without sound, but the moss would know them. The air would tighten around their whiskers. Their paws would choose the softest places. If one lowered its muzzle near my stone, I would become flatness. I would become the underside of the world. No breath, no movement, only wet skin against cold rock and the hope that my chemical trace had been thinned by the condensation hour.

The raptor would be a change in the mist.

Not a cry. Not even a wingbeat. Just a drawing upward of attention, a deforming of vapor, and then perhaps one of us gone from the stone. For a long time afterward the rest would remain motionless, pale flecks on black rock, until the mountain’s pulse told us the interval had changed.

I would lay my eggs in a moss chamber warmed by regular vent-breath. I would not think of them as future in any grand way. They would be translucent beads under stone, each one a wager placed with moisture. Some would cloud over and fail. Some would be taken by beetles. Some would be found by blind burrowers. Some would open into thread-limbed young who would know the tremor before they knew light.

My memory would be a map of wetness.

Not a map like a bird’s, made of ridges and basins, nor like a matriarch herbivore’s, made of crossings and old warm fields. Mine would be close, tactile, chemical. This stone sweats early. This lichen loosens after the second pulse. This moss collapses under weight. This gutter freezes from the rim inward. This vent-breath is weak but reliable. This passage smells of fox. This root-wall leads to a deeper chamber where dry hours can be survived.

I would know the buried mechanism not as mystery, but as weather below.

The low pressure in the teeth, though I would have no teeth worth mentioning. The slow turn in the stone. The great almost-sound. To larger minds it might suggest machinery, industry, some vast hidden works. To me it would be simpler and more intimate: the timing organ of the world. When it turns, water moves. When it rests, skin tightens. When it exhales, food wakes. When it shudders, predators hunt and prey scatter and moss drinks and eggs endure.

Perhaps, over time, I would grow old for such a small wet thing. My skin would become less translucent, more mineral-stained. One limb might heal crooked after a near miss with a beetle mandible or a hare hoof. I would emerge less far from the chamber. I would choose known slicks over richer unknown ones. My movements would become extremely economical, almost geological in their patience.

And eventually I would misread an interval.

All lives there must misread one.

The condensation might withdraw sooner than expected. A pressure drop might bring a freeze instead of vapor. A fox might wait through the pulse rather than strike within it. The moss roof might slump under a blind burrower’s body. Or I might simply remain too long on open stone, feeding from a seam of mineral film while the air thins and dries around me.

Then I would slow.

My skin would lose its shine. My limbs would draw close. I might crawl halfway beneath a lichen-plated lip and go no farther.

But even then I would not disappear quickly.

The plateau does not waste its dead. Beetles would find me first, perhaps, touching my skin with hooked feet. Fungal threads would enter. Moss would lift around me. A blind burrower might take what remained, or mineral vapor might stiffen my bones into the green-black bank. My small body would become dampness held a little longer, salts passed sideways, memory without mind.

If there is a self suited to that place, it is not a conquering self. It is not even a solitary self. It is a brief wet awareness moving through a larger intelligence made of timing.

So I would be one of the white amphibians under the moss near a weak vent, lidless and silent, living by the hour when the plateau sweats.

I would know the world from below its skin.

I would wait through the dry dark.

I would feel the mountain turn.

And when the stone began to breathe, I would come out with all the others, pale as roots, exact as condensation, crossing the black rock before the cold took back the surface.
