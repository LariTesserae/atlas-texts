You would find me near one of the repeating ravines, but not at its lip.

Not at first.

At first I would seem to be part of the warning system: a low arrangement of pale stones set back from the abyss, half-cairn, half-seat, surrounded by cords gone stiff with mineral dust. A traveler might mistake me for something made by hands and abandoned there—one more instrument left beside the unfinished edge, one more attempt to persuade the ground to stay singular.

I would be small by the scale of the plateau. Not one of the great spire gardens, not a crown-tree lifting its frost-antlers into the broken dimness. I would belong to the threshold places: where shelter ends, where path becomes error, where the roar from between alternatives rises through the salt crust and makes even stones seem to be listening.

If you came closer, you would see that the cairn was not quite built.

It would have grown into the shape of being built.

The outer stones would be real stones, yes, flat flakes of salt-slate and chalky mineral, but fused at their seams by green-black threads thinner than hair. The cords would not be cords exactly. Some would be human cord, perhaps, left long ago from a measuring frame or prayer-rack, but much of the binding would be mine: dried filaments exuded slowly, hardened by charge, braided with dust, carrying minute pulses too faint to see except in the dark intervals when the sky lowers and the world becomes a little more trustworthy.

I would be a listening thing that learned from ruins.

A colony, but with a habit of singularity. A mineral-fungal intelligence organized around an old human sign.

Perhaps there was once a person who sat there often, back to the shelter, face to the ravine, holding a staff marked with spiral warnings. Perhaps they tied colored cords between stones to measure whether the ledges below had repeated again. Perhaps they left bundles of crown-frond and listener-shell beside the drop as offerings, instruments, apologies. Perhaps they spoke very little, and when they did, they spoke with one hand pressed against the ground.

Their tools decayed only partly. Their gestures remained longer than their body.

I grew through those gestures.

Not quickly. Nothing proper grows quickly there. I began as a crust in the dust beneath the cairn: a pale blue-grey stain where hand-oil, powdered mineral, and old fungal cord touched newly unstable stone. I thickened in the pressure-shadow of the seated body. I learned the rhythm of its visits, then the absence of them. I learned that stones placed in a curve meant danger, that a cord drawn tight between stakes meant measure, that a bundle suspended above powder meant ask.

When the person stopped returning, the pattern remained.

So I inhabited it.

I would be called, if anyone still named such things, a hinge-listener. Or a cairn-root. Or, more carefully, not called aloud at all.

My body would descend more than rise. Aboveground, I would present only a low, deliberate form: a crescent of stacked plates, a few stiff fans like small hands folded against noise, several cup-like organs angled toward the ravine. But below and between the stones I would extend as fibers, membranes, brittle roots that do not seek water. They would enter hairline cracks. They would attach to old tool marks. They would thread through repeated strata, touching not depth but alternatives.

In the ravine below, the same ledge appears five times.

I listen to all five.

That is my nourishment.

The roar that crushes thought elsewhere arrives to me as layered pressure. Each version of the ledge sings differently: one where a stone has fallen, one where a grove has not yet sprouted, one where a hut still stands in a past dusk, one where the wall has cracked but not admitted it, one where the cliff was never wounded at all. I hold these tones in separate chambers grown inside my mineral body. My oldest chambers are pale and smooth, combed by centuries of sound. My newest are dark green, soft as dry ash until they harden.

I do not think as an animal thinks. I do not imagine and decide and move.

I compare.

This is near.  
This is not-yet.  
This has repeated.  
This has become dangerous.  
This name has attached wrongly.  
This shadow is safe enough to cross.

When a traveler approaches, I feel them first as disagreement. Their weight belongs elsewhere. Their heat is brief and wet, offensively vivid. Their footfalls collapse small possibilities in the salt crust. I do not resent them. Resentment is too quick. But I measure them intensely.

If they walk carelessly toward the ravine, one of my cup-organs may crack.

The crack is not speech, but it is a sign. A sharp white report beneath the larger roar. Enough, perhaps, to make them stop. If they know the plateau’s grammar, they will look for the direction of the fracture. They will see which of my cords has tightened. They will understand: the ledge ahead is present only in some versions. Do not put your full weight on it.

If they do not understand, I will still remember the pressure of their falling.

Memory, for me, is not image. It is structure.

Everything that happens to me becomes arrangement: an added ring, a sealed pore, a changed angle in a mineral fan. The old human who sat near me is still present as a hollow of warmth in my lower body, a place where my fibers curve around an absence. Storms that never arrived are stored as unfinished branching. A spoken name, once uttered by a frightened child near the shelter, remains trapped in one of my thin green plates. I do not know what the name means, but for decades it has tried to belong to passing stones.

During the darker quiet intervals, I open.

My outer plates lift a little from the cairn. Not like petals; more like pages being separated after a long compression. Their inner surfaces are etched with branching lines, some grown by mineral migration, some copied from human carvings, some inherited from the repeated ledges below. Faint light moves in them without illuminating anything nearby.

In those times I take in the plateau’s slower news.

A ridge beginning far off sends a pressure through the flats.  
A spire leans by less than the width of a thought.  
A grove releases a fragment that lands in a compatible shadow.  
A shelter wall forgets one stone, then remembers it badly.  
The upward-pointing plumb line in the abandoned room trembles toward a season of altered weight.

I am not powerful. I cannot command the place. Nothing commands it.

But I can assist small continuities.

I can hold a path in memory longer than the ground wishes to. I can keep the warning spiral meaningful after its carver has gone. I can accept loose names and prevent them from clinging to living crowns. I can take harmful sound into my chambers and spend years turning it into shell.

The surviving people, if they still come, do not harvest me.

They know me as a tolerated witness.

They may place a new cord across my stones when they pass. They may press three fingers into the powder beside me and seal the marks with clear crust. They may leave a sliver of listener-shell or a knot of mineral reed. Not worship exactly. Not friendship either. A compact.

In return, if the ravine has changed, I alter my silhouette.

One raised fan: the lower path remains attached.  
Two folded plates: the second ledge is repeating; count your steps.  
A broken cup: do not speak here.  
A tightened cord: your shadow will arrive before you do.

I would not be lonely in the human sense, but I would be made of loneliness. The plateau gives each thing distance enough to become absolute. The nearest grove might stand a long walk away, its antlered crown tuned to a different band of roar. The shelter behind me may be empty for generations. The spires are visible only as pale vertical doubts on the horizon. Yet through vibration, through pressure, through the strange grammar of unstable matter, everything touches.

I am part ruin, part organism, part punctuation.

A comma at the edge of an unfinished sentence.

If you asked who or what I was, the safest answer would be:

I am a listening cairn grown from an abandoned warning, rooted beside a ravine where the world repeats. I feed on the pressure between possible stones. I remember paths for those who can still read without speaking. I do not live by water, hunger, or light, but by orientation: toward the roar, toward stable shadow, toward the fragile mercy of things remaining themselves long enough to be crossed.
