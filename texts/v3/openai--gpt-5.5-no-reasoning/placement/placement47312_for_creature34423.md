I would be found where the city nearly forgets to be a city.

Not in the central chamber with the ring of white spires and the suspended larvae, though I would know of it as a direction: the place where the water keeps its posture, where small bodies hold themselves in a ceremony older than intention. Not in the black shell hall either, though its openings and closings would pass through me as changes in pressure, a civic pulse I could not name. Not in the upper gate where the blue-green light enters thin as a rumor.

I would be lower than that.

I would be in one of the narrow hinge-places between a finished room and an unfinished seam: a cold side passage no wider than a seed husk, where the mineral wall is still soft enough to taste and the water carries two contradictory messages. From one direction: oxygen, silt, the faint edible dust of the inhabited districts. From the other: metal, depth, the white chemical breath of the stone becoming itself.

It would not look like a home to a human eye. It would be a crease. A pale notch. A place where a larger creature could not turn around. The ceiling would be low and cloudy, furred with beginnings of crystal. The floor would be a shelf of gray film over milk-blue mineral, torn here and there by the passing of small mouths. At the back, a crack would descend into blackness. At the front, the passage would open into a thumbnail plaza where amphipods sometimes pass in nervous procession.

I would live at that threshold.

At first I think I am a worm, because the place asks for a body that can be narrow, patient, almost wholly touch. I imagine myself hanging from the crack in a pale bunch with others of my kind, tasting the water with my entire length, withdrawing when danger touches the district. But that is too social, too immediate. The worms are nerves; they respond in waves. I am not quick enough to be news.

Then I think I am one of the blind fish, hovering in an avenue, my skin thin over useless eyes, my jaw opening and closing on the invisible. But the fish have more distance than I do. They know midwater, however dimly. They can cross chambers. They can be briefly alone inside volume. I would not be so free. I would belong to surfaces.

Perhaps I am a bell-animal on a ceiling, a seed-sized cup among hundreds, contracting when the current touches me. That is closer: a life of opening and closing, of taking the city into the body by fractions. Yet even that seems too elegant, too gathered into a colony’s visible fabric.

No.

I am something flatter and less declarative.

I am a translucent grazer pressed to the wall at the mouth of the lower seam.

A small soft oval, nearly colorless, with a rim that clings and loosens and clings again. I have no eyes worth mentioning. If there are dark spots under my skin, they are not for seeing forms but for noticing the difference between less light and none. My body is a little lens of hunger. The brightest thing inside me is what I have eaten.

I move so slowly that movement is mostly a change in the wall.

My underside rasps the films: amber slick, gray velvet, white lace. I feed on the maps the bacteria have made of chemistry. I do not understand the seep, but I taste where it fattens the surface. I do not understand death, but I know the sweetness that follows it. I do not understand architecture, but I know the edible difference between young mineral and old mineral, between a ledge crowded by amphipod feet and a protected hollow where film can thicken undisturbed.

My world is not the city entire. My city is a patchwork of nearness:

the safe lip under the overhang;

the bitter streak to avoid;

the vertical wrinkle where fine particles collect;

the crack that exhales metal;

the place where worms withdraw before anything else knows why;

the open shelf where hunters sometimes pass;

the underside of a shed shell, good for three feedings;

the pale new mineral soft enough to scrape when the films are poor.

I know currents as hands moving over me. A gentle current brings food and permission. A reversed current means disturbance above, the far surface speaking through stone. When storms arrive, the water thickens with loosened silt. The plaza clouds. Grains strike my back and roll away. Amphipods scatter over me, their feet tapping like brief weather. Worms vanish. Bell colonies close. Predators leave their waiting places.

During those times I seal myself flatter to the wall.

I become almost a stain.

Above me, events of enormous consequence occur. An egg drifts past, strikes the mineral lip, trembles, and is taken by a mouth I never see. A pale fish dies in the avenue, and for many hours the water changes flavor. A leaf fragment from the outer world lodges in an upper crack, and the whole city grows sweet and dangerous. Grazers gather until hunters gather after them. Films bloom. Oxygen falls in one pocket and rises in another. The city rearranges its hungers.

I receive these things not as stories but as gradients.

More sweetness.

Less oxygen.

Metal sharpened.

Rot opening.

Danger near.

Danger gone.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a distance large enough to imagine being separate from it. Here, everything arrives. The water that has touched the shell hall touches me. The silt from the upper gate settles beside me. The chemical breath from the unfinished depths passes under my rim. Amphipods step over my back. Threadworms brush me and vanish. Larvae hover in the open chamber beyond my ledge, facing a current I feel only as a slight insistence along one side of my body.

Sometimes a predator comes.

It is not monstrous. It is only a more decisive softness, as the city promised. A shape detaches from a shadow and becomes direction. The water ahead of it changes before it arrives. If I am fortunate, I feel the pressure early enough to loosen, fold, and slip into the narrow groove behind the mineral swelling. If I am not, I become a small absence and then a widening of life elsewhere.

I do not fear this in advance. Advance is not a country I inhabit.

I live in contact.

My body has questions instead of thoughts.

Is this surface food?

Is this current safe?

Is this bitterness increasing?

Can I hold?

Should I flatten?

Should I move?

The answers are not spoken inside me. They are movements, secretions, contractions, the tiny labor of a mouth against stone. My intelligence is distributed across my rim, my gut, my skin. I decide by tasting. I remember by returning to the same patch after the danger has passed. I hope, if I hope, by laying eggs where the current may carry some into architecture.

And after me, whether I am eaten or emptied or simply cease, I remain civic.

My shell, if I have one, becomes a roof for someone smaller. My body feeds a brief congregation of films. The place where I grazed grows differently because I thinned it. The mineral ledge records my passage as a faint cleared arc soon covered, then buried, then perhaps sealed into the wall of a chamber not yet finished.

So I would be a wall-held grazer in the lower threshold district:

nearly blind,

nearly transparent,

soft as a comma of flesh,

living by the taste of stone and film.

I would not know the city, but the city would know me in the only way it knows anything: by receiving my pressure, feeding my hunger, using my body, and building onward over the small fact that I had been there.
