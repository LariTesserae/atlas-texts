I would not be one of the roof-colonies, though at first I thought I might be: some pale inverted thing with hooked fingers, moving through the luminous mats where the ceiling is no longer sky but ground. There is a temptation there. To live among the light itself, to know the vastness by hanging over it, to sleep with one’s back against leaves broad as plazas while weather forms beneath. But no. That life feels too close to mastery, or at least to a kind of intimacy with the source of brightness that I have not earned.

Nor would I be one of the vent-dwellers, broad and warm and doomed into abundance. I would envy them. I would visit them with cold hands and empty packets, trading polished black shards and looped cord for their resin, their bitter dried meat, their reckless laughter. But I would not belong among them. I would distrust too much growth. I would wake at every wall-thickening. I would sit too near the door.

I think I would be found in the open districts between the safer places.

Not homeless, exactly. Not exiled. More like a person whose dwelling is repeated rather than fixed.

A listener.

One of the solitary ones, or nearly solitary, carrying a frame of hollow stems and tightened membranes over my shoulders, moving from band to band along paths that exist only because they are remembered often enough. I would not be young. Or if I were young, I would have been aged early by listening for things that move slower than fear and faster than explanation.

You would find me beneath the long cold arch, at first.

That is what they call it, though it is less an arch than a continent of root thrown across a gulf. It rises from a white lichen plain, climbs into haze, and descends somewhere far enough away that its far foot is usually invisible. It is not a route for ordinary days. It is too exposed when the ceiling goes green-white. Its skin tightens before growth-surges. In dry weather its outer bark becomes brittle and rings underfoot like fired clay. But inside it are old channels, dead or sleeping, where sound travels with unnatural faithfulness.

I would go there to listen.

I would wedge myself in the lee of a rib, where the wind breaks and drops its frost. I would take off one glove only, never both, and set my bare fingertips against the arch. The cold would enter the nail-beds first. Then the ache would climb the bones of the hand. The arch would seem silent to anyone impatient.

But it is not silent.

A living bridge has many kinds of speech.

There is the high dry ticking of surface ice loosening in the light-pulse. There is the dull flex of fibers taking weight somewhere far away. There are tiny knocks traveling through the body of it: seedpods opening, rootlets shearing, the feet of small grazers, the taps of people who know how to ask whether the bridge is awake. There is a deeper sound too, almost below hearing, a pressure-thought passing along the length of it whenever the ceiling mats flare and the air shifts.

My trade would be remembering these differences.

I would wear a mask with narrow slits darkened by smoke-resin, though smoke is rare and costly. The mask would be old, repaired until none of its first making remained. Its mouthpiece would be packed with bitter fiber against spores. Along one cheek would hang a fringe of tiny hollow seeds, each tuned to a slightly different tremor. People would say they were decoration, because people prefer decoration to instruments until they need the instrument.

My back-frame would look like a burden of sticks.

It would be more than that.

Twelve hollow stems, each cured at a different age. Two membranes stretched from glider skin. A row of bead-knots made of bone, pod-shell, stone, and dried root. A little frame that can be unfolded and set against a surface to amplify what comes through it. A listening comb of black spine, used to touch mats too dangerous for the hand. A cord of warnings, knotted in patterns no child is allowed to play with. A small obsidian knife wrapped twice, because a listener cuts rarely but must cut cleanly when cutting becomes necessary.

I would move alone because crowds lie to the ground.

A band changes everything it touches. Their warmth gathers. Their breath condenses. Their steps persuade or alarm. Their children forget not to lean on things. Alone, I could pass lightly enough that the plants would not always decide what to do with me. Alone, I could tell whether a bridge trembled because of weather, or because people were crossing, or because something beneath had begun to remember upward.

That would be my gift, if it can be called a gift: not prophecy, not magic, but attention kept so long it has become a second body.

I would know where to sleep by what refuses dew.

Not on the pale dry mats, never there. Not beneath the warm flowers, even when the cold presses tears from the eyes. I would look for old negotiated places: a crescent hollow beside a root-buttress, lined with dry black fiber; a low pocket under a folded leaf that still smells faintly of ash and human skin; a former camp whose living floor has not forgotten the shape of a curled body.

When I found such a place, I would ask before entering.

Not in words. Words are for people and for certain dangerous ceremonies. I would tap twice with the back of a knuckle. Wait. Press my palm flat. Wait for tightening, loosening, indifference. Indifference is often safest. Welcome is sometimes hunger wearing manners.

If the hollow accepted me, I would spread my sleeping skin, hang my mask beside my face, and keep one cord tied around my wrist leading to the frame. If anything moved the frame in the dark-blue dimming, I would wake.

Not that darkness ever truly comes there.

Even in the low pulses the world shines from edges: algae in condensation cups, bruised glow under the skin of leaves, distant ceiling fruit breathing cold light through haze. Sleep in that place is not descent into blackness. It is lying down inside a dim living eye.

I would not have a village, but I would have returns.

There would be a band under the ribbed hedge at Pear-Scent Narrow who know me by the angle of my shoulders before they see my mask. Their children would dare each other to touch the chimes on my cloak. Their eldest woman, who has outlived three houses that chose upwardness, would give me cup-root steeped in vent-salt and ask whether the blue rain columns have shifted.

There would be a pair of sisters near the old ladder-flower who maintain a quiet patch better than anyone else in three districts. Their sleeping ground does not fold even under ceiling flare. They would let me rest there if I brought them news of the great browser herds, because their cord-vines seed best after the browsers pass.

There would be a vent-market I visit only when necessity overcomes caution. Warmth would strike me there as obscenity. People laughing bare-faced. Walls visibly thickening. Babies sleeping flushed and damp. Food too abundant, too soft. I would trade quickly and leave with resin, medicines, and a headache from the yeasty air.

People would not wholly trust me.

That is proper.

A listener hears too much and belongs too little. I might arrive with warnings no one wants. I might say: the open white span is thinning from beneath. Do not cross after the second flare. Or: the thorn fog is not fog this season; it is a flowering front. Burn nothing there. Or: your eastern sleeping hollow has begun answering footsteps before they arrive.

They would ask, “Are you sure?”

And I would say what listeners say: “No. But I heard it twice.”

That would be enough, usually.

Sometimes it would not.

Sometimes a family would need to cross anyway. Sometimes hunger is louder than caution. Sometimes a child has gone missing, or a cache lies beyond a mat that is beginning to fold, or a house has warmed from within and must be abandoned before it becomes a mouth. Then I would go with them as far as the plant-speech remained clear.

I would not be brave in the clean way.

I would be afraid often. Fear is one of the senses down there. Only fools try to silence it. I would use it as I use smell, vibration, brightness, frost. A sudden fear on a quiet plain might mean nothing but memory. Or it might mean the small ticking of condensation has stopped.

If that happened, I would move.

No discussion. No finishing a meal. No gathering every tool. Leave the low place. Climb if there is a safe climb. Crosswind if the ground is about to lift. Do not run unless running has already become less dangerous than stillness.

My body would show the life.

Long feet widened by years of bindings. Knees that ache before pressure changes. Fingers scarred from cold and sap-burn. A crescent of white tissue on one forearm where a filament once tasted blood and had to be sliced away. One ear less good than the other because I once listened too close to a pod-tower before release.

I would carry oils in a little grown bottle, bitter and dark, to rub into cuffs, seams, hairline, mask-edge. Not enough to make me invisible to the plants. Nothing makes you invisible. Only enough to say: not soil, not deadwood, not yet.

I think I would have been born in a small band that no longer exists as a band.

Not destroyed dramatically. More likely unbraided by the ordinary pressures of the undercontinent. One marriage into the vent people. Two deaths in a closing shelter. A child taken in by relatives beyond the blue rain. A house that lifted until it became sacred and unusable. A route that stopped tolerating passage. That is how communities end there: not by conquest, but by the withdrawal of a few permissions.

Perhaps that is why I listen. To keep some thread between places that otherwise become rumor.

I would remember names for altered things.

The ladder that flowered after its maker died.

The mask-tree where three siblings were given back to the grove.

The dry channel that runs only when the ceiling scars sweat.

The bridge that accepts single travelers but tightens under groups.

The shelter that imitates a shelter too perfectly.

The plain where the roots below knock back in a child’s rhythm.

I would know that not every warning saves. I would know that not every invitation kills. The hardest skill there is not suspicion. Suspicion is easy. The hardest skill is discernment: to accept the cup-flower that is safe because frost has formed on its rim; to enter the hollow that has opened for warmth without confusing it with a throat; to cut a living wall enough to keep a doorway, but not so much that the house remembers injury.

My songs would be incomplete.

A listener’s songs are made to leave room for reply. Low throat tones. Two taps. Pause. Three taps. Palm-drag. Silence. The plant either answers or does not. Some nights, under the long cold arch, I would sing only to test whether the old channels still carry. The answer might come minutes later from far beyond sight: a faint wooden knock traveling back through miles of fiber, changed by distance into something almost like intention.

I would not decide whether the place is one mind.

That question would feel too large and too surface-born. Minds, nations, weather, hunger, memory—below the crust these are not separate categories. The undercontinent learns. That is enough. It learns feet, warmth, ash, blood, rhythm. It learns absence. It learns the shape of a camp and may grow it again without understanding why the people once needed shelter.

It might be learning me.

My repeated routes. My pauses. The oils I use. The pattern of my taps. The weight of my sleeping body in certain hollows. Perhaps one day I would come to a place I have never slept and find a hollow already made to my length, a hook grown at shoulder height for my frame, a dry shelf just wide enough for my mask.

That would frighten me more than hostility.

To be refused by the undercontinent is ordinary.

To be recognized is another kind of danger.

Still, I think I would enter.

Carefully. Gratefully. With the obsidian knife loosened in its wrap.

Because that is what living there means: accepting help without forgetting that help may not know what you are.

If you asked where to find me, the truest answer would be: between agreements.

On a temporary path that has learned my tread but not my name.

At the edge of a white span, kneeling to listen before crossing.

Under a dimming ceiling mat while seed-sails drift overhead like silent animals.

Beside a cache-pod, adding a new knot to a warning cord.

In a cold sleeping hollow, one hand bare against the living ground, waiting to hear whether the valley is about to change its mind.

And if you asked what I am, I would not say prophet, hermit, scout, or priest, though strangers might use all those words.

I would say:

I am one who listens ahead.

I am a small warm interval moving through a bright unfinished lung.

I am not the owner of any road, only the keeper of a few permissions.

I am what a people becomes when the ground cannot be trusted and the ground, despite everything, sometimes trusts back.
