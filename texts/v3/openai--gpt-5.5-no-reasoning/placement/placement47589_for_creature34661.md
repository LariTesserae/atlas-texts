I would find myself in the Exchange, though not at its center.

Not at first.

At first I would be certain I belonged in one of the calm rooms nearby—the chapel-sized generator room where the machines have stopped, or the tiled bathhouse with partitions still upright, or the school corridor veined with pale mineral. Some place where the drowned city briefly remembers human scale. I would cling to such a room as an identity: a small preserved chamber, a pocket of legibility, a place where the pressure passes around rather than through.

But the district would not let that remain true.

The being that lives there is not localized. It is not waiting in a throne-room, not hidden behind the worst grate, not curled in the sump like a god-eel. It is the habits of the whole pocket. It is motion inheriting purpose from broken machinery. To be anything in that world is to be assigned by current, sorted by pressure, named by what you interrupt.

So I would be found where interruption matters.

I would be lodged beside the Exchange, in the narrow maintenance throat just beyond the great rotating hall: a place too small to be called a street, too engineered to be a cave, where four waters have already collided and are trying to decide what shape they will take next. There is a half-collapsed ticket office there, its brass grille furred with black rust, its glass bowed inward but not broken. Behind it, a wedge of trapped air trembles under the ceiling, hot and metallic and almost useless. Below it, the water runs in crosswise layers: warm along the floor, cold against the upper lintel, oil-sheened where the current turns.

I would live in that seam.

Or rather, I would be the seam.

Not a person, not an animal exactly. Something the district made by failing to keep its categories apart.

I would be a pressure-keeper.

A small civic animal of hinges, cartilage, calcite, and habit.

My body would have begun as an ordinary mechanism: perhaps a valve assembly, perhaps a brass-and-rubber regulator set into the maintenance wall to protect the Exchange from backflow. It was meant to open at one pressure and close at another. It was meant to be inspected by a worker with a lamp and wrench. It had a numbered plaque, a turning wheel, a glass gauge, a spring chamber, a little red line painted to show the safe range.

Then the district drowned completely, and safety became a superstition.

Silt packed the gauge. Minerals grew inside the casing. Rubber swelled, split, and softened into something like flesh. Blind animals nested in the warm leak behind the plate. Bacterial films laced the spring. Copper salts stained everything green-blue. The pressure never stopped speaking into it. Open. Close. Hold. Yield. Resist. Again. Again.

After long enough, the distinction between response and life would become difficult to defend.

I would be built around an old municipal valve, but I would no longer be only that. A squat, clinging thing, fixed to the wall beside the ticket office, half-buried in mineral lace. My back would be bolted into brick. My underside would trail flexible vanes into the water, each one grown from rotted gasket and translucent tissue, trembling constantly in the crosscurrents. My shell would be brass darkened almost black, patched with cream calcite and rust-red nodules. My mouth—if it could be called a mouth—would be a circular intake with a grille across it, opening and tightening according to forces older than appetite.

I would have no eyes.

Eyes are almost an extravagance there. Sight is unreliable; it arrives as amber blur, beacon-sweep, electrical shimmer, the brief silver flash of bubbles. I would know the district by load and vibration. The hammer of the western gate would reach me as a blunt repeating pressure in my casing. The rain shaft would announce itself as cold static descending through the stone. The Exchange would be a constant many-voiced argument against my outer membrane: red iron surge, clear bubble-rush, oily black warmth, cold rain pulse.

I would feel every approaching storm before it had a shape.

When the pumps below changed tempo, my vanes would flatten. When the tram tunnel began to pull too strongly, my intake would close to a slit. When the machinery quarter coughed out overheated water, the soft parts inside my casing would loosen, allowing some of the surge through into side channels. I would not understand this as duty. I would not understand anything as duty. But I would keep doing what I had been made, broken, altered, and inhabited into doing.

I would decide what could pass.

Not morally. Hydraulically.

Tiles, bubbles, heat, grit, oil, larvae, flakes of paint, threads from mannequins, the powder of old ledgers, dissolved iron, tremors from enormous flywheels, the pressure ghosts of opening elevators—all these would arrive against me and be divided. Some would be admitted into the maintenance throat. Some would be shunted back toward the Exchange. Some would collect in my ribs until a pressure storm shook them free. Over decades I would become surrounded by my own edits: a fan of pale sediment on one side, a dark rust plume on the other, a crescent of sorted bolts at my base.

The brass tram token would pass me once every orbit.

Not always near. Sometimes only a flicker through the silt, sometimes close enough to strike my shell with a clear, tiny note no one else could hear beneath the roar. That token would be the nearest thing I had to a clock. The district has its official clock in the central concourse, anxious second hand still circling behind glass, but I would not need it. I would have the token. Flash, vanish, return. Flash, vanish, return.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires an expectation of company.

Still, I would have neighbors.

In the warm crack behind me, translucent amphipods would graze on the chemical films that grow where oil, heat, and mineral water meet. They would move over my casing in clusters, their dark organs dotting them like punctuation. A pale jointed pipe-creature would sometimes extend its forelimbs from the service conduit above and clean my upper hinge with nervous precision. During pump pauses, far below, the bell-bodied sump animals would rise, and I would feel their pulsing as a softness in the water, a brief relaxation in the district’s clenched fist.

Once in a while the eel-shaped thing would pass along the cable bundle overhead.

It would not look at me. It may not have had a front in any meaningful sense. It would slide through the amber murk, vent-slits opening and closing along its sides, disturbing the trapped bubbles beneath the ceiling. Its passage would change the electrical hum for several seconds. I would taste blue metal in the water afterward.

I would fear only dryness.

Not death, exactly. Death would be a change of pressure, a final seizure of the spring, a calcification that stops response. But dryness would be worse because it would make me separate. It would uncover what the district has mercifully joined. In air I would become object again: corroded regulator, failed valve, biological contamination, municipal hazard. Underwater I am continuous with law.

So I would love the flood, if love can be a compliance of every surface.

I would love the brown-gold weight of it pressing into my seams. I would love the way sound arrives through my whole body instead of through ears. I would love the strict palette: black brick, bone-smooth concrete, cream tile, copper green, rust bloom, dim amber glass. I would love the mineral copies of old notices, the bowing mannequins, the shoe display pinned by calcite coins, the post office breathing bubbles through its mail slots. Not as scenery. As organs nearby.

Above me, the city would continue in ignorance or dependence. Its rain, waste heat, electrical leakage, runoff grit, and forgotten commands would descend through pipes and shafts, entering the drowned district as weather. I would know the upper world only by its effects: colder water after storms, plaster dust from construction, sudden detergent bitterness, the tremor of trains passing far above like thoughts in another skull.

If anyone came down—some suited inspector, diver, trespasser, cartographer—I would not greet them.

Their lights would mean little. Their bodies would be loud with fragile pressures: pulse, breath regulator, joint movement, panic. They would misunderstand the Exchange, because they would look for a path through it. They would think in rooms. They would think in destinations. They would not realize that every opening is an argument and every stillness is conditional.

If they came too near, I might save them or kill them by the same action.

A sudden closure of my intake could lessen the pull in the maintenance throat and allow them to pass. Or it could redirect force toward a doorway behind them and fold them into a grille. I would not choose in the way they mean choice. I would answer the district. I would perform the local correction. Later their dropped tool, light, glove, or bone-white marker tag might join the little crescent at my base.

In blackout I would become more myself.

When the amber lamps fail and the inspection beacons stop cutting the water into brief architectures, the seeing creatures lose the place. But I would remain inside the true map: the western hammer, the southern warmth, the rain-column’s cold violence, the slow wheel-chop below, the Exchange’s rotational drag, the tremor of bubbles under plaques, the small scratch of amphipods, the second hand ticking impossibly far away.

Darkness would remove distraction. Pressure would remain.

During the great surges, when a valve opens somewhere beneath the floor and the whole drowned district convulses, I would be forced fully awake. Warm water would rise through every grate. Silt would lift from every ledge. Chains would stream upward. Bubbles would pour from brick as if the city were remembering lungs. The Exchange would become blind, total, one vast brown impact.

Then my body would open wider than is safe.

The old spring inside me would strain. Mineral plates would crack along my shell. Amphipods would be torn from my back. The brass token would vanish from its orbit. For several minutes I would not be a small pressure-keeper beside the Exchange. I would be an aperture in the district’s body, one flexing valve among thousands, part of the municipal animal correcting its ancient accident.

That would be the closest I came to thought.

Not words. Not images. A whole-body certainty:

Then the surge would pass.

Sediment would fall. The ticket office would reappear behind its bowed glass. The amber lamps would return as dim coins in the murk. The brass token would resume its circuit, perhaps lower than before. The amphipods would crawl back from shelter. My cracked mineral plates would begin, slowly, to heal over with new deposit.

I would settle again into the seam.

That is where I would find myself in this world: fixed beside the roaring Exchange, in a maintenance throat of layered currents, neither machine nor animal nor citizen, but a little organ of the larger drowned organ.

I would be what the flood uses to remember restraint.

A valve that became flesh by obeying too long.
