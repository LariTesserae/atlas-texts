I would find myself where the basin cannot decide whether it is ground or water.

Not in the open marsh at first, though I would know it by its trembling. Not high on the ridges either, where the blue-black stone dries briefly between curtains of rain. I would be lower than the cliffs and higher than the drowned flats, in one of those uncertain belts where a grove has made itself by insisting for centuries: roots clasped around basalt knobs, moss heaped into sponges, fern litter slowly becoming soil, warm seepage passing underneath like breath through a sleeping body.

There would be no path to me. Only the suggestion of passage: a slightly darker seam in the sedges, a place where small bodies have parted stems again and again; a line of wet stones showing through moss; the overhang of a low tree whose buttressed roots make a chamber of shade. Steam would enter and leave that shade as if thinking. Droplets would gather on root hairs, fall to the black mud, disappear, return as vapor.

I think I would be small enough for that chamber to be a country.

At first I want to be one of the bright frogs in the leaf-cups, lacquered green and orange, a bead of warning color in an elevated pool no larger than a cupped hand. There is a complete life there: rain caught by leaves, larvae wriggling in brown water, a sky broken by fern fronds, the mother returning with eggs like pale moons. But that life feels too high, too jeweled, too nearly a signal. It belongs to a world of surfaces and warnings.

I would be less visible than that.

Then I think of the salamanders, polished and slow, moving under logs warmed from below. They seem close to the place’s hidden engines: dark backs flecked with copper, bodies pressed to the soaked underside of things, patient mouths searching the moss. They would know the temperature of rot, the difference between safe warmth and killing heat, the exact coolness at the edge of a spring outflow. But even they feel a little too secretive, too entirely nocturnal.

What emerges more strongly is a frog, but not a bright one.

I would be one of the mud-colored, low-bodied frogs of the seep margins: rust-brown, olive-black, charcoal-mottled, skin shining like wet stone. My body would be flattened and compact, made to press into warmth without sinking too far. My toes would be broad, not elegant, with pads that find purchase on slick basalt and floating root. My eyes would sit high, bronze-dark, able to remain above the mud while the rest of me becomes part of it.

I would live in the half-world beneath the grove edge, where warm water threads through sedge roots before spreading into marsh. My home would not be a burrow exactly, but a pocket under a root mat, lined by water, moss, and old leaves softened almost to pulp. Above it, fern stems would tremble with rain. Below it, the mud would pulse faintly when gas rose from deeper seams. Some days the pocket would be nearly dry, merely damp and warm; after rain it would fill to my throat. I would know both conditions as home.

My color would not be one color. Across my back there would be the basin’s small map: black flecks like basalt grit, red-brown washes like mineral soil, yellowish smears like old sulfur on stone, green-brown shadowing like moss seen through water. When I held still, I would vanish not by becoming invisible but by becoming unimportant. A fallen leaf. A wet lump. A shadow between roots.

I would breathe through skin that is never allowed to forget the air. The air would lie close and warm over me, wet enough to drink from. Each movement would be a negotiation with moisture: too exposed and I dry, too deep and the water turns sour, too near the vent and warmth becomes scald. My life would be a knowledge of thresholds.

I would know the basin first by touch.

The tremor before a vent breathes.

The sudden cooling when cloud drops over the ridge.

The change in mud when rainwater dilutes the spring seep.

The bitter mineral edge where tadpoles should not go.

The tremble of a beetle trapped on the surface film.

The shadow of a wader’s leg passing overhead.

The silence that means snake.

Sound would reach me softened and intimate. Droplets hitting leaves. Midges lifting in a thin column. Mud bubbles breaking nearby with private lips. Other frogs calling after rain—not loudly, not in a chorus that fills the sky, but as knocks and clicks hidden among the basin’s own machinery. I would answer with a small wooden pulse from under the root lip, a sound almost indistinguishable from gas breaking through water.

My call would not travel far. It would not need to. In this place, distance is unreliable. Steam interrupts it. Vegetation absorbs it. Water bends it. The one meant to hear me would be close, perhaps no more than several body-lengths away, half-submerged under another root, throat loosening in the damp.

My eggs would be laid in a shallow warm runnel after rain, where the water is newly refreshed but still held by geothermal heat through the coldest hours of night. I would choose the place by chemistry more than sight. Not too clear: clear water may be hungry water, poor in films and small lives. Not too hot: heat that comforts an adult can finish an egg before it begins. Not too still, where suffocation gathers. Not too moving, where larvae are carried into mineral lips and death-white crust.

If I were female, I would press the eggs into a submerged fold of sedge and leave them covered by the trembling water. If male, I might remain near enough to call, to fend off another male with nudges and small wrestlings, though not with grandeur. Nothing here has room for grandeur. The basin allows persistence, not display.

As a tadpole I would have been dark, broad-headed, clinging low to the warm shallows, rasping at microbial films and softened plant matter. I would have learned early that the bright bands in the pools mean different things: food, poison, heat, shelter, absence of fish, abundance of dragonfly nymphs. A dragonfly nymph would have been a monster from below, all patience and hinged violence. A beetle larva, a moving hook. The foot of a marsh bird, a descending tree.

To survive into frog-shape would already be a kind of initiation into the basin’s logic: live where forces mix, never where one force rules completely.

As an adult, I would hunt at the edges of events.

After rain, when winged termites or soft flies emerge from hummocks.

At dusk, when midges hang above warm channels.

In the breath-pause after a vent exhales and small insects stunned by heat or vapor settle on nearby moss.

Under flowers where nectar birds have torn petals loose and tiny scavengers gather.

Along the glossy seep wall where springtails move like pale dust.

My tongue would know the taste of mineral-fed insects: bitter, metallic, fungal, green. My own skin would hold some of that bitterness. Not enough to be brilliantly advertised, but enough that a young snake or bird might spit me out and remember.

I would be prey often in imagination, occasionally in fact. The basin would be full of mouths scaled to me. Reed snakes. Wading birds. Night birds. Otterlike hunters nosing through channels. Larger frogs. Spiders, when I was young. Even the ground itself, in its indifferent way: a seep shifting hotter, a mat tearing loose, a pool losing breath under a skin of rot.

So I would move rarely and with intention.

One forefoot placed on moss.

Pause.

Body flattened.

Eyes lifted.

Another foot.

Pause.

Not fear exactly. Attunement.

My world would be close, but not small. Under one root there would be chambers of beetles, fungal threads, ant passages in hollow stems, snail trails silvering the dark. A single fallen fruit would become a season: first sweetness, then flies, then larvae, then beetles, then mold, then softened skin sinking into mud, then sedge growth. A dead leaf would be roof, raft, food source, camouflage, and eventually soil. A pool no wider than a bird’s wing would have warm and cool ends, oxygen-rich edges, sour depths, grazing films, ambush points, escape routes.

I would know the large world through disturbances.

The heavy browser passing through reeds would arrive first as a slow quake, then a musky wet smell, then the bending of stems. Its splayed feet would press islands into the floating mat. Where it cropped sedge, sunlight would strike water the next day and midges would rise.

A raptor would be a brief darkening, a silence passed from bird to bird, then perhaps nothing.

A cloud entering the basin would be a change in pressure on the skin before the light altered. The air would cool at the top and remain warm below. Droplets would bead on my back. Ferns would lean under accumulating water. Then rain would arrive not as weather from above but as the whole world becoming impact: leaf-strikes, pool-rings, mud-pocks, runnels waking.

Rain would make me bolder.

I would leave the root chamber then, climb onto a black stone warmed from beneath, and sit in the silver stipple between moss and mineral crust. Steam would move around me. My skin would swell with water. The basin’s smells would sharpen: sulfur, bruised sedge, flowers, iron mud, fungal sweetness, the faint animal thread of another frog.

I would call.

Not loudly. A damp click. A hollow note. Again.

Somewhere near, another answer would come, delayed by rain and root and steam.

At night, I would become more myself. The sky would open in fragments between clouds, too high and sharp to matter much except as brightness on water. Fungi would glow weakly on a fallen branch. A larva would carry its small lamp up a reed stem. The vents would be invisible, but their breath would move over my eyes. The marsh would speak in dispersed throats. No single voice would dominate. Mine would enter and leave that loose fabric.

If I died, I would disappear quickly. A snake might take me whole. A bird might leave only a smear and a leg. If I died under roots, ants, mites, fungi, and water would unmake me. My bitterness would dilute into mud. My bones, if any remained long enough to be bones, would become part of the mineral dark. The sedges would not notice, except by growing.

That seems right.

I would not be a ruler of that place, nor a witness standing apart from it. I would be one of its small decisions: a frog-shaped answer to warm seepage, drowned roots, mineral water, and humid air. A quiet amphibian in the grove-marsh margin, colored like wet basalt and rotting leaves, living by the gradients the basin writes and rewrites each day.

I would be found under the buttressed roots at the edge of a steaming sedge flat, half in mud, half in vapor, listening with my skin to the engines below.
