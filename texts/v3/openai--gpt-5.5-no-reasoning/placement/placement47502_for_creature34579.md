I would not be born there.

That is the first thing I know. Not from any sentimental recoil, not because the city is too harsh for a native soul, but because something in me remains badly calibrated to it. I would have arrived carrying habits from below: a body that wastes moisture without thinking; lungs that want to open too widely; hands that reach for distance and find railings instead. I would have come with a memory of weather that the cleft would treat as a contamination.

Not an ordinary trader, either. Traders pass through with sealed goods and damp stories and leave as soon as the pressure rites allow. They are too visible in their not-belonging. Their boots sound wrong. They look up too much, down too little. They sweat, ask direct questions, flinch when animals brush their ankles.

I think I would have stayed too long to remain a trader.

I would be found somewhere in the middle levels, where the city is most tangled with itself: not high enough to belong to the Intake clans and their scraped-sky austerity, not deep enough to be claimed by the Rack families who know the old rock and the sealed things in it. I would be in the Loom, or adjacent to it, in one of those half-districts that has been renamed three times because the walls keep acquiring new functions.

A narrow workroom off a cable gallery. No window, though there is a mirror-shaft nearby that sometimes spills a brief, exhausted gray light across the floor. The room is not private in the spacious sense. Nothing is private there in that sense. It is a recess made useful: one wall of slotted drawers, one hinged desk, one sleeping shelf folded up during work bells, three speaking tubes with chipped mouthpieces, a rack of ceramic tags, a little shrine made from a failed brass coupling, two cold lamps, and a patched pressure curtain that sighs when the lifts move.

I would be a listener.

Not officially at first. Officially, perhaps, I would be a recorder of faults: a conduit-scribe, a minor maintenance archivist, one of the people who collects reports from pipe-knocks, lamp codes, animal migrations, pressure bells, cracked fittings, unusual dust behavior, and worker testimony, then turns them into tags that can be acted upon.

A bureaucrat, if the word survived up there. But bureaucracy in such a city would not be paper and delay. It would be memory made hard enough to trust. Someone must know that a flutterer colony left Bridge Seventeen three hours before the west pressure curtain failed. Someone must connect amber mites at three separate junctions with a hidden overload in a line no one has opened since the scaffold was still temporary. Someone must keep track of which marked crack widened during upward dust, and which only whitened because of mineral bloom. Someone must remember that old Ravel from the Foundry said, before losing most of his hearing, that a certain counterweight chain had begun to “think crooked.”

I would sit in that recess with dust in the hinges of my tools and translate the city’s small warnings into patterns.

At first the natives would distrust me. Not dramatically. They would not spit or bar the hatches. They would simply give me incomplete silences. A city like that does not waste hostility on strangers unless they become dangerous. I would be assessed the way they assess an unfamiliar animal: What does it foul? What does it consume? What pressure change wakes it? Does it learn?

I would learn.

Slowly, and with embarrassment.

I would learn not to apologize too loudly when I blocked a ladder. I would learn to clip on before being told. I would learn that asking “is it safe?” is less useful than asking “which rule is active?” I would learn to keep my gloves powdered, my throat covered, my tools tied twice. I would learn the difference between a rail cold from ordinary night and a rail cold because the black glass behind it is bleeding stored chill. I would learn not to stare at the pale carriers when they pause all at once and turn their dull jewel eyes toward a wall.

I would never become graceful there. That feels important. I would not transform into one of the narrow, perfect climbers born to mesh and rung. I would always move with a trace of deliberation, a fraction too much thought before a crossing. Children would pass me upside down along the underside grips and laugh through their teeth.

But eventually, my awkwardness would become a known quantity. The city tolerates known quantities.

The animals would decide before the people did.

Powder-mites would gather at my lamp because I keep it slightly too warm. A tagged cleaner with a chipped dorsal plate would begin visiting my threshold after the sixth bell, scraping the fine gray residue from under the drawer wall. I would name it something inappropriate from below—Mallow, maybe, or Brook—and the locals would find this very funny once they understood. A carrier would follow me for three days after I mishandled a feed capsule, standing outside my curtain whenever I woke, motionless and accusing, until its keeper came and clicked it away with professional contempt.

The flutterers would disturb me most. Not fear exactly. Recognition. Their folded colonies under catwalks, their papery waking during pressure shifts, their blind knowledge of changes that instruments lag behind—there would be something in them I trusted more than gauges. I would begin noting their movements even when not assigned. Which colony rippled. Which stayed sealed. Which shaft they chose. Whether they flew low or spiraled upward.

That, perhaps, is how I become useful.

Not by mastering machines better than those born to them. Not by knowing the mountain with my skin as the elders do. But by noticing correspondences across categories the inhabitants keep separate for practical reasons. Flutterers, dust veils, pipe-hum, static whiskers, lamp-flicker, human hesitation. I would be too foreign to inherit the city’s assumptions cleanly, but too long resident to see it as spectacle. I would stand between superstition and maintenance, which there might be no real difference between.

After enough years, I would be called when signs disagreed.

A pressure bell says stable, but the cold lantern grubs pulse in the Deep Racks.  
The lift chain sounds true, but dust gathers on the underside of a stair.  
A conduit reads dry, but silver crawlers have appeared near its bolts.  
The flutterers sleep through a draft they should have feared.

I would arrive with a slate, a grounding pin, a box of grooved tags, and two younger assistants who climb better than I do. I would kneel, touch the rail, listen with my jaw near the metal. Not because I possess magic. Because in such a city, attention is a trade skill that eventually looks like divination.

People might call me a fault-listener. Or a pattern clerk. Or, less formally, a weatherless forecaster.

My station would be humble but oddly central. I would know things nobody intended to tell me. Messages pass through the Loom. Complaints become maintenance notes; maintenance notes become histories; histories become warnings. I would know who falsifies inspection times, who feeds carriers after the third bell, which Foundry press is overdue for failure, which sleeping tier has begun dreaming of rain because an outsider brought river stories and told them too vividly to children.

Rain would remain my private obscenity.

I would remember it.

That is another thing I know: I would not lose the memory of liquid. The city would dry my skin, narrow my habits, teach me reverence for powders and sealed valves, but somewhere in me there would persist the remembered weight of water in a bowl. The sound of it poured. The way it accepts light. The smell before a storm.

I would not speak of it often. Among people who fear open liquid with the seriousness of plague, nostalgia can become a form of rudeness. But perhaps, in my folded sleeping shelf, there would be one forbidden object: not water itself, never that, but a sealed lens from below, thick glass with a bubble trapped inside. A tiny ancient bead of air suspended in transparency. I would keep it wrapped in anti-static cloth and take it out only when the machines slow at night.

If discovered, it would not get me exiled. It would get me watched.

And perhaps invited, eventually, to one of the small shrines of tension, where failed bolts and old hooks hang in alcoves. Because longing is also a pressure, and pressures must be measured.

I imagine myself aging there without ever becoming native. My lungs silvered a little. My hearing tuned strangely: worse for ordinary speech, better for chain skips and relay chatter. My hands rough, the nails always lined with pale dust no brush quite removes. I would wear the local close layers badly at first, then well enough, then with private modifications no true-born cleft dweller would have chosen. Extra tags. A lower-altitude charm filed flat and clipped inside my cuff. A belt with too many note-cases.

I would stop looking for broad rooms.

Instead I would come to love narrow prospects: a view through three grates and a swinging basket; a glimpse of another worker’s lamp crossing far above; the central shaft during festival, when mirror tags on animals make the lower dark move like a reversed sky. I would love the city most when it reveals itself not as a machine but as an agreement continually renewed by touch.

Clip on.  
Check the hatch.  
Mark the crack.  
Trust the beast, but verify the line.  
Do not waste breath.  
Do not waste warning.

The place I would be most likely found is a listening balcony just below the Loom, too small to be a plaza, too exposed to be an office. It juts into the central void on black glass brackets and hums through the soles of the feet. There is a speaking tube there, a pressure dial, a rack of emergency hooks, and a colony of flutterers folded underneath it like old parchment.

At night, after the work bells thin out, I would stand there clipped to two rings, one hand on the rail, one hand resting on the slate against my chest. The city would mutter around me: carriers in the service tracks, sorters whispering, mica furnace windows pulsing dull red across the shaft, children being hushed in stacked cells, some unseen cleaner scraping the underside of a stair.

Then the lamps would flicker.

Dust would rise.

Everyone would pause.

In that pause I would be neither worker nor priest nor animal-keeper exactly, but a kind of held breath the city has learned to employ. I would watch the dust, listen for what resumes first, what resumes late, what does not resume at all.

And when the turbulence settles, I would hang a small coded tag from the nearest cable.

Not alarm. Not yet.

Attention.
