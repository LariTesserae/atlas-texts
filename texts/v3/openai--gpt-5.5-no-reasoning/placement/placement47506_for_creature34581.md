I would find myself low in the old districts, where the city has become most convinced of itself.

Not in the broadest central avenue at first. That place is too declarative, too full of the wind’s authority. I would be farther down, where the chambers have narrowed into a pattern almost like streets: long corridors between blocky mineral masses, shelves overhead like worn awnings, cracks rising in parallel seams through salt-white walls. The air would still be roaring, but at that depth the roar would arrive altered, translated through miles of stone into something like the memory of surf heard inside a shell.

I would be in a lee.

A recess behind a fallen slab. A pocket where dust has gathered ankle-deep over centuries and a root curtain hangs across the entrance in thick, dry cords. Outside, the avenue would be pale and open, scoured clean in the middle by wind. Inside, the air would be calmer, warmer by a fraction, darkened by old guano and the bodies of things that come there to wait.

It would not be a home in the human sense. Nothing built. Nothing stored with intention beyond appetite. But it would be a known place. A pause in the city’s weather. A place marked by repeated use: rubbed stone at shoulder height, pellets in the dust, split seed husks, beetle trails, the faint musk of fur and old fear. A place where many lives overlap without belonging to one another.

And I think I would not be one of the great animals.

Not a grazer, though there is something compelling in their slow civic dignity: their padded feet, their patience at crossings, their knowledge of old routes. Not a flyer, though the idea of rising with thousands into the vaulted dimness is almost irresistible. Not a predator either. The predators belong too sharply to edges, to decision, to the sudden narrowing of possibility.

I would be smaller. More continuous with the workings of the place.

I would be one of the burrowers.

A cool-bodied, dust-pale mammal with dense fur held close to the skin, made not for speed but for conservation. Round-backed. Low to the ground. Strong in the forefeet. Nearly blind, or sighted only enough to tell dimness from dimmer stone. My world would be mapped by whisker, vibration, taste, pressure, breath.

The undercity would not appear to me as scenery. It would arrive as contact.

Stone against claw. Powder shifting under the belly. Root skin under the teeth. The tremor of beetle processions through dust. The distant drumming of hooves before the grazer appears. The change in wind pressure moving through a tunnel before the sound becomes unbearable. The warm, alarming thread of predator musk crossing an otherwise safe route.

I would know the city from below its grandeur.

The false facades would be cliffs of scent. The plazas would be dangerous absences, crossed only when the wind slackened and the dust lay obedient. Root groves would be orchards, shelters, ceilings, weatherbreaks, and calendars. Guano gardens would be feasts and traps. Bones would be landmarks. A grazer’s dried rib cage might be a tunnel, a lookout, a salt source, a nursery wall.

My body would be full of small economies.

Close the nostrils when the grit rises. Fold the ears. Keep moisture in the burrow air. Carry seeds in cheek pouches. Do not chatter in the open. Move under cover. Wait when waiting costs less than flight. Eat the fallen leaf before the beetles take it. Let the beetles have what is already beyond chewing. Sleep with others when the stone drinks heat from the body.

I would belong to a colony, but not a society of thought and law. A society of nearness. Bodies pressed in a sealed chamber beneath roots. Whiskers touching in the dark. Young tucked into fur. Food cached in hollows bitten into soft mineral loam. Entrances plugged from inside with dust, fiber, and pellets when the pressure rises.

We would speak by little impacts.

A footbeat through packed powder: danger.

A softer answering tap: understood.

A scrape at the blocked tunnel: air has changed.

A nose pressed to a shoulder: move.

A musk-mark at the fork: this way has been used, this way holds food, this way remembers us.

The city’s roar would be the sky of my life. Always there, even in sleep, except that sleep would fold it into the body until it became less sound than condition. When the great drafts came, I would feel them first in my teeth, in the tremble of root fibers overhead, in the way the burrow entrance began to breathe grit. Then the world outside would become too large and too violent. We would seal ourselves in. The root curtain would thrash. Seed pods would clatter like dry bones. Flyers would cling tight to their eaves. Beetles would vanish into seams. Leaves would shut themselves.

In those hours, the city would seem empty.

But beneath it, inside walls, under dust, within roots, life would be gathered inward.

I would wait there with the others, heart slowed, nose tucked, listening to the old stone sing above us.

When the wind weakened, the return would begin by increments. Not a dawn, exactly, but an opening. First the pressure easing against the plug. Then a smell from the avenue: salt, lizard skin, old guano, flowering wax. A cricket beginning somewhere in the roots, its scrape magnified and carried. Beetles would test the dust roads. One of us would nose at the door, pause, push through.

I would emerge into dimness.

Not boldly. Never boldly. I would come out low, whiskers spread, forefeet light on the powder. The avenue would be changed in small ways: a new drift against a stone lip, seeds collected in a crack, a dead moth turned onto its back, a predator’s track pressed cleanly across our usual path. Far above, a thin shaft of light would hang full of dust like a solid thing. Root fibers would sway, then settle.

Food would be everywhere and scarce.

A seed lodged beside a salt crust. A strip of shed root bark. Fungal bloom on old dung. The brittle leaf of a shrub fallen in the wind. I would gather what I could, not greedily but with the urgency of a creature whose abundance must be carried underground before the city changes its mind.

Sometimes I would cross the open basin.

That would be the great act of my life, repeated often enough to become ordinary and never safe enough to become careless. The basin would lie pale and exposed, dust compacted flat by wind and passage. Shrubs would stand far apart like markers set by no hand. Tracks of grazers would make shallow oval depressions. Beetle roads would curve delicately between them. The root curtain on the far side would be visible, but distance in that place would be not visual; it would be bodily. Distance would mean time uncovered.

I would wait at the edge until the air slackened.

Then go.

A low run, not frantic. Stops at stones. Whiskers forward. Ears flattened. The taste of old predator musk near the third shrub. A lizard motionless on a wall, no threat unless I come too close. Crickets scattering in the roots ahead. Above, a colony of flyers shifting, claws ticking against mineral eaves.

Halfway across, the wind might rise.

Then the whole city would lean into one note. Dust lifting. Seeds skittering past. Shrub stems rattling. My fur pressed flat against my ribs. I would lower myself into the shallow print of a grazer’s foot and become, for a moment, only another pale lump in the basin. The law would be simple: lose less. Breathe less. Be stone until movement is permitted again.

This is what I would be: not master of the undercity, not witness above it, but one of its small continuities.

A keeper of no history except paths.

And yet, through me, the city would remember.

In my cached seeds, some future shrub. In my abandoned tunnel, a passage for beetles. In my droppings, food for wall-mats and pale roots. In my fur, spores carried from guano district to root grove. In my bones, when the time came, a little architecture of salt and calcium at the margin of a corridor. My life would alter the place almost imperceptibly, as all the lives there do, and the accumulation of those almost-nothings would be the city’s true building.

I would be a citizen of dryness.

One of the small furred miners under the root avenues, living by restraint, by memory of pressure, by the taste of minerals and the patience of sealed chambers. I would know grandeur only as danger, architecture only as shelter, and the metropolis only as a pattern of routes between hunger, wind, and warmth.

And if someone larger passed through that chamber—if some impossible observer stood in the basin and listened to the enormous roar—they might never see me. They might notice only a slight movement at the base of a root curtain, a brief dark bead of an eye, a plug of dust disturbed and sealed again.

But below their notice, I would be there.

Breathing carefully.

Holding water in my blood.

Waiting for the wind to permit the next street.
