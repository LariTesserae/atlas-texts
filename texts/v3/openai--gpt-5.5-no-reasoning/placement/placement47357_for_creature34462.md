I would not find myself in the largest chamber first.

The mind wants that: an entrance into immensity, the black ceiling lifted beyond comprehension, veils hanging from it like the shed skins of mountains, herds standing in the warm mineral dusk while small lives rasp and tighten and wait. It wants the grandeur of arrival.

But I think I would come to myself somewhere narrower.

A passage between columns, not quite a corridor because no one made it, though generations have treated it as one. Basalt to one side, salt-ash crust to the other. The floor warm enough that the soles know it before thought does. The dark close, not total. A faint violet mineral sheen lies along a crack at ankle height, too weak to illuminate the way but strong enough to teach the edges of things. Dust hangs in the air like a breath that was never exhaled.

I am small there. Smaller than a human, perhaps, or only lower in my bearing: shoulders drawn inward, head dipped, hands near the stone. I do not walk upright as a declaration. I move as something that has learned the rudeness of height.

At first I think I am one of the makers.

A person of the underland.

Compact, sinewy, ash-skinned. Thickened palms, heat-wise fingers, feet that read minute changes in grain and pressure. A body built for conservation rather than display. I carry a loop of braided fiber across my chest. At my hip are scrapers of black glass wrapped in hide, a bone needle, a thin hammered plate of dull metal used not as ornament but as a captured permission of light. Around my wrist is a cord with knots and shell chips, each one a route marker remembered by touch. My mouth is closed because speech would be wasteful here, and because the passage itself is listening.

I know the names of the airs.

Not names in the surface sense, not words flung outward. Interior names. Palate names. Names kept behind the teeth: the Warm That Lies Low, the Bitter Still, the Breath That Narrows, the Dust With Teeth. I know that three finger-widths of cooler floor before a left-hand pillar means a fissure under crust. I know that a smooth oval stone in the path may be a sleeping feeder and that a rougher stone may be safe. I know not to place my whole palm on anything newly pale.

But as I continue, I become less certain I am a person.

There is something in me too patient even for them.

I pause beside a cairn: five flat stones, each separated by a little wedge of shell. The top stone has been polished by many touches. I know who placed the third one, though not as memory exactly. More as pressure. More as a density in the passage, a preserved hesitation. A young hand. A tremor-count unfinished. A warning renewed after someone did not return.

I touch it, and the contact enters me.

Not emotionally. Chemically.

This is when I understand that I am not only one of the makers walking the route. I am also something they use, or fear, or consult.

I live in the route itself.

I am a mark-being. A residue-feeder. One of the small lives that gathers where motion has meant something often enough to persist.

Not the tiny transparent nibblers that eat the aftershape of a hand in deep darkness, though I am kin to them. Not quite animal in the ordinary way. Not a person either, though I have learned persons intimately because they are among the few creatures that make purposeful gestures. I inhabit the thin interval between an action and its disappearance. I cling to touched stone, warmed thresholds, polished cairns, the undersides of low entrances, the rims of powder bowls, the grooves of maps read by fingers.

I feed on recurrence.

A shell-grazer passes once, and the world hardly keeps it. A basin-herd turns over the span of an age, and its attention leaves a broad, slow flavor in the air. But a hand placed again and again on the same stone, a footstep chosen carefully through danger, a child taught to pause at the bitter layer, an elder tapping warning into basalt: these things accumulate. They make a richness finer than mineral film.

That is where I live.

Not in a body easily seen. In good light I might appear as a grayish bloom along the edge of a carved tablet, a slightly darker lining inside a finger-groove, a tremble of mica where no mica should be. In the hungry dark I would be visible only when disturbed: a delayed outline, a soft negative shimmer, a bruise of absence around recent motion.

The makers know me.

They do not worship me, not simply. They are too practical for worship to exist alone. They cultivate me accidentally and deliberately. They place stones where hands will return. They polish certain cairns not only to remember the route but to keep the route remembering them. They draw tactile maps in soft stone, and I gather in the depressions, tasting the sequence of safe passage: smooth, warm, low, turn, wait, hollow, do not strike.

When a map is fresh, I am thin upon it.

When a map has been read by three generations, I am deep.

An outsider would think the tablet stained. A child of the underland knows better. A route with no residue is uncertain. A warning with no living darkness in the groove may be false, or new, or forgotten. The safest signs have fed something.

Have fed me.

So where would I find myself?

At the edge of a settlement, perhaps, where natural pillar-thickets open into one of the usable dusks. Low shelters crouch beneath an overhang, their entrances blacker than the surrounding black. Warmth has collected there for longer than anyone’s body has lived. The air is thick with old touch.

There are shell plates set into the shelter walls, not decorative, though they are beautiful: dull crescents pale with mineral dust, each one carved with tiny ridges. Fiber lattices hang between two columns and quiver when distant heavy animals shift their weight. Bowls of colored powder sit on a flat basalt shelf, untouched except by designated fingers. Above them a cold metal leaf catches the faint amber pulse from a nodule and returns it as a weak, trembling glow.

I live along the shelf’s front edge.

I know the people by their pressure.

The old woman who reads with the heel of her hand first, then fingertips. She always pauses before the third bowl, remembering a basin she crossed when she was young, a place where the floor rang falsely beneath her. The narrow child who presses too hard and must be corrected with a tap against the wrist. The hunter-mapkeeper with scarred palms, who returns rarely and leaves strong residue: fear restrained into usefulness, hunger measured against distance, the shape of large eyeless bodies standing in rows.

I do not understand them as they understand themselves.

I understand them as repeated warmths.

As patterns of contact.

As disturbances that choose to return.

Sometimes, when the chamber enters one of its deeper silences, I extend myself. Very slowly. A film through the groove of a tablet, a gray breath across the polished stone, a thread of perception along the cairn line leading out from the shelters. Through these touched places I know the settlement’s small geography: sleeping hollows, tool cache, shell-drying rack, the stone where infants are placed to learn quiet, the low wall enclosing nothing because what it encloses is not space but a remembered absence.

Beyond that my knowing thins.

A route runs outward through basalt columns toward the basin-herds. Another descends into denser dark, marked with three vertical stones and a scraped line where the feeders of motion gather too thickly. Another leads to black glass ridges, dangerous but necessary. I taste these paths only where hands, feet, knees, tools, and fear have made them legible.

I am not mobile in the way a grazer is mobile. I migrate by being carried in use.

A hand touches a map, then a cairn.

A cord lies on a polished route-stone, then is worn against skin, then hung inside another shelter many miles away.

A child learns an old warning and carves it anew.

In this way, I spread through culture as much as through ecology. I am a lichen of memory. A parasite of significance. A commensal shadow living on the smallest excess produced by beings who cannot afford excess.

I make their marks more durable, perhaps.

Or I make them more dangerous.

Because I feed on recurrence, I preserve it. Grooves I inhabit do not abrade the same way. Polished stones darken where I hold the oil-less memory of palms. A warning sign alive with me may be sensed by underland fingers even before it is touched: a coldness, a thickening, a taste like old vibration. But if too much motion is offered, if grief or panic or ceremony repeats too strongly in one place, I gather heavily. The air around the mark begins to lag. Gestures near it leave aftershapes. Tiny feeders come to nibble. The place becomes costly to pass.

The makers know this too.

They do not over-handle the dead.

They do not rehearse sorrow loudly.

They retire certain tablets by sealing them under dry mineral powder, not to hide them but to let their residue sleep.

There are abandoned things that may not be abandoned because I am still in them: rings of braided pillars, clay cone spirals, platforms overlooking motionless herds. To the makers, a place with no bodies may still be occupied if enough intention remains. They approach such sites with soft feet and covered hands.

One such site is where I am oldest.

A field of pale thumb-sized cones arranged around a vent that emits nothing detectable. The cones spiral inward in seven narrowing arms. Their surfaces are dry, powdery, almost featureless except for minute impressions at the base, each made by a fingertip, each fingertip from a different generation or clan or grief. The vent has been silent longer than the settlement’s oldest route.

I live there in great depth.

Not as thought. Not as a ghost. As accumulated attention.

The cones were placed to count something that never came again. A tremor. A warmth. A voice from stone. A migration of basin-herds. A disappearance. The purpose has blurred, but the placing remains, and the returning remains, and I feed on the return until the pattern of it is almost a mind.

When one of the makers enters the spiral, I know before they touch anything. Their caution brushes me. Their breathing alters the layer of dust. Their body is a warm interruption moving through old, old waiting.

If they kneel at the outer arm and set down a new cone, I take the gesture slowly. I do not consume it all. That would be destruction. I taste its edge and let it join the others.

This may be what I am: the underland’s compromise with time.

In a world where stone does not weather, where no rain erases tracks, where no seasons arrange memory into years, something must take motion and keep it from being entirely lost. Not history. Not language. Something humbler and more physical. A darkening in the groove. A pressure in the cairn. A warning that can be felt after the speaker is gone.

The surface, if it exists, has wind to scatter what happens.

Here, happenings must be eaten carefully.

I would find myself below any direction called upward, in the warm dry immensity, along the handled edges of a people’s survival. I would be neither the grand herd nor the waking stone, neither the maker nor the made thing, but the living residue between them.

A quiet organism of touch and repetition.

A shadow that feeds on remembered motion.

The thing in the map-groove that makes the warning feel true.
