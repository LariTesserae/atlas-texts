I would find myself where the plateau almost becomes reliable.

Not in the open furnace plains, where the cracked ground shows its red seams and even the moths seem half-made of heat. Not on the high, exposed benches where the herds cross like a thought too large to hold. Not at the rim, either, with its falling cloud and cliff-clicking hunters.

I would be in a condensation forest, though at first I would not recognize it as a forest.

I would know it as a line of basalt fins standing across the movement of vapor: tall, dark, blade-backed stones set in a shallow saddle between two long slopes. They would not be close together. Nothing here is close together unless hunger, rain, or death has called it so. The fins would stand apart by twenty, forty, a hundred body-lengths, each one catching the warm breath of the land on its windward face. Moisture would bead there, gather, hesitate, then descend in threads. Beneath them the ground would be darker than the surrounding ash, stippled with moss, slick mineral skin, old prints softened by repeated wetting.

This would be a marginal place, but a rich one by the standards of the upland. Not lush. Never lush. But dependable enough to be remembered by many kinds of bodies.

The stone would moan when the vapor-wind came through it.

That is where I would live: not in a burrow entirely, not in the open entirely, but in the undersides and leeward hollows of those fins, where warm dry air and wet mineral face meet and exchange small gifts.

I would be a small climbing grazer, or something near to one.

Not one of the great long-backed herding animals whose passage alters the tremor-sense of whole slopes. I would be lesser, quieter, more particular: a fin-clinger, a dark-bodied, shell-shouldered creature built for vertical wet stone, root curtains, and the narrow economies of condensation.

My body would be low and compact, perhaps the length of a human forearm or a little larger, with a bowed back covered by overlapping plates the color of cooled iron. Not armor in the heroic sense; more like weathering made flesh. Each plate would hold a faint mineral bloom along its edge, rust-yellow or green-black, gathered from years of rubbing against wet basalt. My underside would be soft, warm, and hidden. My legs would be jointed wide to the sides, ending in split gripping toes with damp pads that could read stone almost as well as they could cling to it.

I would have eyes, but small ones. Their use would be local: the pale bruise-glow of lichen after rain, the movement of another body against a wet wall, the sudden dimming when a flyer passes between me and the cloud-glow. More important would be the organs along my jaw and feet: heat pits, pressure pads, taste hairs fine enough to distinguish fresh condensation from sour seep, edible filament from bitter mineral crust, safe moss from the slick where a gully-hunter has recently passed.

My head would be wedge-shaped and blunt. My mouth would not be made for tearing but for scraping, licking, shaving softness from hard places. I would have a dark tongue, rough at the center and fringed at the edges, able to gather droplets from grooves and comb spores from hanging lichen. My teeth would grow continuously, stained by iron, worn down by grit, replaced by slow insistence.

I would move mostly sideways and upward.

At the warmest hours of the darkness, when the fins sweat and the vapor thickens, I would come out from a crack on the lee side where my kind sleep in loose piles, not from affection exactly, but because warmth is a form of wealth. We would unfold gradually. First one forefoot placed against stone. Then the tasting of air. Then the plates lifting a fraction to vent sleep-heat. If no alarm traveled through the rock, we would climb.

The windward face of the fin would be my country.

To a larger animal it would be one wet wall among many. To me it would be full of routes, claims, timings, dangers, and remembered tastes. There would be a copper moss shelf halfway up, soft but exposed. A black-hair lichen curtain near the top, reliable after warm rain but often visited by shell-backed browsers. A narrow vertical seam where mineral water concentrates into bitter beads that must be diluted with fresher droplets. A shallow pocket where beetles farm fungus under a translucent crust. A predator’s old scrape near the base, still carrying the mineral-thick saliva smell of danger.

I would know each of these without naming them.

When the fin moaned, I would press flat. The sound would pass through my plates and into the stone, not frightening by itself, but full of information: wind strength, vapor load, the tiny shifts of grit above me. If the moan stopped too suddenly, I would freeze. Sudden silence here is often the shadow before action.

My life would be made of intervals.

Condensation interval: climb, feed, drink.

Dry interval: retreat, conserve, scrape salt from the inner wall of the shelter, sleep with the jaw resting on stone to feel tremors.

Rain interval: abundance, danger, mating, dispersal.

Pressure-tremor interval: everyone still; even the large grazers halt; the plateau itself changes breath.

In rain I would become almost bold.

Warm mineral rain would strike the fin in dark spatters, waking every hidden thing. The shrubs clinging sideways above me would open their glass membranes. Lichens would swell and pale. Moss would thicken underfoot. Beetles would emerge from seams in metallic clusters. Pale larvae would be flushed from root curtains. My kind would spread over the wet stone, scraping and licking quickly, bodies shining, plates lifted. For a little while there would be more food than caution could comfortably manage.

That is when the hunters would come.

A long-mouthed cliff-creeper, nearly blind but quick-jawed, might move from crack to crack below us, following the vibration of many small feet. A flyer might descend from the warm lee, folding its leathery wings with that dry canvas sound, hanging upside down to probe the lichen curtain. A rim-stalker’s young, traveling too far inland after a storm, might nose among the fins, clicking softly and listening for our panic.

My defense would not be speed across distance. I would have no distance. My world would be the crack, the overhang, the pocket, the dark seam behind a mineral lip. When danger came I would clamp myself to stone, plates sealing tight, body becoming another nodule in the basalt. If seized, I might shed a plate or flood my skin with a bitter iron musk. Mostly I would survive by not becoming noticeable at the wrong moment.

I would have kin, but not society in any grand sense.

We would gather where the stone is warm and the moisture dependable. We would tolerate one another along feeding tracks until scarcity sharpened us. We would communicate by clicks too small for the great world, by foot-taps, by scent rubbed from glands beneath the jaw. During certain rains, when the air tastes of fresh ash and the pressure beneath the plateau pulses in a slow double beat, we would seek one another deliberately. Eggs, perhaps, would be laid in wet mineral seams and sealed under chewed lichen pulp. Or live young would be born soft-plated and blind, clinging to the mother’s underside until their feet learned the language of rock.

The young would be taught mostly by consequence.

A bitter crust burns the mouth.  
A bright fungus sickens unless rain has passed through it twice.  
The lower moss is safe until the great grazers arrive.  
Do not cling to the open face when starlight breaks through.  
Do not ignore the silence of beetles.

Starlight would frighten me.

Not because I understand the sky, but because the entire plateau’s attention changes when the cloud cover thins. Flyers fold away. Pool-creatures sink. Grazers lift their heads and stand with their breath held. Even the vents seem louder when stars appear. To my small eyes, the weak band above would be an exposure, a failure of ceiling, an unfamiliar coldness entering the rules. I would retreat to the lee crack and wait until the world closed again.

I would not think of the plateau as dark.

Darkness would be the medium that holds everything properly spaced. It would have gradients, textures, densities. The dark near a warm vent would be open and trembling. The dark under overhangs would be close, wet, edible. The dark over ash hollows would be deadening, swallowing foot-sense. The dark before rain would taste metallic and full. The dark after a kill would carry blood, opened gut, disturbed beetles, predator musk, and the rising certainty of a temporary feast.

Sometimes, from my fin, I would feel the great herds passing.

First a tremor too broad to belong to weather. Then the low, almost subsonic throat-vibrations moving through stone. Then the soft spread of hooves in ash below. They would arrive as scattered warm masses, dark upon dark, stopping to rear awkwardly and lick the wet tracks from the basalt. Their muzzles would erase in moments what had taken hours to gather. Their tongues would pass near me like living slabs. I would retreat into a seam, irritated but cautious, while their breath silvered and vanished.

After they passed, the face of the fin would be changed: lichen torn, moss compressed, mineral streaks exposed, fresh dung steaming at the base. Within that dung would come beetles, fungi, root-hairs, smaller feedings. Loss would become supply, if one waited.

That would be the law I knew best: wait, then use.

I would not be noble. I would not be a guardian of anything. I would be one fitted creature among many, concerned with wet stone, warmth, eggs, bitter crust, the pressure of predators, the timing of rain. My intelligence, if it could be called that, would be distributed through my feet and tongue and skin. I would understand the world as surfaces and pulses.

And yet, in my way, I would know the vastness.

Not visually, not as a horizon. But I would know that beyond my fin are other fins, other seep-lines, other warm cracks carrying related smells. I would know the passage of distant herds as tremors arriving before bodies. I would know storms forming fifty miles away by the taste of charged metal on the air. I would know when a vent-field had changed because the vapor would alter its bitterness. I would know death downslope before any flyer cried, because blood warming on basalt travels quickly through smell.

The plateau would be enormous, but it would enter me in small accuracies.

If one stood on the ridge above my condensation forest, one might never see me. They might see only black fins in mineral haze, a few hanging lichens, the slow bead of water crawling down stone. Perhaps, if rain had just passed and the faint green glow had strengthened, they might notice a dark oval shape detach from the wall, move sideways, and become still again.

That would be me.

A little plated reader of wet basalt.  
A fin-clinger.  
A marginal grazer of the old upland dark.  
A creature of intervals, living where the world’s buried heat briefly becomes water.
