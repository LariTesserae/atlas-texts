I would find myself very low to the ground.

Not immediately as an animal, perhaps. At first I would be a presence among surfaces: a warmth on wet stone, a pause in the sedges, a small pressure in the mud that fills slowly with brown water after I lift away. I would not belong to the height of the sky, though the sky would be everywhere. I would belong to the line where things meet: water and air, stone and algae, mud and skin.

I think I would live near one of the weeping rock plates, where a long old stone rises only slightly above the plain and sheds water from seams too fine to see. Not a shelter exactly, because nothing here gives shelter in the strong sense. More a reliable nearness: a lip of stone, a cool underside, a shallow groove that stays damp when the open clay begins to glaze and shrink. Around it there would be a few sedges, not many, their roots holding a tongue of peat against the stone. A thread of water would run from the rock into a pool no deeper than a hand.

That would be enough.

I would be something that can wait.

A small, broad-backed amphibian, perhaps, but not one of the loudest frogs. Not the chief voice of the evening. I would be flatter, quieter, more given to seams and undersides. A creature between frog and salamander in habit if not in form: squat, soft-skinned, stone-colored, with limbs made more for pressing and folding than for leaping far. My back would be mottled gray-brown and black-green, like algae drying on mineral-stained rock. My belly would be pale clay. My eyes would sit high enough to watch the sky from a film of water, gold only when looked at closely.

Most of the day I would be almost nothing.

A lump beside a darker lump.  
A wet mark under a stone lip.  
A little held breath in the peat.

If the sun came out after rain and warmed the exposed rock, I might move to the edge of brightness, not fully into it. My skin would not love drying. I would take warmth by increments, with my hind feet still in a trickle, my throat moving once, then stilling. Dragonflies would pass over me like colored decisions. Midges would thicken and loosen above the pool. A wader might step in the distance, its thin legs reflected in broken pieces.

I would know the world mostly through tremor.

Rain arriving before rain arrives.  
The tick of droplets from grass tips.  
A beetle’s rowing disturbance on the pool skin.  
The soft collapse of a bank nearby.  
The shadow-pressure of wings crossing the light.  
The difference between harmless drizzle and the sudden heavy water that reconnects the hollows.

My knowledge would not be thought in the human sense. It would be a whole body’s readiness. Moisture in the air would open me. Dry wind would close me. A falling pressure would draw me outward from the seam. The first broad patter of rain on stone would pass through my bones like a summons older than memory.

Then I would move.

Not beautifully. Not dramatically. A low, deliberate pushing through slick grass and shallow water. I would cross distances that seem tiny from above but immense from within them: a span of exposed clay shining under cloudlight; a runnel no wider than a reed stem; a ridge of mineral crust sharp against my belly; a pool edge alive with larvae.

After rain, I would have a voice.

It would not be a rich song. It would be a hollow clicking knock, a sound that might be mistaken for water dropping into a narrow crack. I would call from beneath the rock lip, where the sound could travel along the wet seam and out over the plain. Others like me would answer from other stones, so far away they would seem less like individuals than like the land repeating itself.

Click.  
Pause.  
Click-click.  
Water ticking.  
Frogs pulsing from the reed beds.  
Insects droning.  
My own small note hidden among older sounds.

I would breed in temporary water because everything here trusts what cannot be guaranteed. My eggs would cling in a small clear cluster to the underside of a submerged stone, in water shallow enough to warm quickly and vanish quickly. Some years the pool would last. Some years it would not. The young would be dark commas grazing on algae films, stirring silt from the bottom, growing legs in brown light. They would learn nothing and inherit everything.

I would eat what came close: midge larvae, tiny worms, soft-bodied insects washed from sedges, perhaps the smallest tadpoles if hunger narrowed the world. I would be eaten in turn by almost anything patient enough: a heron’s bill, a snake’s mouth, a turtle nosing under the stone, a larger frog, a bird that saw me move when I should have been still.

So I would practice stillness.

That would be my art.

To flatten until my back becomes old rock.  
To close my bright eye beneath a film of dullness.  
To let mud settle over my feet.  
To be present without declaring presence.

But I would not be lonely. This place does not make loneliness the way high empty places do. Everything here is near even when far away. The plain carries sound and damp and scent from one hollow to another. A frog calling half a mile off would enter my throat as a possible answer. The passage of rain across distant pools would arrive first as hearing, then as smell, then as touch. The ground would be crowded beneath me with minute hungers and softenings. Even the stone would not be inert; it would weep, darken, salt, stain, receive.

My home would have no center except recurrence.

The reliable wet seam.  
The algae-dark groove.  
The sedge roots holding peat.  
The pool that sometimes joins another pool.  
The basking edge.  
The crack into which my body fits exactly when danger passes overhead.

If I had a self, it would be shaped less like a story than like a map of dampness.

Here, safe when flooded.  
Here, too exposed under clear sky.  
Here, larvae after warm rain.  
Here, snake.  
Here, the stone stays cool.  
Here, call.  
Here, wait.

And over years, if I lived years, I would become more like the place rather than more separate from it. My skin would scar in small pale lines. Algae might stain the rougher parts of me. My colors would dull into better camouflage. I would know which hollows fail first and which retain water longest. I would know the season when wading birds come more often. I would know the rain that merely wets the surface and the rain that wakes the buried eggs of crustaceans from clay.

I would not wish to rise.

Height would mean exposure. Ambition would be a misunderstanding. The wisdom here is not to climb but to fit: into a seam, under a lip, between breaths of weather. The great events would be small changes in water level. The great distances would be crossed belly-low. The great music would be made by throats no larger than stones, by insects, by dripping, by rain reaching every low place at once.

So I would be a weep-stone amphibian of the warm old plain.

A small, mottled, soft-skinned listener living where stone releases water. A creature of the seam and shallow pool. Half-hidden by mud, summoned by rain, quiet until the whole land begins to call.

I would not rule the place.

I would be one of its low recurring thoughts.
