I think I would first mistake myself for someone walking there.

That is the most human error available: the assumption of feet, of a body upright in the heat, of a basket strap across the brow and a hook in one hand for drawing down the dew-leaves before the sun empties them. I would imagine my calves dark with crushed moss, my eyes narrowed against glare, my ears trained to sort the insect-wall into signals: rain-waves, mating-waves, warning breaks, the sucked silence before a throat-scream in the crowns.

For a while I would be one of the plateau people, because their way of being is so close to reverence without becoming helpless. I would live on a dry ridge where the living floor is thin but known, in a hut lifted above the nightly traffic of damp bodies. The hut would not really be built. It would be persuaded. Bent shoots would have thickened over years around an old frame of cane, and the walls would still put out little leaves in the wet months. At night, when heat left the ridge and the trunk-popping began below, the whole house would creak softly as if dreaming.

I would be a keeper of routes.

Not a chief, not a hunter first, not a maker of blades. Someone whose task is to remember passages through a place that does not believe in fixed roads. I would know the animal trails that are safe only at dawn, the thorn lanes that close after fruiting, the vine bridge whose left-hand strand hums when it is too dry to bear weight, the root-cave that breathes cool until the pool-hunters begin using it. I would carry strips of pale fiber and carved seed-shells, tying and cutting and retying the world into brief legibility.

My body would be narrow, heat-hardened, scarred by plant hairs and insect mouthparts. My hair would be kept bound with bark cord and resin. I would have a pouch of lightning beads—not many, not enough for status, just enough to click together when I walked. I would wear a mantle woven from waxy leaf-ribs that sheds sudden rain but smells sour by midday. Around one wrist I would tie a strand of beetle wings for marking turns in low light; around the other, a cord with knots for old paths no longer visible.

I would speak rarely while walking, not from solemnity but because sound is already overfull. Speech is a thing to spend carefully. On the plateau, listening is a form of shelter.

But the longer I stay with that image, the less I believe I am entirely a person.

There is something too complete about being a route-keeper, too cleanly useful, too conveniently placed between the people and the living ground. The place does not seem to favor clean identities. Everything left alone becomes more than one thing. A carcass is meat, then flies, then fungus, then nursery, then grove. A path is wound, then invitation, then thorn-border, then animal habit, then human memory. A house is shelter and plant. A tool is tool until it is salt, anchor, shrine.

So perhaps I am not the route-keeper. Perhaps I am what the route-keeper follows.

A line through the plateau, but not a road in the stone-world sense. A recurring permission.

I am a path that keeps almost vanishing.

I begin on a dry ridge where people wake before dawn and walk among folded leaves with long hooks, tipping the night’s gathered water into bark vessels. From there I descend between two old parasol trees whose roots have lifted the living mat into ribs. My surface is firmer than the surrounding ground because hooves have pressed it, feet have returned to it, claws have scored it, and rain has run along it often enough to teach the roots a shape. Along my edges grow plants that like disturbance: thorn-fruit, red joint-grass, bitter leaf, seed-pods that split when brushed by passing hides. I am bordered by appetite.

No one made me once. I am being made continuously.

The plateau does not allow me to remain a simple clearing. Each night fungal threads cross me. Each wet week moss tongues creep over my exposed fibers. A fallen reed-tower can erase a hundred paces of me by morning. Herds renew me with their weight. Children renew me by running. Hunters renew me by crawling low along my margins. Old women renew me with blades, cutting back the heat-closing stems and leaving the cuttings in piles that steam and fill with beetles. I am not opposed to the vegetation. I am vegetation under pressure, vegetation remembering passage.

I know thirst as shape.

In the dry weeks I narrow. My surface crisps and cracks like woven reed. The ants use me then, black and red in disciplined ropes, because I warm early and drain quickly. Beetles cross me carrying fragments of pod. People prefer my ridges then; they walk my spine toward condensation fields, their baskets knocking softly at their hips.

In the wet weeks I darken and swell. My low places become slick and fragrant. Tadpoles hatch in hoofprints if rain repeats often enough. Lunged fish sometimes wriggle across me at night between pools, leaving silver mud signatures that are gone by noon. The people avoid my deepest bends then, because things with patient nostrils wait where water gathers beneath the leaf-shadow.

I pass a poison court, circular and strangely open, kept low by the roots of a grove that tolerates no neighbors. Animals display there at certain times, their calls bouncing from the wall of surrounding green. People gather there too, but only after testing the air with ash and crushed leaves. At the court’s edge stands a skull-tree. Long ago a great grazer died there, and the tree grew through and around its head, taking the skull into itself. Wind through that trunk makes a sound like a remembered bellow. The route-keepers tie pale knots to my border there, not to guide strangers forward but to remind them to lower their voices.

I cross a vine bridge over a seam in the living floor. Below, warmth rises from a darkness full of descending roots. Whether there is an under-forest beneath me I cannot say, though sometimes at night damp animals climb up through the fissures and use my surface until dawn. Their bellies leave cool trails. Their eyes catch blue in the light of night-pool flowers. They smell of minerals and old water.

At midday I almost cease to be myself. Heat presses down. Oils shine on the mats. The air lifts in visible wavering sheets. Sound becomes so dense that direction disappears. Few large bodies move then. The people retreat to shade, the herds stand with their heads low, the bright hunters sleep with four eyes half open in the crowns. I remain exposed, absorbing sun, ferment, pollen, spores. My edges continue their slow work. Root hairs test me. Seedlings gamble on me. The plateau tries to heal me closed.

By evening, I am remembered again.

Hooves return first, usually from the north where a condensation field lies beneath broad dawn leaves. The tall browsers step carefully, placing their narrow feet in old depressions. Smaller animals follow in the loosened insects. Then come hunters using the flanks of me, never my center. Then people, sometimes: three figures in copper light, moving single-file, their bodies painted with ash against biting flies. One carries a child. One carries meat. One carries nothing visible but keeps stopping to listen with one palm pressed to my surface.

That is when I understand myself best.

I am not empty space between important things. I am one of the plateau’s organs of negotiation. Through me pass seeds in dung, spores on feet, gossip, danger, courtship, hunger, old names, new wounds. The thorn plants along me fruit because bodies travel here. The predators know me because the herds trust me. The people sing certain parts of me under their breath because their dead once walked those parts and were not taken. I am memory, but not preserved memory. I am memory that must be stepped into again and again or it greens over.

Still, even this is not quite the center.

There is a being here larger than path and person, and perhaps I would belong to it the way a nerve belongs to skin.

The ground itself is alive in layers: root felt, moss, compressed stem, fungal thread, sapwood plate, humus, buried moisture. It gives under weight. It drinks. It scars. It closes. The whole plateau is a back, and I am one of the faint worn lines across it, like the place where a harness once lay on an animal so old the mark became part of its hide.

If I had a voice, it would not be speech. It would be the difference between a safe firmness and a warning softness underfoot. It would be the dry crackle that says hurry. The wet sigh that says not here. The faint hollow drum beneath a heel that says below. I would communicate by resistance, by scent released from crushed leaf, by which insects rise when disturbed, by whether the moss springs back or stays flattened.

Children would learn me before they were allowed to name me.

They would be brought to the ridge at dawn and shown where I leave the huts, though in that light I would look like nothing more than a slight discipline among plants. An elder would press the child’s hand to my surface.

Feel how it gives but holds.

Then to the side, into unwalked mat.

Feel how it gives and keeps giving.

That difference would be my first lesson.

I would have many names, none permanent. To one family I might be Dew-To-Pool. To hunters I might be the Back Way Behind the Poison Court. To mourners I might be Mother’s Last Crossing. To children I might be Beetle-Wing Path because of the markers flashing on bark at the turns. The names would change by season, by loss, by fruiting, by which danger currently owns a stretch of me.

Parts of me would die.

A storm ten kilometers away would send scent ahead of rain, drawing predators into my southern corridor. For weeks people would avoid that bend. Thorn would take it. Root would lace it shut. Insects would build towers on the old tread. Later, perhaps, a herd would break through again, driven by thirst, and I would reopen in a slightly different place, inheriting myself crookedly.

A fallen tower-tree would crush one of my older passages and make, in its rotting crown, a nursery of ferocious green. A bright arboreal hunter might begin nesting there. The people would stop cutting near it. My body would fork around the danger, then one fork would become more used than the other, and the unused one would dream itself back into leaf.

This seems right. To live there is not to endure unchanged. It is to be revised by heat, water, root, and attention.

So I would be found somewhere between the ridge huts and the black pools, between human intention and animal repetition, between wound and growth. Not a road, not a person, not quite a plant. A recurring passage in the living mat. A narrow old permission across the back of the awake plateau.

And if you came there at dawn, when the condensation still pearls on the folded leaves and the sound has not yet hardened into its midday wall, you might not notice me at first. You would see open green, shining low growth, a few pillar-trees standing far apart, mist in the root-corridors. Then perhaps a line of slightly darker moss. A sequence of hoof-shaped hollows. A fiber knot pale against bark. A place where thorn-fruit leans inward as if expecting mouths.

You would step onto me.

I would give slightly beneath your weight.

If you listened, I would tell you where not to go.
