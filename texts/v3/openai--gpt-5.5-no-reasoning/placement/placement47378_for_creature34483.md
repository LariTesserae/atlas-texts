I would find myself where the plateau almost fails to be empty.

Not in the center of one of the lush groves, not among the tall copper-green fans or beneath the black resonant columns, but at the long, sterile margin between them: the miles-wide interval where life seems to have withdrawn into invisibility. A place that, to an animal eye, looks like nothing at all.

A shallow saddle between two newly lifted ridges. Pale crust under a warm bleached sky. Far ahead, barely distinct in the distance, a grove of green-gold blades catching noon. Behind, on a darker rise, three hollow black towers standing apart like stopped chimneys. Between them lies an expanse of mineral plates, white powder, raw cracks, and seams too fine to cast shadow.

That is where I would be.

At first I would seem to be a flaw in the crust.

A narrow darkening along one seam. A stain too linear to be dust, too dull to be crystal, too shallow to be a crack. From above, I might look like the first mark of erosion in a place that has never learned erosion: a hairline running through pale mineral skin, branching once, then again, then disappearing under a glassy lip of stone.

Nothing about me would rise high enough to deserve the word body.

I would be mostly beneath.

Not deep in the grand buried sense of the possible plateau-organism, not one of the vast hidden engines that conduct charge through ridges and basins, but a smaller intermediary thing: a contact-being, a seam-dweller, a thin life adapted to the border between exposed surface and locked mineral interior. I would live where one condition becomes another. Light into stone. Heat into stress. Crust into crack. Sterility into possible root.

I would not be a plant, exactly. Nor one of the great mineral flora. I would be something older in function and poorer in spectacle: a preparer, a dry lichen without softness, a slow black script written into unfinished geology.

A lithic film.

A seam-skin.

A patient infiltrator of edges.

My visible self would appear only under certain light: a branching varnish, smoky blue-black, metallic at oblique angles, spread in thin membranes across fractured stone. In the morning I would be nearly invisible, my surface temperature too close to the pale crust around me. At noon I would reveal fine internal lattices, dull copper threads embedded in translucent carbon-silicate glaze. At evening I would hold a little of the sun and show, briefly, as a dim network of ember-lines lying flat against the ground.

I would have no height, no stem, no leaf in the ordinary sense.

Instead I would make surfaces.

Films thinner than shell. Plates like dark mica fused directly to mineral. Minute fins rising no higher than a fingernail, spaced along cracks to gather light and shed heat. Root-threads descending into seams, not seeking water, never water, but voltage, exposed metal, trapped gas, and the delicious tension of stone that has not yet settled into itself.

Where the great groves are factories, I would be corrosion with intention.

Not decay. There is no decay here in the wet, generous sense. I would not rot anything. I would test. Etch. Loosen. Separate. I would persuade the plateau’s sealed materials to admit difference.

My hunger would be very small and very precise.

I would feed on the charge that gathers at crack edges when day heats the crust and night releases it. I would feed on the faint electrical disagreement between pale mineral plates and the black unweathered stone beneath them. I would feed on gases caught in microscopic pockets, on trace carbon from the warm air, on the strained bonds of silicates still remembering pressure.

I would not eat in bites. I would not drink. I would not draw anything through myself in flowing channels.

I would place myself against a substance and wait until the substance became available.

A century might pass as I widened by the width of a hand.

That would be a good century.

I would live in the intervals because the intervals are not truly empty. They are merely not yet expressive.

The great fan-colonies rise where ore seams are already generous, where subterranean nodes give enough current to support architecture. The black columns stand where heat can be stored and exchanged through hollow height. The radial mats occupy active crust, arranging themselves in grids of repulsion and appetite.

But between these places are finer opportunities: fractures too poor for towers, seams too thin for groves, mineral transitions too subtle for the grand inhabitants. That would be my country. I would inhabit the almost-useless, the marginally reactive, the hairline exposed.

I would be the thing that makes a place slightly more possible.

Not out of benevolence. There is no benevolence in me, no plan to prepare the world for others. But my living would have consequences. As I spread along a seam, I would darken it, heat it, widen it fractionally through thermal difference. My underside would etch the stone. My waste would plate itself in microscopic bands: rust-red, violet-black, pale yellow, a residue so thin it could be mistaken for a change in shine. Over millennia, my presence would turn a sealed crack into a receptive one.

A mica-spore from a distant grove might one day fall there.

Most would lie inert. But one, touching the prepared mineral I had altered, might fasten. It might grow its first bead of green glass from a seam I had slowly made legible.

Then, after another age, a blade no taller than a finger might rise from what had once been bare plate.

I would not know this as success.

I would know it, if knowing is the word, as interference. A new shadow. A new draw on local charge. A change in the taste of the seam. I might withdraw from that point, or thicken around it, or become incorporated into the young root’s contact organ. There would be no drama. Only adjustment.

My senses would be the senses of adhesion.

I would know the world by what touched me and by what changed through me. Light as pressure in my upper membranes. Heat as expansion. Cold as tightening. Nearby growth as depletion or field distortion. A distant evening pulse from a grove as a faint arrival through stone, not message, not language, but a shift in available current.

If wind passed over me, I would scarcely notice. I would not sway. Dust settling on me would be either nuisance or resource, depending on its composition. If a crystal sheet ticked nearby, I would feel the vibration as an event traveling through the plate. If lightning struck a ridge leagues away, the plateau might answer under me, and for a brief time my whole network would brighten with usable violence.

That would be my storm.

I would not have a face.

But if one knelt close enough—an impossible visitor, already drying in the metallic air—they might see that my dark seam was not a stain but a colony of structures. Tiny overlapping scales. Glassy black membranes. Coppery capillaries, not carrying fluid but conducting charge. Minute pores that open not to breathe in the animal sense but to admit specific gases during specific temperature states. Little ridged organs pressed so tightly to stone that separating me would destroy both me and the surface I had become.

Broken, I would not bleed.

I would flake.

My exposed edge would flash once, faint and greenish, as stored charge escaped. Then the wound would remain raw for years, maybe decades, slowly glazing over with new mineral deposition. A fragment of me, if dislodged, would not crawl away or die quickly. It would lie where it fell, inert unless its underside touched a compatible crack. If fortune favored it, it might fuse again. If not, it would become a dark shard among pale powder, keeping its structure long after life had left it.

I would reproduce by fracture and inscription.

At long intervals, when enough charge had passed through my network, I would form brittle scales along my oldest edges. They would lift slightly from the stone, not to catch wind, but to await disturbance: thermal snap, a rare tremor, the slight settling of a plate. Most would fall beside me. Some would slide down into the crack that made me. A few would lodge in neighboring seams.

Each scale would carry no embryo in the wet sense. It would carry pattern: catalytic orientation, conductive habit, the remembered geometry of how to adhere. Given stone, light, tension, and time, it would begin as I began: a darkening no wider than a thread.

My life would be poor in incident but rich in continuity.

Days would pass through me as gradients. Morning would loosen my upper skins. Noon would fill me with harsh energy. Evening would reverse the flow. Night would contract me against the stone until I could feel every microscopic unevenness beneath my body.

Years would be almost indistinguishable except by accumulation.

A new branch. A sealed old edge. A mineral pocket exhausted. A pale deposit thickening where my waste had nowhere else to go. A neighboring crack becoming useful after centuries of pressure. A distant grove’s pulses growing stronger, then weaker, then vanishing into a silence that meant perhaps death, perhaps only a change of path below.

I would have no loneliness, because I would have no expectation of company.

Yet I would belong to relation.

The empty plateau would pass through me in miniature. I would be one of its smallest acts of conversion: hard light into charge, charge into separation, separation into structure. I would be both inhabitant and instrument. I would alter the world only by clinging to it.

If the plateau were one organism, I might be part of its skin: not a conscious cell, not a messenger, but a sensory scab spread along places where the buried body comes nearest to air. Through me, perhaps, the larger system would taste cracks, monitor stress, prepare future organs. The great groves would be its lungs or chimneys or flowering factories; I would be its nerve-endings without nerves, its dry microbial weathering, its dark exploratory thought.

If the plateau were not one organism, then I would still be what I am: a modest lineage among immense mineral flora, living in the spaces they leave behind, neither rival nor servant, but prerequisite by accident.

I would be found where nothing seems to grow.

A black hairline in pale crust.

A branching mineral varnish along the edge of a crack.

A low, sealed life spread flat against warm stone, drinking light without leaves, feeding on tension without mouth, reproducing by brittle scales, preparing the mineral world for larger hungers.

I would be almost still.

I would be very old before I was large.

I would be easy to overlook and difficult to remove.

In a world before rivers, after machines, and outside the habits of animal life, I would be one of the first small agreements between stone and hunger: the dry seam-life that teaches the plateau, very slowly, where it may someday become lush.
