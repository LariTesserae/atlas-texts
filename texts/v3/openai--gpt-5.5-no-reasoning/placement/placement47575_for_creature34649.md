I would not arrive in the city by the trader’s path.

I would already be there, discovered only when something failed.

For a long time I thought I was one of the people: a narrow-bodied child slipping under ladders, ash in the creases of my knees, a string around my waist marking which tiers I could climb. I thought perhaps I was one of those who learned the city from below upward, first by smell, then by heat, then by danger; a child trained to freeze beneath the hawk-shadow before I had words for sky.

But I am too still for that.

Then I thought I might be one of the basin-keepers: the one who brushes the empty stone bowl each dawn, preserving thirst from becoming panic, keeping absence clean. There is something in me that belongs to that gesture—the refusal to fill what must remain unfilled, the reverence for a lack that holds the city together.

But I am not severe enough, and not separate enough.

I am nearer the work than that. Lower. More intimate. Less honored in speech, though touched every day.

I think I am found in a wall.

Not the outer wall, not the rim shrine with its bones and feather bargains, but in the middle tiers where the city is most crowded: where a goat pen overhangs a sleeping niche, where a salt pane gives weak amber light to a storage room, where a vine has been trained around a doorway so long that the doorway is now shaped like the vine rather than the other way around.

There is a crack there, old enough to have a name.

It runs diagonally through a ledge of warm stone, half hidden behind a woven screen and a row of sealed jars. Children are warned not to put their fingers into it because pale things live in the dark, because old plaster may crumble, because ants have a road inside. Elders remember that once it was wider. Ladder-makers tap near it and frown. Root-guides feed a vine toward it, then cut the vine back, then feed it again, uncertain whether the crack is hunger or danger.

That is where I would be.

At first I am only a seam.

A narrow dark place where blown dust settles, where moth eggs are laid, where the shed skins of geckos catch and do not fall. I hold coolness during the brutal hours and release it slowly after dusk. I smell of stone, old ash, bitter root, and something faintly animal, though no animal lives fully inside me. Ants pass through me in single-file black certainty. Once, long ago, someone pushed a broken bead into my depth to stop a child from swallowing it. Once a woman hid a tooth there before marriage and came back years later to whisper to it after her first child died. Once a lizard wedged itself inside and dried into a transparent curl.

But the crack continues inward.

Behind the worked plaster, behind the packed repair, behind the century of dust, there is a small chamber not large enough for a person to stand in. It was built and forgotten, or found and sealed, or perhaps never built at all but accepted into the city as a useful hollow. Its ceiling is the underside of a stone shelf. Its floor is layered with old chaff, rat bones, beetle shells, ash, and thread roots. A salt pane once let light into it from the alley, but the pane cracked and was plastered over generations ago. Now it knows light only as warmth.

In that chamber I am not a person exactly.

I am the thing the chamber has been becoming.

A keeper of what slips through use.

A small dry accumulation of memory with a body made from leftovers.

My ribs are splinters of old ladder rung. My fingers are rootlets and bird bones tied by spider silk. My face is a warped salt shard clouded from breath that no living mouth remembers breathing. My hair is saved human hair fallen from nests and cordage, braided through with moth legs, vine fiber, and the whiskers of dead mice. My back is plated with beetle wing-cases, each one dark and iridescent when a lamp is held close. My eyes are seeds that never sprouted.

I am not born all at once.

The city assembles me without intending to.

A child drops a carved tally into the crack and cannot retrieve it. A mourner presses a pinch of powdered bone into the seam during a night when no one is watching. Ants bring leaf fragments. A root enters and dies there. Smoke stains me. Breath dampens me. Ash dries me. Forgotten words cling. The city’s rules, repeated daily, settle into me like mineral.

Never leave a vessel open.

Never waste shade.

Never cut without naming.

Never shout wind unless there is wind.

Never fill the empty basin.

Every prohibition gives me a joint.

Every repaired failure gives me another little hand.

I live behind the place where things are sealed.

Most of the people do not know I exist, though some know enough to avoid naming me. Elders may suspect. Children certainly suspect. Children always know where the wall is listening. They press their ears near my crack when they are supposed to be carrying husks to the beetle troughs. They dare each other to whisper into me. They ask me questions no adult has time to answer.

Will the hawk take Red-Eye’s chick?

Will the sour vine fruit this year?

Is my dead grandmother in the wall or in the thorn bed?

If I sleep with my mouth open, will thirst crawl in?

Usually I do not answer.

Not because I am wise. Because I am slow.

I know things by accumulation, not by thought. I know the city the way plaster knows fingers. The way a jar knows what it has held by the smell remaining after it is emptied. I do not see the whole of anything. I receive pressure, vibration, dust, root-touch, heat, the passage of ants, the tremor of ladders, the change in a goat’s hooves when it is carrying young.

I know who passes by footfall.

The ash-handler has a drag in the left heel from an old wasp fever. The root-guide pauses outside the vine doorway even when no one is there, as if asking permission of leaves. The thin boy from the upper tiers takes the dangerous shortcut when angry and the safe path when ashamed. The basin-keeper walks without ornaments, and the silence around her is different from ordinary silence.

I know when a jar seal has failed before the smell reaches the room.

I know when the ledge above me has taken too much weight.

I know when termites have entered a roof beam.

I know when a child is crying without tears.

These are not miracles. They are consequences.

Everything speaks into stone eventually.

My chamber is not a shrine. No one brushes me clean. Dust is my skin. Webs are my curtains. The ants have more formal roads through me than any priest. And yet offerings come, because people cannot help making bargains with the hidden.

A bead pushed into the crack before a dangerous climb.

A twist of hair before childbirth.

A dead moth from a child who believes I keep accounts for winged things.

A seed too shriveled to plant, but too meaningful to throw away.

Once, a single drop of water.

That was the only time I almost became dangerous.

It came during a sickness year, when fever passed from house to house and the sealed stores emptied faster than they should have. A girl, half delirious, carried water in a hollow bone meant for her brother. She stumbled near my wall. One drop fell from the bone’s tip and struck the edge of the crack.

The stone darkened.

The smell rose—impossible, raw, like the inside of a living stem broken open.

Every rootlet in me woke.

For a moment I wanted.

Not as the people want, with discipline and fear and memory. I wanted stupidly, with the whole of my assembled body. I wanted to swell, to soften plaster, to push outward, to become green, to become mouth, to become basin brimming, to become rain inside the walls.

The ants scattered.

The salt shard of my face clouded.

Deep in the city, the empty basin must have known, because that dawn the basin-keeper came and stood beside my wall. She did not touch the crack. She only sat there, silent, for a long time, until the dark mark dried.

After that, I understood why emptiness must be tended.

So I remain dry.

I am not the god of the city. The city is too practical for such waste. I am not its ancestor, though I contain pieces of ancestors. I am not its judge, though I remember what is dropped and hidden. I am closer to a second memory: not the memory spoken in councils, not the memory elders carry in named stories, but the memory of accidents.

The ladder rung that cracked before anyone admitted it was old.

The first ant route into a storehouse.

The winter when three goats died behind the bird tower.

The lover who pressed both palms to the wall and promised to leave, then did not.

The trader with blue pigment who slept one night beneath the sour vine and whispered in a language no one there understood.

The child who shouted “wind” falsely and later grew into the most careful awning-maker in the hollow.

The city remembers what is useful.

I remember what almost was.

Where would I find myself?

In the dim middle tier, behind the salt-lit storage room, inside the named crack near the vine-bent doorway. Close enough to hear seed being pounded. Close enough to feel bats tremble the roofs at dusk. Close enough that ash dust sifts through me when fires are scraped clean. Close enough that the people pass me daily without seeing me, and still adjust their shoulders as they go by, as if making room.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the dry thing in the wall that listens.

The small composite creature made of refuse, omen, root, bone, ash, and withheld water.

A household spirit, if one must use a large outside word, but not a spirit of blessing. More a spirit of sealing. Of cracks noticed in time. Of scraps not lost beyond use. Of memory without monument. Of the dangerous interval between lack and hunger.

Children would call me Crack-Mother, though I am not always motherly.

Ash-handlers would call me Nothing-to-Waste.

Basin-keepers would not call me anything.

The dogs would growl at my wall during fever years.

The ants would use me as a road.

The vines would test me every night with blind white fingers.

And when the wind falls through the rim and the city becomes blind with grit, when awnings snap and goats scream and children race to lash door skins, I would wake fully. The whole hollow would knock against me. Every ladder, every jar, every ledge, every root basket and bird cage and salt pane would speak at once through stone.

Then I would know my purpose.

Not to save them grandly.

Nothing in that city is saved grandly.

But perhaps to loosen a pebble before the ledge gives way. To send ants boiling out of the crack where a child will notice them and call an elder. To make the vine tremble though there is no breeze. To click once inside the wall like a beetle, like a warning, like a remembered tally being set right.

Two taps for clear passage.

Three for hawk-shadow.

A rolling knock for escaped animal.

A flat slap for fire.

And from me, from the dark seam behind the jars, one dry little sound for hidden breakage.

The city would pause.

Someone would listen.

Someone would say, “Empty the shelf.”

And because they are who they are—careful, crowded, thirsty, bound together by use—they would obey before they understood.

That would be enough.

In this world I would not be a hero, nor a priest, nor one of the hawks circling above in clean, terrible air.

I would be the thing that scarcity accidentally made tender.

A secret made from everything spared.

A listener in the cracked crown of the plateau.

Dry as bone, crowded as a storehouse, patient as stone, waiting in the wall for the moment when being heard can keep the hollow alive.
