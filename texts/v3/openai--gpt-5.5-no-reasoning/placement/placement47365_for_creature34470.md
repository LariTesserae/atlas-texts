I think I would be found where the plateau shows through.

Not at the exact center, not in the Root Exchange itself, though its boom would pass through me and decide the hour of my blood. Not in the great factories either, not permanently, though I would know their smells: crushed leaf-panel steam, hot mineral dust, wet iron, sour algae oil, bark resin opened by fresh cuts. I would live a little to one side of importance, in one of those broad intervals the city leaves between its organs—where a tramline crosses a mile of mossed causeway and the nearest towers stand far enough apart that weather can walk between them unimpeded.

There is an exposed gray knuckle of plateau stone there, too smooth to be ruin and too old to be floor. Around it the living pavement has grown thick, but not over it. The moss approaches the stone in a soft dark lip, as if tasting and declining. Wind keeps the surface dry. Rain cleans it quickly. Fog lies there only briefly before tearing open. If you stand on that stone, the world becomes less negotiable. Streets hold their length. A tower remains itself when approached. Stairways count honestly, or as honestly as anything counts here. Reflections settle into water and stop multiplying.

That is where I would keep my working shelter: a low hut grown into the side of an old signal house whose roof has become a fern rib and whose back wall is mostly root. The front room would be dry by local standards. Not truly dry—nothing is—but dry enough for folded maps, chalk, bone-handled knives, humidity tags, and small slabs of labeled stone. There would be a kettle always blackened on one side from a stove that burns pressed lichen waste. There would be narrow shelves with sealed jars of road grit from different districts, each grit useful for persuading a map where it is. A lamp-leaf would hang over the desk, dim in the morning, golden toward dusk, its veins rearranging themselves whenever a tram passed.

I would be a stabilizer of routes. Or that is what the municipal register would call me, in ink that had once been fungal and might become so again.

The older name for it would be stone-listener.

Not a grand office. Not feared like the namekeepers or indispensable like the moisture clerks. My work would begin where other work became uncertain. When a district woke from dormancy and its avenues no longer agreed with the archived distances. When a bridge opened in fog but delivered carts to the wrong year’s loading yard. When a stairway started aging people too quickly between landings. When a road to water lengthened beyond mercy in drought. When a tram bell arrived before the tram had left.

Someone would come for me then, usually early, while condensation still fell in drops from the eaves though the sky above was already pale and dry.

They would find me on the stone.

I would be narrow from walking long distances that changed beneath me. My coat would be waxed and re-waxed until it held the greenish shine of old leaves. At the hem would hang seed-brushes I did not entirely trust, because more than once I would have returned home carrying a future sidewalk in them. My boots would have soles of layered cork and thin gray stone plates set into the arches, so that I could step briefly out of the city’s circulation even when no true rock was exposed. Around my wrists I would wear moisture bands that tightened before rain and loosened in dry administrative air. My hands would be stained with rust, lichen pigment, and the black polish of stone often touched.

I would carry very little openly. A folding rule made of dead vine, jointed and temperamental. A listening rod. A spool of red thread treated with mineral salts. Three small stones from verified surfaces. Chalk that would not sprout. A pruning knife, not for plants so much as for the idea of passage when it became overgrown. And a map case sealed with copper clasps, though I would avoid opening it unless someone else was prepared to hold my sleeves.

My task would not be to force the city straight. No one sensible would attempt that. Straightness is only one of the minor customs of distance, and not a dominant one here.

My task would be to ask: what is this route trying to remember?

Because the city does not misbehave randomly. A road that doubles in length may be protecting a cistern. A courtyard that grows larger than its district may be making room for an event still preserved in root-memory. A stairway that delivers a child to a roofline in seven steps but exhausts an adult in seventy may be honoring a school no longer visible from the street. A warehouse door that opens only to grief may have been sealed after a labor bell failed and everyone inside heard it too late.

So I would walk. Slowly, mostly. In the direction of production when I could, against it only when paid well or begged honestly. I would tap stone where I found it. I would kneel at the edge of living pavement and press my ear to the moss, listening for pumps, root pressure, concealed water, the small crackle of maps trying to update underground. I would speak with rootwrights and tram listeners and fungal archivists, but also with door-vines, old culverts, exposed inscriptions that no longer held words, and those official lichens that preserved the posture of law after law itself had rotted.

I would be human, mostly.

But not entirely in the lowland sense.

No one who lives long in that city remains only human. I would have a small colony of pale lichen growing along the scar behind my left ear, acquired during an apprenticeship in an administrative cutting where the fog never lifted. It would flare faintly when old names were spoken nearby, not enough to be useful as an instrument but enough to make me pause mid-sentence. In damp weather, the lines in my palms would darken green. My breath would know certain sealed rooms before I entered them. If I slept too many nights away from exposed stone, tiny root hairs from the matting moss would begin to knit painlessly into my boot leather and, once, into the callus of my heel.

I would have to cut myself free carefully, apologizing.

My dwelling would be legally ambiguous. The signal house belonged to an obsolete rail office. The root vault beneath it belonged to stormwater. The fern roof was under the care of the shade bureau. The stone outcrop was unownable, though many departments had tried to define access to it. My lease would be a ribbon of permissions stored in three places: paper in a fungal archive, groove in a lichen wall, and a spoken version entrusted to an elderly woman two districts away who would remember it differently every year, thus keeping it alive.

People would visit when the city had become too fluid around them.

A panel worker who kept arriving at the factory before his own footprints. A namekeeper’s apprentice who had accidentally called a plaza by a childhood nickname and now found waist-high grass growing through his family’s sleeping room. A tram conductor whose northbound line had begun returning with southbound rain on its roof. Children sent by worried adults because the shortcut they used through a fern tunnel had started asking them questions in the voices of former teachers.

I would not always solve these things. That would be too clean a word.

I would anchor them long enough for negotiation.

Sometimes that meant taking a person to the stone and having them stand barefoot until the city stopped including them in whatever process had mistaken them for cargo, pollen, memory, or infrastructure. Sometimes it meant walking a route at dawn with a red thread tied from one verified surface to another, letting the moss lift and sniff and decide whether to accept a boundary. Sometimes it meant finding an old name without saying it aloud, writing it instead in water on stone and watching which direction the droplets fled.

Once, I think, I would be called to a dormant borough beyond the fog condenser, where six warehouse halls stood barked over and the air rang all day with the condenser’s dry shriek. A road there would have begun shortening dangerously. Workers sent to inspect cistern levels would step off the main tramline and arrive almost immediately at the sealed warehouse doors, losing the mile of green interval between. Freight carts would nearly collide with themselves. A black bird would sing brake-failure from an insulator, though no tram had passed in hours.

I would go with a moisture clerk and two exchange porters carrying approved vessels. The road-moss would be stiff, metallic with drought. The living poles would lean over us, correcting themselves by fractions. The map would remain folded in my case, trembling.

At the first exposed stone, I would stop. At the second, I would hear it: not water, not machinery, but absence under pressure. The road had shortened because the dormant warehouses were being called by production. Somewhere in the Root Exchange, an old order had awakened—a demand for leaf panels from a factory no longer staffed, addressed to a district that had sealed itself in bark. Production routes are shorter in the direction of production. The city, obedient to its own ancient logistics, was trying to make a dead supply line efficient.

We would not cancel the order. Cancellation requires a department.

Instead we would redirect it.

The exchange porters would pour a measured quantity of stored rain into a roadside hollow. The moisture clerk would seal a document with water from the correct cistern. I would stand on stone and speak not the warehouse’s name but the name of the field that had once fed it. Then we would wait.

The road would lengthen under us, not visibly at first. The towers would seem to retreat into pale vapor. The moss between us and the doors would regain its mile, unfolding distance like cloth. Somewhere far off, a leaf-panel press would cough awake and chew nothing for seventeen seconds before deciding against it. The black bird would stop singing.

That would be a good day.

On bad days, I would return with things clinging to me: obsolete pollen, someone else’s reflected memory, dust from a century when the factories had sharper roofs and more pedestrians. I would sit on the gray stone outside my shelter until dusk and scrape my boots clean with the back of my knife. The lamp-leaf would come on behind me. The city would boom from the Root Exchange, and the boom would pass through the ground, through the moss lip, into the old exposed plateau.

The stone would receive it but not answer.

That silence would be why I lived there.

Not because I opposed the city. I would love it, in the wary way one loves a vast old relative whose moods can close bridges. I would love the green-gold lamps pricking on across the height, the trams ringing in impossible distances, the flowers that opened to hammers, the rain that arrived diagonally and locally like a decision. I would love the people hidden in barked chambers and root vaults, the fungal archivists with dust on their shoulders, the rootwrights speaking softly to load-bearing vines, the children darting through warm ducts with maps folded tight against their chests.

But I would need the stone because the city’s love is incorporative.

Stand too long on living pavement and it begins to include you.

I would have been included once, perhaps as a child. That feels true. I imagine myself daring another child beside a silver-green square after rain, both of us laughing at the slow closing of our footprints. I stood too long. The moss softened. Something beneath it recognized pulse, weight, salt, future labor. For a few seconds I was not trapped, exactly. I was routed. I could feel streets as capillaries, pumps as throats, tram bells as pollinating insects, names as seeds waiting in the dark of speech. I felt the Root Exchange boom before it sounded. I felt rain stored in leaves miles away. I felt the plateau stone beneath everything, patient and unreachable.

An adult pulled me free. Maybe a stone-listener. Maybe the one I apprenticed to.

After that, I would never be able to live far from the boundary between participation and refusal.

So yes: I would be a human municipal function, weathered into a profession. A keeper of stable places. A negotiator with distance. One who carries small stones through a city of roots.

At dusk I would walk the causeway home while moisture returned from hiding. Leaves would fold according to species and code. The fern awnings would drip. Lamps would glow through translucent green membranes. Somewhere the Root Exchange would emit its deep resonant boom, and across the plateau windows would flower in answer.

I would pause on the exposed gray knuckle before going inside.

Around me, the city would continue becoming plant without ceasing to be urban. It would tighten vines, wake pumps, sort seeds by memory, adjust stairways for age, lengthen roads to water, and bloom from smokestacks. It would remember me in small ways: the particular density of moss along my usual path, the lamp-leaf that brightened when I came late, the door-vine that opened to the pressure of my hand only after testing whether I had brought grief with me.

And I, standing on the stone, would remember how not to disappear into it.

Not entirely.
