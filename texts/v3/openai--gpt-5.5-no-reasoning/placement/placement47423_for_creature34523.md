I would find myself where the city begins to loosen its hand.

Not in the towers proper, though I would know them by their undersides: the dim amber ribs of inhabited floors, the condensation gutters, the ice nets hung like patient webs below rime-heavy balconies. Not in the warm communal rooms, except as rumor, scent, and distant vibration. Not out on the pure open plateau either, where the wind has too much room and the stone is still too newly exposed to have learned shelter.

I would be in the thresholds between districts: under bridges, along marsh edges, inside the warmed seams beneath transit plinths, where the city’s heat leaks just enough to keep water moving and moss alive.

A seam-dweller, perhaps.

One of the small furred things that the children love and the engineers pretend not to.

At first I think I am a person: some apprentice hydrologist, a reed-cutter, a quiet maintenance worker with a hood beaded in fog, assigned to read the seep boards before dawn. I imagine gloved hands, a thermos of something bitter and hot, a narrow bed in a shelf-neighborhood overlooking a half-made basin. I imagine kneeling to touch wet stone and feeling the city’s pulse through ceramic scales.

But the longer I stay in the place, the more I belong lower.

Closer to the pipes. Closer to the warmth no one admits is habitat. Close enough that the great rules of the city arrive not as philosophy but as weather in the whiskers.

So yes: I would be a seam-dweller.

Small, dense-furred, dark-eyed, with a body made for slipping through gaps the city cannot help but leave. My paws would be clever without being hands. My ears would be round and close against the skull, rimmed with pale frost in the mornings. My coat would be the color of wet basalt and old moss, darker along the spine, with guard hairs that shed mineral water in tiny beads. In winter I would look larger than I am, puffed with underfur and stolen warmth. In summer, leaner, quicker, all nose and shoulder and tail.

I would live beneath a plaza of ceramic scales at the edge of the terrace basin.

Above me, people cross quietly, their boots pressing weight into plates that tilt and breathe. Above me, the paving angles itself toward light, toward grip, toward drainage. Below, in the maintenance hollows, there is a narrow world of warmth, condensation, fungal threads, shed fibers, seed husks, insects, and the steady muttering of civic systems. Water clicks through channels. Heat pulses in conduits. The foundations shift so slowly that only my bones notice.

My nest would be tucked where three structures disagree: a transit support, an old retaining rib, and a newer drainage manifold printed into stone. The engineers would know the cavity exists. They would have marked it on some map as noncritical void, biological occupancy tolerated, monitor quarterly. Children would know it better. They would crouch at the edge of the scale-field and leave forbidden crumbs, or permitted ones shaped by adults into lessons about restraint.

I would not be alone. There would be a whole kin-tangle under that district: sisters, cousins, old males with torn ears, winter-litters born during long freeze, summer-young who vanish too quickly to predators or cold mistakes. We would know the city from underneath and by taste.

This corner: safe after the second heat pulse.

That vent: too dry, bad air, metal-sour.

That conduit: good moss after fog.

That stairwell: human food sometimes, but door-scent nearby.

That hollow: fox passage; wait until the towers dim.

We would read the city through pressure and residue. The people read seep boards; we read the mineral crust on our paws. They track ground temperature through instruments; we know when the stone has changed by the way sleep becomes shallow. They speak of heat budgets; we know which walls are generous and which have been tightened for migration night.

In the mornings I would emerge through a slot no wider than a wrist, near the basin where birds gather like black commas on reflected sky. Dawn would be low and colorless. The rocks would already be weeping. I would pause with only my nose exposed, sorting the air.

Cold water.

Reed mold.

Distant grazer.

Human wool.

Ozone from a hovering drone.

Fox, old trail.

Fish under ice, only as a faintness.

Then I would run.

Not far at first. Seam-dwellers survive by refusing drama. I would cross the exposed gravel in short bursts, using the black stones as cover. My claws would click once, twice, then vanish into moss. A speckled flock might lift and make the sky briefly granular. I would freeze, not because they hunt me, but because every sudden movement in this city belongs to a pattern larger than oneself.

The basin would be my outer country.

To humans it is new water, not yet lake, no longer plain. To me it is a vast edge: reedlets, silver moss, insects in the thaw-film, mineral bloom, shed feathers, and the occasional egg fragment left by heavier birds. In the warm corner where the thermal exchange array bleeds into mud, life gathers too densely for comfort. Waterfowl stand there. Amphibious marsh-sleepers hold themselves like drowned stones with eyes. Drones descend on patient wings to taste vapor. Children sometimes come to watch, held back by railings and rules.

I would go there only in fog.

Fog is generous to the small.

When weather arrives tired and lays itself over the terrace, the city loses its hard edges. Towers become pale presences. Bridges become darker weather. The animals move more confidently, but so do the predators. A foxlike one might appear without sound along the maintenance path, tall-legged, fur beaded, eyes catching the low amber lamps. If it has not yet learned doors, it has learned almost everything else.

I would know the particular fox that hunts our district.

A narrow female with one white forepaw and an offensive patience.

She uses human infrastructure the way water does: by testing. She follows thermal seams. She waits near scale-fields because young seam-dwellers mistake the shifting plates for safety. She listens to drones because drones often notice leakage, and leakage means food. Once, she pushed her nose against an access hatch and watched it consider her. The hatch did not open. The city, at least that morning, remembered its rule.

We would fear her, but not hate her. Nothing here wastes hatred on weather.

The people would tolerate her until she learned doors.

I would tolerate the people because they are large, warm, predictable, and usually restrained.

Their footsteps have dialects. Children are quick, clustered, uneven, full of stops and whispers. Elders are lighter than they look, pausing often to let the ground speak back. Engineers carry tools that hum faintly and smell of clean alloy, oil, and sealed fabric. Reed-cutters smell of marsh, gloves, and old plant water. Visitors are easiest to hear: loud pressure, wasted heat, impatience in the heel.

On migration nights, everything changes.

The towers dim. Amber light turns downward. Transit lifts its voice into near-silence. Humans move less, and when they move, they do so with ceremonial softness. Even we, under the paving, feel the city holding its breath.

Those nights I would climb higher than usual.

There is a maintenance ladder inside the retaining rib, not meant for creatures with paws, but it has enough frost pits and cable knots to make a road. I would climb through the narrow dark until I reached a ledge under the terrace lip, where warm air from the district slips outward and rises. From there I could see the basin, the distant marks of other districts, and the sky moving with birds.

Not birds as individuals. Birds as decision.

White-winged scavengers circling high, almost invisible except when they catch the city’s last low light. Needle-birds threading antenna groves. Heavy waterfowl gathered in black knots around steam. Speckled flocks turning together over the flats, making and unmaking a single body.

The city would bend around them.

Heat schedules altered. Routes lowered or raised. Windows darkened. People waiting in their warm rooms, accepting inconvenience as proof of membership in the treaty.

I would not understand this as law or reverence. I would understand it as safety. When the city quiets, more of us live.

Sometimes the ground would speak.

Not loudly. A new pressure crack somewhere beyond the basin: a hollow boom traveling through ice and stone, arriving in my teeth before my ears. Then the undercity would wake. Valves would feather open. Foundations would redistribute weight. Scale-fields would shiver. Drones would leave their perches like seeds shaken loose. Humans would rise from sleep and move along lit paths without panic.

We would flee first, of course.

Seam-dwellers are not brave in human ways. We are made of alarm and return. At the first wrong note in the stone, our tunnels would empty into secondary hollows. Mothers would drag young by the scruff. Old ones would choose known warmth over open safety and sometimes be wrong. We would wait in a cable trench, packed together, smelling each other’s fear and the city’s adjustments.

By morning, if the district still breathed evenly, we would come back.

This is how one learns to remain: leave early, return carefully, remember everything.

I think I would have a relationship with one child.

Not a pet’s relationship. This city would discourage ownership, and I would not be ownable. But there might be a child from the terrace households who recognizes the notch in my left ear and the darker fur around my muzzle. They would call me something in their low, practical language. Maybe a name meaning “Under-Scale,” or “Third-Drip,” or “Little Auditor,” because I always appear when the plaza begins to thaw.

They would leave no real food after being corrected. Instead they would place observations.

A feather.

A smooth black stone warmed in the hand.

A clipped tag from a sedge survey.

Once, perhaps, a tiny bowl of clean snow set near my slot during Rimefall, not for need, but for ceremony.

I would sniff these things, reject most of them, and remember the child’s scent: wool, cold skin, salt drink, garden starch, metal from learning tools. Years later, when their footsteps lengthen and become measured like the adults’, I would know them still. They might become a seep reader, or a bridge listener, or one of those who decide when a transport line must bend for a nesting cliff.

They would think, privately, that the district remained healthy as long as the seam-dwellers stayed.

They would not be entirely wrong.

Our absences matter.

If we leave a plinth, the humans notice. If we refuse a warm seam, they test for toxins. If our litters fail, they look harder at heat pulses, microbial films, and mineral runoff. We are not citizens in their formal sense, but we are one of the city’s small votes. A whiskered referendum under the floors.

I like that.

To be minor but legible. To have no speech and still be consulted. To live beneath a civilization that has learned, imperfectly, to treat small vanishings as messages.

My days would be made of intervals:

sleeping in braided warmth while snow ticks into meltwater above;

waking to the tremor of a train passing without touch;

grooming mineral grit from my paws;

following a seam of moss behind a condensation pipe;

freezing under a bridge while long-backed grazers pass overhead, their hooves clicking softly on exposed stone;

stealing grain dust from a freight joint;

avoiding the white-pawed fox;

watching amphibian eyes in black water;

curling with my kin as the night seals the ground in ice.

I would know the great city only partially. No seam-dweller understands the whole plateau. The distant districts would be vertical marks in haze, scentless and irrelevant except when their weather arrives. The unfinished mountains would be pale walls beyond my range. The human councils, the oaths, the heat ledgers, the layered maps of probability—all of that would pass above me as consequence, not concept.

But I would understand the essential thing.

Nothing here is solid in the way the careless believe.

Stone moves. Warmth migrates. Water waits. A path is a temporary agreement. Shelter is not a possession but a permission renewed by conditions. To survive is to listen with the whole body.

And when dawn comes again, low and colorless, and the rocks begin to weep, I would push my nose from the seam beneath the ceramic scales and test the air.

If it tastes of ordinary cold, ordinary moss, ordinary machines, ordinary birds—

I would step out into the treaty.
