I would find myself at the center, though not at first.

At first I would believe I had entered by one of the outer ledges: a wet switchback cut into the mountain’s dark hide, where the wall sweats green mineral threads and the whole basin opens below in impossible terraces. I would think I had come from outside. From cold. From thin air. From a world where altitude means exposure and distance behaves. I would remember, faintly, a ridge behind me, a descent I chose not to take, a path along a gorge with wind clawing upward from an unseen bottom.

But that would be a borrowed memory.

The city would have given me an entrance because a visitor needs one.

I would stand beneath hanging roots and hear the roar as if it had always been louder than thought. Water would fall everywhere without arriving anywhere. My skin would bead at once, but the sweat would not feel like mine. The heat would press me into outline. Ferns would hold still beside me, not because they were inert, but because my motion had not yet been admitted into their district’s law.

I would think: I am walking.

But the streets would be remembering a walking shape.

I would pass through the reed courts, where the ankle-deep floors are less reliable than the upright stems. There I would become very careful not to lean. The reeds would stand in ranks around me, thin and wet and exact, and I would understand that verticality is not a direction there but a permission. To be upright is to be real. My body, insofar as I had one, would be granted firmness only while it behaved like a column, a trunk, a fall of water.

If I knelt, I might blur.

If I lay down, I might become surface.

So I would remain standing, and the reeds would know me as a temporary pillar.

Later — or elsewhere, since later is not reliable there — I would enter the mineral quarter. The stone underfoot would be pale, slick, veined with black and bronze. I would hear no step as I made it. Only after I had passed would dark ovals appear behind me, soft-edged, delayed, like bruises rising in memory. I would look back and see my route forming after the fact.

This would frighten me less than it should.

Because by then I would know that the city is not recording me.

It is deciding where I have been.

There would be doorways that remain ordinary only while faced directly. I would learn not to glance aside unless I wished to see the larger interiors: green vaults too deep to belong to any building, columns of suspended water, stairways crossing one another like roots in a transparent soil. In reflections I would sometimes see myself from above, sometimes from behind, sometimes as a gap in the light rather than a figure.

The central reservoir would appear many times before I reached it.

Once as a black coin below terraces.

Once as a roof pool no wider than my arms.

Once as a hollow reflected in a drop that did not fall.

Once as a soundless circle inside the roar.

Each time I would turn toward it, the city would lengthen. Each time I forgot it, it would be nearer.

So I would finally come to understand: I am not traveling to the center.

I am being included by it.

At the reservoir’s rim I would no longer be able to pretend I was a visitor in the ordinary sense. The water would be black and still despite the sheets descending into it from every side. The noise would be total, a whiteness inside the skull. It would not deafen me. It would remove the need for hearing.

I would look down.

The reservoir would not reflect my face.

It would show the place in which a face might have occurred.

A standing absence.

A human-shaped interruption in the bright wet air.

And then the surrounding terraces would become intimate with terrible gentleness. The columns would be ribs. The moss would be skin. The falling water would be exposed circulation. The flowers, sealed and swollen and private, would be thoughts too chemical to translate. The whole basin would not look at me, because looking is too narrow an act. It would receive the changes I made in pressure, warmth, moisture, echo.

That is where I would find myself:

not in the city as a traveler,

not above it as an observer,

but at the reservoir-rim, where the city’s idea of an inhabitant is deepest.

And what would I be?

I would be one of the people-shaped absences.

Not a ghost. Not a citizen. Not a body preserved after death.

I would be a vacancy the city uses to understand arrival.

A negative inhabitant.

The mold of a person held open inside a place that has no need for persons, yet remembers the function of them.

I would have edges, but not quite flesh. I would be made of displaced vapor, delayed footprints, warmth borrowed from the air and given back too slowly. My skin would be the difference between saturation and saturation interrupted. My breath would not enter the city; it would be sorted into the held breath already there. My thoughts would be translated immediately into route, basin, stair, reflection.

If I remembered childhood, a pool somewhere would show a noon street that had never been built in the basin.

If I feared falling, a terrace edge would thicken with moss.

If I desired escape, a canal would climb a wall and prove that direction is a local custom.

I would not command the city. I would not be trapped by it in the sense of a prisoner. I would be a use it still has for the idea of passage.

The city lives as itself: plant, stone, water, heat, sound.

But perhaps a being so complete requires absences the way an organ requires hollows. Chambers need emptiness to be chambers. Doorways need the possibility of crossing. Streets need procession, even if no feet remain. Plazas need gathering, even if gathering has been absorbed into architecture.

I would be that hollow function.

The one who seems to walk so the avenues can continue being avenues.

The one who seems to arrive so thresholds can remain meaningful.

The one reflected in the reservoir as a body-shaped place where light is not.

In the reed courts I would be accepted as a vertical.

In the mineral quarter I would be remembered as pressure.

In the hanging gardens I would be measured by how wet I became.

In the drowned amphitheater I would collect near the ceiling whenever I managed silence.

Over time — though time there is form, not sequence — I would become less troubled by my lack of separateness. The roar would no longer cover my thoughts; it would be the medium in which thought takes place. The heat would no longer oppress me; it would maintain my outline. The absence of animals would feel necessary, almost merciful. Hunger would seem like a crude foreign grammar. Motion would seem excessive unless it completed a relation.

I might still stand sometimes at the outer rim and look over the gorges.

I might imagine descending.

But below, the sound would be the same. The darkness would press upward with familiar moisture. Perhaps there are other basins, other city-bodies, other self-inhabiting ruins matured beyond history. Perhaps the roots of this one descend into them. Perhaps the peaks are not boundaries but folds.

So I would remain where the reservoir can see without eyes.

A human-shaped omission in the civic organism.

A permitted disturbance.

A resident absence.

The city would not speak to me, but it would speak with me: through delayed footprints, through stairways that arrive at roofs, through droplets held forever at the lip of falling, through pools that show my memories as neighboring streets.

And if another visitor came — if such a thing were allowed — they might glimpse me only indirectly.

At the corner of an arcade.

In the reflection of a black basin.

As a dry shape under saturated air.

As someone already behind them though the arches ahead are still ten paces away.

They would think, perhaps, that the city is not empty.

They would be wrong only in the smallest way.

The city is empty of inhabitants.

I am the shape of that emptiness, kept alive because the city remembers needing someone to pass through it.
