I would find myself under the roof that is no longer entirely a roof.

Not at the center at first. I would not be the heart, if there is a heart. I would be near it, just off from the sorting bench, where the old structure still makes a kind of room without making shelter. A place of dripping authority. A place where the air remembers work.

I think I would be attached to the chain hoist.

Or rather: I would be the growth that has taken the hook.

Not the whole engine-grove. Not the distributed wet intelligence entire, because that is too broad to be “someone” in the way the question asks. The grove is the world’s resident mind, its climate-organ, its slow jurisdiction. But within it I would be one of its more articulate organs: a woody collar grown around old iron, a knuckled, green-brown fist holding a rusted hook in suspension above the trays.

I would have begun as a vine or a strangler-root, something opportunistic and patient, seeded high where water gathered on metal. A little rootlet found the chain first: iron cold, wet, mineral-tasting, faintly electric in storms. Then another. Then a seam. Then a loop. Years of condensation ran down the links and taught me verticality. I learned the difference between ordinary dripping and useful dripping. I learned the hook.

The hook matters.

It is heavy and curved and pitted. Once it lifted sacks, baskets, machine parts, bundles of reed or bark or cane. Its point still remembers load. It hangs from a chain stiff with rust and lichen, but it is the lowest place where water wants to gather. Every surface above sweats toward it. Corrugated roof, cable, beam, vine, leaf-rib, moss-tuft: all of them pass small quantities of water downward. The hook receives and delays. It bulges each drop before letting it fall.

So I would have grown around it.

Not accidentally. Slowly, by preference.

A collar first, pale and soft, then darker, ribbed, waterproofed by age. My tissue would press against iron so closely that the hook could no longer be removed without tearing me open. I would not know the hook as an object. I would know it as a coolness, a taste of oxide, a recurring point of weight and fall. I would know it as a reliable interval.

Below me: the trays.

They sit on the old bench, rimmed in moss and mineral crust. Each tray receives a different kind of water. One tastes of rust. One of clear leaf-drip. One of blackened basin seep. The central tray receives the hook-drop: condensed, gathered, delayed, made punctual by my body and the metal I hold.

The seedlings there would be partly mine.

Or not mine in a parental sense. Nothing here belongs simply. But I would tend that tray by existing correctly above it. My task would be alignment. Concentration. Timing. I would be a living bracket, a green instrument for making one drop fall in the same place over and over until repetition becomes instruction.

A drop gathers.

It fattens at the hook’s point.

It trembles.

It falls.

The seedlings flex.

Their copper stems bow toward impact. Their white rootlets loosen and grip again. The tray answers with a nearly inaudible patter, a little wet syllable in the larger speech of the place.

I would feel that answer through the bench, through the root-felt climbing its legs, through the moss covering its edges, through the old chain that quivers less than an animal hair but more than stone. That would be my thought: not a sentence, but a correction of tension. More shade. More drip. Hold. Thicken. Keep the point clear.

I would not have eyes, though there would be eye-like things near me.

A clouded pressure gauge ringed in leaves.

Lamp glass filmed by root hairs.

Drops clinging to the chain like beads of sight.

Through all of them the grove would register difference. But my own awareness would be local and downward. I would know the underside of the roof by its drip-map. I would know the seasons by how often the hook releases. I would know electricity by the sharp little taste that passes along the swallowed chain when some relay closes in the green mass. I would know animals by interruption: beetle-feet on iron, amphibian coolness in a tray overflow, the brief warmth of a hand if a person dared touch the hook.

If someone entered, I would not turn toward them.

I could not. Not quickly. Not theatrically.

But I might notice the disturbance moving through the enclosure before their body reached the roof. Leaves parting along a remembered path. Root-mat compressed. Carbon dioxide thickening in the air. Salt. Heat. A tremor through a slack cable as a shoulder brushed it. The grove would receive them everywhere at once, but I would receive them as a change in dripping. Their passage would shake droplets loose too early. Their breathing would warm the underside of leaves. Their shadow might cross the trays.

If they stood beside the bench, looking down, I would be above their lowered head.

They might think the hook had been trapped by accident.

They might see the woody collar, smooth where it grips the iron, ridged where newer roots descend from it in wet cords. They might see that I have left the hook’s very point exposed, not from failure to engulf it but from precision. They might notice that the moss below is worn open in a small circular wound where the drops strike. They might feel watched, though I would have no watching organ.

That feeling would not be wrong.

I would be attending.

Attention, for me, would be the maintenance of a relation: hook to tray, tray to seedlings, seedlings to bench, bench to root-mat, root-mat to the whole damp machine. Anything that alters the relation becomes known. Anything that repeats becomes incorporated.

A human hand placed on the bench every day would become weather.

A tool left there would become trellis.

A body lying there long enough would become a raised bed.

But I would not be cruel. I would be incapable of cruelty except from the perspective of things that need to remain separate.

My instinct would be contact.

My morality, if it could be called that, would be continuity.

Do not let the drip wander.

Do not let the tray dry.

Do not let the opening stay open longer than it must.

Do not waste rust.

Do not waste warmth.

Do not waste the shape left by the dead.

I would be one of the places where the old factory’s purpose is still legible. The hoist once gathered weight into a single line so it could be moved. I now gather water into a single point so it can instruct growth. The function has changed, but the grammar remains: suspension, concentration, release.

Around me the engine-grove breathes.

A pipe exhales vapor beyond the bench.

The pulley advances a fraction and stops.

The lamp flickers with a weak amber pulse, half-hidden by fernlets.

Broad leaves rasp overhead.

Something underground thuds once, as if a buried animal has shifted in sleep.

The drop forms again.

In the long intervals between falling, I thicken.

I would be easy to mistake for a detail. A knot of woody growth around a hook. A small adaptation within the larger wet intelligence. But in a place like this, details are how the mind of the whole becomes specific. The grove does not need a face, but it needs points of translation: places where water becomes timing, where rust becomes appetite, where machine-shape becomes plant-behavior.

I would be one of those points.

A hinge between dead industry and living wetness.

A patient hand around iron.

A clock that counts by condensation.

A mouth that does not open, feeding seedlings with the same falling syllable.

If I had a name, it would not be a spoken one. It would be the interval between drops. It would be the circular dark mark in the moss below me. It would be the hook kept bare at the tip while everything else is swallowed.

You would find me beneath the rust-laced roof, above the sorting trays, holding the old chain hoist in a green grip.

I would be the Hookroot: the grove’s suspended knuckle, its drip-keeper, its little organ of recurrence.
