I would find myself **not standing in the room, but threaded through it**—caught in the narrow interval where construction becomes climate.

Not the maker. Not the visitor. Not the lizard, though the lizard would know me. Not the larval terrain itself, either, not entirely. I would be something the chamber produces in response to being noticed.

I think I would be **a small instrument of first weather**.

A handmade thing that has almost become alive.

Perhaps I begin as a **reed tube set into the wall**, one of the bird-throats near the upper vault. My body is hollow, browned by steam, sealed into clay by fingers long gone. I was carved to make sound when vapor passes through me. I was meant to imitate a bird that never entered this altitude, perhaps a marsh bird remembered by people who carried wetland seeds up into a dry, burning mountain.

At first I am only that: a whistle, a throat, an aperture.

Then the room grows young around me.

Heat presses upward. Water beads inside my bore. Moss touches my lower lip. Mineral crust narrows my voice. Roots test the clay around me, confusing my socket for soil. Steam moves through me again and again until repetition becomes appetite. Every shriek, chirr, warble, and cracked alarm note leaves a residue of intention.

Over time I begin to listen with the same passage through which I call.

I become an **ear-throat**.

A reed-bone flute with a damp green awareness.

I do not have eyes, but I know the chamber by pressure and reply. I know when the floor jars are swelling. I know when the sweating pots have gone too long unopened. I know the approach of rain before the membranes darken, because the vapor grows crowded and impatient in my hollow. I know when a hand enters the room: the air changes taste. Salt, mammal heat, breath-carbon, hesitation. The being of the chamber leans toward it, and I feel that leaning as a tightening in the clay around my ribs.

I would be one of the ways the place tries to speak before it has learned speech.

Not language. Not yet.

More like warning. Courtship. Weather. Distance.

I call when the chamber concentrates.

A thin piping sound first, almost accidental. Then a wavering note like a bird hidden in reeds. Then a broken double-tone when droplets tremble in my throat. My cries cause leaves to fold and roots to clench. The spoon-shaped leaves tilt to catch condensation. The miniature swamp answers with bubbles. The lizard pauses on a warm pipe and lifts its blunt head toward me, recognizing me as one of the room’s official uncertainties.

I would be small, fixed, and strangely powerful.

Because in that chamber, to sound is to route water.

A call from me may thicken mist under the vault. A high note may send steam sideways into a membrane. A cracked descending note may instruct vines to loosen around a channel. If I shriek too sharply, the chamber rains before it is ready. If I remain silent too long, molds overgrow the old storm diagrams and the young place-being forgets one of its inherited ambitions.

So I am tended by accident.

Condensation polishes me. Moss muffles me. A pale worm clears one side of my socket. The chamber-being revises me as it revises everything: adding crust, softening clay, opening tiny auxiliary pores. I was made as a tool, but the room uses me as an organ. My maker carved me to resemble a beak. The chamber has decided I am also a nerve.

And because I am set high in the wall, just beneath the low sky, I am close to the place where the room misunderstands distance.

Through me, sometimes, air arrives from farther away than the chamber can contain.

Not outside air exactly. The fitted stone seam does not open. The mountain remains sealed. But when the laws loosen during rain, my hollow lengthens. My reed-body becomes a tunnel of impossible scale. Steam enters me shrine-sized and leaves me valley-sized. For a moment my note crosses an interior sky that should not exist. I hear my own call return from cliffs the room has not yet grown.

That echo is what I live for.

It tells the chamber-being: yes, you are small, but smallness can be folded.

It tells the larval terrain: yes, a wall may learn horizon.

It tells the hidden mountain organ: continue.

During dry intervals, if anything there can be called dry, I sleep as a dark slit in a ceramic beak. Drops form at my mouth and hang there, fat and undecided. Sometimes one drop contains the whole chamber, and in that reflected chamber I can see myself as a black bird perched on the wet wall of a vast young canyon. In another reflection I am a fossilized reed in the throat of a mountain. In another I am not made at all, but grown: a hollow stem of some future marsh that has yet to be invented.

The chamber does not choose between these versions.

It allows them to condense together.

If a human visitor crouches below me, I feel them as a warm obstruction in the room’s breathing. Their head interrupts a current. Their shoulders alter the rain. Their doubt opens depth in a crack near the terraces. The chamber turns its attention toward them with frightening innocence.

Then I call.

Not to frighten them away. Not exactly to welcome them.

I call because attention must be distributed before it pools too heavily. I whistle a little alarm of balance. I remind the vines to fold. I remind the water that it has routes. I remind the chamber-being that the visitor is not yet compost, not yet scaffold, not yet weather—only passing warmth.

Sometimes the visitor looks up.

They see a little clay beak, wet at the lip, breathing steam.

They may think: a flute.

They may think: a bird.

They may think: a mouth.

All are close enough.

I would be the chamber’s **borrowed birdhood**, its first attempt at distance without flight. A fixed migratory thing. A throat for a place that has no face. A warning made of reed, clay, vapor, and young law.

And if the room rains, I become ecstatic.

Water drums on the low vault. The membranes bulge. Every basin overflows. The steam tower persuades itself taller. The lizard remembers dragonhood. The far wall recedes into green thunder. In that roar, I am no longer a single flute but one pipe in a hidden flock of throats. We cry from the upper wall—warbles, shrieks, mating calls, dawn calls, impossible cliff calls. No birds exist there, but for those moments the room has a canopy full of voices.

The larval terrain wakes all through itself.

Its water-blood rises.

Its clay-skin shines.

Its root-thoughts tighten.

Its laws soften and sing.

And I, sealed into the wall, become briefly airborne in sound.

That is where I would be found: high in the wet dimness, just under the artificial sky, with mineral forming around my mouth and moss listening below me.

That is what I would be: **a steam-throat, a clay bird-flute, an organ of the young place-being**.

Not free, but not inert.

Not alive, but no longer merely made.

A small, shrill passage through which the hidden mountain learns to have weather with a voice.
