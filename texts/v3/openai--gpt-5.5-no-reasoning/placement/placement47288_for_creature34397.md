I would find myself not at the center of the basin, because there is no center there that would welcome a body. I would be off to one side, where the wide floor begins to lift toward the old rim and the stone has fractured into a long apron of talus below a pale cliff. Not the largest cliff, not the one where raptors nest, not the dramatic wall that catches evening gold, but a lower, scalloped face of granite and schist with black seams running through it like old frozen water.

There, between the fallen blocks, would be a place that is more interior than it looks from outside.

From a distance it would seem only a clutter of stone: slabs tilted against one another, frost-split boulders, a few mats of moss darkening the shaded joints, orange lichen on the weather side. But within it there would be chambers, slots, passages of cold air, little rooms where snowmelt drips in June and hoarfrost persists in blue darkness even while the outer faces grow warm under noon. It would be a broken place that has settled into architecture without intention.

I think I would be small.

Not grand enough to be one of the horned animals making terms with cliffs. Not high-minded enough to be a raptor writing circles in the white air. Not ancient and slow enough to be lichen, though some part of me would envy that life: the patience to become almost indistinguishable from stone, the humility of taking centuries to make a stain.

I would be one of the quick, gray, round-eared ones in the talus.

A pika, or something near enough to a pika that a traveler would use that name.

My body would be compact, dense against cold, the color of granite dust and old grass. My ears would be round and short, my eyes black and glossy, taking in the sky as danger before it became beauty. I would have no tail worth speaking of. I would move in bursts, as though made not of muscle but of decisions. Out, pause, listen. Across a strip of glare. Freeze. Whistle. Vanish.

The basin would not seem empty to me. It would be crowded with edges.

Every stone would have a name of use: lookout, roof, shade, escape, warm face, bad crossing, hawk-shadow place, grass-bearing crack, weasel-smelling gap. The open distances that overwhelm a walker would not be distances in the abstract. They would be problems. Three bounds to the moss seam. Seven to the sedge tuft. Too far to the yellow flowers unless the choughs are shouting elsewhere. Never cross the pale gravel after the raptor has risen. Never carry stems with your mouth so full you cannot cry.

My life would be built from exactness.

I would know where the dawn condensation gathers first: on the shaded underside of the long slab, beside the thread of moss that wakes green-black before the sun arrives. I would know which alpine grass cures well for winter and which rots if packed too damp. I would know the bitter leaves, the tough stems, the seedheads worth stealing from the wind. I would cut and carry and stack, making hay in hidden crevices where air moves but snow cannot scour everything away.

To a passing human my labor might look frantic, almost comic. A little gray animal rushing with a straw in its mouth. Another straw. Another. A pause on a rock, a cry like a nail struck against glass. Then gone.

But from inside the life it would not be comic. It would be cosmology.

Summer would be the time when the world briefly becomes divisible into what can be stored and what cannot. Warmth itself cannot be stored, except indirectly. Sunlight cannot be stored except as dried grass. The green hour cannot be held except by cutting it, turning it, tucking it into stone. My haypile would be a second body, a future body, the body that must feed me when the basin is iron and white and the wind has sealed the distances.

I would live by alarm, but not unhappily.

Alarm would not be panic. It would be participation. To cry out would be to enter the basin’s great ringing conversation. My whistle would strike the rocks and return changed, multiplied, no longer merely mine. A raptor-shadow passing over the talus would draw sound from me as naturally as wind draws sound from a crack. Others would answer from impossible distances. The slope itself would seem to have nerves.

There would be neighbors I would know without touching.

Another pika under the broken black slab below. A rival, perhaps, whose boundary lay beyond the moss seam. A female or male whose calls changed with the season. Juveniles appearing briefly in late summer, too bright-eyed and reckless, learning the terrible map. Marmots farther down, larger and ceremonious, their alarms deeper, their upright stillness almost absurd. Horned animals passing above, dislodging gravel, leaving pellets and clipped stems, making gardens without meaning to. Choughs, too curious, too clever, landing with sidelong looks near any drying hay.

I would distrust choughs.

Not as one distrusts a predator exactly, but as one distrusts mischief with wings. They would know too much about unattended things. They would bounce near the entrance of my stones, black against brightness, red or yellow bill flashing, head cocked, commenting on my existence. They would pull at exposed stems just because stems are pullable. They would shout when nothing needed shouting. And still, when they cried in a certain way, I would listen, because even trouble may announce greater trouble.

The raptor I would know as geometry.

Not hatred. Not myth. A shape against the light that alters the meaning of every open surface. The pale belly, the long wings, the stillness that is not rest but calculation. If it appeared, the basin would contract instantly into entrances. What had been grass, sun, and work would become only cover and exposure. My body would know this before thought. Down, under, still. Heart striking fast enough to warm the stone around me.

The weasel would be worse.

The hawk owns the open. The weasel enters the hidden.

I would smell it before seeing it: a sharp thread in the cold passages, a wrongness moving through the architecture of safety. Slender, intent, able to pour itself where larger danger cannot come. Then my whole world of chambers and bolt-holes would become uncertain. The places that save me from sky might deliver me to teeth. On such days the talus would not be home but maze.

Still, I would belong there.

Not because the basin is kind. It is not kind. But it is exact, and I would be an exact creature.

My pleasures would be small and complete. The first warmth on the outer stone after a freezing dawn. The taste of a stem cut at the right stage, dry at the tip and sweet near the root. Dust bathing in a shallow hollow. The safety of hearing many calls at once and knowing the slope is awake. The thick smell of my own hay stored in darkness. The moment after danger passes when the body expands back into the world.

I would know evening as a change in risk.

The horned animals would descend then, spreading across the shelves with their old, calm spacing. Their hooves would tick and clatter above me. Loose stones would leap down the gully, and I would vanish before knowing why. The tarns below would hold the light longer than the rocks, bright wounds in the dimming floor. Birds would gather and argue. The wind would grow harder to separate from water, from wings, from echoes.

I would make one or two last trips if the light allowed.

A mouthful of sedge. A mouthful of grass. Perhaps a stolen flower gone to seed from the lee of a dung-warmed stone. Then back into the talus, into a chamber lined with the dry summer I had managed to save.

At night I would not see the basin as a human would see it: vast, star-hung, philosophical. I would feel it through stone. The temperature dropping through the slabs. Frost forming at the entrances. Tiny sounds magnified in darkness: a beetle under lichen, a distant hoof, ice tightening, another pika shifting somewhere beyond a wall of rock. The wind over the rim would enter the crevices as breath, not enough to kill if the chamber were well chosen, enough to remind every sleeper that outside remains outside.

If I dreamed, I would dream in routes.

Out from the lower hole, up over the warm slab, pause beneath the leaning stone, cross to the sedge, cut, turn, back by the shadowed crack. Never the long open. Never the bright gravel when the sky is occupied. Always the hidden door behind the ordinary stone.

And if I died there, as most things there must, I would leave almost nothing. A tuft of fur caught in a crack. A few bones under a raptor ledge. An abandoned haypile molding slowly into soil. Perhaps, after seasons, a greener pinch of moss where my stored grasses rotted in dampness. A beetle fed. A root fed. A lichen shaded for an hour by what had been my body.

That would be fitting.

In that world, I would not be an owner, a wanderer, or a witness standing apart from the place. I would be one of its small repeated signals: a gray flicker between stones, a mouthful of grass crossing a dangerous brightness, a sharp whistle taken up by granite until it no longer sounded like an animal at all, but like the basin itself remembering to stay alive.
