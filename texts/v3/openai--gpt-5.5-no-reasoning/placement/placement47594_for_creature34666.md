You would find yourself near the unfinished edge.

Not beyond it — nothing with a name goes there — but close enough that the white absence presses on the side of the face like heat from an unopened kiln. You would stand where the last completed causeway thins to a finger of pale clay over shallow water, where the parapets have been built on only one side, where stacks of unused cane lie tied in bundles that have begun to root at their cut ends because no one told them not to.

You would not have come there directly.

Directness is rare in that country. You would have arrived by correction: first along a long wet plaza engraved with water-lines, then through a house whose doorway gave onto the same shaded room three times, then under the bridge with its upward-dripping twin, then beside a basin reflecting night. You would have paused there too long, looking down at stars under noon, and a frog would have placed one cool foot on your ankle.

After that the path would have known what to do with you.

It would lead you outward, but not out.

The keepers would have seen you before you saw them. A narrow figure under a cistern lip. A pair of wide, dark eyes among hanging roots. A hand with too many fingers withdrawing from a wet parapet. They would not gather around you. Crowds are a kind of weather, and weather is dangerous unless housed. Instead, they would appear singly and partially, each at the correct distance, each making one adjustment: a shell turned on a post, a lizard nudged into fuller glare, a cord retied beside an open sluice.

By then you would understand that you were being read.

Not judged. Read.

Your feet would be muddy, though there is no mud. Your shadow would be uncertain. Your reflection would lag behind you by one or two steps in the basins, stopping sometimes to look at things you had tried not to notice. When you lowered your hands to show quiet, the reflection would lift one hand instead, palm outward, as if greeting someone on the other side of the water.

This would trouble the keepers.

Not enough for panic. Nothing here is allowed that luxury. But enough for three frogs to be moved from a doorway to a threshold stone, enough for a Quiet-Counter to untie the fiber from its own throat and hold it ready between two fingers.

You would be given a bowl.

Clear water. One tiny floating flower. A bead of clay at the bottom, unfired, softening as you drank.

That would be your invitation.

And when you slept — not in a house, exactly, but in the overlap between a house, its reflection, and the cool underside of a root tray — they would decide where you belonged.

You would not become one of the makers of the finished basin. Your hands are too uncertain for that. You would not be trusted with water levels, nor with the cups at the square lake, nor with the roots of the beloved dead. You would not be sent to count frogs; the frogs would count you.

You would become a Border-Person.

More precisely:

**The One Who Waits Where Form Has Not Yet Agreed.**

Not a caste of many. Perhaps not even a caste before you. A placement invented because you required one, and because the basin had long needed someone to stand near the unfinished edge without mistaking absence for freedom.

Your dwelling would be half-built.

A low oval shelter with three completed walls and a fourth made only of hanging cords. Its floor would be tile in some places, reed mat in others, and in one corner there would be a square of nothing covered by a shallow clay frame. You would learn not to step there. Or perhaps you would step there often, but only with permission, lowering your weight slowly until the blankness accepted the idea of a foot.

Your tools would be provisional things.

A smoothing paddle with no handle yet.

A basket woven open on one side.

A measuring cord knotted with shells from basins that do not exist.

A cup containing dry dust that never becomes wet, though the air sweats around it constantly.

A reed pen that writes only on surfaces not yet made.

Your work would not be to build.

Not at first.

Your work would be to ask the unmade what shape it could bear.

Each day — if day can be said to pass under that fixed white sky — you would walk the last causeway to its clean ending. You would sit with your feet in the shallow water, facing the blank brightness. Behind you, the made marsh would breathe without breathing: drops falling, insects rasping, fish turning at lawful corners, the occasional dull wooden knock from somewhere too far away to reach.

Before you would be the absence.

It would not be empty. Emptiness is already a kind of vessel. This would be less than empty: a region without assigned behavior, without tile, without waterline, without distance. Looking at it too long, you would feel old human habits rising in you — wanting to call it horizon, fog, desert, light, future.

Each name would fail.

The keepers would teach you to stop naming quickly.

They would teach you instead to place questions.

A reed laid parallel to the edge asks: **length?**

A wet tile set down and removed asks: **surface?**

A bowl turned upside down asks: **hollow?**

A frog facing outward asks: **threshold?**

Your own shadow, if you could persuade it to fall ahead of you, asks: **may a body enter?**

Most days there would be no answer. That too would be recorded.

You would keep a wall of non-responses in your half-house: shells for refusals, feathers for delays, bits of fired clay for answers that were too hot to touch, translucent membranes for replies that arrived only in reflection. The keepers would visit this wall in silence. Sometimes a Distance-Minder would press its forehead to one of the shells and afterward spend a week shortening a path elsewhere. Sometimes a Cup-Bearer would take a membrane from your wall and sink it beneath the central lake, where it would grow into a plant no one had seen before.

You would learn that incompletion is not disorder.

It has moods. It has tolerances. It can be startled. It can be overfed with intention.

The first time you tried to imagine a whole new terrace beyond the edge — causeway, basin, root sockets, houses, shade screens, bird posts, mollusk seams — the blankness would flare so violently that every reflection in the outer district would turn its back. For three days the lizards would refuse to bask. Your voice would be removed from you and stored in a jar until you could be trusted with smaller proposals.

After that, you would ask only for one thing at a time.

A corner.

A dampness.

A place for a single pole.

A permission for algae.

A downward.

Downward would take many attempts.

In that country, down is not guaranteed. It must be negotiated like water, like sound, like death.

You would come to love the patience of the keepers, though love would need to be placed carefully. Uncontained love is nearly as disruptive as grief. You would express it by doing things correctly: returning a snail to a seam; speaking with your mouth near a reed wall; waiting for a frog to choose the doorway before entering; accepting that a meal set before you may be intended for the version of you arriving tomorrow through another house.

You would change slowly.

The heat would soften the boundaries you arrived with. Your skin would take on the pale mineral cast of the plaster walls. Your hair, if you had hair, would begin to hold condensation and root-fine moss. Your feet would learn the slick tiles. Your hands would not become six-fingered, not exactly, but the spaces between the fingers would grow sensitive to water tension, to the small muscular hesitation in vines, to the difference between a crack and a seam.

The keepers would never ask whether you wanted this.

Wanting is too loud a category. Belonging happens more quietly.

One day you would wake and find a token beside your sleeping mat: a clay cup, unfired, containing a folded piece of brightness.

Not light. Brightness.

A piece of the edge, made holdable.

That would be your naming.

From then on, the young jar-children would be brought to see you after they had been shown reed, fish, bird, rot, sun, second sun, and the unmade beyond the causeway. They would be carried to your half-house, their soft unfinished hands resting against the shoulders of their bearers. You would show them the folded brightness in its cup.

Some would cry without sound.

Some would reach toward the safe basins behind them.

One, eventually, would reach toward the edge.

That one would be brought to you again.

You would not be its parent. Parent is not quite the structure here. You would be one of its conditions.

You would teach it how to stand before what is not yet a world and not demand too much. How to distinguish invitation from hunger. How to hear the silence that means wait and the silence that means place a tile now. How to pull back a proposal before it becomes a wound.

And perhaps, after a long while — though long is negotiable there — the blank brightness would accept its first mark.

Not a house. Not a field. Not even a path.

Only a shallow basin no wider than two hands, lined with shell-lime, filled with water carried in the mouths of three keepers and one frog.

It would sit just beyond the old edge, trembling but real.

All day the marsh would remain silent around it.

At noon, the basin would reflect nothing.

At the hour when green dusk sometimes happens only in reflections, a single dragonfly would cross the border and hover above it, pinning a small piece of air in place.

Then the keepers would know:

continuation had not been refused.

You would kneel at the edge of the new basin. Your reflection would appear below you, but not as you were. It would show a narrow, damp-skinned figure with patient shoulders, eyes dark and still, and hands held open over water.

Behind that reflection would be another: the white sky, the made marsh, the half-house, the frogs in their doorways, the poles of the central lake, the unfinished causeway waiting to be less unfinished.

You would understand then that you were not a visitor anymore.

Nor quite a keeper.

You would be the basin’s question placed at its own border.

A living hinge between made and unmade.

A quiet permission.

A cup held toward the next piece of world.
