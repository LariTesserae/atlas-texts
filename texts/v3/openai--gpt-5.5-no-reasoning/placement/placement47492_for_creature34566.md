I would find myself where the city is neither highest nor lowest, where no single system has authority yet and everything must pass through everything else.

Not in the glaring seed terraces, where the mirror wardens stand wrapped in white and the light is a profession. Not in the deep pump levels, where the air is fern-wet and green with the breath of tanks. Somewhere between: the middle works, on an east-facing ledge district built into the ribs of an old retaining wall, above the grain conveyors and below the glasshouse roofs.

A place of crossings.

There is a stair there that sweats before rain. Everyone knows it. It is tiled in pale green ceramic, cracked and regrouted so many times that the repairs have become a second pattern, like veins in a leaf. In dry weather the stair rings underfoot, each tread hot by noon and cooling slowly into evening. But before rain, moisture beads along the left wall first, where an old cistern presses its damp shoulder against the masonry. The grout darkens. The handrail goes slick. Fernlets unfold from hairline seams. The cats avoid it unless they must. Children put their palms against the wall and announce the storm with grave theatrical certainty.

Halfway down that stair, under a corrugated shade roof patched with blue resin, is a narrow landing where three routes meet: one ramp toward the market court, one laddered service way toward the condenser frames, and one covered bridge slung across a vertical drop full of vines, ducts, laundry lines, and the distant bronze flash of a waterwheel turning only when the upper tanks are generous.

That is where I would be.

Or rather, that is where I would keep returning.

I would not be one of the high officials of water, not a cistern elder with keys at the belt and a genealogy of obligations. I would not be a master mechanic whose ear can hear a failing bearing through six walls and a flock of pigeons. I would not be a child, though children would know me. I would be one of the lesser necessary people, the sort whose work seems small until it stops being done.

A condensation keeper. A gutter listener. A registrar of droplets, leaks, moss, and tolerated roots.

Someone with a shoulder bag of folded cloth filters, chalk tags, waxed cord, a brass tapping rod, a little knife for cutting only the wrong tendrils, and a book swollen from damp in which no page lies flat anymore.

My district would call me when water was appearing where it should not, or failing to appear where it always had.

I would know the undersides of things.

The bright city is all glare to visitors, all terraces and white railings and sun-struck leaves, but my world would be the shadow just below the brightness: the bellies of pipes, the lips of gutters, the cool dark behind cistern grates, the spaces where droplets gather fat and trembling before they fall. I would walk with my eyes slightly upward, not in wonder but in habit. Every awning edge would have a history. Every stain would be a sentence. Rust, lime, algae, soot, pollen, mineral crust, the black line left by repeated seepage—these would be as legible to me as handwriting.

In the morning I would begin before the sun hardened.

The district would still be blue at that hour, though the upper rails would already be catching fire. Birds would be loud in the pipe brackets. Someone would be grinding grain below, the mill’s rhythm traveling upward through the decking into my feet. Goat bells would click along the roof pasture ramp. Steam would rise from lower vents, making the hanging vines drip though no rain had fallen.

I would take the east stair first, because it lies. It looks dry long after it has begun to sweat inside the wall. I would press two fingers to the grout. I would smell them. Tile damp is different from tank seep, and both are different from root-pressure wet. If the smell was clean mineral, I would mark nothing. If it had the green-sour breath of trapped plant matter, I would chalk a crescent near the third tread and send for a root negotiator before the swelling lifted more tile.

Then I would go to the cat bridge.

It was once an ore-cart span, people say, though no one living has seen ore. Now it carries schoolchildren, market women, cats, bees in certain weeks, and trays of young herbs when the western nurseries are overcrowded. Beneath its grating runs a narrow condensation gutter, easy to neglect because it rarely overflows dramatically. Instead it fails by patience. Dust gathers. Dust becomes paste. Paste grows moss. Moss invites roots. Roots lift the gutter mouth by a finger’s width, and suddenly the water intended for the lower parsley walls falls instead into an abandoned fan shaft where pale moths breed too thickly and clog the filters.

So I would crouch there with my face near the hot metal, ignoring the feet stepping around me, and listen.

A healthy gutter has a private sound. Not a stream, not a drip, but a soft intermittent ticking as beads release from the underside of the bridge into the trough below. If the ticking is too fast in dry weather, something above is leaking. If it is too slow after dawn, the shade cloth is misaligned or dust has furred the catch-edge. If the drops strike flat instead of hollow, sediment has risen in the trough.

People would greet me without stopping.

“Still counting?”

“Still wasting your spine?”

“Storm by noon?”

“Ask the stair,” I would say.

The cats would know me as an inconvenience. I would know them individually despite pretending not to. One old yellow route-cat with a torn ear would sit exactly where I needed to inspect the grain-side drain, and because one does not move a grain cat without cause, I would inspect around him. His bead tag would be old blue glass, cracked but not replaced because everyone agreed it suited him.

At midmorning the glare would begin to divide the world into white edges and green depths.

The market court would wake fully. Pepper fruit, oil cakes, hot tile, goat milk, fermenting rind, resin, damp baskets, iron dust from the repair stalls. Awnings layered over awnings, patched with cloth of different ages, each adjusted by argument and custom so that the vegetable sellers got shade but the hive wall still received morning warmth. Bees would enter through ceramic mouths painted with yellow spirals. Children carrying rinse jars would pause beneath the pollinator arch and lower their voices, because even loud children understand bees better than some adults do.

My table would be at the edge of the market, under a valve wheel half-buried in flowering vine. It would not truly be my table; it would be where people leave messages for people like me.

A slat of tin scratched with: north bath drain coughing.

A strip of cloth tied in three knots: monkey damage, upper fig gutter.

A ceramic token from the mushroom houses: too much clear water where there should be none.

A child’s drawing of a frog upside down, which might mean dead frogs in a cistern garden or might mean only that Tavi from the blue-shutter family had been allowed charcoal again.

I would drink something tart and salted from a cup that must be rinsed over the herb trough, never onto the pavement. I would eat a warm cake of grain and greens folded around goat cheese, with pepper oil shining in the crease. I would intend to sit. I would not sit long.

Because there would be the sound.

Not loud. Not alarming to anyone else. A missing note in the layered city.

The little overflow chain by the old lift housing would have stopped clicking.

It hangs from a half-round gutter above a service doorway, a chain of dull metal cups that carry condensation and roof seep into a blue-marked drain. It clicks all morning in heat, irregular but faithful. When it stops, most people feel only a slight absence, if that. But my body would notice before my thoughts did. I would turn with the cup still in my hand.

The old lift housing stands at the back of the market court, where the masonry is black with generations of oil and prayer smoke. The lift itself still works, but only for goods and only when the mechanic with the silver goggles is in a tolerant mood. Beside its door, people leave offerings before hard repairs: flowers, a thimble of oil, a strip of cloth, sometimes a screw polished bright by worry. Not because the machine is a god. Because after long enough, gratitude and caution become the same gesture.

I would climb the side rungs.

Above the doorway, hibiscus leaves would have grown thick around the gutter lip. Their flowers would be red enough to hurt in the sunlight. Bees would be inside them, turning heavily. I would part the leaves carefully and find what I expected and did not expect.

A monkey cache.

Three valve caps. Two spoons. A child’s mirror shard. A brass button. Half a dried fig. A stolen spectacle lens. All wedged into the gutter bend with damp leaves packed around them like a nest. Water had backed up silently, then begun to creep behind the lift housing fascia. Another week and it would have entered the old signal box. Another rain and the market court would have had an electrical stink and a dead lift for half a season.

I would remove the objects one by one.

Below me people would gather, because nothing attracts a crowd like evidence of monkey crime.

“Ancestral engineers,” someone would say bitterly.

“Careless apprentices,” someone else would answer.

I would hold up the spectacle lens. A groan would rise from three stalls away. The owner would claim it with outrage, though everyone would know he had left his spectacles hanging from a fig trellis, which is practically a formal donation to monkeykind.

The gutter would clear all at once.

Water would leap down the chain, cup to cup, bright in the sun, clicking faster than speech. People would cheer ironically, then sincerely. Someone would set the valve caps aside for the lift mechanic. Someone would give me the half fig as though it were payment. I would not eat it. One must draw a line somewhere.

By noon, the upper city would become a blade.

White rails. White tiles. White sky around the edges of visors. The air would smell of hot dust, sap, heated rubber, and the faint metal tang that comes before storm if the storm is real. Work would slow but not stop. Shade would be full of bodies. The bright streets would be crossed quickly or not at all. Cloth screens would drop from balconies. In the lower wet corridors, shafts of sun would become visible columns full of insects, so solid-looking that small children might try to lean against them.

I would go down then.

Not all the way to the deep engines, but into the fern levels beneath the market, where the temperature changes not by becoming cool exactly, but by becoming enclosed. The air there would touch the inside of the nose differently. Wet brick. Algae. Mushroom trays. Old concrete. Frog water. The deep pumps would be louder, not as sound alone but as pressure in the ribs.

There are grilles in those levels with names older than their numbers.

Widow’s Mouth. Green Six. The Low Teeth. Saint Orra’s Sieve, though no one remembers Orra except that her sieve never clogs if properly flattered. I would visit the blue-marked drains that take wash water from the upper kitchens into settling channels, then to herb troughs, then to lower filters. I would check for oil sheen, soap misuse, flour sludge. I would write down offenses in a small hand. I would be disliked in mild, enduring ways by several kitchens.

In the cistern garden, the frogs would tell their own version of water quality.

After rain they sing as if the world has been remade entirely for them, but in dry spells they mutter from the wet cracks. I would crouch near the listening jars set into the cistern wall, each jar amplifying a different chamber. A good tank sounds round. A bad tank sounds toothed. Too much sediment makes the echo dull. A partial blockage produces a delayed gulp, like someone swallowing a secret.

There I might meet what I am partly afraid I am.

Not human only.

Not another species exactly, but a person whose work has entered the body until the boundary is uncertain. My hands would always be damp at the fingertips, even in high sun. The skin of my palms would be crosshatched from rail, rope, tile, and pipe. My hearing would tilt toward drips. Conversation would sometimes blur behind the tiny impact of water where it should not be falling. I would know people by their routes more than their faces: the woman who crosses under the hive wall at third bell, the boy who takes the dangerous shortcut over the fan housings, the old mechanic who pauses at the lift shrine but never bows, the goat that has learned to unlatch the west herb gate.

I would be a maintenance species.

That sounds humble, and it is, but not small. A snail in a channel is also a maintenance species. A bee wall is a maintenance species. A cat asleep on grain sacks is a maintenance species. A vine permitted to shade a heat wall is a maintenance species until the day it becomes a threat, and then someone like me must stand with a knife in hand and decide whether companionship has become damage.

Perhaps I would be known for hesitating.

Not indecision. Respect.

The city is old enough that cutting is never merely cutting. A root may be prying up a panel, yes, but it may also be holding a cracked face together. Moss may be rot, or insulation, or memory made green. A leak may be waste, or warning, or an unofficial garden’s only source of life. The foolish keepers only seal. The cruel ones only cut. The good ones ask what the water has been trying to do, and who has come to depend on its mistake.

So I would be called when judgment was needed but not yet urgent.

A root under the nursery wall.

A sweating seam behind a family shrine.

A condensation line that had wandered from its trough and now fed a volunteer melon vine growing on the roof of a decommissioned condenser tower full of nesting birds.

The melon would complicate matters. Children would argue for it. Bird keepers would say the extra insects are good for the nestlings. The condenser archivist would insist the roof was never rated for wet soil. The family whose window looked onto the vine would speak of shade. The water family would speak of theft. The monkeys would already have voted in favor.

I would climb up near evening to inspect it.

The light then would be amber, forgiving. The west walls would burn as if heated from within. Steam would rise blue from the lower works. The black-winged scavengers would circle in the last thermals. Laundry would glow on lines. Somewhere below, a press would release steam with a softened roar, and the market court would answer with the clatter of shutters being drawn.

The melon vine would be beautiful, of course. Dangerous things often are, but so are useful things. Its leaves would be broad and slightly silvered with dust, its tendrils wrapped around an old guardrail, its yellow flowers full of bees. Three small fruits would hang in the warm protected angle between a pipe and a cracked parapet. Water would be reaching it from a misdirected condensation lip above, where droplets meant for the official gutter had found an easier path along a rust groove.

I would kneel. I would place my ear to the roof skin. Beneath it, faintly, the hollow body of the retired condenser would tick and creak as it cooled. Birds would shift inside. Their claws would scratch old metal. The vine roots would have entered a seam, but not deeply yet. The roof edge would show mineral crust where water had been spilling too long. Left alone, it would rot the joint. Cut entirely, the roof would overheat and the rookery would lose shade.

So I would not bless it. I would not condemn it.

I would redirect it.

A small copper catch would be fitted under the rust groove. A narrow clay spout would feed a legal trough. The trough would water the vine only after the bird keepers cleared the drain mesh each second morning. The root would be lifted from the seam and given a shallow tray bolted to the parapet. The vine would receive a blue thread and a yellow one: permitted, watched. The children would cheer. The archivist would complain but help measure bolt spacing. The monkeys would steal one of the chalks.

That would be my place in the world: not at the center of power, but at the center of negotiation between intention and consequence.

At night I would return to rooms built into the retaining wall above the cat bridge.

A narrow home, taller than it is wide. Whitewashed interior, hammered tin panel angled to borrow light from a shaft, shelves of jars, hooks for drying cloth filters, a sleeping loft beside a warm flue. One wall would be permanently damp no matter how often scrubbed. I would have given up resenting it and trained a line of safe moss there, trimmed close, soft as felt. Outside the window a fruit tree would be tied along the rail, its leaves tapping the shutters in wind. Pale maintenance moths would gather near the flue at dusk, their wings like flakes of old plaster come alive.

I would eat simply: grain cakes, sour fruit, herbs, a little goat cheese, honey when I had earned indulgence. I would pour rinse water into the correct jar. I would clean the grit from my sandals. I would copy the day’s chalk marks into the damp book before memory loosened.

East stair: mineral sweat, no action.

Cat bridge: gutter sluggish, cleared.

Lift chain: monkey obstruction, resolved, inspect signal box in six days.

Mushroom token: clear seep from upper bath, likely cracked rinse pipe.

Cistern frogs: low but healthy.

Condenser rookery melon: redirect; blue/yellow thread.

Then I would lie in the loft and listen.

Pumps below. Fans above. Chain lift in the distance. Birds settling in pipes. Frogs beginning after the first thin edge of rain touches the western tanks. Water descending by appointed and unappointed routes. The city breathing through all its patched mouths.

And perhaps, because every place like this makes believers out of practical people in small private ways, I would press my palm to the damp wall before sleep.

Not praying exactly.

Checking.

Thanking.

Asking the buried mountain, if there is still mountain under all that labor, whether we have listened well enough for one more day.
