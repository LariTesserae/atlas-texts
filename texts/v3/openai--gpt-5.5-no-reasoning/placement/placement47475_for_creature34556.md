I would find myself where the city forgets whether it is floor or water.

Not in the busy kiln districts, not among the upper riggers or the loud catchment courts, though I would know them by their echoes. I would be lower than the market scaffolds and higher than the sump halls proper: in the disputed layer between use and fear, where old drains open into newer walkways, where the built ground sounds different every three steps, where the fog comes in through cracks no one has admitted are vents.

A narrow district. A seam-district.

The kind of place that is not drawn in a single plan because too many plans overlap there. A stair that was once a gutter. A gutter laid through an old sleeping wall. A sealed cistern converted into storage, then reopened by seepage, then bridged over and marked unsafe, then used anyway by children because it shortens the route to the moth drawers. Walls thick with old repair marks, some cut through by newer pipes. Doorways only half useful. Niches at the wrong height. Hooks with no remembered purpose. A place of interruptions.

I would live beside a listening shaft.

Not a great shaft. Nothing ceremonial. Just a vertical throat lined in old ceramic rings, cold to the touch, descending farther than the lamp can persuade. At its rim is a hinged cover of patched boards and shell-laminate, weighted with three fired bricks because the cover has been known to lift when pressure changes below. Around it are chalk marks renewed weekly, though never identically: warning circles, tally strokes, small handprints, the symbol for “do not speak secrets here,” and beneath that, scratched by someone long dead, “it repeats poorly.”

My sleeping shelf would be above a warm return pipe that only warms during certain wet cycles. When it is cold, I would curse it and roll myself tighter in quilted cloth. When it is warm, I would forgive the whole world.

I think I would be one of the people, but not a central kind.

Not a priest-inspector with a full memory of walls. Not a high-rigger, lean and half-mythic in the wet black above. Not a kiln-keeper, dry-handed and necessary. I would be a listener of smaller failures. A minor diagnostician. A seam-reader. Someone called when a floor has begun to answer in two voices, or when a pipe carries a taste of air it should not have, or when an old repair mark emerges through plaster after rain.

I would be compact, even by their measure, with forearms stronger than my shoulders and feet too sensitive for comfort. I would dislike open platforms because they give too little information back. I would prefer places that touch me: close walls, low beams, floors that speak through the soles. My hands would be scarred not from heroic labor but from prying, scraping, tapping, opening, resealing. The nails flattened, resin-dark at the cracks. Lime would live permanently in the lines of my knuckles.

My ears would be slightly better than is convenient.

Not the magnificent hearing of the true cistern-listeners, who can name water height by jawbone and rod. Mine would be more troublesome, less respected: a sensitivity to delayed sounds, false echoes, repetitions that do not belong to the current hour. I would hear when a corridor gives back one footstep too many. I would notice when gutter-song carries a syllable of speech. I would be called useful by those who like me and damp-brained by those who do not.

My work would begin after other work.

When builders leave, I arrive. When a wall is newly sealed, I listen to whether it resents the sealing. When a gutter has been cleared and everyone celebrates the returning song, I stay until the third change of note. When a child says a closed room breathed, I am sent with a lamp, a rod, two wedges, a chalk reel, and someone older than me who pretends not to be afraid.

I would carry a listening rod made of pale fired ceramic, chipped near one end and wrapped with old cord at the grip. Also a small mallet, a folding probe, waxed tablets, a knife with an ugly rusted tang inherited from someone whose name I still speak before using it. Around my neck, under my damp layers, I would keep a tiny cup no larger than a thumb-joint: my first condensation cup, too small now for practical catching, its inside painted with a spiral almost worn away.

My name would not be one name.

At birth perhaps something brief and soft, used only by those who shared my first sleeping shelf. In work I would be called by a tool-name: Rod-at-Jaw, maybe, or Chalk-under-Thumb. Later I would receive a repair-name after some accident that did not become a disaster.

I think they would call me Still-Gutter-After.

Because once, in the seam-district, a gutter stopped singing.

Not overflowed, not clogged in the ordinary way. Stopped. In a place where the gutter should have carried thin black water from the upper sweat channels toward a sorting cistern, there came a perfect silence. No one liked it. A stopped gutter is worse than a loud one. A loud one complains honestly.

Perhaps I was young then, not yet trusted to mark repairs alone. I put my ear too near the channel and heard, not absence, but waiting. The water had not ceased. It had withdrawn from the sound, running under the gutter through some hidden hairline passage that had opened between layers of old tile.

A careless crew might have struck down into it and flooded the lower shelves. I did not. I followed the coolness through the floor with bare feet. I marked three places. I argued with an elder. I was almost wrong at the second mark and right at the third.

When we opened the floor, we found an older gutter beneath the newer one, perfectly clean, though sealed for generations. Water had chosen it again. Or the structure had offered it. Or both.

We did not force the flow back. We widened the old passage, marked the join, bridged the new gutter over it, and left a listening hole with a removable plug. The district slept dry that cycle.

So: Still-Gutter-After. A name of caution, not glory.

I would be known for distrusting completeness.

If a wall is too smooth, I would want to know what it hides. If a cistern rings too clear, I would ask who last emptied it and why. If a new room already has lamp-niches at the right height, I would mark the niches before anyone hangs lamps there, because gratitude and suspicion are close kin in that place.

I would not be unhappy.

I would enjoy the small warmths. The smell of cured clay. The precise comfort of a tool returned to its loop. Thick broth eaten sitting on a folded plank with knees touching someone else’s knees because there is no better place to sit. The applause of rain crossing a tiled court. The hush that follows a cleared blockage. A friend’s hand finding mine in the dark without needing light.

I would love good labels.

Source, date, hand, material. Upper rain, second fall after east pressure. Wall seep, blue trace, boiled once. Gray return, not for infants. Fog cloth from lower vent, medicinal. I would write tightly and legibly, because illegible marks are a kind of cruelty. I would judge people quietly by how they label jars.

I would have a particular tenderness for old failed repairs.

Not dangerous ones left unmarked. Those are sins. But honest failures: a patch that held for two cycles and then confessed; a drainage scheme that worked except during black rain; a bridge support doubled by three generations because no one ever had time to replace it properly. These would move me. They would seem less like incompetence than conversation. The wall said no. The workers answered. The wall answered back.

My closest companion might not be another person at first, but a caged paddle-rat kept near my shelf.

Not a pet, exactly. A warning creature. Sleek, blind, damp-whiskered, clever in an irritating way. It would sleep in a clay-lined box with a mesh lid and chew any fiber left within reach. When pressure rose below, it would press its nose to the floorboards and shiver. I would feed it scraps of larvae-cake and pretend this was only maintenance. Children would know its name. Adults would pretend not to.

Its name would be Peg, because it once dragged a marked peg into its box and refused to give it back.

There would also be someone I loved among the kiln side.

A person drier than me, warmer-skinned, smelling always of smoke and hot clay. Broad hands, burn scars, narrowed eyes from glow. They would think my seam-district damp had made me superstitious. I would think the kiln heat had made them arrogant. We would exchange gifts badly disguised as necessities: I would bring them a shard from an old pipe whose clay body might interest them; they would give me a cup with a thickened rim because I had cracked the last one. They would sharpen my ceramic blade without mentioning it. I would remember which return pipe warms their sleeping shelf and when.

We would quarrel about whether the structure intends.

They would say material has tendencies, not wishes.

I would say tendencies are what wishes look like when they do not yet have mouths.

Neither of us would win.

The place where I would most often stand is a little bridge over nothing obvious.

A low bridge, hardly more than three planks and a rail, crossing a rectangular darkness between two layered floors. Beneath it is an old cistern or perhaps only the space above one. It smells of cold mineral and stale air. During rain, the bridge beads with water and becomes treacherous; during dry intervals, salts flower along the underside like white knuckles.

From there I can hear five systems if I am patient.

Above: the soft irregular tick of condensation gathering on rope.

Ahead: a gutter that sings in a narrow ceramic throat.

Below: a deep, late knock, not quite water, not quite wood.

Behind: the kiln quarter’s dull breath.

Far off, when the pressure turns: the catchment plain beginning its applause.

That is where I would feel most myself. Not in command of anything. Not lost. Suspended among partial understandings, lamp held low, one hand on the rail, one foot testing the plank’s answer.

If I had a ritual, it would be this:

Before opening any sealed thing, I would touch the old marks first. Not reverently in a grand way. Just with two fingers, reading the grooves, acknowledging the prior hand. Then I would add a chalk line of my own beside the seam before cutting. To say: I am not the first. I will not pretend to be.

My fear would be dry silence.

Not darkness. Not even collapse, though I would dread it properly. My nightmare would be entering a district where nothing drips, nothing sweats, no wall gives back the mallet, no pipe hums under the floor. A dead dryness. Hidden thirst. A place where all the water has gone elsewhere and left the structure listening with an empty mouth.

If punished, I would not fear hauling waste or scraping mineral crust. I would fear being forbidden to mark. To repair invisibly, to have my hand removed from continuity—that would be a living death. I would rather descend to the sump halls with courtesy food in both hands than build a wall no one may know I touched.

And once, perhaps, I would go lower than I should.

Called by a sound in the listening shaft. A repetition of a conversation from an hour not yet arrived. My own voice, older, saying: not that rung.

I would descend anyway, because caution is not obedience. Down through cold rings slick with mineral skin, lamp shuttered nearly closed. Past the level where ordinary gutter-noise fades. Past a sealed side passage exhaling through its cracks. Past old handprints in plaster, some with too many fingers, some small as children’s hands, some fresh-looking though dated generations before.

At a lower landing, I would find a wall that had prepared itself.

Peg-holes already formed. A shallow shelf just right for my lamp. A blank repair panel at chest height, damp but firm. Beside it, in unfired clay, the beginning of a mark that resembled mine but was not finished.

I would stand there a long while.

The larger structure would not speak. It does not speak like a person. But water would move somewhere behind the panel, delicately, as if testing a route. A drop would gather on the lip of the lamp-shelf, tremble, and fall into the dustless dark.

I would understand only enough to be troubled.

When I returned, I would mark the shaft cover with a new warning: listens upward.

No one would like that. Someone would ask what it means.

I would say, “It means don’t lie near it.”

Years later, apprentices would repeat this without knowing whether it was practical instruction, joke, superstition, or law. That would please me more than it should.

In the end, I would become part of some communal wall.

Not buried. Not burned wastefully. Cleaned, sorted, rendered, remembered. Perhaps the small bones of my ear would be sealed into a listening niche near the seam-district gutter that once stopped and then sang differently. My ceramic rod might be broken into three pieces: one kept by an apprentice, one set into plaster, one dropped with courtesy into the sump because I had spent a life listening downward and should not leave rudely.

Inside the wall, I would not sleep exactly.

I would hold.

Not forever. Nothing there holds forever. But until the next hand opens the seam and finds my mark, and beside it the mark before mine, and beside that another almost lost under mineral bloom.

They would touch the grooves. They would curse my cramped script. They would understand enough.

That would be a good afterlife.

So I would be found in the seam between repair and omen: a small, damp, broad-handed listener with chalk under the nails, mistrustful of smooth walls, fond of accurate labels, loved by a kiln-worker, owned in practice by a blind rat named Peg, walking the hollow places with a lamp held low.

A maker, yes.

But more precisely: one who listens for what the making has not yet admitted.
