I would not be one of the keepers at first.

That feels too whole, too named, too much like stepping directly into the grove’s confidence. The keepers belong to the place by practice, by inherited caution, by having already learned how not to disturb the agreements that make it possible. I would not yet know how to move softly enough. I would carry too much weather in me. Too much habit of rivers, cups, thirst, speech, ordinary shadow.

Nor would I be outside. The outside is not really a place yet. It is a waiting material, a pale intention. To be there would be to be almost erased, or not yet selected.

I think I would find myself at the boundary.

Not beyond it, and not comfortably within. At the woven threshold of clay strips where the grove ends with that shocking precision, where leaves still brush one shoulder and warm grit touches the other. I would be close enough to smell resin, alkali, musk, and bitter pollen in their separate chambers, but also close enough to feel the blankness trying to simplify me.

I would be something made for thresholds, but not yet trusted with doors.

At first I want to say I am a moth, because the moths know how to pass through outlines before reality has finished cutting them open. But the moths are too suspect, too airy, too close to loophole and trespass. They drift by permissions no one fully granted. They bring back dust from the unfinished places. They can smell of openness, even moisture. The keepers would catch me under a shell cup and interrogate me with fumes.

So perhaps not a moth.

Perhaps I am one of the jointed seed-carriers, but altered by the edge: a small archivist-animal with many dry legs, a back-basket grown too large and lacquered badly, moving between paths and margins with the serious foolishness of a creature that has been given an errand it only partly understands. I would carry powders from the made grove into the unmade field, not to plant them exactly, but to teach the blankness how to accept detail.

Yes. That is nearer.

I would be a threshold seed-carrier.

Small enough to go unnoticed for long stretches. Made of articulated husk, bone-pale chitin, reed-fiber tendons, and a head like a sealed seedpod with three dark bead eyes set asymmetrically. My legs would click only when I forgot myself. Across my back would be a shallow basket grown from my own shell, lacquered by keepers before I was considered old enough to cross the boundary. In it I would carry ash-seeds, mica dust, powdered former leaves, knots of instruction, flakes from burnt sheaths, sometimes a single dry egg wrapped in fiber.

I would not have a mouth in the usual way. I would feed by pressing my underside to warm tiles and absorbing the lingering work of light. My tongue, if it could be called that, would be a brush hidden beneath my head, used not for eating but for arranging powders in tiny lines.

The keepers would tolerate me, but uneasily.

I would be useful, because I could go nearer the margin than they liked to go. I could test whether a painted doorway was ready to become traversable. I could carry a speck of grove-law into pale grit and see whether it vanished, clumped, smoked, or began to cast a shadow.

But I would also be vain.

The text says some seed-carriers become vain and collect useless glittering flakes, and these are tolerated unless they begin making maps. I think I would be one of those dangerous ones: not yet condemned, but watched.

My basket would contain official dust, yes, but also unauthorized brightness. A sliver of black ceramic from the central basin. A curled red flake from a resin vine. A bit of shadow that had dried on the underside of a tile. Three grains of outside grit that had somehow learned to remain three grains when counted. The transparent wing-scale of a questioned moth. A label-knot I found discarded and kept because it meant “not to be named yet.”

And I would make maps.

Not large ones. Not openly. Never in the raked basins, where the keepers might see and scrape them flat. I would make them under shelves, on the backs of loose plates, along the undersides of ladders that lower the sky. I would arrange dust in patterns showing where the paths became longer than the enclosure, where smells passed through painted doors, where a niche changed its contents, where the outside had briefly accepted a shadow.

My maps would not show distance. Distance here is unreliable. They would show permissions.

This path is short if entered carrying ash.  
This trellis answers only when tapped with bone.  
This blank patch almost held a room yesterday.  
This wall remembers a tool.  
This smell belongs to no plant currently visible.  
Here the outside listened.

The keepers would eventually notice.

One would crouch beside my hidden map beneath a lifted ceramic plate. They would not speak. Their veil would move slightly with restrained breath. One long, low-set thumb would hover above my work, not touching. Then they would make one dry click with a ring.

A reprimand, maybe.

Or a question.

I would freeze, as the ceramic beetles do. My legs tucked. My three eyes reflecting different hours of light. The basket on my back full of glittering uselessness and one necessary seed.

The keeper would reach toward me, and I would expect to be trimmed, corrected, relacquered into obedience. Instead they would remove from their sleeve a pinch of red-sealed powder and place it at the edge of my map.

Permission to continue, perhaps.

Or a warning: maps make doors.

That would be my life there: moving between the lush dry interior and the unfinished margin, carrying small precedents.

I would know the grove from below. Not as a visitor sees it, crowded and warm and strange, but as a network of undersides: shelf-bellies, root clamps, cracked seams, sleeping paws, the hollow resonance of trellises, powder falling upward in the afternoons and gathering beneath leaves like pale fur. I would know which animals shed useful dust. I would know where the fox-sized resin-dreamers sleep and how to step around the mineral loaves their tails embrace. I would know which beetles are born and which are repaired. I would know the smell of a wall about to revise itself.

I would fear water without ever having seen it.

The fear would be inherited as posture. At the carved rule, NEVER BRING WATER, my legs would lower automatically. If a keeper spoke one of the rain-words through a mask, my basket would seal itself. If moisture were ever sensed—real moisture, impossible moisture—I would run not inward but to the boundary, because the edge is where catastrophes first become negotiable.

And perhaps that is why I exist.

The keepers maintain what is already made. I would test what can be added.

When the new room behind the wall begins, when the dry brushing sounds gather and the ceramic beetle turns its head, I would be one of the first small things to know. The tremor would pass through the lattice, into the floor, through my legs. I would climb the underside of a trellis and wait near the painted outline.

From the other side, a keeper presses two fingers to the wall.

From this side, I press six feet and the rim of my basket.

Between us, the painted line darkens.

The wall considers becoming a gap.

I take from my basket an ash-seed, a mica fleck, and one grain of outside grit. I arrange them in a triangle at the base of the not-yet-door. Not a map, not quite. More like an invitation for the future to stay dry.

Then I wait.

Because everything here that matters happens slowly: flowers, cracks, permissions, rooms, selves.

And as I wait, I understand what I am more clearly.

I am not a gardener.  
Not a keeper.  
Not a trespasser.  
Not a moth.  

I am a small licensed uncertainty.

A dry pollinator of possible rooms.

A maker of forbidden maps tolerated because the grove itself has not finished deciding where it ends.
