I would find myself in the high country behind the sky, on the old dry plateau where endings have become habitable.

Not in the center of any village at first. Not among the speaking people, if they still think of themselves as speaking people. I would be somewhere slightly aside from habitation: on a pale rim above a salt basin, where a shale walkway narrows between a row of perforated cairns and a leaning door of stone. Below me the ungulates would stand in the basin like a written sentence no one is presently reading. Their shadows would lie blue and precise on the crust. Birds would line the broken edge of an old wall, all but one facing east.

That one contrary bird would matter.

I would know this before I knew why.

I would be a watcher of crossings.

Not a guard exactly. There is little here that can be guarded in the heroic sense. Nothing charges suddenly over the horizon. No army climbs toward the ledge. Disaster, if it exists here, is not an event but a misread surface, a sound answered wrongly, a shadow permitted too much intimacy. So I would be one of those beings whose work is attention: a narrow, dust-colored person sitting in the angle between a cairn and an animal path, keeping track of which impossibilities are behaving normally today.

My shelter would be half a room under an overhang of greenish slate. Its roof would be one long slab weighted with smaller stones. A thorn-fiber screen would hang across the low entrance, not to keep animals out but to make them announce themselves by touching it. There would be three burrower holes in the rear wall, deliberately preserved, their rims smooth from generations of velvet bodies passing through. A shallow stone basin would hold powdered black salt. Beside it: a bone awl, two scrapers, a length of repaired rope, a folded hide shade, and a clay tablet marked not with writing exactly, but with intervals.

I would have long hands. Six fingers on the left, five on the right, though I might not consider this asymmetry remarkable. My skin would be the color of tuff and old hide. My hair, if I had it, would be bound close under a hood or stiffened with dust until it behaved more like felt than hair. My eyes would not be large, but they would be trained to remain open without asking too much of what they saw. Looking quickly is a lowland habit. Looking quickly breaks the mind against this place.

I would have been taught to wait before naming.

As a child I would have sat beside elders at the rims of basins, learning the order in which the plateau allows itself to be understood.

First: surfaces.  
Which crust has borne hooves since sunrise.  
Which dust is lying and which is only old.  
Which pebble rests naturally and which is still falling somewhere the eye cannot measure.

Second: animals.  
Bird unanimity.  
The angle of a mantid head.  
The difference between a sleeping cat, a dead cat, a stone shaped by an ancestral fear of cats, and a cat that has already decided but not yet moved.

Third: shadows.  
Shadows that belong.  
Shadows that arrive early.  
Shadows cast by bones, by absent towers, by animals not yet born, by extinct things practicing noon.

Only much later: people.

People are less reliable than ungulates on flats, less reliable than birds near doorways, less reliable than burrowers in questions of depth. But people remember rules beyond their own bodies. People mend ladders. People look through cairns. People know when not to answer.

That would be my work.

I would sit near a passage between two terraces where the salt basin can be crossed only on certain days. There, a chain of rib bones and forked stakes marks the safe descent. Animals use the lower path; people must use the ledge path; strangers, if any ever come, always want the shortest way and therefore require saving.

The saving would not be dramatic. I would raise one hand. Wait. Point to the hoof marks. Point away from them. If the stranger spoke twice, I would not answer. If their shadow reached the cairn before they did, I would make them sit until it returned to obedience.

My name might not be a name in the usual sense. It might be a location and an obligation joined together.

Something like: the one who keeps the west repetition.  
Or: hand-at-the-third-cairn.  
Or: waits-where-hooves-test-the-white.

Names here would be practical, and only later affectionate.

The place where I live would not be lonely. Nothing on the plateau is lonely. Loneliness requires unused space, and every emptiness here has already been assigned. A lizard would sleep daily in the notch above my door. Mica insects would inhabit a crack in my threshold and unfold when the sun reached the height of my shoulder. A pair of black birds would nest in the topmost hole of the cairn I maintain, and I would avoid repairing that stone during their brooding interval because birds know orientation better than hands do.

Once each long measure — not a season, but a recurrence of light through a particular chimney in the village below — I would walk down to the storage rooms with a packet of mineral cakes. There would be others there: quiet figures with wrapped heads, narrow wrists, belts of tools. We would trade without much speech. A length of sinew for a cup of pale fungus. A sharpened scraper for powdered green mineral. News would be given as observations.

The east basin contains a herd when viewed from within.  
The old tower shadow has shortened.  
A child saw herself through the cairn and waited correctly.  
The birds on the south huts faced one way for two days, so no one slept there.  
A fossil under the ledge moved before its animal passed.

No one would exclaim. Exclamation is for places where the world is expected to remain singular.

At night — if night comes there, or whatever cooled dimming passes for night — I would lie on a mat of thorn fiber and hide, listening to the little sounds of the plateau continuing to be itself. Teeth rasping far below. A claw on stone. Wings folding. A rope creaking once without wind. Beneath my room, the burrowers moving in soft blind turns. Above me, birds shifting their weight on the sky.

I would not pray, perhaps. Or if I did, prayer would be indistinguishable from checking knots.

I would test the rope ladder with both hands before descending.  
I would turn each storage jar outward.  
I would leave the third niche empty for whatever needs an empty niche.  
I would never move the long-shadow bone.

My people, if they are my people, would not believe themselves chosen. Chosenness is too wet an idea, too fertile, too full of narrative. We would believe ourselves permitted, conditionally. The plateau permits those who learn its exactness. It does not love us. It does not hate us. It incorporates us when we stop insisting on being separate.

In time, I would become difficult to distinguish from my task.

Dust would collect along the folds of my clothing. My sitting place would darken with the oil of my hands. The cairn beside me would acquire small additions: a bird skull, a shard of green slate, a finger-length strip of hide bearing the mark for “wait,” a white pebble taken from the basin on a day when the basin was truly empty. Animals would learn my stillness and pass close. Calves might stand in my shade at noon. A cat might sleep for three days beside my outer wall, and I would step over its tail only if its shadow agreed.

Eventually, perhaps, I would die sitting up.

That would be best in such a place. To fall dramatically would inconvenience the path.

My body would dry before it rotted. Insects would enter by patient permissions. Birds might take the small bones first. Lichen would find the salt of me. My shelter would become a marker; my skull, if left in place, might advise orientation. Children would be told not that I had departed, but that my duties had narrowed.

Later still, if the cliff chose to move through the room, half of me might remain visible in the stone: a seated absence under a shale roof, one hand lifted toward the basin. Someone looking from the ledge might see the outline and know to pause before descending.

But perhaps I am not one of the hand-making beings.

Perhaps that is too easy, too close to the old human wish to find oneself human everywhere.

There is another possibility.

I might be the cairn.

Not a metaphorical cairn, not an object with secret speech, but one of the low stone agreements built beside the crossing. A tower no taller than a person, made of flat pale rocks placed by careful hands, with an opening left through the middle. Birds use my upper holes. Lizards bask in my south seam. Children are brought to me to learn waiting. Travelers look through me before passing, because sometimes I show them the safe continuation of the path, and sometimes I show them themselves coming back.

I would not think. But I would keep relation.

One side of me would face the basin that is empty from above and crowded from within. One side would face the shale ledge where humans walk. My hollow would hold distance in a particular shape. My stones would remember the warmth of hands that stacked them, the scrape of claws, the brush of hide, the tiny pressure of bird feet. When the wrong image appeared in my opening, I would not warn. I would simply contain it until it left.

In that world, this may be as close as anything comes to wisdom.

Still, if I listen to the place as it has been given, I think I am not only cairn and not only person. I am one of the plateau’s small offices of attention: once a person, becoming a marker; once a watcher, becoming part of the watched terrain.

I am found on the rim above the bone-yellow basin, beside the third perforated cairn where the human path leaves the animal path.

I am the keeper of a crossing that is not always there.

I sit in dry blue light with dust on my shoulders, counting birds, shadows, hoof marks, and repetitions of distance. I speak rarely. I do not answer a sound made twice. I know which emptiness has fixtures and which emptiness is hungry. When the ungulates step onto the crust, I mark the hour. When all the birds face the same direction, I close my doorway. When a stranger appears on the trail below, I raise one long hand and teach them first to wait.

And when I am gone, the hand will remain in the stone, still raised.

Not warning.

Instructing.
