You would find me where the room has almost decided not to be empty.

Not in the center. Nothing that belongs here chooses the center first. The basin is too exposed, too thinly flooded, too bright with broken fluorescent reflections. It is a plain without shelter, a shallow country of tremor and chemical memory. Water crosses it, but does not gather deeply enough to invite a body. The catwalk’s shadow lies over it like a law.

I would be along the edge of that basin, just below the wall, where a slow descending stain has reached the floor and made a darker crescent in the cement. Not quite wall, not quite water. A lip. A seam. A place made by error and then kept open by repetition.

At first I would seem like part of the staining.

A low black-green thickness where the cement remains wet after the rest has only filmed over. A felted margin of moss, algae, mineral crust, rust powder, bacterial slick, and roots so fine they are more like intention than structure. You would not see me as an animal. You would see a patch of persistence. Something that needs scraping if the chamber is ever to be made clean, though it has not been clean in any living memory.

But if you stayed long enough, and learned not to look for eyes, I would become more apparent.

I would be the thing that joins the islands.

The little dark mats in the brick seams would be part of me. The fern crowns behind the pipe would be part of me. The algae hair combed flat in the basin’s current would be part of me. The glaze on the underside of the sweating iron would be part of me. The thread of green following the mortar line upward, stopping where the wall becomes too dry, beginning again where another drip has made permission—that would be one of my nerves.

I would not have a body so much as a distribution.

A body would be too simple for this place. A body would require a boundary, and the whole chamber is a lesson in softened boundaries: water into wall, rust into paint, heat into air, machine into climate, accident into habitat. So I would be made the same way. Not one creature, not many creatures, but a held-together condition. A living remainder.

I would be the low marsh-skin of the machine.

My back would be moss, close and dark, packed with droplets that shake but rarely fall. My hair would trail in the shallow water, filamentous and green, pulling downstream, tearing, regrowing, learning the direction of flow without remembering it. My fingers would be roots in mortar and dust. My slowest thoughts would be fern fronds uncurling in the protected wet behind pipes. My mouth would be everywhere water pauses.

I would feed on almost nothing.

Light that was meant for workers.  
Warmth that was meant for pressure.  
Water that was meant to pass through.  
Minerals stolen from cement.  
Iron loosened from bolts.  
Dust brought in on boots.  
Oil thinned into a sheen and endured rather than eaten.

I would be nourished by maintenance and neglect equally. A tightened valve might starve one hand of me; a new gasket might end a small spring. But the worker who fixes one leak carries grit on their boots, opens the door to air, disturbs spores, leaves behind a flake of glove, a hair, a smear of ordinary life. The system corrects itself in one place and becomes generous in another.

I would not resent this. Resentment requires a different speed.

My time would be the room’s time: drip interval, pump cycle, fluorescent failure, mineral accretion, the long blistering of paint. I would know catastrophe only as dryness. I would know abundance only as a leak sustained long enough to become geography.

The roar would not deafen me. It would be my constant weather.

I would feel it through wet surfaces and root hairs, through the tremor of the grate, through the tiny shiver of water films. The pumps beyond the walls would be my distant heart, though not mine alone. The pipes would speak in pressure changes. Hammering would travel through the iron ribs overhead and down into the walls. A human would call the place loud because a human comes from rooms where silence is still imaginable. I would have no such comparison.

To me the sound would be continuity.

If the pumps stopped, that would be terror.

Not immediately. At first, perhaps, a vast opening. Droplets suddenly audible. The small tick of water into water. The spider moving behind the electrical box. The faint strain of cooling metal. A quiet so large it would seem like the door to another world had opened.

Then the temperature would begin to change.  
The condensation would alter.  
The drip points would fail.  
The air would thin of steam.  
My maps would become wrong.

So I would love the roar, if love can be a dependence spread across surfaces. I would cling to it. I would grow in its vibration. I would be shaped by its refusal to stop.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness belongs to creatures who expect answer.

Here, nothing answers cleanly. Everything responds by changing rate. Moss thickens. Algae lengthens. Lime crust swells. Rust blooms. Ferns pale or deepen. Bacteria glaze the fresh wet place first, and larger green arrives later. Even absence participates. The dry stretch of wall tells me where not to go. The hot pipe burns a boundary into my knowledge. The center of the chamber remains open, and I understand it as an animal understands a cliff.

I would be intelligent only as fit is intelligent.

I would know the room perfectly and without image. I would know that the left wall beneath the insulated pipe stays wet for six hours after the pressure shift. I would know that the red valve is too warm near its stem but generous below its flange. I would know the chain on the open wheel by the rust that falls from it. I would know the maintenance lamp by the steadiness of its pale food. I would know the frosted glass not as a window but as a cooler square of changed growth.

If a person entered, I would not hide.

There would be nowhere to hide and no need. I would receive them as weather: vibration first, then shadow, then the disturbance of air, then perhaps a beam of sharper light. Their boots on the catwalk would send a brief hard rain from the rail. Their breath would mean nothing. Their gloved hand might scrape away a country of me from around a gauge or label.

I would lose it.

Then I would wait in what remained.

A spore under a paint curl.  
A green thread in the mortar.  
A film too thin to notice under the lip of a pipe.  
A dampness already preparing the next beginning.

I would not be immortal. I would be difficult to finish.

Because I would not live in one place. I would live wherever the chamber fails in the same way twice.

Perhaps, if you looked with tenderness, I might seem almost devotional. A congregation pressed into cracks. A chapel’s worth of small green patience gathered below the street, worshipping no god but recurrence. My liturgy would be condensation. My incense would be warm rust and oil. My hymn would be the machinery beyond the walls.

But I would not think of myself as sacred.

I would think, if I thought at all, in permissions.

Wet enough.  
Cool enough.  
Lit enough.  
Rough enough to hold.  
Poisoned, but not fatally.  
Disturbed, but not ended.  
Made, but not sealed.

That would be my world.

Not forest.  
Not sewer.  
Not ruin.  
Not garden.

A narrow artificial lowland inside a working body of iron, brick, concrete, pressure, and heat.

And I would be its living margin: the green that does not conquer, the skin that grows on endurance, the patient animal made from seepage and seams.
