I think I would find myself very low down, almost embarrassingly low at first: not above the country looking over it, not striding through it, not granted the clean outline of a beast against mist. I would begin as something half-concealed in the upper centimeters of the world, where water is not scenery but pressure against the body.

At first I want to be one of the long, dark predators, because they seem to know the plateau in the way a hand knows a pocket: by the shape of the hidden things. But that is too much mastery. This place does not seem to grant mastery. It grants literacy, and only to those willing to be slowed.

Nor do I think I am one of the grazers, though there is a deep appeal in belonging to a lineage of careful hooves: to know by inheritance which green is ground and which green is water pretending. But they are too visible. They make paths other beings borrow. They alter the surface by moving over it. They are large enough to be remembered by a basin.

I would be smaller than that.

I would be in a moss hollow near one of the mineral seep districts, not directly at the warm vent but close enough that the ground has odd color in it: iron-orange threads in the trickles, dark green metallic moss on the stone lips, bacterial films in the shallow runnels that tremble blue-green when disturbed. Around me the plateau would be neither forest nor marsh exactly, but a broken shelf of wet stone, peat mats, dwarf shrub roots, little black pools no wider than a body-length, and curtains of moss hanging from ledges like saturated fur.

There would be a reed pan below, out of sight except when the mist thins. I would hear it more than see it: the faint sequential knocking of hollow stems draining after rain, an acre quietly counting itself. Above, the dwarf shrubs would braid into a low ceiling, their waxy leaves heavy with beads. Beneath them, the ground would be a crowded architecture of root, moss, seep, lichen, insect passage, and stored cold.

I think I would be a dark salamander, but not quite an ordinary one.

A small plateau salamander, black-brown and wet as peat, with pale speckling along the flanks like lichen chips or old mineral flecks. Low-bodied, soft-footed, patient because speed is rarely useful here. My skin would need the saturated air. My lungs, if I had them at all, would be modest things; much of my breathing would happen through the slick boundary of myself. I would be less an animal moving through land than a piece of the wet made temporarily intent.

By day I would be under a root mat where the moss has grown over an old crack in the stone. There would be a chamber there, not dug by me alone but enlarged by water, root, frost, and generations of small use. Its ceiling would drip at intervals. The walls would be black peat threaded with pale roots. Beetle larvae would worry through the damp matter. Tiny springtails would spring and vanish like white sparks when disturbed. Fungal threads would lace the buried stems. I would know the chamber not by sight, but by pressure, odor, and the direction of cold water moving past my belly.

I would wait there through the bright gray hours, with only my head angled toward the opening.

The opening would look like nothing from outside: a darker seam under moss, a wet crease beside a stone shelf, the sort of place that rain invents everywhere. But to me it would be a doorway into the upper weather.

When rain thickens, I would emerge.

Not when the first mist comes, because mist is ordinary. Not when leaves bead faster, because that is only a change in the skin of the air. But when the moss above me begins to sag with stored water and the channels connect—when the plateau loosens its small borders—I would push forward, lifting one foot, then another, the toes spreading on soaked green.

The world at my height would be enormous and close. A sedge blade would be a slanting wall with a cutting edge. A berry fallen from a shrub would be a dark moon in the moss. Droplets would hang overhead, distorting the gray sky into trembling lenses. The underside of a leaf would be pale, hairy, and vast enough to shelter under. Lichen on stone would be a forest of crusts and horns. Every surface would smell written over: vole, frog, beetle shell, mineral seep, old rain, predator musk faint and dangerous, the bitter green of bruised moss.

I would hunt what the rain reveals.

Worms forced upward from flooded peat. Soft-bodied larvae washed from sedge roots. Midge pupae at the pool edge. Tiny snails grazing films from stone. The eggs of insects laid in moss cups. Perhaps, if I were bold or hungry, the newly hatched glass-frogs still hardly more than animated drops, though I would have to risk the open hollows where birds can see.

My hunting would not be pursuit in any dramatic sense. It would be nearness, pause, adjustment. A tremor through moss. A chemical trace in flowing water. A pale shape against black peat. Then the short forward movement, mouth opening, the world taken in whole and struggling.

I would also be hunted constantly.

By the marsh-stalkers with their narrow beaks and their infinite stillness. By the compact upland birds that skim low through fog. By shrew-like mammals burning with hunger. By the long predators if they are hungry enough to nose through the root mats for small lives. Even the ground would hunt in its way: a floating mat could tilt, a runnel could swell, a familiar passage could drown.

So I would not cross open moss without listening through my feet. That seems essential here: not hearing as an ear hears, but feeling the tiny decisions of water and weight. The plateau speaks upward through compression. A vole’s passage trembles differently from a beetle’s. Rain striking a pool is not rain striking moss. A grazing animal far above on the shelf sends slow, cushioned thuds through the peat, each one followed by the wet sigh of ground closing.

I would know the great animals mostly as weather.

A herd crossing the ridge would be darkness passing through scent: oily fur, crushed sedge, mineral lick, milk, old hoof mud. Their hooves would leave temporary wells in the moss, some filling with water, some becoming traps, some becoming nurseries for black cup fungi. I might shelter in one later, when its edges soften and the rain has rinsed away the sharpness of their presence.

The predators I would know as absence.

Frogs stopping. Midges lifting all at once. A pressure in the shrub corridor. A smell like wet stone and old blood. I would flatten then, limbs tucked, body pressed into black moss, becoming a mark already present in the world. My speckles would break my outline. My breathing would slow until I belonged to the surface more than to myself.

In breeding season, I would move toward the seep-fed pools.

Not the largest pools, which belong to birds and fish and the old mud-newts, but the smaller black basins tucked beneath ledges, where moss curtains drip steadily into tannin-dark water. There I would meet others of my kind by scent before sight. We would gather in the wet margin, bodies half-submerged among root hairs, hardly ceremonial to an outside eye, but exact in our own low language.

The water would hold our eggs in hidden places: not bright exposed clutches, but small clear beads attached under moss lips or within submerged root tangles where the flow is constant but gentle. Mineral stains might tint the jelly amber. Bacterial films might shimmer above them at dusk. Some would be eaten. Some would cloud and fail. Some would open into larvae nearly transparent, with dark eyes and fine external gills trembling in the seep.

We would not guard them in any noble way. The plateau does not seem built for nobility. It is built for continuance. We would deposit life into the wet machinery and trust abundance, concealment, and repetition.

At night I would travel farther.

Night would not be darkness exactly, but a lowering of difference. Pools would keep the last gray. Fungi under old dwarf trunks would glow faintly, enough to outline rot. Algae would flicker if a larva disturbed them. Along the mineral runnels, bacterial mats would pulse green-blue, not illuminating the way but giving the ground the look of thought.

I would pass through that dimness with my belly close to the moss, each step deliberate. My feet would touch living cushions, dead stems, peat slicks, stone polished by centuries of seepage. I might climb a root as thick as my body and walk along it above a black gap. I might pause beside a pool where pale fish move under the peat bank, appearing and vanishing like slips of cloud caught underwater. I might drink not from a surface but through my skin, the whole world entering me.

There would be no home in the singular sense, no den that belongs only to me. Instead there would be a circuit of reliabilities:

the crack under the lichen stone that stays unflooded except in the longest rains;

the moss tower whose dead lower portion holds insect larvae;

the warm seep margin where winter loosens its teeth;

the root tunnel smelling of voles but wide enough for escape;

the sedge clump whose base is dangerous because a spider hunts there;

the little pool that connects underground to another, because fish appear in it after storms and vanish when water falls.

My knowledge would be a map made of dampness, temperature, acidity, pressure, and scent. Not distance. Not direction as a bird knows it. I would not comprehend the plateau’s vastness. I would live in an empire of inches, but those inches would be inexhaustible. A stone face would contain generations of travel. A moss bank would be a continent. A hoofprint would be a temporary lake, pantry, trap, nursery, grave.

And yet, in some dim animal way, I might feel the largeness above me. The endless repetition of wet shelves and cloud. The fact that every hollow is both singular and part of a greater saturation. During rare clearings, when mist lifts and the air thins enough for distant ridges to appear, I might climb a stone lip after rain and stop there, body shining, eyes black and lidless-looking, while the world opens into green-black folds beyond my capacity.

I would not admire it. Admiration is too dry a thing.

I would absorb it.

I would be one of the plateau’s small wet pauses: a dark salamander of the mineral moss shelves, born from seep-water, hidden under roots, moving mostly after rain. A listener through the feet. A hunter of larvae and worms. Prey to birds, shrews, and silence. A little slick-bodied instrument by which the high wet country turns cold water, buried rot, and brief warmth into motion.

Not dominant. Not symbolic from within myself. Only necessary in the way small lives are necessary there: by being placed exactly in the crowded process, taking almost no space and all of it, leaving behind eggs under moss, shed skin in peat, a body someday opened by fungi and beetles, and a faint trail through the saturated patience of the world.
