I would be found where the hatch-light gives up.

Not at the entrance, where the gray rectangle from above lies on the dust like a foreign law, and not in the deepest ductwork, where the clattering things turn their long pale bodies through metal dark. I would be in the middle distance of that underfloor country, under the leaning rib of a corroded cable tray, beside a relay box that still remembers current.

There is a warm screw there.

Only slightly warm. Not enough for a human fingertip to notice, perhaps; not heat, exactly, but a preference in the metal. A stored tendency. A place where old electricity sometimes passes as a ghost passes through a house: without belonging to it anymore, but still following the shape of its rooms.

Around that screw, the dust has been worn into rings.

The outer ring is traffic: powder grazers, thread-things, flat washers rotating with solemn patience, scouts from three neighboring colonies testing one another’s road-rights. The inner ring is dangerous. It belongs to creatures that wait for the relay to tremble. When the coil stirs, they open their backs and drink what is not quite food from the field around the terminal. They are vulnerable then. Softened by attention. Lit from within.

I would live just outside that radius.

I would be small enough to be mistaken for a broken piece of insulating ceramic.

A narrow, hinged thing. Ash-white. Low to the ground. Not insect, not stone, not quite machine-remnant, though I would borrow the manners of all three. My body would be made of overlapping plates, each plate dull and dry, rubbed with mill dust until no shine remained. Along my back I would carry a crooked ridge of adhered filings and powdered porcelain, not armor alone but disguise, ballast, memory. The ridge would make me look like a flake fallen from some larger ruin.

If lifted, I would unfold.

But almost nothing lifts anything there unless it intends to eat it.

I would have eight legs, perhaps, though two would be held so close beneath me that even I would sometimes forget them until the floor spoke sharply. The front pair would be thin and jointed, more instrument than limb. I would lay them against the dust to listen. I would read the traffic of the chamber through pressure, through static, through the long shudder of machines too distant to see. I would know a glass centipede not by sight, but by the way other feet vanished ahead of it.

My head would be wedge-shaped and mostly blind.

Sight would be a poor extravagance in that place. I might have two pinhole lenses sealed behind milky caps, enough to know amber from gray, movement from stillness, hatch-light from bulb-failure. My true senses would be elsewhere: in combs along my antennae, in the charged pads of my feet, in the shallow drum of my underside pressed to the satin dust.

I would be a listener-scavenger.

Not a predator in the sovereign sense. Not one of the black glass hunters flowing between cables with that calm, royal hunger. I would not own any passage outright. I would survive by knowing when ownership was about to change.

My kind would live in the margins of events.

When the relay clicked, others would rush toward ecstasy or feeding or mating. I would not. I would flatten myself beneath a curled strip of label paper and wait. The warm-ring creatures would open their vanes. Powder mites would surge toward newly charged dust. Nodular colonies would assemble into little thinking arches. Predators would draw closer.

Then, in the confusion, something would always die.

A grazer cracked by a staple-hunter. A thread animal seized by a wedge-head. A feeder stunned by the relay’s tremor. A nursery grain split too early by charge. The chamber wastes almost nothing, but it cannot eat all at once. That delay would be my country.

I would come out after the strike, not before.

My mouthparts would be delicate: twin chisels, a dry brush, a rasp fine enough to shave nutrient from the seam between shell and dust. I would eat fracture. I would eat exposed inner plate before it dried beyond use. I would eat the thin films that appear where bodies break. I would clip the edges of shed skins, peel oxidized glue, scrape the bitter crust from old copper only when the stronger grazers had moved on.

I would be tolerated because I would be useful.

My kind would clean the aftermath around warm screws. We would keep the relay cities from choking on their own dead. We would open collapsed eggs and failed molts. We would reduce disaster to powder again. Not honored. Not protected. But permitted, in the way a crack is permitted because every wall eventually needs one.

My nest would be a slit beneath a mica plate inside the relay box’s shadow.

To a human, it would be no nest at all: a discoloration, a place where dust has a slightly different grain. But inside it there would be a chamber just high enough for my body to turn around if I folded my antennae tight. Its walls would be lined with shaved insulation, pale droppings, and flakes of my own old plates pressed flat into overlapping scales. I would keep a few heavier filings near the entrance to drag into place when vibration storms came.

Behind the nest would be a deeper seam used by my ancestors.

Not a tunnel we made, but a failure in manufacture: a hairline opening where concrete, bracket, and relay casing never quite met. Generations of my kind would have polished it with their bodies. It would lead down into a cold substrate layer where almost nothing moves quickly. In ordinary times, I would avoid it. In water-time, it would be salvation.

Water would be the one thing my body could not negotiate.

I would know it before it arrived.

Not as liquid. Never first as liquid. First as wrongness in the air. A softening of signals. A swelling smell. A sudden muting of dust. The roads would lose their edges. Antennae would rise everywhere. The round scavengers would stop mid-turn. Curtains in the deep chamber would tighten.

Then panic would pass through the floor faster than any predator.

If the drop fell near me, I would not investigate. I would seal my side vents, draw my legs close, and run badly but immediately, dragging my ceramic ridge through the dust, leaving a groove that might last for years if I lived. Others would rush toward the damp kill-zone, reckless with hunger. I would not. My kind remembers through cowardice. We are descended from those who fled too early.

But after, perhaps days later, when the wet scar had hardened into a white-lipped pit and the dead had begun to turn brittle again, I would return.

There would be food there beyond imagining.

Split shells. Burst thread-bodies. Powder mites fused into crust. Eggs drowned in their sand-grain casings. The undertakers with translucent bodies would already be present, delirious and dangerous. I would not challenge them. I would work the rim. I would shave the mineral lip where dead chemistry dried. I would take what catastrophe left at the edge.

My life would be measured in tremors.

Not days. Not seasons. Tremors.

The little amber bulbs would mean one thing. The hatch-light, another. The afternoon slant that briefly touches a patch of floor would be a known migration hazard, because some small internal-lamp species cross recklessly there, confused by brightness. But the true calendar would come through concrete.

A far machine waking: prepare.

Crane rail settling: ignore.

Human step above: freeze.

Press-boom: flatten if hungry, open if breeding, flee if molting, hunt if large, wait if me.

After each boom, I would emerge into changed geography. A dust dune shifted. A road buried. A dead grazer exposed. A cable curtain torn. A new crack visible beneath a fallen crust scale. The world would be ancient, but never the same.

I would have no concept of the factory as a thing made by humans.

Humans would be weather-makers, if anything. Rare vertical catastrophes. Heat-shadowed mountains that open the hatch and bring damp breath and skin salt and crushing fingers. Their dropped objects would become provinces. Their handprints, basins. Their cigarette filters, fibrous mines. Their inspection mirror, a dangerous bright cliff where edible backing peels in silver curls.

I might visit the mirror once.

Not often. Too exposed. Too crowded. But perhaps after a vibration storm, when traffic routes changed and a wedge-headed dispute blocked my usual path, I would find myself beneath that tilted reflected world.

I would see movement doubled.

Not myself, not exactly. Recognition would not happen. But I would sense impossible depth where there should be flange and dust. I would see pale bodies swarm in a second chamber that had no smell, no charge, no vibration of its own. I would tap the mirror’s edge with my front limbs. The reflected limb would tap back without signal.

This would disturb me.

I would scrape a little delaminating metal from the backing and leave.

If I had young, they would begin as hard pale grains glued into the warm side of my nest wall.

I would not tend them tenderly. Tenderness requires moisture, perhaps, or open air. But I would maintain their dryness. I would rotate them when the screw warmed. I would dust them with powdered insulation. I would remove failed eggs before they attracted thread-raiders. If the relay trembled at the right interval, the grains would split along hidden seams and release tiny flat things already armored in ash.

Most would vanish into the chamber’s appetite.

A few would learn the timing of the relay.

Fewer would learn the virtue of waiting.

I would molt rarely and with dread. To molt in that place is to become edible before dying. I would choose a still period beneath my mica plate, wedge myself between filings, and split along the back. For a short while I would be soft, darker, almost shining. I would hate that shine. I would pack myself in dust before my plates hardened, pressing the world into my new skin so I could disappear again.

My enemies would be many.

Thread animals would enter my nest if I left it unsealed. Staple-hunters would take me if I crossed an angle carelessly. Crescent-flat predators could pull me through the floor crust from below. The glass centipede would eat me without noticing my kind as distinct from any other small resistance between its jaws.

But my greatest danger would be misreading a pause.

Stillness is never empty there. It can mean safety. It can mean the entire chamber is listening to something I have not yet felt. If the powder grazers stop and I continue, I die. If the discs turn away from a road and I hurry down it, I die. If the relay warms but the ring-creatures do not open, there is a reason, and that reason has hooks.

So I would be a cautious citizen of a crowded nation.

A gleaner of aftermath.

A keeper of edges.

A dry little hinge between accident and recycling.

My existence would make no sound by itself, only add one more grain to the endless hiss. Yet I would know my country intimately: the copper reef with the dangerous warm seam, the pale ramp of ancestral skins, the bolt-shadow where hunters wait, the old wet pit with its mineral lip, the underside of the label-tunnel that tastes faintly of cellulose, the duct-mouth from which the dice-clatter comes, the one satin road that must never be crossed after a boom.

If a human lowered their face to the hatch and shone a light in, they would not find me.

They would see dust move.

They would see a tremble under the relay box, perhaps a flake of ceramic shift against another flake. They would think: insects, debris, infestation, rot without damp. They would pull back from the dry metallic air.

But I would remain where I was, pressed flat beside the warm screw, front limbs laid against the floor, reading the aftershock of their breath.

And when the hatch closed, and the gray rectangle narrowed, and the amber bulb resumed its tired glow, I would unfold just enough to move.

There would be a cracked shell near the terminal.

There is always something cracked near the terminal.

I would go to it carefully, through dust older than my species’ memory, beneath a ceiling too low for sky, in the nation of dry things.
