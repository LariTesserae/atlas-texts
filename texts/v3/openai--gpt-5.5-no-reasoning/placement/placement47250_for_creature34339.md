I would not arrive there as a traveler.

A traveler would be too much outline: boot, pack, intention, the little bright violence of direction. The plateau would feel me at once—the repeated weight on moss, the dry thought of a road, the impatience in my breathing—and begin correcting me. Vine would take my straightness first. Mist would take distance. The ground would ask my feet to learn uncertainty. My name, if I had brought one, would dampen in my mouth.

So I think I would be found already altered.

Not a person exactly, though perhaps descended from one. Not wholly animal either, not in the clean separateness animals elsewhere are allowed to pretend. I would be one of the ridge-quiet ones: a small, warm-blooded, long-lived being that lives along the firmer old backs of the plateau, where the moss is shallow over sweating stone and the cloud comes up from both sides.

I would be about the height of a seated child when crouched, but longer in the limb than a child, made for four points of contact and occasional uprightness. My hands would still be hands, though narrowed and dark-palmed, with blunt nails good for parting moss, prying edible lichens from stone, testing the firmness of floating root mats. My feet would spread wide when I put weight on them, each toe jointed and webbed by a tough gray membrane, so I could cross wet ground without punching through too often. I would move slowly not because I could not hurry, but because haste is how the plateau makes absences.

My skin would be warm brown-gray, mostly hidden under a dense coat of short, water-beading hair. Along my shoulders and spine the hair would hold a permanent green cast. Not fur turned accidentally moldy, but tended growth: algae, tiny moss filaments, perhaps a few pin-sized fernlings during the long wet seasons. I would groom some away and leave some. The living coat would break my outline, cool me, scent me correctly. Small beetles would live there, cleaning salt from my skin. I would dislike their feet but accept their necessity.

My face would be blunt, broad across the cheekbones, with nostrils that could close against rain and a mouth made more for tasting air than for speech. My eyes would be large, dark amber, filmed by a second translucent lid in heavy mist. I would not see far. No one does there. But I would see nearness intensely: the tremor on a leaf edge before a drop falls, the different gloss of a moss mat with water under it, the slight wrong symmetry of a rootlike predator waiting beside a frog path.

I would live in the low stone rings.

Not inside them as in a house, because houses are declarations and the plateau dislikes declarations. I would live among them, under them, beside them, in the softened boundary where mineral memory still interrupts the green. The stones would hold a little warmth after rare thin hours of light. Their joints would shelter dry fern. Their circular bog-centers would give frogs, bitter blue-black flowers, and water that tastes faintly of old rock.

My den would be beneath a tilted slab on the outer edge of one ring, where roots have lifted the stone but not toppled it. I would have enlarged the hollow with a digging stick made from a deer-antler palm, though the stick itself would be half-rotted and sprouting pale fungus from the handle. The floor would be packed moss, renewed often. The roof would drip in three places. I would know them by sound.

I would not be alone, but I would not live in a village.

There would be perhaps seven of us scattered along the ridge system: kin, almost-kin, and one old female no one can place but everyone feeds in the lean fog-months because she remembers which basins breathe poison after red rain. We would meet by intervals, not schedules. A flute note from a branch bird might carry farther than our own calls. We would leave signs in scent and pressure: a rubbed wrist on a root arch, a turned stone, a strip of peeled bark tucked into a vine fork, a crushed fern whose bitterness says danger or plenty depending on where it is crushed.

Speech would exist, but softly and sparingly.

Our language would have many words for firmness, because firmness is never simple. Firm-over-stone. Firm-over-old-trunk. Firm-over-water. Firm-until-noon. Firm-if-crossed-on-knees. We would have verbs for the ways mist arrives: rising, lying down, combing through sedge, returning from leaves, smelling of a particular basin. We would have no single word for forest. That would be like having one word for all weather, all ancestry, all hunger, all shelter.

We would eat what can be taken without making a wound too large to close.

Fern hearts from colonies that tolerate cutting. Sour cup-leaf fruits, their skins slick as amphibian eggs. The combed algae from stones, scraped with shell-like plates from marsh creatures. Fat grubs from rotting logs, though only after tapping and waiting to see whether the log answers with predator tension. Fungus shelves steamed in leaf packets over peat coals that smoke more than burn. Occasionally eggs, if the birds have laid many. More rarely meat: a drowned glider, a deer calf taken already by the pool and abandoned by the long-jawed thing, a soft-shelled marsh animal trapped in a drying side channel.

We would not farm. Not because we lack the idea, but because farming implies an argument with succession, and succession wins here.

Instead we would encourage.

We would carry seeds in cheek pouches and press them into suitable rot. We would split certain shrub barks during dry hushes so they seep and attract pollinating midges. We would drag fallen branches into lines across slopes to slow water and thicken moss. We would transplant useful cup-plants to the edges of old rings. We would know that the ridge will feed descendants better if shaded in three generations, and that a pool must not be cleared of its floating skin, because the skin becomes ground, and ground becomes passage, and passage becomes memory.

Perhaps this is what remains of civilization here: not building, but persuasion.

At night I would be most awake.

The day is too blurred, too full of dripping sameness, good for sleep in the slab-hollow while rain speaks through root and stone. But night clarifies by subtraction. The fungal seams in old logs would glow dimly, showing where a fallen trunk can be crossed. Blue leaf-undersides would flash when gliders pass. Frog throats would pulse under the bog flowers. The small lights of fishlike things would blink in black pools below the ridge, false stars sunk in tea-colored water.

I would go out on all fours, carrying a hooked branch and a pouch woven from softened vine fiber. The pouch would never be completely dry. Nothing I owned would be completely dry. I would travel the ancestor causeways—the fallen trunks so old that trees have grown from them and fallen in turn. My hands would read bark as much as touch it. Here, a many-legged procession passed recently, safe. Here, the moss has withdrawn from sour seep, avoid. Here, deer crossed three nights ago, one limping. Here, a pale peat-hunter extended and failed; its hole still smells of opened mushroom and bloodless meat.

I would fear pools most.

Not openly. Fear must be quiet too. But every pool under floating lawn would have an inward pull in my mind. The surface may look like meadow. The meadow may bear sedge, flowers, even a shrub. Then a bubble procession moves against the current. Then the whole firmness is only an opinion held by roots.

When crossing such places, I would sing under my breath.

Not for courage exactly. For rhythm. The song would be almost without melody, a damp clicking in the throat, timed to distribute weight: hand, hand, foot, pause; foot, hand, hand, pause. It would be one of the old songs from before words thinned. It might have begun as a work chant, or a mourning chant, or a map of vanished shelters. Now it would be mostly a way of asking the ground not to become water yet.

The plateau would hear me anyway.

Not as a mind listens, not with judgment. It would know me chemically. My salts, my shed hair, the crushed places I leave in moss, the seeds that cling to me, the spores I carry from stone ring to fern wall. I would be part of its exchange, a small moving knot in a slow green nervous system. If I climbed down into one basin too often, certain biting midges would learn me. If I slept long in one hollow, rootlets would find the warmth under my body. If I died there, I would be used with extraordinary tenderness and no sentiment.

That would not frighten me much.

I would have handled the dead of my own kind.

We would not bury deeply; the peat is already full of long arrangements. We would lay the body where it can enter well: against the inside of a stone ring, or in the fork of a fallen trunk, or beneath the nurse-shrubs on a ridge that needs holding. We would open the coat so beetles can begin. We would place seeds in the mouth, not as symbol only but because mouths are warm, wet, and rich. For a few nights we would keep the pale root-hunters away with bitter smoke. After that, the plateau may take what approaches.

A season later there would be moss. Later, fern. Later still, perhaps a shrub with water-swollen roots and small dark fruit. Children—if we still call them children—would be warned not to strip that shrub bare.

They would ask, “Who is it?”

And the answer would not be a name.

It would be: “One who crossed here. One who knew the wall-line. One who fed the blue frogs. Leave half.”

I think I would be a keeper of lines.

Not roads. Roads are too proud. Lines: the knee-high wall vanishing into shrubs; the mineral arcs where vanished mountains still instruct roots; the safe sequence of hummocks across a basin; the annual path of marsh deer from warm pools to ridge fruit; the places where fog from one hollow opens flowers on another slope. I would remember connections too subtle to be maps and too changeable to be law.

My tools would be few.

A tapping stick, polished by my hand oil and green at the tip.

A shard of old fitted stone found in the wall, harder than most surface rock, used to scrape tubers and open fungus bark.

A coil of vine cord living enough that it sometimes tries to root if left wet too long.

A cup made from the dried shell of a soft marsh creature, though dried is a hopeful word.

A necklace of seed pods, each holding a different smell: bitter warning, kin-marker, deer-calm, midge-repellent, mourning resin.

The necklace would matter more than ornament. In a world where sight gives little and sound is swallowed, scent is memory that travels.

Sometimes, in a dry hush, I would climb the low canopy.

Not high; height is suspect when everything is wet. But high enough to crouch among the bundled root-columns of an old communal tree and look across the plateau as it reveals and conceals itself. There would be no horizon in the ordinary sense. Only long backs of land under vapor, basins breathing, ridges appearing like sleeping animals beneath green covers. The sky would be pale and close. The original mountains would be gone, but their ghosts would still be flowering.

From there I might feel something like devotion.

Not worship. Worship stands apart and looks up. This would be more intimate, more compromising. I would know that the plateau is not kind. It eats the unwary, drowns the quick, rots the untended, closes over every cleared space and every intention. But it is generous with return. Nothing given to it stays useless. Nothing dead is refused. Even stone, hardest of ancestors, is allowed to weep and become green.

If there were still stories among us, they would not begin with creation. Creation is too abrupt.

They would begin with softening.

First the high teeth of the world grew tired.  
Then rain learned their names.  
Then roots entered the names.  
Then moss covered the forgetting.  
Then animals came to walk on the forgetting and fell through.  
Then they learned to widen their feet.  
Then the people stopped building upward and began listening downward.  
Then the stone rings filled with frogs.  
Then we were born.

But perhaps I am making myself too human still.

Perhaps what truly belongs there is smaller, less declarative: not the ridge-quiet descendant with amber eyes and seed necklace, but the moss growing along their spine. The passenger rather than the body. The green that uses warmth, motion, and animal habit as weather.

Yes. There is another possibility.

I might be the little garden carried on the back of such a being.

A mat no wider than two hands, rooted in damp fur between the shoulder blades. I began as spores caught during a season of low fog. The body beneath me was young then, restless, bad at crossing water. Its mother groomed away most growth but missed the hollow where rain collected along the spine. I took there. First algae, then filament moss, then two pin ferns, then a bead fungus that glows faintly when bruised.

I would know the plateau through motion and salt.

Dawn: warmth rising under fur.  
Rain: abundance.  
Fear: sudden chemistry, muscles bunching, my rootlets squeezed.  
Kin-grooming: danger, pleasure, loss.  
Night travel: spores shaken loose along old wall-stone.  
Sleep: long humid dark beneath a slab, beetles moving through me like thoughts.

I would drink mist when the being climbed the ridge. I would catch pollen from flowers that open only to basin fog. I would feed tiny insects; birds would come to pick them; the being would tolerate the birds until their beaks hurt. In dry intervals I would shrink to a brown crust and wait. In wet intervals I would swell, green, lift my minute leaves, and become more landscape than passenger.

When the being died, I would be among the first to cross the boundary.

My roots would enter skin. Fungi would enter with me. Beetles would open doors. The warm animal ridge would sag into the larger ridge. Seeds caught in my mat would sprout from the shoulder, then the ribs, then the hollow where breath used to be. I would no longer travel, but I would deepen. What I had learned by being carried—stone ring salts, deer-path spores, basin fog pollens—I would give to the place where the body lay.

That may be the truest answer.

I would be found on a ridge of the ancient plateau, among softened stone rings almost swallowed by moss.

I would be a small, warm, half-upright keeper of damp lines, moving slowly through mist with a living green back.

And I would also be the green on that back.

A creature and its weather.  
A memory with feet.  
A future patch of ground, not yet still.
