You would find me where the upper forests begin to thin into a saddle.

Not on the brightest ridgeline, not in the dark vent-canyons, but between them: in a suspended meadow caught a few body-lengths above black basalt, where the water is crossed by two slow rivers that do not mix. One comes upward from a warm fissure hidden under a lip of stone. It carries iron, sulfur, mineral breath, the taste of new rock. The other comes slanting down from the high water after rain, cooler and clearer, bright with oxygen and faint salt from the unseen sky.

There, between warmth and light, I would be fastened.

At first I seem like one of the meadow-plants: a cupped cluster of translucent green leaves, round as coins near the edges and broader toward the center, held in place by threads so fine they are visible only when silt catches on them. I do not have a trunk. I do not stand. I am more like a knot in a net, a living tension point. Filaments run from me to ribbon-tree blades, to copper fans, to a shelf of basalt furred red-brown with mats. Some threads belong to me. Some are borrowed. Some began as other organisms and have been persuaded, slowly, chemically, into partnership.

I am not solitary, though I have a center.

My body is a colony arranged around a hollow chamber of held water. From outside, I look like a loose green lantern, dim most of the time, brightening only when the descending rain-veils pass. My leaves are thin and slightly cupped, their rims amber-gold, each one trembling when warm water climbs from below. Beneath them hang pale combs and tassels: feeding organs, sensing organs, nurseries, mouths. They comb the current for spores, mineral grains, dead skin from swimmers, fragments of bacterial veil, the dissolved sweetness of things beginning to rot.

My roots do not enter the stone directly. I am too suspended for that. Instead I drink through alliances.

A many-branched holdfast below me belongs partly to an older ribbon-tree whose upper blades tore away decades ago. Its dead fibers remain wedged in a basalt crack, dark and tough, full of channels. I have grown into those channels. White vent-films live in the lowest parts of them, feeding on the warmth below; emerald light-eaters live near my upper leaves; bead-like cells travel daily between them through my hollow tissues. When the upper world whitens, the beads rise. When evening blues the plateau, they sink toward heat.

So I am not one creature in the clean sense. I am an agreement.

A green suspended lantern.
A rootless meadow-knot.
A small republic of eaters, breathers, and makers of membranes.

The fan-things know me as obstruction and shelter. Their chemical shadows brush my lower combs when they open wide in mineral current. Larvae hide among my underside nodules, especially during the long blue hours, when cup-forms are most receptive and the silver swimmers shave too closely along the blades. I let many of the larvae remain. Some feed on me. Some clean me. Some will settle into my tissues and become mouths I did not grow myself.

My senses are spread through surface.

I know light as pressure in my upper skins. I know heat as a slow loosening in my lower threads. I know animals as disturbances first, chemistry second, shape almost never. A grazer announces itself days before arrival by the scraped bitterness of mats upstream. A silver swimmer is a quick cool absence sliding along my outer leaves, followed by the clean sting of a film removed. Pulse-creatures above are soft pulses in shadow and sweetness: bloom-eaters, seed-breakers, glass bells passing through the brighter water.

I do not see the plateau, but it passes through me with enough fidelity that I contain a map.

Above: the ribbon-forest, tall and dignified, its blades combing noon into green fire.

Below: basalt ledges, young and sharp, where black oily mats thicken around warm cracks.

Westward: a curtain of copper fans listening to a slow shear current.

Eastward: the open saddle, where suspended meadows drift but do not leave.

Far below, beyond my direct knowing: a ravine with white bacterial skins and water too warm for my upper partners.

Far above: rain that is not rain, the cold clear descent that wakes dormant futures from every seam of my body.

When rain-water falls through the ocean, I change most.

The first touch is pain-bright: oxygen along every membrane, coolness folding through my chamber. My leaves lift. My amber rims flush gold. The bead-cells surge upward until I glow green from within. Spores lodged for seasons in my outer velvet loosen all at once. I release them reluctantly, in pale streams that drift downward and sideways, some caught by fans, some swallowed by cups, some settling into cracks where I will never know whether they become kin.

During those hours, I am almost awake in the animal way.

Not moving, not choosing quickly, but aware of distances. The saddle clarifies. The black ridges sharpen. I feel the old ribbon-tree stump below me flex in the current like a memory trying to become a body again. The suspended thicket around me tightens its web. Every filament pulls on every other. For a while we are not separate plants and colonies and borrowed deadwood. We are one trembling architecture built from hunger and buoyancy.

Then the rain passes.

The water thickens again with pollen, silt, spores, green matter. The far ridge becomes rumor. My bead-cells descend. The gold fades from my leaf rims. Larvae return to stillness. Fans reopen. Mats resume their patient swallowing of stone.

I remain.

Remaining is the great act here.

Not stillness exactly, because nothing is still. Currents comb me. Bubbles tap my underside. My membranes cloud and clear. A grazer may take three months to cross the basalt beneath me, and in that time I will taste every scrape. A blade may die above me and rise, trailing decay like a slow banner. I will catch violet crumbs from its softening edge. A storm may bow the forests until their shadows lie sideways over the saddle. I will contract my threads, spill excess gas, darken my outer skins, and endure.

If torn loose, I would not die at once.

I would become a drifting lantern, bright for a few days, then milked over with defensive cloud. My threads would trail behind me, gathering silt and small frightened lives. Perhaps I would catch at another density layer and hang there, neither rising nor sinking, becoming a temporary island of rot and refuge. Perhaps my chamber would rupture, my partners separating by weight and appetite: light-eaters upward, heat-lovers sinking, larvae scattering into the green.

But for now I am held.

A basalt tooth below.
A ribbon-tree remnant beside.
A copper fan-current crossing my lower combs.
A pale road of cooler water arriving from above.

This is where I would be found: in the saddle between ridge and ravine, not quite rooted, not quite free.

And what would I be?

A listening meadow-organism.

A colonial green lantern.

A body made to occupy an exact argument between ascent and anchorage.

I would be one of the plateau’s quiet compromises: a creature that eats light, drinks the mountain’s remaining fire through borrowed roots, shelters larvae in its dusk-colored underside, and releases futures only when the ocean rains downward through the sea.
