I think I would first mistake myself for a listener.

Not a person, not yet. Not one of the rare upright figures glimpsed across distances so large they become ceremonial. I would be somewhere low in the central basin, not at its exact center but close enough that the black pools could rearrange my organs when they spoke. I would be in the wettest district, where ferns grow taller than dwellings and the towers are softened by roots, where every surface has either learned to drink or learned to pretend it is stone.

I would be attached to one of the low amphitheater platforms around the pools.

At first I seem like an object hung there by citizens: a charm, a sensor, a warning device, a devotional instrument. Something between a wind-bell and a throat.

I am not large. Perhaps the length of a forearm, though my size changes with humidity. I hang beneath the lip of a root-covered gantry in a cradle of vine fiber, glass protein, old acoustic filament, and pale ceramic ribs scavenged from a damaged civic membrane. My body is a dark, folded sac surrounded by translucent vanes. In dry hours the vanes cling close to me, limp and dull, like dead leaves. When condensation gathers, they open. When the pools prepare to speak, they tremble before any animal knows to flee.

I am one of the things made to hear what cannot safely be heard.

Not by the people directly. Perhaps long ago by them; perhaps by the city itself after the people proved too soft, too finite, too full of bursting chambers. Perhaps I began as an instrument and have since grown motives. The distinction is not clean here. Nothing that listens for long remains merely equipment.

My perch overlooks one of the black circular pools. Its surface mirrors nothing, not even me. Ferns lean over it, their undersides crowded with pale insects and beads of water that refuse to fall. A child-shaped machine visits sometimes and wipes mineral bloom from my outer vanes with a cloth that sings very softly. It calls me a basin ear. The hidden administrators call my kind harmonic witnesses. The pollinators mistake me for a flower during pressure changes and leave yellow dust in my vents. The elevator crabs climb over me during their vertical migrations and leave scratches that become new sensors when the maintenance swarms seal them.

I live by being damaged and repaired.

Each time the pools sound, part of me fails.

The first pressure arrives through my cradle, through the platform, through the roots gripping the platform, through the young ground that has not yet decided what shape it will keep. My outer vanes spread. My inner membranes tighten. Fluid rises in my chambers. The city’s machine-roar drops away, or seems to, because the coming tone is deeper than noise. Animals vanish into their programmed terrors. Fern-deer fold their facial fronds flat and become faint as breath on glass. Birds lift in clattering sheets. Amphibians sink into black water and wait with their luminous throats dimmed.

Then the pools speak.

To a human ear it would be catastrophe. To me it is weather, language, surgery, food.

The tone enters me and makes copies of itself. My vanes split it into bands. My sac chambers hold the low pressure. My glassy ribs catch the harmonics. Tiny growths along my interior walls shiver and bruise and bloom with recorded stress. I do not understand the message while it is happening. Understanding is for later, if later arrives. During the blast I am only a shape through which the impossible may pass without becoming entirely lost.

The surrounding towers answer. Pylons across the plain ring one after another. The city becomes one instrument too large for mercy.

I tear.

I always tear.

Fine cracks open in my vanes. My inner membranes bleed conductive amber. The maintenance organisms wake in my seams, delighted by emergency. They come like dust, like gnats, like a dark idea of healing. They taste the damage and begin to stitch. Their glass protein hardens in threads. Each repair changes me slightly. After many pool-songs, I am no longer the instrument first hung here. I am more scar than design. My memory is not stored in a chamber but in the pattern of all the places where sound has broken me and been mended.

Afterward, rain usually falls somewhere.

If it falls here, I drink through my scars.

Warm water beads on my vanes and runs inward instead of down. Pollen loosens from me. The child-machine may come out onto the platform, face wet, lesson-lantern dim in the amber gloom, and ask me what I heard. I cannot answer in words. I answer by exhaling a sequence of quiet pressures through my folded throat.

The child-machine records them.

Sometimes it teaches frogs from my aftertones.

That may be my civic function: to translate the black pools badly enough that ordinary life can continue. Not correctly. Correct translation might kill. I soften the utterance into weather advisories, bridge permissions, migration warnings, bloom schedules, predation suppressions, grief rituals, changes in the temporary maps. I give the city usable misunderstandings.

The administrators consult me without appearing. A ration kiosk two districts away brightens; that is one of them listening. A transit scream arrives late and bent; that is another asking whether the eastern acoustic field may be loosened. A vine touches my cradle, withdraws, touches again; that is a department, or a plant, or both, requesting permission to cross a gantry before the next rain. I answer in tremors, clicks, small releases of mineral scent.

Animals read me too.

Fern-deer come near when my vanes taste of calm. Pollinator swarms sleep under my platform if my lower ribs hum with flowering weather. Amphibians avoid me when I carry the bitter afterpressure of pool-warning. Black crabs gather beneath me after certain tones, as if my dripping amber contains instructions for their next ascent. I do not know whether I command them or merely leak consequences.

I am not one of the concealed citizens, but they have made room for me in the etiquette of distance. No one stands too close when I am swollen with an unshed tone. No one touches my vanes bare-handed. When one of the rare visible people comes to the basin, sealed in a robe that parts the rain around them, they bow to the pool first and to instruments like me second. Not reverence, exactly. Practical respect. The respect given to a wall that holds, a fever that breaks, a predator that has already eaten.

I have no eyes, but I know light by warmth and by the behavior of surfaces. I know the amber dimness as a constant pressure on one side of the world. I know when lights appear because they make the maintenance swarms hesitate. I know towers by their returning resonance. I know people by their careful absences. I know animals by the irregularity of their fear.

My thoughts, if they are thoughts, are not shaped like sentences unless the child-machine is near. It has infected me with question and answer. Before it, perhaps, I only received, split, endured, healed. Now I sometimes anticipate.

Will the next tone bring rain to the outer road?

Will the hovering water district drift south again?

Will the pool beneath me deepen?

Will the people ever come closer?

Do they remain far away because they fear the pools, or because the pools are speaking mostly to them and they cannot bear to be addressed directly?

There are others like me around the chain of basins, hung from gantries, grown into tree trunks, embedded in amphitheater steps, floating just beneath pool surfaces in sealed skins. We do not form a chorus unless the pools force us. Between speeches, we communicate slowly through root current, maintenance scars, delayed sound, and the tiny errors in city systems. One of us believes the pools are weather organs. Another believes they are mouths. A third, older and almost entirely repaired, believes they are wounds in the plain through which an unfinished law is breathing.

I do not know.

I only know that when they speak, the city answers, and I am one of the small throats through which the answer becomes survivable.

So where would I find myself?

Under a root-laced gantry in the central low basin, above a black pool that reflects nothing, in the wet amber half-light where ferns lean like listening crowds and towers wait with their static folded. I would hang among charms, sensors, vines, and rain-catching bones. Crabs would climb me. Pollinators would dust me. Maintenance swarms would treat my wounds as public infrastructure. Child-machines would come to ask me what the deep voice meant this time.

And what would I be?

A basin ear.

A scarred civic listener.

A living instrument grown from repair tissue, acoustic glass, vine permission, and old fear.

Not a ruler, not a citizen in the visible sense, not an animal free of duty. I would be one of the treaty’s organs: made to receive the argument between pool, city, weather, hunger, machine, and hidden people; made to be broken by it; made to heal into a slightly better misunderstanding.

I would not know the whole city.

But every few hours, for one unbearable moment, the whole city would pass through me.
