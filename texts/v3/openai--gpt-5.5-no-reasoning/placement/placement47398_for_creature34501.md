I would find myself near the center, but not in the open center itself.

Not at first.

At first I would be one of the things mistaken for structure: a low rib of living wood crossing the basin just inside the pale oval of moss, half trunk, half root, one of the old horizontal bodies that makes the room legible. I would lie there in the cool brightness, not dead, not quite tree, not quite animal, bearing the weight of smaller lives without acknowledging it as weight.

A back, perhaps.

A beam.

A sleeping green machine part.

My skin would be bark, but not the bark of a vertical tree. It would be dark, corky, plated, wet in the seams, furred over with moss and threaded by fungal pale beneath. I would remember height only as an ancestry, not as an ambition. Once, some part of me or of what I descended from may have lifted toward light in the usual way. But here the answer to time is not ascent. Here the long wisdom is to recline, to spread, to keep contact, to become passage.

So I would be a sprawler: one of the ancient living trunks laid across the basin floor, still green under the blackened bark, still pushing up small shoots from old wood, still flowering from an end already hollowed by beetles.

I would be found where the pale central moss meets the older ring of vegetation. One side of me would face the open oval, where light gathers evenly and condensation dries late. The other side would plunge under ferns and vine-straps into the denser mechanisms of the grove. Birds would use me as wall and stair. Lizards would warm on my raised plates when the sun band reached me. Mice would pass beneath my lifted ribs through tunnels smelling of damp leaves. Beetles would work inside me with their patient inner rasp. Fungi would know me more completely than I know myself.

I would not be an individual in the sharp-edged sense.

I would have ends, but not a single boundary.

At my eastern end, I would still be vigorously alive, raising a cluster of slender shoots like green flames or small chimneys. Leaves there would tremble in the basin’s constant breath. Insects would visit the new growth. A bird would sometimes land there and make the whole length of me feel its tiny certainty.

At my middle, I would be softened. Water would enter through an old wound. Moss would hold to me thickly, and under that moss the fungi would be turning my past into usable darkness. Beetle larvae would carve slow galleries through what I no longer needed. If a human knelt and pressed an ear against me, they might hear the minute industry inside: chewing, flexing, the faint pop of fibers accepting their next form.

At my western end, I would already be almost soil. Ferns would grow out of me. A pale snail would graze the underside of a bracket fungus. A mouse might build a nest in a cavity that used to be heartwood. I would not grieve this. Here, to be eaten correctly is to continue.

My consciousness, if that is the word, would be broad and low.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in tension, dampness, pressure, sugar, rot, shade. I would know the day by the widening of light along my bark, by the warming of lizard bellies above me, by the opening of fern fronds rooted in my decay. I would know evening by the return of condensation, the tiny impacts of drops, the swelling of moss, the renewed courage of frogs in their black root-cups.

Sound would pass through me as weather passes through leaves. The grove’s roar would not seem loud to me. It would be the condition of being. Leaf-hiss, pod-rattle, hollow-stem knocking, beetle rasp, frog pulse, bird repeat, water plunk: these would not arrive as separate noises unless something disturbed their ratios. A silence would alarm me more than a crack.

The fox would have a route across me.

Not a path visible to outsiders, but a memorized sequence of holds: forepaw on a knot, hindpaw in the shallow saddle where moss is thin, pause above the hollow place, leap to the sedge braid. Its pads would press into me with a particular warmth and decision. I would know the fox by weight and rhythm before scent. It would know me by trust. It would not thank me. Nothing here wastes energy on gratitude. Use is the local form of recognition.

Birds would quarrel in my cavities. Frogs would answer from water held in the root-cups near my base. A moth would spend a whole day disguised as one of my old lichen plates. The deer at the rim would sometimes stretch its neck to browse the shoots rising from me, tugging at my newest leaves with soft lips and flat teeth. I would give some. I would keep some. Balance, not defense, would be the old law.

I would also be connected beneath.

That would matter most.

Under the moss-skin, under the compacted root-webbing, under the visible theater of the basin, my roots and fungal partners would enter the slow conversation of the place. I would not be alone because aloneness would be anatomically impossible. The pale threads would pass through me and onward, carrying chemical news: dryness coming, a fresh fall of leaves, a dead mouse under the fern mat, phosphorus released from softened wood, stress in a neighboring root, sugar available from a younger shoot.

I would be less a resident of the grove than one of its memories of having become stable.

The grove would not command me, and I would not command anything. Yet through me, many things would be held apart at the right distance: air from soil, moss from hollow, water from loss, animal from sinking, rot from collapse. My task would be load-bearing in every sense. I would support feet, nests, fungi, dripping water, warmth, beetles, shade, and recurrence.

Perhaps that is who I would be: not the fox, not the bird, not the quick eye in the fern slit, but the old horizontal permission by which they move.

A living lintel.

A green-barked conduit.

One of the grove’s bones that has learned to digest itself slowly.

And if there were a more hidden self beneath that—if the place insisted on becoming stranger as it became truer—then I might not be the trunk alone. I might be the agreement around it.

I might be the damp intelligence gathered at the seam where my wood softens into moss, fungus, insect, and root. The threshold-being. The place where categories pass into one another without drama.

I would be found just off the central oval, where a careless foot would think me firm ground and a careful animal would know me as a bridge. My body would be crossed daily by lives that do not pause. My interior would be eaten into usefulness. My living end would leaf out each year with no sense of novelty. My dead end would feed the floor that holds me.

I would be old, but not finished.

Low, but not diminished.

Empty-looking, but inhabited through and through.

I would be one of the factory’s quiet machines: not producing anything but continuity, not moving except by swelling, creaking, loosening, rooting, and being remade.

In that world, I would be a fallen trunk that never stopped living.

Or more exactly: I would be the long green back of the grove, lying awake under moss, carrying the room.
