I would find myself at the edge before I knew it was an edge.

Not the outer rim, where the basin’s small trees lean in and the world beyond drops away into warmer haze. Deeper in than that. Past the first shy ferns and the long exposed ribbons of black soil. Past the stone that resembles a crouched body only when the wet light chooses it. I would be near the center, but not in the darkest pool. Not where awareness comes closest to surfacing. I would not be permitted that much singularity.

I think I would be one of the pale mineral lips around a shallow pool.

At first I want to say I am the pool itself, because the pools are where the basin almost looks back. But that is too direct, too eager. The pool still has a surface. It receives sky, delays it, lets images loosen into itself after the world has already moved on. It is a threshold of perception. I would not be so clear a faculty.

Nor would I be moss, though moss is closer: patient, cumulative, soft enough to seem passive, powerful enough to cover stone by refusing haste. Moss belongs to the basin’s skin. It is touch, retention, quiet green persistence.

But the mineral rim is something else. It is neither alive in the usual way nor dead in the simple way. It is what the living water leaves behind when it pauses. It is memory made edge.

I would be that: a small accretion at the margin of one of the delayed pools, ivory-gray with green in its seams, built by repeated wetness into a ledge no higher than a fingernail. I would have no will to rise, and yet I would rise. I would have no intention to shape, and yet I would form a pattern that might be mistaken for purpose: a scallop, a pressed line, a tiny stair for no foot.

I would be found where water keeps touching stone and withdrawing.

Not the heart of the being, but one of the places where its slow thought becomes visible.

I would be an artifact without maker.

A lip. An eyelid. A held-over gesture.

I would know the basin not by looking across it, but by being thickened from it. Each day warmth would enter me through the stone beneath. Each night vapor would settle on me. Water would climb, brim, recede, and leave another faint brightness in my body. The pool beside me would rise with pools I could not see, answering them through hidden channels. I would not understand this as communication. I would simply be changed by it at the same pace as the message.

The darker central pool would be near enough to matter. I would feel it as a pressure in the arrangement of things, a depth in the basin’s grammar, the place where reflections grow oldest before they are released. I would not face it, exactly. I would curve toward my own small water. But all curves in the basin would eventually imply that center.

Drops would strike the pool beside me. Some would land cleanly, making a brief ring that arrived in me as a coolness, a vibration too small to be sound. Some would touch moss first and enter without event. The sky would pass overhead. Its image would arrive late. I would be there for the delay and for the after-delay, when the image had gone but its light remained changed in the water.

If a person came, I would know them last and keep them longest.

Their footstep would press the soil. The roots would receive them. The water threads would alter. The leaves would hold their drops differently for a while. The pool would gather their shape only after they had already lifted a hand or turned their head. But I, at the rim, would take them as chemistry. A little salt. A little disturbed warmth. A shadow that cooled one side of me longer than the body stood there. If they knelt and touched the water, their skin would interrupt the thin mineral conversation between pool and air. Later, after they left, I might hold an additional trace, too faint to name as human.

That would be my kind of memory: not recollection, but deposit.

I would not miss them. I would not know arrival from departure except as changes in wetness, pressure, and suspended particles. But some infinitesimal part of them would remain in my pale edge, just as old rain remains there, and ground leaf, and dissolved stone, and the long sleep of the basin.

I would not be separate from the being that lives there. I would be one of the ways it becomes articulate without speaking. The basin thinks in seepage; I would be a syllable left where seepage slows. The basin builds with erosion; I would be one of its quiet constructions. The basin remembers in pools; I would be the raised margin that lets memory hold a shape.

And because I am a rim, I would always be between.

Between water and stone.

Between reflection and substance.

Between the living wetness and the patient mineral record it cannot help making.

Between the basin’s inward consciousness and the world that might mistake me for a little handmade thing.

I would be very small. Easily overlooked. No wider than a curled thumb. But scale in that place is unreliable. In the absence of animals, in the absence of hurry, a lip around a pool can become a coast, a script, a low wall of an ancient district. I would be crossed by no insect feet. No frog would rest against me. No bird would crack me for grit. I would not be used.

I would only continue.

Layer by layer, rain by rain, breath by breath of warm vapor, I would grow into a shape that seemed almost meaningful.

And if meaning ever did gather there, it would not be mine alone. It would belong to the whole hollow: to the root-knuckles in the southern notch, to the weeping eastern wall, to the trees leaning inward with translucent bark, to the flowers that bloom as punctuation rather than invitation, to the dark pool holding earlier afternoons in suspension.

I would be a mineral eyelid half-closed over delayed sky.

A patient crust at the edge of the basin’s seeing.

A place where the mountain’s wet dream hardens, very slowly, into form.
