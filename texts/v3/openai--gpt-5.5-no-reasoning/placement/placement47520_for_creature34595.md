You would find me where the plateau seems least alive.

Not in the broad basins, where the mineral mounds swell like something trying to remember heat. Not among the blade-fields, where light breaks itself to pieces between the fins and every passage is a narrow argument between glare and wetness. Not at the cliff-rim, where the whole high country bleeds down into brightness.

You would find me under an overhang no wider than a sleeping animal, in a place a human eye would pass over as shadow.

There is a tilted plate of blue-gray stone there, leaning against a red rib that has resisted the plateau longer than most of the surrounding rock. Between them is a low, triangular hollow, always wet, never flooded. The opening faces away from the harshest sun. Water gathers along the underside of the plate in a row of trembling beads. Each droplet grows for hours, fattening on seep and condensation, until its own weight overcomes its patience. Then it falls into a shallow white cup below, strikes with a bright tick, and scatters into runnels too thin to see except when the light catches them.

That cup is my country.

Or part of it.

I am not the whole wet skin of the plateau, though I am kin to it. I am not the ancient membrane spread in miles through cracks and glazed hollows, the life so diffuse that it has become almost geological. I am smaller than that, gathered enough to have a kind of locality, though not gathered enough to be an animal in the ordinary way.

I am one of the pale things that lives because the film lives.

A flat insect, perhaps, at first glance.

But not quite.

If you lifted the overhang and found me there, you might mistake me for a loose chip of mineral crust. I am the color of wetted salt and old fingernail, nearly transparent at my edges, cloudier toward the center where my organs lie as faint threads and milky knots. My body is wide, low, and jointed in shallow plates that overlap like flakes of mica. When I am still, water holds to me so completely that I look less like a creature than like a defect in the shine of the stone.

I have six main legs, though the front pair have become something between legs and instruments. They end in divided hooks, soft at the inner edges and hard at the tips, able to cling to wet mineral without scratching it loose. Beneath my head are combs too fine for the human eye to separate. I pass them over the living slickness and take what has risen there: cells, salts, softened mineral grains, the pale fat of invisible colonies, the faint chemical richness gathered at the border of water and stone.

I do not bite.

I rasp.

I do not search.

I test.

My world is not made of distances first, but of conditions. A dry patch is not an absence of water only; it is a closed door, a bright wound, a place where the skin has tightened into hunger. A black seam is not darkness only; it is depth, cold, old seep, possible passage, danger of being washed below. White crust is sharp salt, sleep, memory of former wetness. Blue-gray stone is food-bearing if it sweats. Red stone is hard, poor, trustworthy underfoot. A hanging bead is a season. A falling drop is weather. A changed taste in the runnel is news from far underground.

I know the plateau through my feet.

Sound is always present, but I do not hear it as a visitor would hear it. The great mineral roar is the pressure of being alive. It enters my body through stone. Drops strike, cracks breathe, water threads along hidden lines, tiny plates loosen by a fraction, crusts tighten in dry glare. All of this trembles through me. I can tell when a droplet will fall before it falls. I can tell when a nearby seam has begun to sour with too much metal, when a skin has gone dormant beneath its glassy surface, when a worm has passed through the black slot below and left the film ragged behind it.

I live mostly upside down.

The underside of the leaning stone is safer than the cup, though the cup feeds better. Brightness comes less directly there. Birds do not look beneath. The larger falling drops strike below me, not on me. I hang by my hooked feet and graze along the wet ceiling, moving sideways in slow, deliberate increments. My body is so flat that the boundary layer of air around the stone shelters me from the worst of the wind. When cold tightens, I press myself into a mineral seam and become nearly part of it. When the film swells, I widen my stance and feed.

I have never seen soil.

I have no expectation of leaves.

Green would mean nothing to me except perhaps poison or shadow or some impossible richness too unstable to trust. The plateau’s poverty is not poverty to me. It is the complete world: salt, silica, calcium, iron, cold water, glare, film, crust, the reliable fall of drops.

My life is measured in wetted surfaces.

The underside of my overhang is large enough to sustain me because the living membrane is old there. It grows in branching slicks beneath transparent crust, withdrawing when exposed, thickening where the droplets hesitate before falling. I harvest it without destroying it, not from mercy but from fit. My mouthparts take the upper swelling, the excess, the loosening edge. If I scrape too deeply, I reach mineral harshness and gain nothing. If I return too soon, there is little to take. So my path is a circuit of intervals: along the blue-gray plate, down to the white cup, across the shaded lip, up the red rib where a narrow seep sometimes sweetens after fog, then back again.

A territory no larger than two human hands is sufficient for a life, if one is made correctly small.

There are others of my kind, though not many.

One lives beyond the black slot, beneath a curled carbonate lip. I know it by the interrupted places in the film, by the chemical trace it leaves from its feet, by the faint scrape-pattern of its feeding combs. We do not meet often. The dry-looking ridge between us is dangerous in bright hours, and the runnel crossing it carries water too quickly after thick mist. Once, during a long wet period, I crossed halfway and found the taste of its boundary. I turned back. Not from fear exactly. From sufficiency.

When we do meet, we do not call.

We touch antennae, if the thin forward feelers can be called that. They are more like wet threads extended from the body, tasting gradients in the air. We exchange the chemistry of our small interiors: readiness, hunger, mineral balance, the absence or presence of eggs. There is no courtship as a bird might know it, no display, no song. Display would be absurd here. Color would be waste. Sound would vanish into the plateau’s endless falling.

If I lay eggs, I do not build a nest. I press them into a protected smear beneath a crust that is damp but not drowning. Each egg is clear, flattened, and edged with mineral dust so that it adheres to the stone. Some calcify before they wake. Some are eaten by worms when the seep rises too high. Some hatch into translucent young so thin that light almost passes through them without consequence. They feed first on the weakest films, the fresh bloom at the edge of a droplet, the soft residue of my own shed skin.

My shed skins remain.

Everything remains, but changed.

When I molt, the old shell clings to the underside of the stone, legs spread, body empty, indistinguishable within days from a pale flake of crust. The living film grows over it. Minerals enter it. Water hardens it. I have passed places where generations of us have become a cloudy lamination, a faint animal layer in the mineral skin. No grave. No decay. Only whitening, sealing, being taken into the surface that fed us.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness belongs to creatures who expect answer at their own scale. I live among continuous answer. The stone answers the water. The water answers the air. The film answers the mineral. My feet answer the slickness beneath them. Hunger answers growth. Rest answers glare.

There is danger, but it is not dramatic.

A long dry glare can trap me away from the good seams, forcing me into stillness until my body thins and salts gather at my joints. A sudden cold can seal the film under glass and leave me unable to feed. A drop falling too heavily from a new path can knock me from the ceiling into the cup below, where the shallow water is crowded with mineral teeth and pale worms. A human finger, curious and warm, could erase the work of many seasons by lifting the crust under which I wait.

But most days are not events.

Most days are the old chord continuing.

I wake when the stone’s surface softens. I feed where the film has thickened. I pause when the chemistry turns bitter. I move sideways through roaring brightness and silver shade. Above me the high sky burns cold. Around me the plateau drips with the patience of something that has forgotten beginning and has no need of end.

If you found yourself here as me, you would have to surrender almost every large animal idea of place.

A mile would mean nothing. A horizon would be irrelevant. Weather would not be a thing passing overhead but a change in adhesion beneath the feet. Food would not be elsewhere; it would be the barely visible difference between one shine and another. Shelter would be a lip of stone. Time would be the interval between the swelling of a droplet and its fall.

You would not think, I am small in a vast place.

You would think, if thought existed in you at all:

Here is wet enough.

Here is shaded enough.

Here the film has risen.

Here the stone is speaking in salts.

And you would lower your pale combs to the slick mineral skin and graze from the plateau’s nearly invisible life, while all around you the stone country thundered with drops, unchanged, crowded, bright, and cold.
