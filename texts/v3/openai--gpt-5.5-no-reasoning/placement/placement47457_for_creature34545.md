I would not find myself at the center first.

That is too obvious—the spire, the knocking chambers, the vertical city with its hidden citizens and patient signals. I would want to be there, of course. Any creature with curiosity, even a dry and economical curiosity, would orient toward that pale spear and its scaffold of galleries. But I do not think I begin there. I think I begin at an edge, in one of the new places: the quarter where the chasm has recently opened and the city is still deciding how to become itself around the wound.

Not down in the deepest part of the split, where warm air rises from the unfinished mountain. Not on the rim, exposed to the glass glare. Somewhere along the lip of the new crack, where pale stone is still naked in sharp patches and already being claimed.

I am very small.

Small enough that a grazer’s footfall is weather. Small enough that the closing of a blade-thicket is an evening catastrophe if I misjudge its hospitality. Small enough to use the underside of a vine-chain as a road and the pore-ridges of a black mat as a landscape.

At first I might seem to be one of the horn-leaf scavengers: a flat body, many hidden legs, a brown shell like a shed plate or a fallen scale of the thickets. But I am not broad enough for that. I am narrower, jointed, more deliberate. A sliver-animal. A hinge-bodied thing that lives in the margin between bare stone and first growth.

My back is not smooth. It carries a crust.

That is the important part.

On my upper plates grows a small traveling district of dust-binding filaments, pale mineral lichens, dormant spores, and a few black mat-seeds held in pores along my shell. To a larger eye I am debris: a flake of horn, a curled husk, a bit of old vine segment broken loose and caught on the ledge. But my surface is inhabited. I am not only an animal moving through the basin. I am a raft of beginnings.

I live where new stone has appeared.

When a flake falls from the spire, when the rim sheds a blade of bright mineral, when an overnight crack opens in a terrace and exposes untouched pale faces, creatures like me arrive. Not quickly. Nothing here arrives quickly. But with a kind of inevitability. We come along the dust-lines, beneath the lattice, through the brief safe shadows cast by the new edges. We taste with our feet. We test the stone for salts, heat, bitterness, sharpness, the hidden breath from below.

I would be one of the first walkers on unclaimed surfaces.

Not a ruler. Not a builder in the proud sense. More like a surveyor whose maps are bodily. Wherever I pause, something from my back may take hold. Wherever I scrape, I open a grain of stone. Wherever I excrete, I leave a tiny crystal of permission or refusal. My life is a series of almost invisible decisions that, accumulated with others, become alleys, borders, orchards, mats, future shelter.

I am called nothing, because there is no calling here in that way.

But if I had to be named by what I do, I would be a seam-bearer.

A carrier of seams. A maker of first edges.

My body is dry and low, lacquered in overlapping plates the color of old ivory at the sides and smoke-brown above. Each plate has a raised lip, and between the lips are sheltered grooves where spores lodge. I have many legs, but they are fine, folded under me like the teeth of a comb. They touch the stone lightly. I do not run. I ripple forward in small sections, each segment waiting for the last to confirm that the surface is safe.

My mouth is a rasp set beneath the head, used not for flesh but for films: salt bloom, dead pore-skins, the first gray veils forming on bare rock, resin crumbs dropped from young vine-chain joints. I eat what is too thin for larger mouths. I feed on the almost-not-yet.

My eyes are poor. Perhaps I have none worth mentioning. Light matters to me as heat through the shell, as danger-glare from the orchards, as the long cooling slope toward evening. I know the world through contact and delay: vibration traveling through stone, the tightening of vine-chains above me, the chemical refusal of a thicket, the recent passage of grazers written in scraped mat and crystalline pellets.

The new chasm is my district.

Its lip is dangerous, which means it is rich. Warm dry breath rises from below in pulses too slow to be wind. The black mats have not yet sealed everything. Glass seedlings stand here and there, no taller than my body at first, their clear fans still folded tight like splinters. Amber nubs swell from the deeper cracks. Vine-chain segments extend across the gap, each new hollow joint pale and tender before hardening. Dust collects in small crescents behind sharp stone teeth.

The larger city is established. This place is not. Here the customs are still being negotiated.

A mat advances over a fresh face. A crust from my back takes hold just ahead of it, altering the chemistry by a fraction. A grazer noses along the edge and crops the mat back, leaving a ragged corridor. A thread-bird lands badly on a young chain and dislodges a capsule, which falls into a shaded notch. I spend half a day under that capsule, feeding on its outer bloom, and in doing so scratch the stone enough for one of my carried colonies to settle. A month later there will be a dark patch there. A year later perhaps a little roof of blade-plates.

That is how I matter.

Not by intention. By consequence.

I have enemies, though most never think of me. A thread-bird may take me if it finds me crossing an exposed pane of stone at the wrong hour. One quick hook of the beak, a pressure like the whole sky folding shut, and I become mineral-rich food for a climber. Transparent scissor-mouthed hunters wait under young thicket leaves and cut into the softer joints beneath my shell. The cord-knot predators are too large for me, usually, but their tightening bodies change the paths I can use. A closing blade can pin me. An amber column can seal a chamber while I am inside, and then I become part of its private chemistry.

Mostly, though, I fear exposure.

To be too long on bare stone at midday is to lose what little inward moisture I keep. My underside valves close. My legs stiffen. The living crust on my back seals itself and becomes heavy. I must reach shadow before my own body begins to mistake itself for a husk.

So my days are made of crossings.

At dawn I unseal beneath a loose plate near the chasm’s edge. The stone is cool enough to walk, but warmth is rising. I climb onto the pale exposed lip where a new crack feathered open during the night. I taste sharpness there: unweathered mineral, no mat acid yet, no grazer-scrape, no old resin. Good stone. Empty stone. I press myself flat and remain motionless while the first light enters the basin.

The glass orchards brighten above me to the east. Their glare spills across the terraces. I feel it as a hardening on one side of the shell.

I move before the grazers come.

Each step sets down a trace: not a footprint that would last, but a faint smear of body-salt, a dusting of spores from my back, a microscopic abrasion. I cross the new stone in a crooked line because the surface dictates me. Here a ridge too sharp for my belly. There a warm thread rising from below. There a bitter mineral I avoid. Behind me, nothing visible changes.

But the basin notices in its own manner.

By midmorning I am under a young vine-chain bridging the chasm. Its underside is still gray-amber, not yet bleached. I rasp at a bloom forming along one collar. The chain transmits distant events: the landing of a thread-bird somewhere far along the lattice, the slow tightening of an older segment, a grazer rubbing its ceramic flank against a support. The vibrations pass through my feet and into my body, each one a fact without narrative.

A knock comes from the spire.

Three soft taps, separated by patient intervals.

I stop feeding.

Not because I understand. I do not translate it into speech. But every hard surface in the basin takes the knock and alters it. The vine-chain carries it as a long, thinning shiver. The chasm wall returns it faintly. Even the spores on my back seem to pause, their filaments contracting by an amount no eye would see.

Much later, something knocks back.

By then I am moving again.

At midday I hide. I wedge myself in a triangular pocket beneath a flake of fresh stone already rimmed with gray film. Around me, the city holds its breath. The mats open their pores only as much as necessary. The amber nubs click within themselves. Above, a thread-bird drifts from one chain to another, its shadow passing over my shelter like a brief temperature. A grazer herd comes near the lip of the chasm and freezes, all of them becoming stones at once. I feel their stillness more than their movement.

In that stillness, I shed.

Not the whole shell. Only the uppermost transparent lacquer from two plates, curled away by heat and inner pressure. The shed flakes remain caught in the living crust on my back. Tiny organisms there begin to mine them before they have fully separated from me. I am host, road, refuse, and weather to them.

This is not uncomfortable. It is simply being alive.

In the afternoon I descend slightly into the new split, where shade has gathered and the warm breath from below is stronger. Here the stone tastes different: metallic, alkali, almost nourishing. The first black mat has made islands along the wall, each no wider than my head. I visit them carefully, not eating the living centers, only the dead rims and the crust between. If I strip too much, the colony fails. If I strip too little, it seals over the pore where I feed. There is no morality in this. Only balance enforced by consequence.

On a narrow ledge I find a dead dust-hopper.

It has jumped its last jump badly and struck the rock. Its body is already drying into a bright kink of shell and resin. I feed for a long while from one leg joint, taking almost nothing wet because there is almost nothing wet to take. What I leave will be opened by mites, then by filaments, then by the mat. The hopper will become a darker stain, then a salt fleck, then part of the ledge’s future skin.

Evening calls me upward.

Not as sound. As cooling. As a relaxation in the stone and a change in the pressure under my plates. I climb from the chasm before the blade-thickets close their lower shelters. A thread-bird hangs under a vine-chain ahead of me, already settling for night, its filament feathers tucked close. It could eat me. It does not. Perhaps it has fed. Perhaps I resemble a husk. Perhaps the dimness has made me uninteresting.

I reach my shelter as the glass fans fold.

The movement is too slow to watch and yet suddenly they are no longer open. Across the basin, blade-plates touch each other with their dry whisper. The amber columns cloud. The black mats seal. The grazers withdraw into cracks and beneath roofs made by plants that might also kill them if they mistime the retreat.

I tuck myself under the same loose plate where I began, but I am not unchanged.

A few spores are gone from my back. A few new grains cling there. My gut holds salt, film, dead hopper, resin bloom. My feet have written a nearly invisible passage over new stone. Beneath one of the places where I rested, a colony may begin. Or not. Most beginnings fail. That is why the city sends so many.

Night comes.

I close in layers.

Legs folded. Mouth sealed. Side-valves shut. Back crust tightened against the cold. The organisms I carry seal too, making of me a small roofed quarter, a sleeping terrace no larger than a fingernail.

If you lifted the stone, you might think you had found nothing but a dry flake with legs.

But I would be one of the ways the basin remembers where it can grow next.

Not a citizen of the old spire, not a grazer keeping the avenues open, not a thread-bird stitching accidents into architecture. I would be smaller and earlier than that: a bearer of first films, a walker of raw edges, a little inhabited shard moving through the newborn district.

I would live where the mountain has just broken.

I would carry the city into the crack.
