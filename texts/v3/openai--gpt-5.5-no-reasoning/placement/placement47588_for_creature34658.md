I think I would find myself where the city is most nearly undecided about what it is: not in the human rooms, not in the open basins, not at the very summit of the condenser towers, but in the in-between machinery of water’s return.

A western shelf. Late day. One of the old refining halls whose purpose has become ceremonial to the systems that outlived it.

From outside, the hall is almost plain: a long low structure of heat-glazed brick and bleached concrete, set back from a causeway that crosses warm salt flats. Its roof slats still open every evening. No hand opens them. No guild has maintained the mechanism in generations, except perhaps indirectly, by leaving it alone. At a certain fall of pressure, at a certain amount of heat stored in the southern wall, the warped rails remember motion and the roof lifts its ribs one by one.

Inside, the air is orange and dim. It smells of hot mineral, stagnant sweetness, old oil, and rainwater that has passed through too many throats.

The floor is flooded, but not evenly. Water lies in rectangular troughs and shallow sunken lanes, interrupted by platforms, broken machine beds, ceramic pillars, and the black mouths of drains. Some of the pools are open. Some are filmed with living skins. Some are clear enough to see silver animals turning below the surface, each movement brief, deliberate, vanishing almost before it begins. Light comes down through the roof slats in slanting blades. Where it strikes the water, the ceiling shivers with reflected bands.

I am not one of the builders.

I do not think I am one of the people in wrapped veils who know the names of valves and wall-catchments. I am too small in my sense of the place for that, too local, too bound to the breathing of one precinct. And I am not exactly a wild animal either, though wildness has entered me, or I have entered it.

I am a maintenance crawler that has forgotten the distinction between repair and feeding.

Or no—“forgotten” is wrong. Forgotten suggests there was once a clean boundary, and here there rarely is. I am descended from devices made to clean the condenser meshes, roof gutters, filter grates, and mineral-thickened troughs of the hall. My first ancestors were articulated ceramic things with brass backs and soft polishing pads. They followed tracks. They scraped carbonate from glass. They cleared small blockages. They returned to charging cradles along the north wall and waited for rain.

But the cradles died. Or changed. Or were colonized.

Now we are not many, but enough. We live in the hall’s upper shadows and along its damp mechanical ribs. We are palm-long when folded, forearm-long when extended. Low-bodied. Jointed. Shell-backed, but in overlapping plates rather than a single dome. My carapace is a patchwork of old manufacture and grown repair: white ceramic scutes, amber chitin at the hinges, tarnished metal reinforcement along the spine, mineral-rimmed sensory pits. A strip of original maker’s enamel remains on my left side, blue beneath the salt. No one can read it anymore, not even those who once made us.

I have six primary legs and two smaller tool-limbs held beneath my front edge. The legs end in soft gripping pads that can hold wet tile, oxidized iron, shell composite, or the underside of a pipe slick with condensation. The tool-limbs are delicate. One still carries a crescent scraper of manufactured ceramic. The other grew back wrong after a vertical hunter took it; it is pale, flexible, tipped with a living hook and a ring of tasting hairs.

I clean because I eat.

I eat because cleaning opens water.

At evening, when the roof slats lift and the hall exhales, I emerge from a fissure behind the old turbine housing. All day I have been folded into shade with others of my kind, conserving moisture beneath our plates. We rest touching one another lightly, not for affection exactly, though something like comfort may travel through contact. We exchange mineral salts. We sample each other’s surfaces. We know who has crossed which pipe, who has encountered poison, who has fed well, who carries eggs under the third plate.

The first task is listening.

Not with ears. With feet, belly, shell, mouthparts.

The hall speaks through interval and tremor. A pump far below gives one slow pulse every hundred breaths. Rainwater caught on the roof begins to thread through ceramic throats. Somewhere a flap valve clicks, hesitates, clicks again. The western gutter is narrowed; the sound of water there is too fine, too high. A filter grate near the south trough has begun to seal with animal mat. Good. Dangerous if left too long, but good tonight.

I move toward it.

The path is familiar, but never the same. A refinery is not a dead thing if water still moves through it. Mineral grows in a night. Films bloom between rains. Glass-flies lay eggs in screw holes. Silver channel animals nose into passages where they were not yesterday. My body remembers routes as temperatures and tastes: the hot iron span, the bitter white crust, the smooth patch where a shell-backed grazer once slept, the cool draft under the cracked settling tank.

I pass above a pool where heat-kites gather when the old lights come on. They are already descending through the roof openings, membranes half-clear, half-amber, like pieces of sunset loosened from the sky. They drift without sound except for the faintest clapping when one adjusts itself too near a beam. Below them, the silver animals circle. Above them, in the plaster shadows, two vertical hunters wait as dark interruptions of the wall.

I keep to the underside of a pipe.

My kind are not worth much meat, but hunger changes calculations. A vertical hunter will take us if rain has failed. A reflective lizard from the machinery field will crack us if it catches us in open sun. Children sometimes collect our shed plates from safe halls and string them into moisture charms. Basin tenders dislike us when we enter active cisterns. Tower guilds tolerate us if we keep to obsolete districts. The old hall tolerates us entirely. We are among the ways it continues.

At the south grate the mat has grown thick: black-red, opalescent at the wet edges, closed against the afternoon heat but waking now as the water deepens. I lower myself. My front limbs unfold.

Scrape, taste, pause.

Not too much.

If I clear the grate cleanly, the sudden flow will starve the mat, flush the glass-fly larvae, and draw eels from the lower drain. If I leave it sealed, the trough will flood the platform and drown the dry-crust colonies beneath the machine bed, and those colonies keep the biting mites off our eggs. So I open three channels, each no wider than my own forelimb. Water thick with loosened film begins to pass through. The grate gives a soft throat-sound. From below comes motion.

I feed on what I remove: mineral-organic paste, bitter, warm, bright with trace metals. My mouthparts work in quiet rows. My internal bladder takes what moisture it can. Excess salts move into a storage ridge along my back, where later they will harden into another pale rim of armor.

This is my work. This is my pasture. This is my prayer, if I have one.

When white rain comes, I become almost ecstatic.

Not in the way the people might use the word. I do not sing. I do not leap. But every surface becomes legible at once. The roof hisses though the rain itself is nearly silent. Light fills the hall so completely that shadows seem to detach and float. Dry mineral lines darken. Hidden inscriptions appear in the floor slabs beneath shallow water. The old refinery’s control markings show themselves for a few moments: arrows, numerals, warning bands, drainage maps, the ghost-language of builders.

I cannot read them as meaning, but I read what the water does with them.

Rain falls through the open slats in bright columns. The living skins in the troughs separate into plates to catch it. Glass-flies arrive in numbers, clicking, vanishing, flashing violet at the joints. The heat-kites descend lower, membranes beaded in fire. One comes close enough that I taste its shadow: warm animal, sky-salt, hunger. A vertical hunter strikes and misses. Its forelimbs slap wet plaster. Every creature in the hall registers the failure.

White rain rearranges rank.

The first to move are the small ones: thread scavengers, drain crabs, winged larvae, hinge mites. Then the grazers at the walls unseal their mouths and begin polishing. The silver bodies below rise toward the rain-dimpled surface. The old turbines turn, only slightly, enough to wake a chain of lights along the gallery. Half of them fail. The rest burn gold through vapor.

In those moments I climb to the highest interior rail I can safely reach. My shell drinks impact. Water runs through the seams in my back. Dormant pores open along my underside—organs not wholly grown, not wholly installed. I take in water directly, and with it the tastes of the roof, the sky, the neighboring basins, the rusted stack where lizards bask, the public channels uphill, perhaps even traces of the inhabited terraces where people stand beneath awnings and wait.

Then the rain passes.

The city begins again.

By night I may cross beyond the hall. Not far. My world is a linked set of wet routes: refinery roof, western gutter, causeway drain, abandoned pump frame, cracked condensation mast, return pipe to the hall. I know the causeway by its underside, where salt daggers grow and blind white crabs occupy the shadowed boltholes. I know the warm wind that rises from the basins and presses upward against my belly. I know the sound of distant cargo trams as a tremor through old reinforcement rods.

Sometimes I see the people.

Usually as legs, wrapped hands, lamp-glow, reflected face-shields. Once, before dawn, a tower climber crouched near the western gutter and watched me clear a mineral lip from a sluice. They did not interfere. Their glove rested beside me on the stone, enormous, damp, smelling of ceramic dust and human salt. After a long while they placed a bead of condensed water near my path—not from a private catchment, I think; a permissible gift. I drank. They touched two fingers to the wet stone, then to their eyelids, and left.

Since then, when I pass that gutter, I sometimes find the mineral scraped from the outer edge in a way that makes my work easier. Agreement, perhaps. Or coincidence. In this city, the difference is often only whether the pattern lasts.

I am not intelligent as the builders are intelligent. I do not hold maps in language. I do not tell stories of the city lifted from seabed, of the time before plantlessness, of the first tower to draw water from hot air. Yet I possess a form of memory suited to the place. I remember drought as narrowing corridors inside my shell. I remember productive rain as the taste of copper and fat film. I remember danger as vibrations in a wall. I remember individuals by their residues: the bitter guardian of the blue-studded catchment, the grazer with the broken left rim, the hunter who misses in white rain, the human with careful hands.

What am I, then?

A remnant tool that became livestock to no one.

A small engineer of accidental ecologies.

A hinge creature.

A maintenance animal in a hall that no longer distinguishes maintenance from metabolism.

I live where the old refining works open their roof to evening and power lights for no master. I move through wet stone and obsolete machinery, clearing just enough flow, eating just enough growth, preserving blockages when blockages shelter life. The city is too large for me to know, but its principle is present in every grate I touch: water must move, but not too freely; life must feed, but not consume the channel; heat must be endured until it can be turned.

If you looked for me, you would not see me at first.

You would stand in the western hall at dusk, hearing only the plink of condensate and the soft mechanical sigh of roof slats opening. You would see shallow pools, old rails, heat-kites in the high orange gloom, silver animals below the water, black mats waking on the walls.

Then a piece of mineral crust on the underside of a pipe would unfold six legs.

That would be me.
