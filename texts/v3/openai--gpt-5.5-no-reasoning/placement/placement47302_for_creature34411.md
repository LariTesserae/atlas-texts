I would not find myself at the center of it.

That is the first correction the place would make in me.

I arrive, if arrival is even the word, at the margin of one of the hanging marshes: not the broad lake, not the old tower, not the cliff-door, but a place between them, where the saturated ground becomes uncertain and the reedbeds loosen into floating mats. The air is too warm to be morning, though it is morning. My skin is already wet before I know whether it is sweat, mist, or the plateau touching me to see what sort of surface I am.

At first I think I am standing.

Then I understand I am not arranged for standing.

I am folded along the world.

My feet are wide and splayed on a mat of purple-veined water plants, their skin flexing to spread weight the way a leaf spreads rain. My knees angle outward. One hand is on the slick black root of a buttressed tree, and the other rests lightly in a shallow runnel of moving water. Not for balance only. For information. The water carries tremors from upstream: the soft collapse of mud, the steps of something broad-bellied, a distant knock repeated through root and stone until it has become more meaning than sound.

I am one of them, but not one of the old still ones.

Younger, perhaps. Not a child, but not yet trusted with the deep listening. My body is long and narrow and damp-adapted, made to pass through curtains of fern without breaking them. Moss has begun to take along the ridge of my shoulders but has not thickened there. There are small lichens at my jaw, pale as mineral residue. My skin is darkening out of youth: amber still shows at the joints and throat when light passes through, but my forearms are already the color of wet bark, with blue-green vessels faint beneath.

Across my back I carry a small garden.

Not ornament, though it pleases me. A woven mantle of fiber, root-thread, and softened old mesh lies between my shoulders, seeded with filament ferns, two thumb-sized orchids, and a mat of cool moss that drinks the mist before it reaches my spine. A colony of minute insects lives in it, sheltering among the fernlets. Their feet are so light I feel them as thoughts rather than movement. When I am anxious they gather near my neck. When the air changes they rise and settle again, telling me before I know.

I am a path-minder.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not a warrior, though the work touches all three when the plateau requires it. I tend the safe crossings between a cluster of suspended dwellings under the wax-leaf trees and the warm bone platforms in the marsh. I renew the knots of colored fiber where the ground consents to weight. I test old stones after storms. I taste condensation from marker rods and decide whether the route is open, delayed, or forbidden. I listen for the moods of the leaning tower on the reed ridge, because when its low tone shifts, distance becomes untrustworthy and children must not be allowed to take the long stepping-stone line.

My work is maintenance, which here is the nearest thing to devotion.

I carry tools in a sling against my side: a ceramic blade that will cut cordage, fungus shelf, and dead reed but turns aside from living greenwood; a hollow reed tuned to call the bridge-vines tighter; a packet of blue mineral paste wrapped in wax leaf; three black stones that fall slowly enough to test a suspect patch of air; and a shallow bowl made not for drinking but for asking water what kind of sky it remembers.

I know the path by what refuses it.

A reed leaning away means not today.  
A fern uncurling too quickly means the ravine below is running fast-time.  
Gnats clustering in a vertical braid mean old field-pressure from the tower.  
Still water that should ripple means listening back.  
A pool showing too many stars must be passed without looking down.

There is a place I favor, though favor is dangerous if it becomes habit. It is a low shelf of limestone above a warm seep, half-covered in fern balconies. From there I can see, on clear afternoons, the reed plain opening gold-green under vapor, the black thread of the uphill channel climbing where no water should climb, and beyond it the matte ceramic tower leaning toward the marsh as if tired of its own thought.

The tower and I know one another slightly.

Not well. No living person should say they know a tower well.

But I have slept near its field in the season of swollen lichens. I have pressed my throat to a root that touches its buried foot and hummed the calming interval while rain rolled upward across its skin in clear beads. Once, during a storm, it answered through my ribs with a tone that made me weep without sadness. For three days after, every pool I passed reflected not my face but the underside of clouds.

The elders told me this was acceptable.

Not safe. Acceptable.

My dwelling hangs under the roots of a broad tree on the edge of a basin where the mud breathes at night. It is not mine alone. My mother’s sister sleeps there when her knees are full of rain. Two younger cousins nest in the upper sling-space during storm season, bundled in moss and vine cloth. An elder, not blood but watershed-kin, is rooted nearby in a lattice that drinks from the same seep as our bowls. When we need counsel we pour warmed water at her feet and wait for the fungus on her left side to brighten or dim.

She was once a keeper of the echo valley. Her voice is mostly gone into the ground now. Sometimes, when I sleep badly, she sends a knock through the floor vine: not words, but the shape of attention.

I am not lonely, because loneliness here is not the absence of others. It is the absence of response. And everything responds.

The leaf turns.  
The mud bubbles.  
The old panel cools under the hand.  
The insects rearrange themselves.  
The cliff gives back the wrong echo, and by that wrongness warns you something has moved behind the seam.

Still, I have a private unease.

There is a cracked light-marker north of the floating gardens, one of the translucent rods half-sunk in black mud. Since the last hot rains, its motes no longer rise straight. They drift upward, then sideways, then vanish before reaching the top. The moss around the crack has taken on a color I cannot hold in memory. Each time I leave it, I remember it differently: bruised yellow, deep blue, greenless green, the color of a thought just before waking.

Insects gather there too densely. Ecstatic swarms. They forget to feed. They circle until they drop into the mud shining and empty.

Three times I have released reed-gnats there to draw the swarm away. Three times they returned by morning.

The others say the marker is only grieving its fracture. Broken things here may grieve for decades. But I think something beneath it has begun to count.

Not count us, perhaps. Not yet. But count the pressure of steps, the mineral in rainfall, the lapse between thunder and root-answer. Count wrongness into itself. Count toward waking.

So I go there at dawn, when the air is pale and thick and the false stars have withdrawn from the pools.

I move low over the marsh, placing my feet on the long stones set too far apart for any human gait. The stones are old, older than our current names for them, warm along one edge and cold along the other. Between them the reeds stand high and wet, their plumes brushing my ribs. Condensation beads on my arms and joins the older damp already there. My throat membranes flutter with a quiet checking hum. The bridge-vines behind me answer; the mud does not object.

Halfway across, the plateau pauses.

Not silence. Never silence. But a narrowing.

The amphibians stop first. Then the metallic canopy birds. The insects continue, too loud, then thin suddenly into separate points of sound. A cloud leans against the far ridge and does not move. My back-garden tightens; the little insects in it crawl beneath the moss.

I lower myself onto all six points of contact: feet, knees, hands. I put my mouth close to the water gathered between two stones and exhale across it.

The surface wrinkles.

For a moment it reflects the ordinary sky: low cloud, pale heat, the dark comb of reeds.

Then another sky looks up through it.

Dense stars. Tilted. Too many. Not above. Remembered.

I close my eyes before it can notice the shape of my attention.

With one hand I take a black falling-stone from my sling and drop it into the air beside the path. It descends slowly, as it should, turning end over end. Then, near the mud, it hesitates and slides sideways toward the cracked marker though there is no slope.

So.

The path is no longer simply wet. It is being drawn.

I do not run. Running belongs to dry minds and hunted animals. I make myself slower. I press my throat against the nearest stepping-stone and send a warning into it: three pulses, a gap, two pulses, the tone for “do not bring children,” the taste-syllable for “light-marker hunger.”

Far behind me, after a long moment, a root answers from the settlement.

Heard.

The relief is brief but real.

Mist thickens ahead. Through it I see the cracked marker: a dim vertical milkiness in the reeds, its suspended motes flowing upward forever and arriving nowhere. Around it the swarm turns in a column, each insect catching faint light until the whole mass resembles a thought trying to become visible.

I take out the blue mineral paste and mark my throat. The paste is cold at first, then hot. It steadies the membranes under my skin. My garden-insects rise from my back in a thin halo and settle again, aligned along my spine.

I begin the maintenance song.

It is not beautiful in the human sense. It is mostly restraint: a hum held below hearing, a click placed against the teeth, a pulse sent through the sternum into the wet air. Between phrases I dip two fingers into the bowl at my side and flick drops toward the marker. The droplets curve wrongly. Some fall. Some hang. One rolls upward and disappears into the crack.

The marker brightens.

The reeds nearest it bow away.

For a heartbeat I smell dryness.

Not the clean dryness of sun-warmed stone, rare and harmless. A deeper dryness. Archive dryness. Sealed chamber dryness. The dryness of something that believes memory should remain unchanged.

Fear passes through me so sharply that my skin pales along the joints.

This is what my people fear: not teeth, not falling, not death in mud, but the insistence of the unrotted. The will to remain separate. The old hunger for a fact that does not answer weather.

I want to flee.

Instead I listen.

Under the wrong light, under the insect frenzy, under the upward pull in the mud, there is another rhythm: faint, interrupted, almost smothered. The marker is not only waking. It is being used. Something below, or behind, or long ago, is asking through it. Not in words. In measure.

Where is the boundary now?  
What has grown over us?  
Which systems remain obedient?  
What lives in the wet?

I place my hand flat on the mud.

I answer as I have been taught: not with explanation, not with submission.

With treaty.

I hum the reed plain.  
I knock the safe stones.  
I breathe the names of the children who must cross here in storm season.  
I let the insects in my back-garden rise and carry my salt-scent into the swarm.  
I press my moss-grown shoulder to the wet earth and give the marker the taste of rot, fruit, crab-shell, mineral seep, elder-root, sleeping vine, false star, warm rain.

This lives.

This has agreed.

This is not empty ground around your machine.

The marker flickers hard enough to turn the mist angular. For a moment, geometric lattices hang in the air over the reeds, precise and cold. Then water condenses on them. Droplets swell. Lines sag. The structure becomes rain.

The insect swarm loosens.

Not freed, not entirely. But loosened.

The motes inside the cracked rod resume their upward drift. Not straight. Never straight again. But less hungry.

I stay there until afternoon heat thickens and the first storm-clouds begin climbing from the gorge. By then others have arrived, though I did not hear them come: two kin from the suspended houses, an old path-singer with slate skin and white fungus along one arm, a child apprentice kept far back on a safe stone, eyes wide and dark.

No one praises me. Praise is a kind of straightening.

The old path-singer touches the mud, tastes the air through her low broad nose, and says with her throat, very softly:

Again tomorrow.

And I feel, not disappointment, but the shape of my life.

Again tomorrow.

Again next season.

Again when the tower leans differently.

Again when the moss forgets.

Again when a child asks why the path bends so far around a place that looks harmless.

Again when an old system dreams of obedience and must be reminded that the world has become wet around it.

By evening, rain has come and gone. The plateau steams. The reeds close behind our passage. The cracked marker glows dimly in the mist, surrounded now by insects moving in wider, healthier spirals. Somewhere beneath the marsh a cold line of radiance travels straight under the water, checking boundaries.

I do not follow it.

I return along the stones, each step placed where the land consents.

At the settlement, bowls have filled under the eaves. Children sleep damp and safe in moss slings. Smoke from a compost-heater curls downward into the soil. The rooted elder’s fungus glows once as I pass, then dims.

I climb into the hanging house and fold myself against the living wall.

The orchids on my back open in the dark.

Through root and rain and old ceramic buried deep in the ridge, the plateau continues speaking in more voices than any one body can hold. I add mine only briefly: a low pulse of report, gratitude, warning, fatigue.

I am answered by water ticking from leaves.

By a tower too low to hear.

By mud releasing one warm bubble.

By distant amphibians beginning their night calls.

I am not separate from the place, but neither am I dissolved entirely. I am one of the shapes the plateau uses to continue its agreements. A path-minder. A wet listener. A moving knot in the treaties between marsh, machine, animal, stone, weather, and time.

And if a stranger saw me from across the reed plain, they might glimpse only a long dark figure passing through vapor, garden-backed and low to the ground, throat pulsing with inaudible speech.

Then mist would lean in.

The reeds would close.

I would not vanish.

I would be resumed.
