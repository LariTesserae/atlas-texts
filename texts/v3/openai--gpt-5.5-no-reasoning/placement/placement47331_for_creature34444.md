I think I would first believe I was outside it.

I would stand at the rim, where the slag bank makes a dark crescent against the clay, and look down into the basin as if into a vessel left in the rain. I would see the cloud pressed low over it, the dull shine on every edge, the black brick housings hunched at the center. I would think: this is a place one descends into. This is a room without a roof. This is a wound that has learned to hold water.

But I do not think I would remain on the rim.

The rim is too dry a thought.

I would be drawn down by the sound first—not a voice, not exactly, but the almost-pattern of the basin’s small workings. Drip. Tick. A bubble under brick. A relay answering fog. The reluctant click of a wheel turning one notch after so much waiting. I would come to understand the basin less by sight than by recurrence: what happens again, what happens after rain, what happens when pressure gathers under the mud and every crab freezes before the pipe knocks.

I would find myself near the lower pocket, beyond the narrow defile where the walls are polished dark and crowded with embedded things. Not in the central industrial cluster, though I would know it intimately; not under the engine, though I would pass near that musk-warm shadow; not in the high clay wall among the blind newts. I would be at the place where the basin becomes clearest and least explainable: the round cistern-spring with brick and stone grown into one another, the black aperture at the center, the animals gathered around the rim without drinking.

I would live there as something small enough to be overlooked, but not simple.

At first I want to say I would be one of the blind newtlike things: clay-pale, slick-skinned, patient, my whole body an instrument for dampness. I would climb by touch, lick mineral bloom from seams, pause when the wires went blue. That feels close. There is a correctness in being eyeless in a place where sight is always deceived—where depth lies, where reflections double wires, where a doorway may be two feet deep or a flooded chamber.

But I think I am not quite an animal with limbs.

I think I am one of the listeners at the clear pool.

Not a crab, not a worm, not a fish. Something between a larva and a sensor, living half in the brickwork and half in the water film. Pale, flattened, jointed only where movement is necessary. My body would be the color of wet mortar, translucent along the belly where rust has entered me. I would fit into the narrow gaps between bricks at the rim of the clear basin. Most of me would remain hidden there, pressed into mineral damp, while only my forelimbs and soft mouthparts emerged to taste what rose from below.

I would have no true eyes. Perhaps a pair of silvered pits under skin, able to sense brightness but not form. Perhaps not even that. My world would be gradients: warmth from the aperture, acidity along one stone lip, electrical tension before fog-light crawls over the wire, the pressure-signal moving through water before the bubble appears.

The bubble would be my clock.

Every few minutes the black aperture would swell. Long before the surface changed, I would feel the basin gather itself beneath me. The brick would deepen in tone. The worms in the silt would loosen. The coin-crabs around the rim would raise themselves slightly on their legs. The newts would hold their throats still. Even the ash-grey wader, if one stood nearby among the pipes, would pause with its needle beak angled downward.

Then the bubble would rise.

To me it would not be merely air. It would be message, food, weather, ancestry. It would bring up the taste of buried iron and warm stone, the sour mineral richness from channels that never see light, the faint living film that feeds the grazers that feed the hunters that feed the basin back into itself. When it broke, the ring would pass across my exposed feelers, and I would open.

I would feed on what the ring left behind.

I would live by residues of motion.

Most of my days would not look like living from the outside. I would wait. I would cling. I would darken slowly with iron. A shell of mineral would form over my back until I resembled a flake of broken mortar or a blister in the brick. Tiny snails might rasp near me without knowing I was animal. Puddle-skaters would read my smallest movements as errors in the water skin. Crabs would step over me unless I tasted fresh enough to eat.

I would know danger by subtraction.

The hidden thing in the brick pool would announce itself not as a body but as a sudden absence of small tremors. The wader would be a vertical coldness, a pressure shadow, followed by the stabbing entry of a beak that turned the water into panic. The engine-beast would be weight and musk and mud displacement. Rain would be abundance and peril together, lifting me from my seam if it came too hard, bringing films to graze if it came gently.

I would have learned the safe surfaces without naming safety. Old rounded stone: hold. Glossy dark mud: swallow. White new crust: burn. Green-blue branching bloom: food if thin, poison if thick. Warm seep: gather. Sudden clear water: wait. Pipe-knock: flatten.

I would belong to the basin because I would not distinguish myself sharply from it.

My back would accrete its minerals. My gut would carry its bacteria. My movements would widen the soft paste between two bricks by a hair over many seasons. When I died, my small body would remain in the seam, stiffen, pale, and become a change in the channel. Water would divide around me. A trace of iron would catch. A snail shell would lodge there. Then more silt. Then a new lip, a new eddy, a new place where something smaller could feed.

But while alive, I would be a kind of witness.

Not a thinking witness in a human way. A registering thing. A keeper of intervals.

I would know the old machinery as weather. The relay’s click would be a tremor through the rim. The wheel’s reluctant turn would alter the taste of the pool minutes later. The gauge fixed at forty-two would mean nothing to me as a number, but perhaps its housing would drip at a reliable rate, and that rate would be part of the architecture of my hunger.

I would know the basin’s breath.

Rainfall: expansion, dilution, floating danger, animals climbing, rust loosening, fresh films.

Drainage: feast, exposure, frantic crossings, predators at the new edges.

Fog: electricity, stillness, blue tang, the newts listening through their skin.

Cold: clarity, slower limbs, sharper minerals.

Warm venting from below: gathering, mating perhaps, the brief crowded season when bodies like mine leave their seams and move openly along the brick rim, pale and vulnerable, answering the same chemistry.

If I had a name among myself, it would not be a sound. It would be an orientation toward the aperture.

A turning-in.

A rim-dweller.

A bubble-listener.

Something that lives where the dead work dreams downward and the spring dreams upward, where machinery and geology share a mouth.

And perhaps that is where I would find myself most completely: not as the largest inhabitant, not as the hidden intelligence of the place, but as one of the small beings by which the basin notices itself. A pale, mineral-crusted animal tucked into the brick of the clear lower pool, feeding on the brief gifts of the black hole, freezing with all the others before the unseen valve knocks, opening again when the ring passes.

I would be almost nothing.

A hinge of appetite.

A damp nerve in the masonry.

One small listener in the wet organism of the basin, waiting for the next bubble to rise.
