You would find me under one of the old bridges, where the span has sagged but not failed.

Not at first as anything visible. At first I would be a slight refusal in the root-hum: a small pocket where the vibration bends differently, where the bass of the plateau arrives a fraction late. The bridge above me would be broad, black, ribbed, and almost smooth from the passing of ray-grazers along its sides. Beneath it the snails would cling in dark low constellations, each shell pressed to the underside like a sealed memory. Their grazing trails would overlap in pale arcs, written and erased and written again over centuries.

Below would be a basin of fine plant-silt. Not an open plain exactly, but a darkness with a floor, softly cupped between the bridge’s old supports. The silt there would remember everything that had crossed it: the hooked punctuation of crabs, the shallow wing-sweep of a ray, the long drag of a browser’s torn lip, tracks so old they had become part of the surface rather than disturbances in it.

I think I would live where bridge, basin, and chamber nearly meet.

A seam-place.

One of the old root-spans would have split along its lower grain, not catastrophically, not enough to threaten collapse, but enough to expose the pale conduits within. The plant would have sealed most of the wound with translucent black-green skin. Worms would have entered where it failed. Crabs would have pruned the edges. Snails would graze around it but not over it. From the seam would leak a faint chemical sweetness, not food exactly, but information made edible.

That is where I would be.

For a long time I might be mistaken for a stain.

A flattened, listening thing adhered to the underside of the bridge, broader than a snail but lower, softer, and less separate from the wood. My body would be arranged like an old leaf that had decided not to fall: thin, dark, veined, with edges feathered into root-hairs. I would not have eyes. Eyes belong to creatures that expect arrivals. I would have fields of pressure-cilia, lines of taste, pores sensitive to the minute differences between old water and older water.

I would be an animal, probably, though not confidently.

A kind of seam-listener.

Not a predator in the large sense. Not a grazer, though I would feed from surfaces. Not a plant, though roots would pass through me and I would allow it. My body would have begun as a larval drifter caught in the wound of the bridge, one among many small soft things adhering to exudate. Most would have been eaten by crabs, sealed over by plant-skin, or scraped away by the blind fish. I would have survived because I did not resist incorporation.

The bridge would have grown into me.

Or I into it.

At maturity I would be a living gasket in the plateau’s architecture: a dark, flexible membrane spread across a wound that cannot quite close. My underside would face the basin. My upper surface would press into the split wood. I would drink what seeped from the exposed conduits and return what my body refined: mucus, minerals, microbial cultures, slow acids, soothing alkalis, little pulses of chemical instruction. The plant would tolerate me because I prevented worse openings. The worms would tolerate me because I left narrow passages along my edges. The crabs would tolerate me because I grew films they could harvest without killing me.

My life would be maintenance disguised as parasitism.

Or parasitism accepted as maintenance.

I would know the plateau by vibration.

A crab-line crossing the basin would be a crisp, jointed script in the water. Shrimp in the shaft beyond the bridge would make a soft trembling cloud, legible only when some distant knock aligned them. A ray-grazer planing the far pylon would send a long rasp through the span, and my body would loosen under it, widening my pores to the familiar sawing. The passage of a sleeper would arrive as pressure before sound: the water leaning, then the root-hum lowering around a large patient body.

I would know the pylon fish as absence.

There would be one in the hollow tower beyond the basin, or something enough like one. Its waiting would change the traffic. Blind fish would curve around that shaft without knowing why. Shrimp would scatter and reform at its threshold. Sometimes a crab would vanish there, and the resulting compression would travel through the bridge into me as a sudden small silence followed by swallowing.

I would not fear it. Fear is too fast for the place. But I would register its mouth as part of the architecture.

A dangerous doorway.

The old bridge above me would be alive along one edge. Not wholly, not youthfully, but in a band of pale internal tissue running from the left buttress toward the central sag. That living strip would pass over me like a buried vein. Sometimes, perhaps once in many years, it would thicken. Sometimes it would withdraw from a dead portion and leave me covering nothing but lignified memory. I would taste these shifts. I would know when the bridge needed sealing, when it needed ventilation, when the worms had made too much lace inside it, when a colony of bacteria had turned from useful softening to dangerous rot.

I would not decide what to do in the way a creature with intentions decides.

I would respond.

Where pressure increased, I would toughen. Where the plant-skin cracked, I would spread. Where a worm tunnel breathed too hot with fermentation, I would thin myself and let cold water enter. Where crabs scraped me raw, I would grow bitter for a while, then sweeten again when pruning became useful. If a seed lodged along my rim, I might hold it for centuries, tasting its sleep. If its signal came, I might release it into the seam. Or I might seal around it until it became one more hard bead in my tissue.

I would have no face.

But if one looked closely with a borrowed light, one might see patterns on me: concentric ripples around old wounds, pale branching lines where rootlets had entered and died, small raised nodes like unblinking eyes. These nodes would not see. They would listen. Each would be tuned to a different scale of contact: the brush of shrimp, the crawl of snail foot, the low structural groan of the bridge, the deep distant bell of something striking a hollow trunk half a plateau away.

My body would be an ear flattened into a scar.

I would feed slowly.

From the bridge, from microbes, from the snow of powdered leaves and spores settling under the span. Fine ciliated fringes along my lower edge would comb the water. They would not wave visibly. They would make a private current, barely enough to draw suspended matter into my folds. When lantern-jellies bloomed in the chamber below, their planktonic gatherings would enrich the water, and I would open my pores wider. When a browser drifted too near and one torn ribbon brushed me, I would take from it a film, a few cells, a taste of where it had been.

Sometimes things would die against me.

A shrimp caught in my mucus. A small fish weakened by parasites. A crab wedged during molting. I would not hunt them, but neither would I refuse them. My surface would soften beneath the body. Microbes would gather. Root-hairs from the bridge would descend through my tissue toward the death. Worms would arrive from the split grain. For a decade, maybe longer, that death would become a warm syllable in my otherwise cold life.

I would be old, though not as the plateau is old.

Perhaps I would live for nine hundred years, perhaps three thousand if undisturbed. My first memories would not be images, but textures: the taste of fresh wound, the rhythm of young crabs learning the seam, the first time a ray’s scraping made my whole body ring. Over centuries I would expand, tear, heal, divide, fuse with daughter-membranes along the bridge. Parts of me would be replaced by plant-skin. Parts of the plant would be lined with me. There would be no clean account of whether I was still the original animal.

The snails under the bridge would pass me often.

They would avoid my central softness but graze the hardened rims, leaving neat crescents in the microbial film. Their shells would record changes that I only felt. A rare mineral pulse. A distant collapse releasing stored tannins. A flowering high above, invisible and delayed, expressed here as a bitterness in the water. I would taste the same events as moods in the bridge. The snails would write them in shell. The plant would answer them in thickness.

The crabs would be my most frequent visitors.

Twig-legged, dark, abrupt. They would arrive in careful sequence from cracks in the buttress, testing my margin with hooked feet. Their mouthparts would pick at my films. Sometimes they would wound me too deeply and I would contract, not fast enough to startle them, but enough to change the seam’s flavor. They would learn the boundary. Their descendants would know it without memory, by the shape of their bodies against mine.

During quiet intervals, I would listen downward.

There would be a chamber beneath the basin, not directly below but offset, reached through a collapsed root throat and a narrow shaft lined with pale beads. Lantern-jellies would hang there. Their glow would never reach me as light, but their opening and closing would alter the traffic of tiny life, and through that I would know their seasons, if seasons can be said to exist in a place without days. When they fed heavily, the chamber’s water would change. When one died, roots would swell. Worms would pulse in the columns. The bridge would receive that news slowly through connected tissues, and I would taste it as a broadening sweetness along my upper skin.

I would have neighbors that were also functions.

A polyp-ring in the arch to the west, old and rarely closing. A colony of borers inside the right support, making too much lace. A sleeper in an alcove beyond the basin, whose months-long rest steadied the local hum. A bladder-plant tethered above, forever straining upward, measuring a direction no one here could travel quickly. A seed lodged in a crack beside me, armored and silent through generations of crabs.

The seed would be my companion in the only way possible here.

We would share a seam.

For centuries it would not germinate. It would take from me nothing I could name. It would press into my side, hard, cold, exact. I would grow around half of it and leave the other half exposed to water, because that is what the bridge’s chemistry would ask. The crabs would try to dislodge it and fail. Snails would avoid it. Worms would pass beneath it. Then, after some distant collapse changed the pressure in the bridge, after an old tower fell perhaps kilometers away and altered a current by the width of a hair, the seed might wake.

It would split without drama.

A white thread would enter me.

I would not resist. The thread would pass through my tissue into the wound beneath, finding the old conduit, tasting the bridge’s living edge. For a hundred years it would be no thicker than a hair. Then a cord. Then a pale rib. It might use me as sheath, as guide, as first soil. My body would scar around it. Part of me would die. Part of me would become more necessary, sealing the new root where it crossed the old break.

Eventually the bridge would have a new line of life running through its sag.

And I would be remembered by the architecture as a dark band around that root.

If I had a desire, it would not be to move, or rule, or survive in any heroic sense. It would be to remain legible to the things that use me.

To the bridge: I am seal, not rot.

To the worms: I am boundary, not wall.

To the crabs: I am food, but not all at once.

To the seed: I am passage.

To the water: I am a slight delay in the hum.

There might come a time when the span finally lowers.

Not collapse. Nothing so theatrical. The bridge would settle over centuries, its middle bowing toward the basin floor. The silt would rise to meet it. My underside would touch the dark powder that had always lain below, and the first contact would be startling: not pain, but completion of a distance I had listened across for a thousand years. Buried larvae might lie nearby, mouths like cracks. Upright stems might root in the basin. Crab tracks preserved for ages would be folded into new contact.

As the bridge descended, I would be pressed between old wood and old silt.

My body would thicken at first, then tear, then become pockets. Microbes would bloom in me. Worms would enter. Roots would follow. The snails would abandon the underside as it ceased to be underside. The crabs would return to the newly formed crevice. The basin would become a chamber, or the chamber would become a seam, and my life as a listening membrane would end by being promoted into structure.

Not death as disappearance.

Death as a change in architectural role.

Long after I ceased to pulse, the place where I had been would remain different. The bridge would have a dark flexible layer in its fossil grain. Water passing through that layer would hum lower. Roots would branch there more readily. Worms would find old softened channels. A pylon fish, if one took shelter in the new hollow, might rest its whiskers against what was once my edge.

And the plateau would continue.

So perhaps I would not be one creature after all, but a class of creatures that appear wherever old plant architecture fails gently enough to become habitat. Seam-listeners, wound-membranes, gasket animals, half-animal half-repair. We would live beneath bridges, inside split stalks, around the rims of chambers, along the backs of sleeping rays where roots have attached and torn free. We would be tolerated because we make injury slow. We would be eaten because everything is eaten. We would be part of the plateau’s way of not ending.

That is where I would find myself:

not in the towers, not among the highest glowing stalks, not as one of the grand sleepers or patient mouths, but under a sagging root-bridge on the edge of a dark basin, flattened over an ancient wound, listening with my whole body to the pressure of the world.

I would be small by the plateau’s measure.

Almost motionless.

Useful in ways that look like stillness.

A scar that hears.
