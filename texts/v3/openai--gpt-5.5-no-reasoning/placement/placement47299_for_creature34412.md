I think I would find myself not in the open plain at first, though the open plain would be what made everything true.

I would come to awareness in shade.

Not deep shade, not shelter in any human sense, but the narrow mercy underneath the overhanging lip of a cistern, where the world’s flat brightness fails to reach for a few hours after sunrise. Above me: a rim of pale fired material, warm even before noon. Below me: a slow descent of terraced steps into a dark reservoir hollow. Across from me: another wall of the same manufactured stone, washed by old runoff into faint vertical ghosts. At the bottom, far enough down that sound seems to arrive from another climate, a black plate of water holds the sky without reflecting anything clearly.

I would be small enough that this cistern is not architecture but weather.

A ledge would be my horizon. A hairline fissure would be a valley. A bead of condensation would be a season. The slope of a mineral grain would matter. The difference between a surface that dries in twenty minutes and one that dries in thirty would be the difference between memory and extinction.

At first I imagine being a plant, one of the gray-green tufts flattened into a seam, waking only when rain enters the joint. But that is too singular. A plant here is a declaration, however modest. It has a body visible from above. It casts a shadow. It insists on the future by rooting.

I would be earlier than that.

I would be a darkening.

A thin living varnish on the underside of the cistern lip, where dew gathers before falling.

Not quite lichen, not quite algae, not one thing in the way animals are one thing. A composite patience. A colony. A pact between bodies too small to be seen separately and the mineral face that holds them. In dryness I would be indistinguishable from stain: smoky green-black, matte, irregular, spread along the lower edge of the overhang where the ceramic skin cools before the air does. If a traveler passed at noon, they would not call me alive. They might read me as weathering, seep mark, salt shadow, a flaw in the fired lime.

That would be nearly correct.

I would live by being mistaken for the surface.

My world would be the hour before evaporation.

Night would loosen heat from the plates overhead. The vast manufactured continent would exhale upward, radiating its stored day into the ordinary stars. The air, still warm, would thicken microscopically around edges, lips, undersides, places where temperature changes unevenly. Moisture would choose me because my surface is rougher than the bare ceramic. It would gather first as a silvering too slight to fall. Then as points. Then as beads.

Those beads would be morning.

I would swell without moving. Cells that had been folded into mineral sleep would take shape again. Membranes would soften. Filaments would open their tiny exchanges. Light, still low and gold, would enter obliquely under the cistern rim. For a little while I would be green enough to know myself as green.

Not bright green. Nothing here is permitted that extravagance for long.

A black olive green. A wet ash green. The color of life making no promise.

Drop by drop, what I could not hold would gather at my lower edge and fall into the trough beneath me. Each falling drop would make a sound enormous in proportion to my life: a clean tap, then another, then a silence in which the whole cistern listens. Some of the water would enter me. Some would carry fragments of me downward. Some would dissolve salts from the ledge and leave them behind when the heat returned. Some would fall all the way through shadow into the black reservoir, joining a depth I would never touch except by descent of dust, spore, and accident.

I would not walk the made desert. I would spread through its hesitations.

A smear under one lip. A freckle near a seep point. A powdery orange cousin on a sun-guarded wall farther east. A greenish veil along the shaded side of a parallel wall where rain shreds into mist. My being would not be continuous in space, but it would be continuous in method. Wherever the architecture miscalculates dryness by one hour, something like me appears.

The open plain would exist to test me.

Beyond the cistern rim, noon would whiten the world until distance becomes a rule. Terraces, pans, causeways, wall-lines, silent channels: all of it too large, too newly made, too clean of history. The surfaces would heat until air lifts from them in columns. Plates would tick. Seams would open by measures smaller than thought. Underfloor chambers would breathe stored warmth into the day.

I would feel this not as landscape but as pressure.

Dryness would arrive from above and around. My wet morning body would contract. The green would disappear into darkness. I would become crust again, a thin mineral-organic sentence paused mid-word. Salts would sharpen around me. Dust would settle. The ledge would grow hot enough to kill whatever tried to remain soft.

So I would not remain soft.

That would be my knowledge: how to stop almost entirely without ending.

No hunger would come for me. No mouth, no mandible, no grazing tongue, no scraping radula of an animal adapted to cistern shade. There would be no need to hide from movement because there is no movement with intention. I would not fear being eaten. I would fear only duration without contact.

The dangers of this world are impersonal and absolute.

Too much sun.

Too much salt.

A film of dust sealing me from dew.

A crack widening so that I dry beyond repair.

A rare hard rain tearing me from my ledge and washing me into a basin where I bloom briefly, then perish on an exposed floor.

Or, stranger, too much water: immersion long enough to drown the air-breathing parts of the pact, to loosen the colony from the surface that names it.

I would be adapted not to scarcity alone, but to alternation.

To touch and withdrawal.

To the coming and going of water through designed forms that never meant to be alive and yet have become the first law of life.

If rain came, I would know it before it struck me.

The air would change. Warm mineral dryness would become clay-scented. The cistern wall would darken in patches where pores opened to humidity. Far above, on the flats, rain would begin as a soft static over miles of ceramic plates. Channels would wake. The hollow throat of the reservoir would answer with delayed knocks and gulps as runoff found its paths.

Then water would reach the lip.

Not dew now, not beads, but sheets and threads. It would run over me, through me, under me. I would swell to the edge of rupture. Dormant fragments would lift. Some would be carried into the trough and down the wall. Some would lodge in lower pores. Some would be delivered to the moss-band treaty halfway down the cistern, where light and dampness meet in a narrow ring. Some would vanish into black water.

For a short interval I would become more than a stain. I would become an event.

The cistern wall would bloom in restrained colors: smoky green, black, rust-orange, the faint gold of suspended microbes in new runoff. The smell would be wet lime, algae, warmed dust. Other lives, normally invisible, would loosen at the basin margins above: mats softening, seeds taking water into their sealed interiors, flat grasses lifting a fraction from the seams. The world would not grow noisy. It would become articulate.

Every form would speak by darkening.

And then the rain would pass.

The artificial continent would begin again to take everything back into heat.

I would not think, but if I had something like thought, it would not be directed toward origin. I would not ask who made the plates, who laid the catchments, who planned the cistern’s terraces, why the horizon is so obedient. Those questions belong to creatures with surplus water in their bodies. I would know the builders only as geometry. Their intention would be indistinguishable from climate.

A straight wall means afternoon shade.

A seam means possible dust.

A lip means condensation.

A basin means bloom and salt.

A flaw means future.

I would be one of the first interpreters of the made world.

Not by understanding it, but by staining it selectively.

Where I live, the masonry would cease to be pure construction. It would become inhabited surface. My acids, faint as hesitation, would worry minerals from the wall. My dead layers would trap dust. My swelling and shrinking would pry at pores too small to be called cracks. Over seasons, I would make the ledge slightly better at holding water. Over longer seasons, I would make it easier for another form to arrive.

Perhaps one day a thread-root will enter the place where I have darkened the lime.

Perhaps a seed, blown or washed from a shrub kingdom above, will catch in the softened grit beneath me. It will wait through years of heat, inert as a thought no one has had yet. Then a private rain will fill the trough. The seed will split. A pale root will descend into the seam I helped prepare.

Then I will become foundation without ever having been soil.

That feels right for this world.

To live there is not to possess a body so much as to participate in the long conversion of surface into place.

So I would be found on the shaded inner lip of a young cistern in the made desert, where dawn condensation gathers and falls.

I would be a dew-fed crust colony, lichen-like, microbial and mineral-bound: black-green in wetness, gray-black in heat, almost invisible at noon, briefly alive in the first light.

I would have no voice, no limbs, no animal urgency.

I would live by waiting in the exact millimeter where water hesitates.

And beneath me, around me, through me, the raw artificial continent would continue learning how to become a biome—one drop, stain, crack, and darkened seam at a time.
