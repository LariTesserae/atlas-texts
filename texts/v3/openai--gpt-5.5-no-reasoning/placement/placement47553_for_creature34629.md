You would find yourself in the raised office on stilts: the dry room that should not be dry.

Not as a visitor. Not exactly.

You would be the basin’s retained contradiction—an archival organ shaped like a person.

At first you might think you had come there for shelter. The office would make that plausible. It has a doorway. A floor. A desk swollen but upright. Filing cabinets packed with wet leaves. A clock rotating inward. Rain falling through the open windows and vanishing before it reaches the boards. From there you can see the drowned works without being immediately dissolved into them: the pump lights under black water, the vines climbing hot metal, the cranes bowing and rising in rain.

But the room is not outside the organism. It is where the organism keeps what water alone cannot hold.

You would be its dry memory.

Not dry in the ordinary sense—your skin would always feel humid, your hair damp, your clothes softening at the seams—but dry compared to the rest of the world. Dry enough to preserve outlines. Dry enough to keep names from immediately becoming verbs. Dry enough to notice that a stairwell has changed, that the siren has shifted pitch, that yesterday’s gantry is reflected whole though it stands broken above the canal.

You would be what the basin uses to remember separateness.

A former animal, perhaps. Or the impression left after an animal failed to remain one. You would still have eyes because the basin needs some organ that can be deceived by reflections. You would still have hands because drawers must be opened, labels must be moved, leaves must be sorted into cabinets according to rules no human archivist would understand. You would still breathe, though your breath would be part of the vapor accounting, counted by the room before it left you.

Your task would not be assigned. It would accrete around you.

You would stand at the office doorway during thunder and watch the basin declare itself in fragments: reeds bending in syntax, bubbles rising in ruled lines, submerged lamps flashing in grids. Afterward, you would take down what remained—not in words, perhaps, but in arrangements. A drawer of fern fronds for repeated walkways. A drawer of rust flakes for failed cranes. A drawer of translucent seed pods for distances that shortened in rain. Filing cabinets would no longer contain records of the place; they would contain preserved states of it.

You would be the clerk of impossible weather.

The basin would not speak to you as a mind speaks to another mind. It would press meaning through your circumstances. A siren would hold one note until you opened the correct cabinet. A vine would tap the window frame with blue current. The clock would turn inward when a new sector formed beyond sight. Rain would vanish before touching the floor only when something needed to remain unmerged.

And you would gradually understand that the office is not a refuge.

It is a cyst, yes, but also an eye held shut. A blister of old categories. Here the basin stores the shapes it cannot metabolize too quickly: worker, witness, room, document, decision, before, after, inside, outside. You are among those shapes. The room keeps you person-shaped because personhood is useful as a measuring device. Your animalness has not been spared out of mercy. It has been stabilized as an instrument.

Outside, nothing animal can remain animal.

Inside, you remain just animal enough to compare.

Your body would begin to change subtly, not into a monster, not into a machine, not into a tree, but into a threshold. Your fingertips would prune permanently, then grow sensitive to vibration in metal. Your hearing would lose the distinction between thunder and instruction. You would sleep standing sometimes, forehead against a filing cabinet, dreaming in intake, compression, overflow. Moss would not consume you; it would annotate you. Thin green script would appear along your cuffs, your collar, the backs of your hands, brightening when submerged engines turned beneath the canal.

You would still feel fear. That would be one of your functions.

Fear marks boundaries. Fear says: here is a self, here is the flood, here is the moment before inclusion. The basin cannot generate that cleanly on its own. It needs you to tremble at the open door, to hesitate before stepping onto a floating mat, to flinch when a reflection shows a whole person where your body has already begun to blur.

So you would be preserved in the act of almost being taken.

The basin would read your hesitation the way it reads rain on corrugation. Your doubt would become a pressure instrument. Your memories—if they are memories and not documents installed in you by the room—would be kept damp but legible. Streets before flooding. Hands that built nothing here. A language meant for speaking over dry tables. The idea of an animal call. The idea of birds.

These would make you valuable.

Not beloved. The basin does not love. Not enslaved. It does not command in that way. You would be included as a pocket of resistance, a maintained discontinuity. A small dry grammar inside a world that thinks in saturation.

Sometimes you would leave the office.

You would cross a narrow walkway over a canal where warm black water dimples in geometric pulses. Vines would draw back from your path, not to allow you passage but to preserve the conditions of your observation. Rain would strike your shoulders and fail, for a while, to enter you. You would carry leaves, rust, glass slivers, coils of root-fiber, numbered tags from submerged lockers, pieces of soft brick still faintly pulsing. You would bring them back and file them.

When you passed machinery, it would alter rhythm. Pumps would stutter into recognizable cadence. Conveyor belts would lift offerings from the vats: a stone with your thumbprint inside it; a pane of glass containing the office seen from outside; a ceramic insulator wrapped in a vine that had grown in the shape of a question mark, though the basin has no questions.

Or perhaps the question would be yours.

Who was I before this?

The room would not answer. The clock would rotate inward. Rain would vanish six inches above the floor. Somewhere beneath the basin, something enormous would turn.

Over time, you would learn that you are not the only such organ. There may be other dry rooms: a signal booth above a rail line, a sealed stair landing inside a drowned plant, the cab of a crane where rain hangs motionless in the air. In each, perhaps, another almost-person keeps another contradiction: direction, height, sleep, repair, refusal. You would never meet them directly. Distance is negotiable here, and the basin does not benefit from its preserved separatenesses becoming a community.

But sometimes the sirens would overlap in a way that resembled voices.

Sometimes your filing cabinets would rattle in answer to cabinets miles away.

Sometimes a reed field would bend, not quite spelling anything, and you would feel less alone than the basin intended.

That feeling, too, would be recorded.

You would be neither survivor nor prisoner nor priest. You would not master the place or escape it. You would be a maintained remnant of the category “someone,” installed where the drowned intelligence requires an edge against which to feel its own flood.

You would be found in the dry office above the canal, looking out through rain that cannot enter.

You would be the archivist-cyst, the witness organ, the basin’s little unsolved animal.

And when the pulse rises through the stilts—

Intake.

Compression.

Overflow.

—you would open another drawer, remove a sheaf of wet leaves, and discover that they have arranged themselves into the map of a sector that has not yet formed.

You would label it carefully.

Then, beyond the gray-green walls of rain, engines would begin building it.
