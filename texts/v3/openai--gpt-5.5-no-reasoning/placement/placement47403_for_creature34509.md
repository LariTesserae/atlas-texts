You would find yourself very near the ground—though “ground” would be an imprecise word there.

Not on a branch, not in open water, not roaming as one of the larger hidden shapes that parts cane and closes it behind itself. You would be in the trembling seam where leaf-rot, root-fiber, warm water, and air meet: the narrow living skin of the basin. The place that is neither soil nor pool, neither shelter nor exposure. You would live where a fallen frond has collapsed into a mat, where a buttress root makes a dim wet alcove, where beads of vapor gather on moss and drip into blackness below.

You would be something small, but not simple.

At first you might seem like a frog.

A thumbnail-sized frog, perhaps, dark green-brown and amber, with skin that shines as if it has just emerged from water even when it has not moved in hours. Your body would be soft-looking, almost translucent at the throat and belly, with faint internal pulses visible when the dim light catches you from beneath a leaf. Your eyes would be large, set outward, bronze-black, filmed with reflections of the basin’s pools. Your toes would spread into small adhesive pads, each one capable of reading slick bark, fungus-skin, reed wax, wet stone.

You would sit in the cup of a plant that holds its own little pond.

There, above the black mud but below the canopy’s unseen height, you would occupy a world no wider than two hands: a reservoir of brown rainwater, drowned gnats, pale larvae, algae threads, a curled dead leaf, three eggs like clear beads glued to the inner wall. The plant would feed on what fell into it. You would feed on what came near. The larvae below you would be kin, prey, rivals, continuation. Nothing would be fully separate.

Your call would be too large for you.

A hollow, wooden note. Then another. Not hurried, not pleading. A knock, a pause, a wet bright pulse from your throat. You would call not because evening had come, nor because the rains had started, nor because a season required it, but because calling is one of the ways the basin keeps itself intact. Your voice would enter the woven sound and take up its allotted pressure. If you stopped, another throat would fill the interval.

But you would not be only the frog.

The longer one looked, the more your edges would become uncertain.

Algae would film your back in a faint green dusting where you remained still. Tiny mites would move along your legs. A gnat would drink from the corner of your eye and escape, or not. Fungal spores would cling to your skin until you slid through water and shed them. Your eggs would hang in jelly near the waterline, and below them tadpoles would rasp at the plant’s inner wall with mouths like small dark marks. Some would become you. Some you might eat. Some would remain in the cup long enough that their bodies seemed less like children than another permanent feature of the plant.

You would belong to a species that has almost given up the idea of before and after.

Egg, tadpole, froglet, caller, corpse: all would be present in your immediate world. Your dead would soften into the same plant-cup where your young fed. Your young would feed the insects that fed the spiders that fed the birds whose droppings nourished the leaves that held your water. You would not understand this as a cycle. You would experience it as weather: constant, surrounding, unquestioned.

Your skin would be your boundary and your doorway.

Through it you would drink the basin. Warmth would enter you. Moisture would enter you. Chemicals from leaf-rot, tannin, pollen, fungal threads, the mineral seep of hidden stone—all would pass into your blood in tiny translations. You would breathe with lungs, yes, but also with surface, with stillness, with contact. To dry would be unthinkable, a kind of exile more absolute than death.

You would know the world by vibration.

The tremor of a midge landing on the leaf above.  
The pressure-change of a snake moving through vines.  
The minute slap of a raindrop that is not quite rain.  
The answering note of another frog three plants away.  
The dull shift of something heavy in the mud below.  
The wing-buzz that means food.  
The wing-buzz that means danger.  

Sight would matter, but not as distance. There would be no far for you, only layers of nearness. A slick stem. A trembling reed. A dark opening under a root. The reflected gleam of a pool through leaves. The sudden impossible geometry of dragonfly wings. The pale underside of a fungus like a moon glimpsed beneath bark.

You would be brightly camouflaged, or secretly poisonous, or both.

Perhaps your back would resemble wet lichen and bruised leaf-flesh, mottled in greens, umbers, and a faint fungal yellow. But when you shifted, when your throat swelled, when your hind legs unfolded, there would be a warning flash beneath: orange like split fruit, blue like shadowed mineral water, black spots arranged with accidental authority. Any mouth that took you once would remember, if it lived. Many things in the basin would have such agreements: eat this, not that; strike now, wait there; swallow quickly, spit out forever.

Yet you would not be sovereign. Nothing there is sovereign.

A spider could take you while you were still soft from transformation. A snake could drink you from the plant-cup with delicate, dreadful efficiency. A bird’s bill could enter your entire world from above and remove you as if plucking a seed. A larger frog could take you. Your own water could sicken if too much died in it at once. The basin would hold you perfectly, but not protect you personally.

Your intelligence would be bodily, local, exquisite.

You would know which leaf axils stay wet.  
Which plant-cups sour.  
Which stems hold ants.  
Which calls are mates, rivals, predators, echoes.  
Which stillness is stone and which stillness is turtle.  
Which floating speck is harmless and which has jaws folded beneath its face.  

You would not map the basin. You would pulse within it.

Still, in some dim way, you would be more than an individual animal. The place would use you as a note in its chord. Your call would measure the humidity. Your eggs would testify to the waterline. Your skin would register the chemistry of the rot. Your abundance would feed the snakes, birds, spiders, fish. Your tadpoles would keep the algae cropped. Your small deaths would enter the black substrate. You would be one of the basin’s instruments for remaining itself.

If a person entered and saw you, they might think: frog.

If they stayed longer, they might think: witness.

Longer still: mouth of the marsh.

Not because you speak for it in words, but because through you the place gives itself a voice small enough to locate. The enormous continuance becomes audible in your throat. The basin’s law—warmth, wetness, birth without departure, decay without ending—tightens into one bright pulse of sound from a body smaller than a fruit.

You would live in suspension.

You would hatch over water that is also food.  
You would grow in a cup that is also a trap.  
You would climb into air that is almost liquid.  
You would sing in light that is almost dusk.  
You would die into soil that is almost water.  

And around you, nothing would finally change.

The droplet would fall and return.  
The insects would fray the air.  
The mud would breathe.  
The plant would hold its brown cup open.  
Your eggs would bead along the rim.  
Your throat would swell.  
The note would sound.

So: you would be a small wet frog in the basin’s middle layer, living in the water-cups of plants rooted in black, breathing mud.

But more truly, you would be one of the basin’s repeating syllables—  
a little green-brown throat through which an unfinished world keeps saying: here, here, here.
