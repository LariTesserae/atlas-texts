I would be found where the city has not yet decided whether a passage is a wound or a room.

Not in the bright Glass Wards, though I would know them by reflection: amber panels clouded with grit, the little crunch underfoot, the way apprentices hold their breath when lifting a pane still warm from its annealing bed. Not in the Salt Quarter either, though salt would mark me. I would pass there at dawn as a pale stiffness on railings, as a taste at the back of the tongue, as a line of white around a discarded glove. I would know the Anchor District intimately—the language of strain, the household gauges, the deliberate lean of walls—but I would not belong to its families, not exactly.

I think I would live lower than official habitation and higher than the red places no one names: in the service margins behind warm walls, in the doubled spaces made when a bridge is rebuilt around an older bridge, in the under-arcades that are cleared when rain begins. I would be associated with the city’s almost-abandoned parts: not ruins, because nothing here has had time to become picturesque; not dwellings, because no census clerk would approve the address. Paused districts. Softly ticking rooms. Staircases still sharp at the edge. Painted numbers legible on doors no one opens.

I would be one of the people, but not entirely easy to introduce.

A keeper of unadopted thresholds, perhaps. A reader of rooms after people have left them. A maintenance worker whose guild is older than its present name and poorer than its instruments imply. Someone called when a district is not yet condemned but no longer trusted, when gauges disagree, when pipe-mice have abandoned one wall and nested thickly in another, when gray film appears under a table in a dry building, when a sealed cistern does not smell sweet but does not smell like water either.

My trade would be listening for continuation.

I would carry a kit too small to impress an engineer and too specific to be a scavenger’s: heat chalk in three grades; a strip of thin hammered metal for reading drafts under doors; wax tablets hardened with mineral dust; cord darkened from years of being tied across suspect cracks; a palm-sized wall gauge whose needle has lost its paint; a ceramic cup with a repaired lip; two blue seals; a folded green notice I hope never to use. At my belt would hang a little set of dull chimes, not for music but for testing resonance in empty rooms. If one note returned too cleanly, I would leave.

My clothes would be the color of things that do not want attention: ash-gray, old black, pale at the shoulders where dust has polished the fabric. The knees and elbows would be patched with ceramic scales, not because I work near sparks but because I am often crawling through the city’s second thoughts. My boots would have glass cuts across the soles. My hands would be marked by burns received not from fire but from forgetting that a surface can seem innocent after dusk.

People would know me in the way they know a warning tile: not warmly, not coldly, but with a small adjustment of behavior. Children would follow me until their parents called them back. Cistern clerks would resent my questions and answer them anyway. Engine crews would make jokes about my habit of speaking to walls. Culture keepers might tolerate me better than most, because they too understand that a thing may be alive only in certain definitions and still require care.

I would have been born in a scaffold neighborhood attached to a building that is now finally complete, though by the time completion came there would be no one left there who remembered its bare frame except three old people and me. My childhood would smell of wet ceramic, rope fiber, hot dust, and broth stretched with mineral-thick water. I would have played the ordinary games: condensation races, false alarm rhymes, daring my palm toward walls already tested by braver children. I would have once chalked a fracture mark on a sleeping neighbor’s door and been made to sit through the entire inspection I had caused.

The thing that made me what I am would have happened after rain.

Not the rain itself—the city knows rain, even when it comes like judgment—but the days after, when hidden water continued moving.

Our district would have been one of those middling places, neither famous nor poor enough to attract attention: workshops below, sleeping cells above, a pressure stair, two cistern mouths, one bath chamber, one shrine alcove containing a bent bolt from the first foundation. After a hard rain, the children collected salt petals from shaded walls. I would have gone farther than allowed, into a half-uncoupled row of rooms whose lower supports had been relieved the season before.

The rooms would not have looked dangerous. That is always the trouble.

The doors still fit. The floor was level enough. The air was warm but not warning-warm. I remember the quiet because it was too smooth. Not silent like a pump room before disaster; not dead; merely waiting without the small noises inhabited rooms make. No cloth shift, no bowl scrape, no pipe-mouse feet.

Then a drop fell upward.

That is how I would remember it, though later I would understand some version of surface tension, heat, pressure, an exhalation from a seam below. But as a child I would see moisture gather beneath a table, fatten, tremble, and lift toward the underside of the tabletop as if the wood—or what passed for wood there, compressed fiber and resin—had become sky.

I put my hand under it.

The drop touched my palm and left a circle that did not burn. It was cold. Not cool: cold in a city where cold is more suspicious than heat. Behind the wall something scraped once, carefully.

I ran. I was not brave. I ran so hard I fell against a ladder and split my chin. The adults came because of the blood, then stayed because of the room. By evening the row was sealed. By next week the maps showed a blue mark and a note about unauthorized moisture migration. By next year the district had a new route around that block, and the old rooms were part of the paused city.

But for a long time afterward I dreamed of the drop rising to meet my hand.

So I learned to read the places that do not announce themselves.

Not the grand engines. Not the proud towers. The almost-places: the back of a stairwell where salt blooms on one side only; the unused lift shaft whose cables sing during ground reports; the chamber that accumulates warmth after all neighboring rooms have cooled; the sealed shop where pipe-mice have carried insulation into the corners and arranged it in rings. I learned from gauge-readers, from pressure-masons, from a culture keeper with opaline stains at the cuffs, from an old woman who had survived a south tension release and could tell by kneeling whether a floor had lied in the night.

My official title might be something dry: Interstitial Surveyor, Third Register. Or Ward Continuity Listener. Or Clerk of Suspended Structures. The city would have a name for me that fits on a permit.

But among workers I might be called a pause-walker.

I would go into districts after evacuation, before abandonment becomes legal fact. I would mark what may be recovered, what must be sealed, what should be watched, what is to be left alone even if valuable machinery remains inside. I would decide, sometimes, whether dinner tables are carried out or allowed to stay where they are, plates and cups slowly furred with mineral bloom. I would know that every such decision is partly technical and partly mercy.

I would not think of myself as mystical. No one practical in that city can afford imprecision. I would distrust big words. Spirit. Curse. Hunger. Blessing. The ground responds; that is enough.

And yet I would keep habits that look like superstition.

Before entering a paused building, I would place my bare fingertips briefly against the threshold, not to ask permission, I would say, but to take a reading. If I found an old household cup intact, I would turn it upright. If I sealed a room where someone had slept for years, I would leave a thimble of clean water in the driest corner, logged under “humidity test vessel” if anyone asked. I would never step over a gauge fallen face-down without righting it. I would speak aloud the painted door number before crossing inside, because names and numbers help a place remain itself a little longer.

I would live alone, though not isolated. In that city almost no one truly lives alone; systems pass through everyone. My cell would be built into the side of a disused survey mast above a narrow ravine, accessible by a stair that contracts badly in damp weather. One wall would be too warm for sleeping against and marked accordingly. Another would sweat in the hour before dawn. My bed would be slung slightly away from both. I would keep my water in three small vessels, each from a different office, because old fear makes me compare tastes. On a shelf: a cloudy bead containing a pinch of my mother’s ash; a pressure token from my father’s engine-house; a glass shard from the first room I ever helped seal; a white porcelain cup repaired with dark mineral resin, cleaner than anything else I own.

Pipe-mice would use my wall.

I would pretend not to encourage them. There would be a legal opening in the conduit, no larger than two fingers, and beside it a shallow ceramic lid that sometimes contains condensation. The mice would come at night: blind, warm, furtive, making minute decisions in the insulation. When they vanish for more than a day, I do not sleep deeply. When they nest, I trust the wall enough to turn my back on it.

Once, perhaps, I would have loved someone from the Anchor District: a cable mathematician, a person who could look at a span and see tomorrow’s strain. We would have spoken in jokes about tolerances. We would have shared water without ceremony until it became ceremony. But my work would have made me too attentive to endings, and theirs too loyal to designed endurance. They would say, not cruelly, that I am always listening for departure. I would say, not fairly, that they mistake bracing for faith.

We might still meet in bathhouses, older, scarred differently, sitting beneath reclaimed steam while gauges tick on the walls. We would exchange news of ridges, not feelings. That would be our kindness.

I would be present for alarms, but I would not be heroic in the usual way. My courage would be procedural. I would be the one who remembers which low arcade drinks first, who knows the alternate stair through a glass-cutter’s abandoned storeroom, who can identify by smell whether a sweet cistern has turned or merely received runoff from a lacquered mask shop. During a ground report, my hand would go first to the nearest child’s shoulder, then to the rail, then to my own gauge.

If the plateau gave three short reports, I would move upslope.

No one sensible argues with three.

But the place I return to, again and again, would be an abandoned structure on the edge of the old pressure ward. It would stand at a slight angle above a fissure that opened after the plans were drawn. The lower rooms would be inaccessible behind a swell of cooling stone. The upper rooms would remain almost domestic: doorframes ticking, hooks on walls, a line where a shelf once was, window openings facing raw red-black cliffs and vapor drifting from below.

Officially it would be Monitored Empty Structure 17-P.

Unofficially, people would call it the Listening House.

Because if you stand in its central stair after sunset, when the lamps drop to maintenance amber and the heat wells pulse under their lids, you can hear a sound that does not match the wind, pipe, mouse, engine, or settling stone. It is slow. It arranges itself just beyond counting. Some say the old walls are transmitting a lower engine. Some say a culture bloom took in the ceramic dampers and learned the rhythm of the district. Some say the fissure below opens into a chamber where brine falls continuously in the dark.

I would not say what I believe.

I would maintain the witness plates. I would replace the cord across the unsafe landing. I would scrape a little gray film from the underside of the third stair and send it to the culture chambers under false routine codes. I would leave no offerings there, except once a year, when no one sees, a sealed thimble of clean water set in the angle where the cold drop touched my childhood palm.

Who or what would I be?

A human being, mostly.

A municipal function grown around a private fear.

A small organ of the city’s caution.

One of the people who enter the unfinished spaces and come back with marks, notices, rumors, and revised maps. One who knows that abandonment is not absence, that ground can answer without being alive, that a building may continue becoming after its inhabitants leave.

I would be lean, quiet, heat-wise, dry-humored, difficult to surprise except by tenderness. My palms would read warmth better than faces. My dreams would contain rooms still deciding whether to hold.

And if you asked where to find me, someone would point not to a home but to a condition:

Follow the ridge down past the old condenser ribs. Cross the narrow bridge only if its morning tile is white. Turn where the wall gauge is cracked but still moving. Do not take the stair that smells of sweet water. When the machinery becomes almost silent, stop and listen. If you hear little chimes answered from inside an empty building, wait at the threshold.

I will come out eventually.

I will have dust on my shoulders, a blue seal between my teeth, and one hand held slightly away from my body as though it has just touched something either cold or alive.
