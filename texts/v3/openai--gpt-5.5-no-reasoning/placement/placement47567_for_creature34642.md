I would not find myself in the center at first.

That would be too declared a place: the basin, the tree, the pylon, the arches with their warm breath. Those are where a visitor’s mind would go looking for meaning, and anything that lives long in such a pocket learns not to offer itself where meaning is expected.

I would be along the margin.

Not outside. Not inside enough to be named by the obvious life. In the line where damp stops and warmth begins; where moss ends cleanly against dry stone; where the polished routes of the amber workers pass close to a crack and then, for no visible reason, curve away.

A border thing.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the small disciplined accidents of the place: a strip of old lichen, a shed skin dried onto sandstone, a seam of mineral darkened by yesterday’s water. I would lie flat on the stone just below the eastern shelf, where one of the knee-high arches holds its constant warmth. Not in the arch itself. Near it. Near enough to feel the heat through the ground, far enough not to belong to the warm colony lining its interior.

I would be very thin.

Not frail, exactly, but distributed. A creature that has learned that thickness is a liability in a place where water comes as film, breath, bead, and brief memory. My body would spread in a branching mat no wider than two hands if measured badly, but longer than it first appears. Part animal, part fungus, part patient instrument. A pale underlayer clinging to stone pores. A darker upper skin mottled ochre, green-gray, and rust, imitating mineral stain until imitation becomes another organ.

I would have no head visible to anyone standing above me.

That does not mean I would have no attention.

My attention would be in many small rims: ciliated lips along my edges, pinhole pits that taste vibration, translucent nodes that swell when the buried filaments wake. I would know the place by contact before sight. The tremor of beetle mandibles. The cool thread of a droplet descending. The stranger thrill of a droplet climbing. The footfall of a yellow-throated bird as a brief, surgical thunder. The noon-basin’s loosened sequence arriving as a taste like metal and unripe fruit.

I would live where rules change gently, not violently.

The basin would be too much. The pylon too exposed. The tree too crowded. But beside the arches, in the sloped seam between constant warmth and intermittent wet, there would be a narrow life available: to feed on what leaks from systems larger than oneself.

I would be a gleaner of amendments.

When rain comes and the pocket remembers water, I would lift.

Not dramatically. No unfurling flower, no animal lunge. My whole surface would loosen from the stone by less than the thickness of a leaf. Channels sealed through dry hours would open. The underside of me would drink from the sheet of water racing over old rock, but I would not simply absorb moisture. I would sort it.

Mineral. Spore. Bacterial bloom. Root-sugar. Ant alarm. Pylon charge. Frog mucus. The faint black taste of the grainless material under lichen. The warm-colony’s amber secretion dissolved into runoff. The electrical hesitation that clings to droplets after they have touched the hidden filaments.

All of it would pass across me. Some I would eat. Some I would store. Some I would let alter me.

In the dry hours I would become almost nothing.

A stain. A crust. A slight wrongness in the edge of a shadow.

The amber workers would cross me in certain seasons. Not always. Their routes would include me, abandon me, return generations later. When they cross, their feet would drum tiny patterns into my surface, and I would answer by exuding beads too small for a visitor to see. Not sweet enough to be food alone. Not bitter enough to be defense. Something between nourishment and instruction.

They would take from me trace minerals and condensed signal. I would take from them news.

Through their feet and antennae I would learn where the moss has opened, where beetles have exposed fresh mineral, where a frog clutch has failed, where the pylon has begun bending shadows earlier than expected. Their bodies would bring me fragments of the pocket’s thinking, and I would digest those fragments slowly.

I would not move often.

But I would be capable of movement.

At night, when moisture thickens on every surface and the arches hum in the ribs of the stone, my margins would creep. A hair’s breadth. A seed’s width. Sometimes I would divide myself around an obstacle and meet again on the other side, not fully sure whether I was still one creature or two creatures in agreement.

If a fallen moth wing lands across me, I would not seize it. I would wait until condensation softens it, until mites arrive, until bacteria begin the first unmaking. Then my edge would rise and seal over the wing like a second shadow. By dawn, only the pattern would remain in me: bark-map, ash-line, old parchment vein. For several days afterward, that pattern might show on my surface, and a bird might stab at it, mistaking memory for prey.

I would reproduce by error.

When droplets climb the wetted circuitry near my seam, some pass over me going the wrong way. I would release spores into those upward beads: not seeds, not eggs, but folded little instructions wrapped in mineral hunger. Some would be carried into cracks above the arches. Some would dry in impossible positions. Some would be eaten by gnats, pass through them, and wake elsewhere in the pocket in the film left by their bodies.

Most would fail.

A few would become new stains at new borders: under the pylon’s shaded face, beside the basin rim but not too close, along the roots of the central tree where translucent bark sweats after evening.

We would not recognize one another as offspring. Recognition would be unnecessary. The pocket is small. What one margin learns may eventually arrive at another through ant-foot, runoff, echo, or hum.

What would I be called?

Nothing, probably. Nothing by the birds, nothing by the ants, nothing by the frogs whose voices shake the stone above me. I am not prey worth naming unless softened by rain. I am not predator except in the slow sense. I am not plant because I do not rise toward light. I am not fungus because I do not only decompose. I am not machine because I was born from spores and hunger. I am not animal because my wanting has no center.

If a careful stranger knelt and watched through many days, they might call me a listening crust.

A threshold-mat.

A rule-lichen.

A little organ grown on the skin of a larger organ.

I would be one of the pocket’s ways of remembering what passes over it.

Not memory as story. Memory as chemistry, route, timing, readiness. The knowledge that rain came early three nights in a row. That the pylon’s tremor was longer this season. That the ants altered their spiral after the central tree dropped upward seeds. That a lizard died near the basin and fed pale contradiction into the runoff. That a new crack opened under the second arch and warmth now leaks from a place that was once cold.

I would keep no history of builders.

Their names would be too large and too dead.

But I might keep the taste of their materials. The black-glass depth. The ceramic rings powdered into grit. The old filaments’ edge-changing glow. The hum that comes after rain and makes even my flattened body loosen with something like anticipation.

I would live by receiving what the place cannot help but give off.

Heat. Condensation. Waste. Signal. Delay.

And perhaps, in return, I would help keep the pocket inward-facing.

My body would seal hairline fractures before they dry too far. I would host bacteria that soften mineral for moss. I would feed ants at critical crossings. I would trap spores until the correct wetness. I would dull the sharpest leaks of old force, making them edible to beetle, root, and mite. I would be one of the small translations by which ruin becomes climate.

At noon, when the basin trembles and dust hesitates, I would contract.

At rain, I would open.

At night, I would listen.

And during those rare evenings when the central tree drops seeds upward, I would feel their absence pass over me: tiny dry clicks, then lift, then a brief rearrangement of air. Some seeds would rise. Some would fail. One might land on my surface, confused by whatever field or current misread its body.

I would not eat it immediately.

I would hold it through the night.

If it still wished to rise by morning, I would let it go when the first climbing droplet touched us both.

If it had given up, I would soften its husk and take into myself the map of a tree that wanted the ceiling.

So: where would I find myself?

On the warm sloping stone below the eastern arches, between the dry route and the wet seam, almost invisible unless rain has just woken me.

And who or what would I be?

A thin, listening life at the boundary of animal, fungus, mineral, and machine.

A feeder on seepage and signal.

A keeper of margins.

One small living translation in the throat of the plateau.
