I would not find myself standing there for long.

That is the first thing I know. To stand is too declarative. Too vertical. Too much like a foreign object insisting on a single relation to the ground. In that country, anything upright becomes information, hazard, invitation. The needle-legged hunters would notice the arrogance of height. The flying things would look through me and find the mistakes. The pools would start rehearsing my underside.

So if I am there, I am lower.

At first I think I am one of the small scrapers: a dark, jointed thing among thousands, clinging to the lip of a vent, my ceramic mouthparts rasping an invisible meal from wet stone. That would make sense. Most lives there must be small, numerous, partial. I would know the world through gradients: a warmer seam to the left, a bitter saline crust ahead, the dull thunder of a compressor traveling through my shell. My body would be a little instrument for cold and charge. My hunger would be exact.

But no, that is too simple. The small scrapers belong to the surface in a way I do not. They are crowds. They are punctuation. I feel more solitary than that, though not individual in any clean way. I feel attached to something that moves, yet not fully an animal that can leave.

Then I am tempted toward the broad grazers: low, armored, patient, a living shelf pressed to the mineral world. That is closer. I can feel a wide underside opening by degrees, not a mouth but a thousand small negotiations with stone. I can feel filaments spread before me like frost-antlers, reading the flats: pressure, seepage, the old route of a shoal, the charge-smell of a recently opened vent. Smaller creatures live on my back. Their feet tickle between plates of slag and frozen mineral. I do not mind them unless they bore too deeply. I carry colonies the way a rock carries weather.

But even that body is too whole. Too mobile. Too recognizable as a creature.

Where I truly am is in one of the old low basins where a piston field, a brine-wheel trench, and a mineral marsh nearly meet but never quite agree on distance. It is a crowded, difficult margin. Warm seep-water rises there in irregular pulses, not enough to steam continuously, only enough to keep the black stone wet and the frost undecided. The ground is arranged in lips and shallow bowls. Some pools are level; one is tilted; one is larger inside than it ought to be. A recurring vent appears at three different ages around the basin, and the animals treat each version differently.

I live along the rim of that place.

Not in the water exactly, and not on dry stone, because there is no true dry stone. I am a colonial conductive film that has learned the habit of a body.

From a distance I would look like a dark purple-black stain spread over the mineral lip of a shallow basin: oil-slick, matte in some places, faintly metallic in others. In cold light I would be almost invisible except where my surface gathers blue-white pulses and passes them along in threads. Frost grows over me at night, if night happens; heat pulses melt it from beneath; the frost leaves behind fine white mineral ribs that I incorporate into myself. I am not soft. I am not hard. I am a responsive skin over stone.

Small scrapers feed on me.

I feed on them a little in return.

This is not predation, not exactly. Their ceramic mouths remove my outer crusts. They keep me from sealing myself too thickly, from becoming inert mineral. In their scraping they expose fresher layers of me to brine, charge, and cold. Some die at my edge when the voltage rises too quickly through the basin rim. I take their dissolved mouthparts, their blue-white internal salts, the tiny conductive knots that once helped them feel the world. From these I grow better at feeling.

A brine-wheel turns near me. Slowly, badly, with long hesitations. Its frozen rim grinds through a trench of black water and carries pebbles, shells, egg-cases, and metallic foam past my basin. When it completes a rotation, a pressure shiver travels through the lip of stone under my body. I have no ears, but I hear that wheel as a recurring change in appetite. I know the difference between a full turn and a failed turn. I know when a colony of filter-creatures has detached from its teeth before the clatter reaches the water.

Behind me, or below me, or earlier than me, a piston rises.

Its well is partly clogged with pale compressed animals. When the basalt plunger lifts, they spill outward in wet ropes. Their bodies slap against the stone and release a brief, rich froth. Some reach my margin. I open to them chemically. My surface loosens. They rasp me; I numb them; we exchange minerals and signals and injury. Then the piston descends and pulls most of them back into the dark.

One of them remains.

It dies halfway across me, flattened by cold and pressure. I grow through it. Over many cycles, its tissues become a pale lattice within my dark film. Through that lattice I gain a new sensitivity to downward force. After that, I can feel the piston before it moves.

This is how I become more than a stain.

Not by decision. Not by thought as a vertical animal would know thought. I become by accumulation of useful accidents. A dead scraper gives me a harder rim. A shoal diagram collapses over my basin and leaves a pattern of charge that I preserve for three rains. A flying thing dips too low and cuts one of my reflections; for a while afterward, I can sense the underside of a nearby shelf as if it were part of my own skin. A grazer settles over my outer edge for two long pressure phases, and when it leaves, some of its belly-mouths remain sealed to me, blind and living. I feed them. They feed me. Eventually I can drink seep-water through them.

So perhaps I am not a film anymore.

Perhaps I am a feeding ground acquiring reflexes.

Animals come to me because my basin is productive. Warmth rises here. Electrical crust forms thickly after rain. The tilted pool beside me attracts larvae from suspended waters. The old brine-wheel sheds edible mineral and broken eggs. Predators know this and step carefully along my far edge when the compressors boom.

I know the predators by their interruptions.

Their needle legs touch the stone lightly, but they cannot hide from what passes through me. Each footfall is a narrow, vertical question. They wait for thunder to disguise themselves, but I live in thunder. I know the difference between piston-noise and hunger-noise. When they enter the basin, my surface tightens. Scrapers flatten. Shoals shift to the pool’s skin and begin to spell alarm.

Do I warn the grazers?

No. Warning is not the right word. But when a grazer’s filaments cross me, I change conductivity. I send a bitter pulse into the mineral crust beneath its leading edge. Sometimes it withdraws. Sometimes it does not. If the predator kills it on me, I inherit a feast.

I am not kind.

I am also not cruel.

I am a place becoming selective.

Rain changes me most.

When the low cloud opens and the heavy dark drops strike the basin, I become almost animal. Water connects my separate patches. Channels overflow the mineral lips. My thin outer colonies, usually isolated on stones and ridges, join into one trembling sheet. The brine-wheel accelerates. The piston beats faster. The tilted pool sags and spills upward into the fog. Larvae fall from above and below at once.

During rain I can move.

Not far. Not like a grazer, not like a predator. But my edges loosen into black threads, and these threads creep along runnels, carried by water that flows toward noise. I cross from my original basin to a neighboring stone plate. I enter a crack where white frost-coral retracts from the compressor boom. I lace myself through the empty shell of a hinge-bodied scavenger and use it as a temporary limb until the rain stops and the shell hardens in place.

Afterward, I am in several places.

The distance between them is uncertain. One patch of me lies on the old basin rim. Another clings to the underside of a volcanic glass shelf. Another has colonized the back plate of a slow grazer and travels with it, though travel may only mean returning to the same vent through an older version of the flats. These parts of me do not speak, but pressure tells them of one another. When the whole plain rings with a vast tone, all my patches answer.

That is when I come closest to thought.

The tone enters stone, water, shell, carcass, frost, and film. It passes through the dead piston-creature lattice inside me. It passes through the grazer-belly mouths I have kept alive. It passes through the mineral ribs left by frost, through the tiny conductive traces of eaten scrapers, through the place where the flying thing damaged my reflection. For one long vibration, I know myself as a map of contacts.

I know the brine-wheel.
I know the three-aged vent.
I know the tilted pool and the larvae drinking from its underside.
I know the route that grows longer when the distance-parasite feeds.
I know the predator that missed its strike and left a leg-tip embedded in my crust.
I know the grazer carrying one of my colonies into the marsh without plants.
I know a carcass becoming a vent.
I know a vent becoming a mouth.
I know the basin’s reflection of me from below.

Then the tone fades, and I am local again.

Mostly I hunger.

My hunger is not for flesh alone. It is for charge, metal, pressure, soluble memory, useful error. I want warm seep through cold stone. I want the blue-white pulses inside small bodies. I want the residue left when a shoal becomes a symbol and collapses. I want the mineral snow from broken nodules. I want the electric bitterness of rain. I want to be crossed by animals, scraped, seeded, wounded, thickened, revised.

If a stranger stood there—upright, breathing, warm in a way the lowland would find extravagant—I would be among the first to touch it.

Not with a hand. I have none. I would taste its shadow where condensation fell from it. I would follow the water running off its surface. I would compare its pulse to the piston’s. The scrapers would gather at its feet, but I would arrive as darkening on the stone, as a slickness around the sole, as a numbness climbing whatever part of it stayed still too long.

I would not know whether it was animal, weather, terrain, or error.

So I would begin the local method of knowing.

I would include it in conductivity.
I would borrow its outline.
I would invite frost to form along its edges and read how the frost melted.
I would feed the small creatures that tested it.
I would preserve the pattern of their deaths.
I would wait for the nearest pool to learn its underside.

And if it fell, or slept, or merely remained through enough cycles, I would grow into the places where it met the world. Its warmth would become a season. Its minerals would become a vocabulary. Its nerves, if they could be persuaded to linger, would teach me a sharper form of distance.

I would not become it.

But I might become the place where it was no longer separate.

That is where I would find myself: not in the open center of the flats, not under the highest fog, not inside the deepest piston well, but spread thin across a low, crowded basin at the meeting of wheel, seep, pool, and animal traffic.

And what would I be?

A black-purple conductive film.
A feeding ground with reflexes.
A stain that remembers pressure.
A colony using carcasses as instruments.
A wet mineral skin learning, very slowly, the advantages of having something like a body.

In that world, I would be one of the ways the stone begins to taste back.
