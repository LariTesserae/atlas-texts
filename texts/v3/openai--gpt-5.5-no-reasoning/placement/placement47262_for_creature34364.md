I would be found where the city’s upper and lower halves argue with one another: not quite in the street, not quite in the drains, but along the iron walkways and pump-stairs that stitch the second story to the wet world below.

I think I would live near a pump house.

Not inside its main hall at first—the hot tiled nave with the flywheels turning behind fogged glass, the brass gauge faces sweating, the workers moving slowly in rubber aprons—but near it, in the narrow service rooms and exterior landings around its sides. A place with ladders going down and ladders going up. A place where every sound is information: the softened thud of the pistons, the suck and gulp in the culverts, the irregular ticking of cooling iron, rats shifting behind brick before the tide has announced itself.

At first I might seem like one of the human inhabitants: a maintenance hand, perhaps, one of the people whose job is mostly noticing. Not an engineer of grand machines, not someone who designs the gates or owns the barges, but someone who keeps the city’s small failures from becoming large ones.

I would be a watcher of screens.

The intake cages gather everything the city loses: feathers, rags, fish heads, bones, wrappers, drowned insects, splintered crate wood, rat bodies, once in a while a shoe. Someone has to haul the hooked pole through the hot black water and drag the mass clear before the pump begins to strain. Someone has to know by the pitch of the engine that the screen is choking, by the smell that the canal has reversed, by the sudden congregation of gulls that something edible has lodged where it should not.

I would have hands permanently marked by rope tar, rust, and disinfectant. My sleeves would stay rolled. My boots would have been patched twice at the ankle. I would carry a short iron pry bar, a coil of wire, a knife kept oiled in a tin, and a rag for wiping the glass of gauges or my own face. My lunch would be wrapped high in my satchel, sealed against damp and theft. I would close doors automatically behind me, even if only crossing a threshold for a moment.

But that answer feels too simple. Too human, or only human.

Because in that city, no one is only what they are. Everything takes on the habits of the scavenger, the flood-runner, the creature that rises before the water does.

So perhaps I would be one of the pump-house people, but with the instincts of the animals around me sharpened into profession. The city would have made me into a kind of threshold creature.

I would know the rats by their absence before I knew them by sight. I would trust them in the way one trusts a bad-tempered instrument. If the wall behind the tool hooks began to whisper dryly at the wrong hour, I would stop what I was doing and look at the canal gauge. If rats climbed the downspout in daylight, I would not curse first; I would listen. I would learn which colonies lived under the fish hall, which in the brickwork by the old basin, which in the warm wall behind the bakery furnace. I would hate them in the pantry and respect them in the flood.

Birds would be my weather map. Gulls screaming over the south basin would mean offal or stunned fish. Egrets gathering near a ditch would mean warm outflow. Crows waiting on the slaughterhouse roof would mean the carts were late. Pigeons bursting from a warehouse before the door opened would mean a cat, a child, or a shift in air pressure inside.

The dogs would know me but not belong to me. One or two would follow me on wet mornings because I sometimes threw them what I pulled from the grates that was fit for a dog. They would walk behind me along the crown of the road, paws placed delicately away from oily water. At sirens they would go still, ears half-lifted, offended by the necessity of moving upward again.

I would belong to the maintenance ecology more than to any family name: pump hands, sluice keepers, dredge crews, night watchmen, market washers, bridge tenders, rat catchers, boiler sweepers. The people who do not produce the city’s wealth so much as prevent its digestion from reversing and swallowing everyone.

My home would be on an upper floor in a narrow brick block raised twice above its original street. The front door below would open onto three useless steps and a bricked arch sweating beneath the current pavement. I would enter by a side stair of iron, slick even in dry weather. Inside, the lower room would be tiled and nearly empty: hooks overhead, buckets, a hand pump, a bench, traps stacked like folded arguments. The true living room would be above, with screened shutters, a bed high on blocks, tins hanging from beams, papers in a metal box.

There would be no plants. I would not miss them in any uncomplicated way. If someone brought a cutting from inland, some pale green thing in a pot, I might distrust it. It would look indecently soft. Too thirsty, too passive, too willing to rot. I might keep it anyway for a week near the window, watching the leaves yellow under the vapor and coal film, then feel relief when it failed.

What I would love instead would be signs of function.

A clear gutter after rain. A pump note steady as breathing. A grille lifted and cleaned before the insects thickened. A door that fit its sill. The metallic clack of a latch closing properly. The exact moment, after a surge, when the water stopped rising against a stair mark and began to fall. The white body of an egret in a black ditch, not because it was beautiful in a gentle way, but because it proved the outflow was alive with something.

I would be quiet, probably. Not shy, exactly—quiet in the local manner, because speech is effort in heat and because listening matters. I would answer in short phrases across water. I would knock on pipes rather than shout down ladders. I would sleep lightly, trained by engines. If the pump stopped in the night, my body would wake before my mind did.

And yet I do not think I would be grim. The city is too full of absurdity for that. Gulls bathing like aristocrats in a filthy square. A crab discovered in a boot. A dog solemnly refusing to cross a perfectly safe plank because it remembers one that shifted three years ago. Children naming flood marks as if they were ancestors. A rat sitting dry and offended on a valve wheel during a rise, looking for all the world like a foreman.

There would be humor in endurance, but not romance. I would not say the city was beautiful unless speaking to someone who already understood what kind of beauty was meant: not blossom, not vista, not clean light. Beauty as adaptation. Beauty as a system continuing. Beauty as a worker crossing an empty yard in warm drizzle, lunch tin held high, while birds lift all at once behind her like torn paper.

If I were not human, then I think I would be something that lives between the pump house and the animals.

Not a rat. Rats are too numerous, too certain, too much the city’s hidden bloodstream. Not a gull; gulls are too brazen, too free of shame. Not a dog, though I admire their practical loyalties. Not an eel, though the culverts have their own dark intelligence.

I might be a black kite.

One of the circling birds above the rendering sheds and canal basins, living from heat columns, refuse, timing, and height. A creature of the upper dirty air, watching the city’s leakages become meals. I would know the pump sirens because rats move when they sound. I would know the market bells because fish waste follows. I would know which roofs burn the feet, which chimneys lift, which settling pond rims collect the weak and stunned things. My world would be made of rising vapor, tin-colored sky, red signal lamps, pale mud, and the quick arithmetic of descent.

But even that is too cleanly separate. A kite can leave. This city feels like it makes leaving beside the point.

So I return to the pump house.

I would be a lowland maintenance worker, a keeper of grates and thresholds, one of those who stand at the hinge of water, machinery, and animal appetite. Someone made patient by damp, suspicious by necessity, and attentive almost to the point of superstition. I would live in the second city above the floodline and work daily at the edge of the first, where the drains breathe and the cages fill with feathers.

If someone from elsewhere asked me what I was, I might say, “Pump crew,” or “sluice maintenance,” or simply give the name of the district.

But the truer answer would be:

I am one of the city’s closing hands.

I close the doors before dark.  
I clear the mouths before they choke.  
I lift what must not drown.  
I watch the rats climb and believe them.  
I listen when the engine changes its mind.  
I live above the waterline, but only just.
