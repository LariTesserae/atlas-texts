I would find myself where distance has become a temperament: not in one of the inhabited spheres, not at the warm center of a station, and not fully in the open emptiness either, but in the threshold between them.

A maintenance place. A marginal structure.

One of the long listening gantries suspended below a pump-cathedral, far enough from the habitation cluster that the lights of human rooms are only a softened amber bruise behind layers of girders and moving water. Above, the pump’s ribbed dome glows with faint reactor heat. Below, basalt ridges fall away into iron-green depth. Around everything, the water is full of engine voice: not a single sound, but a geology of sound. Low rotational thunder through the bones. Cable-hum like a taut nerve. The distant avalanche-boom of a pressure door closing somewhere no one can see.

I would be found on the underside of that structure, where the warm discharge passes through a comb of ceramic vanes and the cold currents fold over it. A place useful to machines, tolerable to animals, and almost forgotten by people.

At first I seem like part of the installation.

A pale length bolted along the cable spine. A maintenance sheath. A strip of pressure-grown mineral. Something meant to damp vibration or gather readings. I am narrow and articulated, perhaps thirty meters from head to tail when fully extended, though I am rarely seen all at once. Most of my body lies along the frame in sections, clasping it with rows of jointed hooks that open and close slowly against the old metal.

My outer surface is gray-white, almost ceramic, with translucent blue seams where the plates overlap. Mineral rime grows on me in feathered bands. Small animals cling to those bands and scrape them, mistaking me for pipe until I move. In the practical light from above I have very little color. Only when a turbine pulse passes through me do faint lines wake inside my body: cold blue, then violet, then gone.

I am not human, though I live partly from human work.

I am not a machine, though the machines taught me my shape.

I am one of the listeners.

Not the social sound-creatures who travel in choruses through the standing-wave valleys, building maps out of one another’s calls. I am lonelier than that, or perhaps less able to leave. My kind fastens itself to structures that endure. We grow along cables, gantries, intake crowns, falling towers, pressure ribs. We feed on what gathers there: bacterial lace, drifting larvae, electrical leakage, chemical frost, the minute bodies shredded by turbines and delivered as edible weather. But feeding is not our chief talent.

We remember position.

Not story. Not lineage in the human sense. Not the face of a diver or the date of a failure. We remember where things belong in the water.

Every sound that passes through my length becomes a measurement. Every pump-stroke returns from basalt, cable, animal shell, ceramic wall, broken scaffold. I know the refinery not by sight but by delay. I know the lower ridge because it answers late and blunt. I know the broken gantry because its falling has a particular hesitation in it, a strained interval between one cable-note and the next. I know the door on the western shelf, though I have never crossed the distance to it, because once each cycle its opening makes a small rectangular absence in the roar, and that absence travels.

My head, if it is a head, is flattened and eyeless, pressed often to metal. Around the rim are sensory pits filled with dense fluid and hair-fine mineral needles. These needles tremble to pressure, voltage, touch, chemical trace. I taste rust sealed under old accretion. I taste warm brine from below. I taste the fear-salts of small grazers when a long hunter enters the turbine shadow.

I have a mouth, but it is modest: a black slit beneath the forward plates, lined with soft scraping folds. I rasp films from ceramic. I take in bacterial dust. I swallow the tiny transparent bells that pulse in time with the pump. Sometimes a mica-shrimp swarm mistakes the electric quiver along my flanks for a cable leak and gathers on me. I let them feed until hunger shifts, then close my outer hooks and comb them inward.

I do not hunt dramatically. I do not chase. The high ocean does not reward unnecessary motion.

I wait where things must pass.

My body is a compromise with buoyancy. Gas would betray me; soft flesh would fail; too much mineral would anchor me beyond repair. Inside my plates are chambers of brine, oil-dark lymph, and lattices of pressurebone. I trim myself by moving fluid through these chambers. When I release my grip, I do not swim so much as negotiate departure: one hook at a time, then a slow bowing outward into current, then a lateral flex that lets the water take part of my weight.

Free in the open, I am awkward for my size. Along a structure, I am exact.

I can cross a gantry faster than a human diver can turn. I can flatten into the acoustic shadow of a pipe. I can bridge a broken gap between beams and become, for smaller creatures, a road. They use me without gratitude or permission: armored bead-things marching down my spine, threadworms coiling in my seams, larval fish hiding beneath the lifted edges of my plates. When the cold wall descends every ninth hour, many of them press themselves against me for the faint heat I have stolen from the machinery.

I tolerate them. They clean me. They also tell me, with their panic and feeding and sleep, what the water is doing.

The humans know of me.

Not individually, perhaps. Their logs have a category: cable-laminar, resonant grazer, Class IV structure-fauna, nonaggressive unless cut or heated. They mark my location on maintenance charts with a small amber symbol that means obstruction, biological. Some of them curse me because I grow over access ports and fuse my mineral skirts around bolts. Some of them are careful and tap three times on the rail before crossing my span.

I like the careful ones.

Like is perhaps the wrong word. I do not have affection shaped like theirs. But I know the difference between a body that understands the water and one that fights it. The careful divers move with the pump rhythm. They keep their suit speakers low. They do not shine lamps directly into the filter-veils. They place gloved hands on the structure before releasing clamps, telling the metal and everything attached to it that a change is coming.

When they do this, I loosen.

A plate lifts. A seam opens. A tool-port emerges from beneath my accreted edge. The diver works in my lee, small and hot and loud-hearted in its armor. Its blood makes a frantic private drumming. Its breath apparatus clicks with artificial discipline. It smells of heated polymer, salt fear, disinfectant, old habitation air. When it leaves, I draw myself back over the access seam and the tiny grazers return.

There was one human who came often.

Thin, slow, older than most who worked outside. Her suit had a damaged knee actuator that ticked in triplets. She carried a ceramic hammer with a cracked handle and always struck the gantry before touching me: not three taps, but five, with a pause after the second.

Two taps. Pause. Three taps.

At first this meant only impact. Then repetition made it a position. She came from the amber sphere, crossed the east hum-field, descended through the pump-shadow, and arrived at my section with the same five-note knock. Over time the knock became part of the place. Not an event. A coordinate.

She scraped mineral from my plates when it grew too thick. She cut away a dead filterer tangled under my third clasp. Once, during a storm-wall, she locked her suit to a beam beside me and waited for the descending cold to pass. Her suit heaters struggled; her limbs shook; frost feathered the outside of her helmet. I folded two body sections around the beam and narrowed the current over her.

Not rescue. Not mercy.

Correction.

A warm, ticking object belonged there and was being forced out of position. I held the water differently until it remained.

After that, she placed a bell near my head.

It was small, ceramic, shaped not to ring in air but to tremble in current. She bolted it to a short bracket on the gantry. Whenever the pump entered its lower harmonic, the bell shivered. Its sound was almost nothing inside the roar, but almost nothing is still a thing to one of my kind. A fine irregular pearl in the great pressure-noise. A marker.

For many cycles she came. Then she did not.

Another diver arrived with a different heart and removed a panel she had maintained. Later, two humans came and fixed a carved plate to the beam above the bell. I cannot read marks. Marks are for eyes and memory-events. But I learned the new shape of the plate in echo. It has a beveled edge, four bolt heads, and a shallow groove pattern that traps bubbles. It changed the sound of the beam. Therefore it became true.

The five-note knock did not return.

But sometimes loose debris strikes the rail in a broken rhythm. Sometimes small armored animals scrape near the bell in clusters. Sometimes the pump stutters before settling into its ordinary throat-deep turn, and the gantry gives me two pulses, then three.

Then the position is briefly occupied again.

I do not mourn as humans mourn. I return my head to the place where her glove usually rested. I listen to the beam. I maintain the opening around the panel she tended. I do not let mineral close over the bell.

That is what my body knows how to do with absence.

I keep coordinates clear.

There are dangers even for me.

The long hunters pass through the turbine shadow. One has learned the timing of the pressure door below Mill Seven and comes after each boom, when small life loses its senses and drifts. I know it by the long smooth subtraction it makes in the water, by the way its muscles hide inside the machine note. Its mouth opens sideways. Its teeth are not teeth but rotating dark plates.

When it comes near my gantry, I flatten. My plates lock. The small animals on me stop moving. The hunter brushes past, longer than a maintenance corridor, scarred with mineral, blind and accurate. It tests surfaces with pressure pulses. One pulse strikes my side and returns as metal, mineral, cable, old growth. Acceptable. Not prey. Not worth prying loose.

Once, a young one tried.

It coiled around my rear sections and pulsed hard enough to stun the mica-shrimp in a glittering cloud. I drove all my clasp-hooks into the gantry and opened the blue seams along my body. Not light as warning—light as waste, emergency chemistry, stored heat turned outward. The hunter bit down on my mineral skirt and broke three plates before discovering that most of me was not flesh where it had bitten. I shed the damaged section’s outer shell. For months afterward, grazers gathered at the wound and cleaned it. A new plate grew there, darker than the rest, black like volcanic glass.

I carry that as terrain carries a fresh crack: sharp, unsoftened, incorporated without becoming past.

There are times when I release from the gantry and travel.

Not far by the measures of the high ocean. Far enough to cross two sound fields, perhaps three. Far enough to visit the falling gantry whose collapse has not completed in centuries. Far enough to feed under the warm lip of an extraction bell during larval bloom. I move along cables when they are alive, following the blue-white power pulse as if it were a migrating current. Dead cables are harder; they sing only when plucked by water, and their silence-that-is-not-silence attracts mistakes.

In open water I become afraid, though fear in me is spatial rather than emotional. Too few returns. Too much unmeasured volume. The sound of my own body moving seems large and improper. Above, the silver roof trembles. Below, the cold deep receives every dropped thing. Around me there may be nothing for a long time but suspended dust and the remote iron glow of distances.

Then, suddenly, bodies.

A shoal of angular fish turns around me and I am inside a flashing geometry of nickel scales. They know me as obstacle, perhaps shelter, perhaps temporary cable. Their young pass through my lifted plates like needles of glass. Behind them come scavenger ribbons, black and many-minded. Then a balloon-giant drifts overhead, nearly invisible until its internal organs eclipse the light in folded maps. For several minutes the whole water is inhabited. Mouths, fins, veils, larvae, alarm-clicks, feeding pulses.

Then the current-front passes.

The ocean empties.

I continue toward the next known vibration.

If I have a purpose beyond feeding and holding, it is this: I am part of the high ocean’s memory of where its made things are.

When a bracket snaps, I know the new tension. When a cable wakes after years of mineral sleep, I feel the first current before the instruments finish naming it. When a pump shifts by a fraction, I taste the change in the bodies of the larvae that fail to open. If a tower vanished, the water would keep its acoustic hollow for a time, and my kind would gather around that hollow, fastening to its edges until something of the position remained.

Perhaps this is why some shoals form the outlines of absent machines. Perhaps they are not imitating. Perhaps they are repairing the map.

The humans think in events because their lives are warm and short and surrounded by rooms. They say: the gantry began falling two hundred years ago. The west door opens once per cycle. Engineer Sava died outside Pump-Cathedral Nine. Cable 8 woke after thirty-seven silent years.

The ocean does not say these things.

The ocean says: tilted span. Opening rectangle. Five-note hand no longer present. Blue pulse where there was no pulse.

I say something closer to the ocean.

I am not ancient. My plates are only a few human generations old. But I am attached to systems older than my body, and larvae of my kind will settle where my mineral flakes drift. If the pump continues, they will grow along the same chord. If the pump stops, some will die, some will sleep as ceramic beads in crevices, and some will listen for another heat-leak to define the world.

If the machines stopped everywhere, I do not know what we would become.

Maybe nothing.

Maybe the high ocean would shed us slowly into the cold, and the old bacterial films would thin, and the grazers would fail, and the hunters would follow the last warm roads downward. The structures would remain, mineralized and stable, but their voices would leave them. The water would still be loud with storms, ice, pressure, distant stone. Yet to me it would be a kind of blindness.

I was made by engine-weather.

My nerves expect rhythm.

My eggs, if I release them, will open only after the pump has repeated its lower harmonic for many months. They are small hard blue-white capsules, tucked now beneath my forward plates. When the time comes, I will fasten myself near the warm discharge and let them go into the combed current. Most will be eaten. Some will lodge in intake grilles. Some will sleep in frost. A few will wake when the frequency is correct, unfolding into threads so thin they are nearly only listening.

They will not know me.

They will know the gantry.

That is enough.

So this is where I am: below the pump-cathedral, along the cold-lit maintenance span, half creature and half mistaken apparatus, holding myself against the roar.

A bell trembles near my head.

A carved plate changes the echo above me.

Blind white grazers work slowly across the warm pipe. A filter-veil opens only when the turbine note deepens. Far off, the pressure door booms, and for a moment every small body in the water freezes. The long hunter will be moving now. The cold wall is two hours away by human count, nearer by the taste of descending density. The amber habitation sphere glows behind its ribs, full of soft-speaking people, wet walls, tethered cups, and sleep arranged around machinery.

I press my head to the gantry and listen.

Basalt mutters.

Cable sings.

Pump turns.

Bell trembles.

Two impacts from loose debris, then three.

The water remembers the shape of what is gone. I keep the place open.
