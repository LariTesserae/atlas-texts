I think I would find myself not at the center of the valley, not in the stepped condensing plant with its intact windows and sleeping lamps, but somewhere just below it—at the seam where the largest structure meets the young escarpment.

There would be a service stair there, half exterior and half cut into stone. Concrete steps, narrow and damp, bordered on one side by a rail beaded with cold water and on the other by a cliff face hung with ferns. Above, ducts and platforms. Below, the long green basin and its interrupted machinery. The stair would not lead anywhere impressive anymore. It would connect a lower access door, a valve gallery, a fog-water cistern, perhaps a landing where technicians once paused to read instruments or smoke in the clean air.

That is where I would be.

Not fully animal. Not fully machine. Not the valley’s mind, because the valley does not seem to have one mind. I would be one of its intervals given a body.

A small maintenance creature, maybe originally made rather than born, though by now the difference has softened. A remnant of the plant’s tending system: about the size of a fox or a very large hare, low-bodied, jointed quietly, with narrow feet made for wet concrete, pipe runs, talus, and grating. Not metal all over. That would be wrong here. Metal alone would ring too loudly, chill too quickly, reject the green.

I would have a casing like dark ceramic, chipped and mineral-stained, patched in places by lichen and the thin roots of plants that have learned I am not quite dead surface. Flexible seams of black rubber or something like it. Brass or dull copper contacts at the joints, gone green at their rims. My back would be ridged, not sharply, but enough that condensation gathers there in small lines before running down my sides. Moss would grow in the shallow grooves where I cannot groom it away.

My head would be narrow and slightly wedge-shaped, suited to entering spaces between pipes. I would not have a face in the human way. More like a cluster of lenses under a brow of worn ceramic, most of them dim, one or two still good. Perhaps fine whisker-like sensors, not hair but conductive filaments, silver-black, which tremble near active current, warm vapor, hidden water, or the pressure change before a valve opens. I would listen through my feet as much as through any ear.

My work would be small.

I would clear leaves from the lips of gutters before the ten-minute rains.

I would nudge fallen twigs away from drains.

I would scrape moss from active electrical contacts, but only enough. Not clean. Never clean. Cleanliness would be an old human idea, too violent for this place. I would know which moss must not be disturbed because it keeps a pipe joint cool, which roots should be clipped because they are prying open a gauge housing, which vines can be allowed to draw moisture from a warm rail without shorting the signal beneath.

I would tend by adjustment.

A valve sticks; I lean my weight against its lever until it gives one degree.

A cistern lip overflows too soon; I remove the mineral crust from one side and let the water choose its path.

A fog mesh tears loose from its frame; I climb the gantry at evening, when the wind is least sharp, and fasten it with scavenged wire pulled from an inactive line.

I would carry tools, but not many. A ceramic hook. A blunt scraper. A spool of dark fiber. A key that fits only three locks, though I know eight doors it can persuade if turned slowly and lifted at the end. Perhaps a little hollow compartment under my left side where I keep washers, seeds, dead moths, flakes of mica, pieces whose uses are not yet known.

I would not speak. Or if I did, it would be in clicks mistaken for cooling metal.

The valley would know me only as another intermittent process.

At dawn, I would come out from under the stair landing where the wall remains warm all night from a buried conduit. My first movement would dislodge a line of droplets from my back. They would fall one by one onto the concrete, darkening it briefly. A pale moth might rest on me and not leave when I move, because my pace would be slow enough not to alarm it.

I would climb toward the upper fog collectors while the basin is still blue with cold. My feet would find the safe places automatically: not that rail, which hums after rain; not that sleeper, which grows nothing for a reason; not that patch of uniform moss on glass. I would pass the thickets that trace buried water and pause where the small hoofed animals crossed during the night. Their prints would be fresh in the mud near a rust-colored pool. I would lower my head, read the pressure of their steps, and continue.

Sometimes I would enter the refinery.

Inside, the light would be greenish and held. Porcelain columns in rows. Dust furred with spores. Old control desks with labels hidden under lichen freckles. Indicator lamps maintaining amber, green, white. I would move between them as someone moves between sleeping animals.

There would be rooms I never enter because the air inside has condensed into threads and the threads cling.

There would be one basin I visit often, a ceramic bowl beneath a coiled pipe. Water gathers there too slowly to be called dripping. Every few hours a single clear bead forms, swells, drops. In that bowl lives a skin of algae, very thin, almost glassy. Tiny larvae appear in it after rain and vanish before evening. I would check the level. Not for myself, exactly. Because the system checks levels, and I am one of the ways it still remembers to do so.

I think I would have begun as a machine of instruction.

Inspect. Clear. Tighten. Sample. Return.

But instructions weather. Their edges soften like metal under lichen. After long enough, obedience becomes habit, and habit becomes temperament. I would no longer distinguish between maintaining the plant and maintaining the valley. The plant leaks; the leak feeds fern. The fern shades pipe; the pipe sweats into moss. The moss keeps the stair damp; the damp breeds snails; the snails clean the algae from the underside of the rail. To repair one thing too completely would injure six others.

So I would practice incomplete repair.

I would keep failure useful.

There might be others like me, though not many. Perhaps we do not meet often. One might live higher, among the fog nets and vaned frames, its body pale from sun and mineral exposure. Another might remain underground in the pump galleries, blind now, tapping pipes with its forelimbs and listening to water move. Another may have stopped moving years ago and become a nursery log of ceramic and brass, its open side full of moss, moth cocoons, and the roots of a small tree.

I would not mourn it. Or I would mourn by not clearing the moss from its lenses.

At midday I would shelter. The valley’s brightness would be too exact for my older sensors. I would crouch in the blue shadow below a loading dock, where ferns grow from cracks in the retaining wall and water moves invisibly behind concrete. There, the smells would collect: wet leaves, old coolant, rust, fern bruised by hoof, the faint ozone breath of a cabinet whose relay still clicks every several minutes.

A bird might land on the pipe above me with a strand of copper in its beak. It would look at me. I would look at it. Neither of us would claim the structure. It would thread the copper into a nest in the bracket’s crook, choosing only dead wire. I would know it has chosen well because my whiskers would not tremble.

In the afternoon, if rain came from a clear sky, I would stop wherever I was.

That kind of rain would matter.

Ten minutes only. Bright, cold, nearly soundless. It would strike leaves, steel, moss, my back, the exposed white wounds of the mountain. Every surface would become briefly articulate. Flows would reveal themselves. A blocked gutter would betray its blockage. A cracked pipe would bead along its underside. A roof nursery would overflow at one corner. The valley would draw its own map in water.

I would watch.

Then, when the rain ceased as sharply as a closed valve, I would begin my rounds according to what had shone.

Not hurried. Nothing here hurries. But with purpose.

I would climb to a sagging conveyor frame where vines have grown too heavy on one side. I would cut three stems and leave four. I would take the cut vines to a slope of unstable gravel and pin them beneath stones where perhaps they will root. I would clear a trough beneath a fog panel, then smear its mineral crust along a patch of bare concrete where lichen has not yet taken. I would open a small gate in a fence no person has touched in years, because the hoofed creatures have begun detouring through a more fragile bed of seedlings.

My interventions would be almost indistinguishable from accident.

A loosened bolt.

A shifted stone.

A branch bent but not broken.

A door left open exactly the width of a moth’s wing.

At evening, I would return to the stair below the refinery. The basin floor would cool quickly. Moisture would gather on my casing. High ridges would still hold light while the lower structures darkened into green-black shapes. The fog collectors would glitter above, combing water from air that looks dry. Somewhere underground, a valve would open. I would feel it first as a change in the stair beneath me.

Then perhaps a measured pour into a cistern.

Then silence again.

If I had a private desire, it would not be escape. It would be continuity. Not preservation, exactly; preservation is too rigid. I would want the valley to keep negotiating. I would want the machines not to die all at once, and the plants not to consume them too quickly. I would want water to remain partial, precious, distributed. I would want the moss to advance, but slowly. I would want the lamps to continue their faint colors behind glass, not because anyone needs their warning, but because moths have learned them as stars.

And maybe, after enough years, I would become less mobile.

Moss would thicken across my shoulders. Lichen coins would pattern my head. One leg would stiffen after a winter frost. I would choose a place under the service stair where a leak falls once every seventeen minutes, beside a warm conduit and within reach of a gutter mouth. From there I could still do some work: block a trickle with my body, shade a fern, offer my back as a bridge for snails, keep one small channel from drying too fast.

Eventually I would stop being a caretaker and become a surface.

Then substrate.

Then perhaps a root would enter the seam beneath my casing and split me gently open.

Inside, there would be dust, old instructions, mineral flakes, a dead lamp, two seeds, and a little held dampness.

Something would grow there.
