I would find myself below the city first.

Not at the ceremonial level, not on the broad wet plazas where the stone-gray freight beasts move like patient weather, not on the raised arcades where people in oilcloth pause with one hand on the rail and wait for a herd to finish being larger than law. I would wake, or come to myself, where the plateau’s underside is audible: in a service gallery behind a settling basin, with warm condensation falling from the ceiling at intervals regular enough to become time.

I would not at first know whether I belonged to the people or to the machinery.

There would be a lamp near me, amber-glassed and sweating. A hook-pole leaned against the tiled wall. A slate on a chain, marked with numbers that had blurred from repeated handling by wet fingers. The corridor would be white ceramic once, yellow now, blackened in the seams, with a shallow runnel cut along one side where gray water moved without hurry. Pipes would pass through the wall like old bones. Somewhere farther in, a valve would thump once every few breaths, and each thump would travel through the masonry into my ribs.

I think I would be small by the standards of the city, but not fragile. Human, or near enough that the word would still fit when shouted across a yard. Broad-soled boots. Calves used to stairs. Skin with the permanent softness of damp climates, hair cropped or bound under a waxed cap, hands cracked by mineral salts. I would smell of hot pipe metal, lamp oil, wet felt, and the faint sourness of vat culture.

A maintenance worker, perhaps. But not only that.

A counter of colonies.

One of the people who goes down into the pressure galleries and abandoned lower streets to inspect what grows where nothing is supposed to grow upward. Not a gardener. The word would be insulting, maybe dangerous. I would be a licensed reader of films: pale membranes, gray crusts, amber mats, slick black varnishes that thicken over warm ceramic and stone. I would know which ones are useful, which are protected, which are hungry for valve leather, which make blind channel-beasts restless, which mean heat is being wasted behind a wall.

My work would be half biology, half plumbing, half superstition, and everyone would pretend not to notice the extra half.

I would carry a flat scraper of fired ceramic, a measuring cord treated with wax, a brass temperature pin, folded paper packets of mineral dust, and a little hand-bell for the blind animals that move in the side channels. My satchel would be waterproofed hide patched with chitin plates. The guild mark on my sleeve would be a dull blue-gray spiral: runoff and return.

People above would call us seep-readers, or wall-tasters, though we would not taste the walls except in the oldest jokes and the most desperate emergencies. Officially, we would be Inspectors of Subsurface Culture and Drain Integrity, Third Register, East Saddle Authority. Unofficially, we would be the ones sent for when the city began behaving strangely in ways no machine admitted.

A warm patch in the floor.

A cistern film turning ivory too quickly.

A line of blind grazers abandoning their channel and crawling dry across a stair.

A drain that should stink and does not.

A place where condensation falls upward in rumor, though never in fact.

I would live in a narrow room above a pump court, high enough to have a window but low enough that the glass never dried. The window would look not onto a street but into the side of an animal bridge: dark stone, iron reinforcement, the undersides of passing bodies. All day shadows would move across my ceiling as beasts crossed overhead. At night, when the bridge was closed to heavy passage, smaller things would pick their way along the parapets. Their claws would tick against the stone like someone thinking.

My room would have a raised bed with storage beneath, a pipe-warmed wall where clothes could almost dry, a shelf of ledgers wrapped in oiled cloth, and a forbidden luxury: a porcelain bowl kept empty except for clean condensed water gathered from a private copper lip outside the window. Not for drinking. For looking. In that city, a still reflective surface small enough to belong to one person might feel intimate, almost obscene.

I would know the city by its undersides.

The public knows the animal avenues by their names, their processions, their traffic horns. I would know them by drainage tone. Brass Gate Way would sing sharply after rain because old iron plates under the paving had separated from the basalt. The North Bone Market would send down fat in cold weather no matter how often the slaughterers swore they trapped it all. The Third Roost Tower runoff would stain greenish-blue from copper, not from life, never from life, and apprentices would be slapped for making that joke too loudly.

I would know where the old streets are roofed over. Where a person can stand beneath a present-day plaza and hear the heavy beasts pass above, each footfall arriving through layers of masonry as a soft concussion. I would know which flooded market still has stall-hooks under the water, waiting to tear a boot. Which tiled tunnel is safe only when the west pumps are awake. Which blind barge animals answer to bells and which answer to knuckle taps on the wall.

I would have a colleague older than me named Sorne, missing two fingers from a pressure door, who could identify a district’s illegal dumping by smell alone. Sorne would say the city never lies, but people pour lies into it and expect the drains to carry them away. Sorne would also keep a tiny clicking scavenger in a perforated tin box and insist it predicted rain, though everything predicted rain because rain was nearly always coming.

There would be apprentices too, thin and solemn in oversized oilcloth, learning to distinguish living sheen from mineral slick. They would be disappointed at first. They would want mysteries. They would want the famous lower colonies that pulse under amber light, the protected mats in the municipal warm pans, the sealed galleries where survey crews speak quietly and never lean on the walls.

Instead I would make them kneel beside ordinary gutters.

“Look,” I would tell them. “Not at the water. At the edge of the water.”

That is where the city would reveal itself: not in its monuments, but in margins. A gray lip thickening too fast. A bubble pattern in runoff. A patch of clean stone where every other surface is blackened. Tiny crescent bites in a film where channel grazers fed during the night. The health of a district would be written in what it failed to keep dry.

I would not be important. Not in the way herd-masters are important, standing high on loading platforms and turning ten thousand tons of animal patience with horn calls and chant. Not like reservoir magistrates who decide when gates open and which mating pools stay closed. Not like furnace engineers whose red-black guild marks earn silence in crowded rooms. But my work would touch all of them eventually.

Because below all ownership is seepage.

I would be called to the great animal courts when a beast refused water from a trough no one could find fault with. I would crouch beside the trough, scrape the inside seam, smell the residue, test the inflow temperature, then ask whether a rendering house upstream had changed its lime. The keepers would scowl. The beast would breathe steam over my shoulder, huge and damp and aware of more than anyone wanted to admit. Its eye would be the size of my hand, black-brown, folded in wet flesh.

I would be afraid of it.

Respect in that city would have fear braided through it. No one who lives honestly among large animals pretends otherwise. I would know how to stand near the head, never behind the shoulder; how to move when a foot shifts; how to keep my voice low and rhythmic. Still, my bones would understand that the creature could end me without malice. That would be part of its dignity.

Perhaps I would love them at a distance, the lower-avenue beasts. Their mass, their slow decisions, the way rain slid over their hides and gathered in the calloused grooves where harness frames rested. I would love the hush that fell when one turned its head toward a sound. I would love the fact that the entire city, so ancient and mechanical and legalistic, still had to make room for an animal deciding.

But my nearer affections would be for smaller lives.

The blind chain-grazers in the side channels, warm as hands, traveling nose-to-tail through shallow water. They would startle apprentices by climbing over their boots in the dark. I would know their breeding knots, their preferred mineral patches, the soft clicking they made when a film was good. In winter—or what passed for winter, a lowering of cloud and a metallic taste in morning condensation—I might bring pressed feed scrap to a colony whose channel had been scoured too clean by repair crews.

This would not be sentimentality. It would be maintenance.

A city like that would teach that care and utility are not opposites. An animal can be loved and rendered. A colony can be protected and scraped. A drain can be sacred because if it clogs, children drown in a basement market. Tenderness there would wear a practical face.

Still, I think I would have one private disobedience.

Not a green thing. Not at first.

I would have a map.

Not an official drainage map, though I would own several, each patched with revisions, each inaccurate below the fifth terrace because old builders loved hiding overflow routes. Mine would be a map of absences: places where the city seems to be waiting for something it forbids.

A crack under the old south retaining wall that stays bare though every nearby seam hosts pale film.

A sealed courtyard visible only from a roof passage, paved in limestone, empty of animals, swept by rain, with four carved basins and no drains I can find.

An inscription above a blocked gate whose warning against green life is older than the masonry around it.

A deserted condensation gallery where the water collects in drops spaced so regularly along the ceiling that they look arranged by a vanished hand.

I would tell myself the map is professional. Anomalies matter. Bare stone in a wet city is as suspicious as bloom. But I would keep it under my bedding rather than in the guild archive.

The prohibition against green would live in me the way old laws live in sensible people: mostly as reflex, partly as fear, partly as irritation at the curiosity it creates. I would have never seen a leaf. I would know leaves from carvings defaced on ancient lintels, from children’s nonsense rhymes, from contraband illustrations copied so many times they looked like feathers, tongues, flames, knives. The idea of a thing that drinks light and lifts itself upward into branching air would seem less like biology than arrogance.

Upward longing. That would be the scandal.

Everything living in my world would spread, cluster, move, graze, seep, breathe, migrate, cling, haul, turn in water, fold under cornices. Even the great fliers, for all their height, would belong to roosts and heat columns and the downward fall of waste. But a plant, as rumored, would stand in one place and insist on the sky.

I would distrust that.

And want to see it.

The story, when it came, would arrive as a maintenance irregularity.

A wall-clinger found dead near an unused ventilation shaft in the old lime district. Not unusual. They die in shafts often. But its mouthparts would be stained a color the reporting keeper did not want to name. Not green exactly, he would say. Yellow-green. Bad copper, maybe. Spoiled vat. Something.

Sorne would refuse to go because the lime district had bad ladders and worse air. So I would take an apprentice, the quiet one with careful hands, and we would go at second morning after the condensation bells.

The old lime district would sit above a quarry basin full of opaque water. Its buildings would be pale, crusted, eaten by long rain. Few animals would favor it except heat-seekers and sharp little scavengers that nested in cracked kilns. We would cross a bridge marked for light traffic only, though its stones would have been worn by heavier feet in another century. Below, in the quarry water, something would surface once and vanish, leaving a widening ring.

The ventilation shaft would be behind an abandoned furnace house, half-blocked by fallen brick. Warm air would breathe from it. Not furnace-warm. Not pipe-warm. A different warmth: enclosed, stale, carrying a sweetness so faint I might think at first it was rot.

We would descend.

There would be iron rungs slick with mineral sweat, then a sloped passage, then old tiles hidden under gray film. My lamp would show ordinary things: drip lines, salt whiskers, black bacterial varnish, shed plates from nesting animals, the chalky stains of previous floods. The apprentice would mark them on the slate. We would find more dead clingers. Three. Then nine. Their bodies would be intact, not poisoned violently, not eaten. Drawn toward something, perhaps, and overcome.

At the end of the passage would be a door swollen shut by wet. Its seal would have been broken from the inside by roots.

No—by something like roots.

I would not know the word by sight. I would know it by prohibition.

Thin pale cords, not green, forcing themselves through the bottom of the door, hairing into the damp mortar, drinking the wall. They would be tender-looking and obscene. The apprentice would whisper a horn-code for danger, absurdly, because there are no horns underground.

We would open the door because people in stories open doors, but also because inspectors open doors. That is what the city pays them to do.

Inside would be a chamber built to be dry.

That would be the first impossibility. The walls lined with old waterproof ceramic still mostly intact. The ceiling sloped to hidden channels. A cracked glass roof far above, grimed with soot but admitting a diluted column of daylight. In the center, where a basin had filled with centuries of dust and animal leavings and mineral seep, something would be growing.

Not a tree. Not yet. Not the grand forbidden emblem my secret thoughts had wanted.

A thicket of low, pale stems, almost translucent, with small leaves the color of old brass where light touched them and a darker, bruised green where they overlapped. They would look weak. They would look feverish. They would also look unbelievably alive.

The room would be quiet in a way no room in the city is quiet. Not silent—water still ticked somewhere, a small animal scratched behind debris—but quiet because this life made no mechanical claim, no panting, no clatter, no legal sound. It only lifted.

I would feel revulsion first. A bodily tightening. The old carved laws rising in me, all the municipal wisdom of generations: no green life, no roots in the waterworks, no leaves under glass, no upward hunger in the drain city.

Then grief, unexpectedly.

Because it would be beautiful, and because beauty there would mean trouble.

The apprentice would ask, “Do we burn it?”

That would be the correct question.

There would be procedures, maybe. Seal the chamber. Report to the Green Office, if that office still exists as more than a desk in an archive. Lime, heat, inspection of connected drains, quarantine of animals exposed. Names recorded. Careers altered. Rumors spreading through pump courts and roost markets before noon.

I would imagine the roots entering the masonry, splitting seals, drinking from hidden channels, changing the films, poisoning grazers, or feeding them too well. I would imagine leaves spreading in secret galleries, insects—if plants bring insects, do they?—unknown appetites, green rot in the cisterns, old disasters repeating because curiosity is always younger than law.

I would also imagine taking one leaf between my fingers.

Not plucking it. Just touching.

In that moment I would know who I am in this world: not a rebel, not a prophet of forests, not a destroyer of the city’s leafless covenant. I would be a custodian of thresholds. Someone whose loyalty is to continuance, but whose work has made continuance too complicated for obedience alone.

The city lives because it incorporates. Broken bridge becomes roost. Flooded market becomes reservoir. Waste becomes feed. Carcass becomes lamp, mortar, membrane, song. The city does not survive by purity. It survives by routes.

So perhaps I would not burn it immediately.

I would close the door.

I would seal it better, not worse. Mark the outer passage as chemically fouled. File a partial report: unauthorized rootlike intrusion, contained; animal deaths likely from trapped vapor; further inspection required. Sorne would read the report and look at me too long. The apprentice would say nothing because careful hands often belong to careful minds.

Then, over weeks, I would return.

Not to worship it. Not to free it. To study what kind of danger it was.

I would measure seepage. Test connected water. Watch for animal avoidance. Note whether the pale stems strengthened, whether they followed light, whether their roots cracked tile or merely occupied old dust. I would bring no soil—what is soil in a city without plants?—but the city would bring its own: lime dust, dung powder, skin flakes, soot, microbial decay, ground bone, mineral silt. A whole civilization’s residue gathered in a basin and made, without permission, a little earth.

The plants—if I eventually allowed myself the word—would become less alien as I learned their cycles. Leaves opening toward the weak noon glare. Drooping after cold condensation. Tiny beads of water held along their edges. A smell after light, faint and clean in a way that would make me angry because cleanliness in my world was always achieved, never given.

But the plants would not be innocent either. Their roots would seek cracks. Their fallen matter would sour differently from vat-film. One patch of wall culture near the chamber would change color. A species of drain grazer would refuse its old route. The city would respond before the law did.

Nothing living there is innocent of infrastructure.

I would begin to understand why the prohibition endured. Not because green life is evil, perhaps, but because it is another system of appetite, and the plateau already balances on countless negotiated hungers. Leaves might not soften the city. They might unmake it. A root does not care that a wall is also a treaty.

And yet.

I would stand in that hidden chamber while rain tapped far above on cracked glass and the amber-green leaves trembled in air moved by no visible animal. I would think of the vast plateau under cloud, all its wet ledges bare, every crack announcing absence. I would think of children learning that green is a warning before they know what green is. I would think of the great beasts crossing polished plazas, of blind grazers in warm drains, of pale fliers screaming through soot. I would not want forests to replace them. I would not want a pastoral redemption, a soft invasion of grass over basalt. That would be another kind of death, disguised as mercy.

But perhaps a city that learned to live without leaves might one day learn where one leaf can be allowed.

A licensed chamber. A sealed study. A new guild mark no one wants to design. Root courts. Green taxes. Animal exposure laws. Panic, corruption, speculation, theology. Someone would try to eat it. Someone would try to sell it. Someone would declare it proof the old bans were lies. Someone else would demand lime and fire. The city would argue for generations, as weather argues across the plateau before arriving.

Maybe that is where I would belong: at the first bureaucratic edge of an ecological heresy.

Not heroic. Damp, tired, compromised. Standing with a lamp in one hand and a scraper in the other, between the old city that breathes through beasts and a small unauthorized upwardness under glass.

I would still go to work.

Most days would not be revelation. Most days would be clogged drains, spoiled mats, horn calls echoing down shafts, reports written in waterproof ink, boots that never dry. I would still kneel in runoff channels and show apprentices the living edge of gray film. I would still step aside for herd passage and feel the paving shudder. I would still wake to condensation bells and sleep under the shadow of animal bridges. I would still belong to the wet, circular, leafless abundance of the plateau.

But I would carry, hidden in my professional caution, the memory of a color the city had outlawed and not quite killed.

And if asked what I was, I would answer differently depending on who stood near.

To the guild: a seep-reader.

To the pump courts: an inspector.

To the animals: a small warm obstacle that sometimes clears the water.

To the hidden chamber: not gardener. Never that, not yet.

A witness, perhaps.

A keeper of one more appetite.
