I would be found low in the city, not in the central basin itself, but near the quarter where all water must be persuaded to behave.

Not at first glance in any remarkable place. A side-run, a service lane, one of those passages too necessary to be beautiful and too constantly used to be forgotten. It would run between the back walls of a cookhouse, a hoof-washing shed, and a store of broken tile. The paving would be uneven but known, every slab named by the feet that trust it. Under the slabs, water would move with a hidden steady speech. Above them, ropes would sag from wall to wall, hung with hides, patched aprons, lengths of gut-cord, children’s clay animals left to harden and then abandoned when their legs cracked.

I think I would belong to the drains.

Not one of the high coordinators at first, not a water-keeper of the central court with shaved forearms and measured authority. I would be one of the people who knows what the city sounds like from underneath.

A drain-listener, perhaps. A gutter-clearer grown into something more precise.

My station would be at the meeting of several small flows, where a household runnel, a slaughter-room sluice, and the overflow from a turn-beast yard all pass through a set of stone screens before joining the stepped channels of the drainage quarter. It would be a foul place by outsider standards, though not by mine. It would smell of wet lime, old blood, warm clay, dung-water, sour mash, and the black iron odor of soaked stone. The air would be close enough to chew. Flies would trouble the slaughter side; pale lizards would wait for them on the warm wall. Drain crabs would click in the gaps below the grates, offended by every hook and pole.

There would be a roof over part of it, patched with bitumen and hide and fired lattice, low enough that a tall uplander would strike his head twice before learning. I would not strike mine. I would move bent without thinking, shoulder first through the narrow places, one hand always touching wall, post, rope, pipe, screen, the city telling itself through my palm.

I would be compact, thick through the forearms, with one hip a little higher from years of bracing against current and lever. My calves would be bound in dark strips, always wet below the knee. My feet would know the difference between safe slime and treacherous clean tile. I would have scars in the soft places: shin scars from hidden grate teeth, a white crescent under the chin from a slipped clearing hook, little puckered bites from trench things that objected to being disturbed.

My hands would be my real face.

Wide palms. Short nails. Knuckles swollen from cold water though the basin is warm. Lime cracks at the finger joints. Black pitch permanently caught along the cuticles. A groove in the left palm where the hooked pole rests when I lean my weight into a blockage. I would smell of rinsed leather, mineral water, and the bitter grease used on gate hinges.

People passing would call to me by function as much as name.

“Ho, under-stone!”

“Level-hand!”

“Listen here, the south mouth is speaking wrong.”

My given name might be something plain and basin-made: Tollen, Meraq, Hask, Belit-of-the-Low-Run. But most would use the work-name. In this city a work-name is not less intimate than a birth-name. Perhaps they would call me Silt-Ear.

Because that would be the gift: not prophecy, not magic, nothing luminous or impossible. Just hearing.

In that immense city of water-thunder, where all voices must be raised and every surface answers with drip and clatter, I would have learned to separate sounds the way others separate colors. I would know the hollow slap of clean runoff from roof gutters, the thick cough of mash-water, the granular hiss of plaster wash, the faint irregular tick of tile chips in a channel, the soft smothered knocking that means a rag has caught and is gathering a skin of waste around itself. I would know when a sluice gate sits crooked by the change in echo three turns away. I would hear a trapped eel worrying at a screen. I would hear a bucket chain laboring before its crew admits fatigue.

My work would be repetitive and exact. At dawn, or what passes for it, when the city exhales mist and the roof-edges begin their measured dripping, I would walk the small stations with two apprentices and an old woman who no longer lifts but misses nothing. We would check the marks. Not grand marks, not sacred to outsiders: a scratch on stone, a plug-hole stained dark around the rim, a clay bead tied to a cord, a bone wedge set at the lip of a channel. We would test gates, clear screens, scrape fat, pull hair, lift drowned refuse, curse households that let leather scraps go into the run.

If a drain was moving well, we would touch knuckles to the stone twice. Not prayer exactly. Acknowledgment.

It held. It passes.

By mid-shift the noise would grow enormous. Animals turning wheels. Kilns being fed. Mortars pounding. Children shrieking from roof ropes. Dogs barking at traders. Women shouting measurements from tile yards. Men singing the drag rhythm to coax a balking beast through ankle-deep slurry. Over all of it, water.

And I would go where the water’s voice changed.

Sometimes that would take me under rooms.

There would be crawl spaces below raised sleeping platforms where dark rings of seepage bloom through floors. There would be household drains accessed by lifting a stone slab slick as fat. I would lower myself down with a lamp held high, its flame flattened by wet air, and lie half in black water, reaching blind into a throat of brick. Above me, a family would continue living: a child coughing, a pot lid clattering, goats stamping, someone arguing about salt, someone else telling them to move their foot because the drain-worker needs room.

No one would be embarrassed. Privacy in such a place has valves in it. It opens when needed.

I would pull out the city’s little sins: a wad of hair, a broken spoon of horn, clay too wet to have been dumped, a dead rat swollen pale, a child’s toy boat made from imported wood, shamefully wasted. I would hand each thing up without comment unless comment was useful.

“This clay came from a turning floor. Tell Harun’s yard to settle their wash before sending it.”

“Too much fat. Slaughter side is rushing.”

“This knot is from a roof rope. Who cut over the grate?”

I would not need to be angry often. In the basin, everyone understands consequence. Neglect has a sound, and I would be one of those who names it early.

I would live close to work because everyone does. My dwelling would be a low half-room above a lateral channel, shared with a sister’s family, two apprentice boys not related by blood, a lame dog, three roof birds that refuse eviction, and a very old goat who belongs technically to no one but is fed by all. My sleeping platform would be higher than most, because I dislike damp at the shoulder after years of working in it. Under it: tools wrapped in oiled hide, spare plug-stoppers, measuring cords, a clay box of tokens, a bone flute I rarely play, and the sealed ash jar of my father built into the wall near the floor drain.

He would have been a gate-man in the stepped basins, killed not dramatically but stupidly, by a load-shift during a night clearing when everyone was tired and the third wheel had been groaning wrong for hours. His lesson would be repeated often enough that people younger than me roll their eyes: never stand below a gate whose rope has gone quiet.

His ash-lime would be in the wall. I would trust that wall.

My mother might still be alive, one of the old technical memories seated near sorting trays, her wrists ruined, her voice still able to cut a lane in half. She would correct my knots even after I had apprentices of my own. She would say, “You listen well, but you tie like rain did it.” This would be love.

I would eat when there was time. Thick broth, sour vat loaf, shell meat, warm curd salted hard, sometimes blood cake fried crisp at the edge if a slaughter room had a good day. I would drink mineral water cut with sour ferment. I would carry salt in a horn toggle because drain work steals salt from the body. My leisure would be small: gambling with counters in a kiln-warm doorway, repairing my own straps with unnecessary neatness, lying awake and identifying distant channels by sound.

I might be known for not singing.

This would make me slightly suspect, or at least mockable. Most workers sing, chant, shout, stamp. The city trains sound out of the lungs. But I would save my hearing. When others make themselves large with voice, I would go quieter, head cocked, jaw slack, listening through the soles of my feet.

Children would test me. They would drop chips of brick into gutters two lanes away and see if I knew. I often would. I would shout, without turning, “Red tile, three pieces, and if one reaches the crab screen you clear it with your teeth.” They would scatter delighted, then return later to ask if drain crabs can eat lies.

I would tell them yes, but only small ones. Big lies clog.

The sacred center of the city would not be my home, but I would go there when summoned. The waist-deep basin with the stable level, its surface calmer than anything deserves to be. I would stand at its edge with the water-keepers, feeling always a little rough beside them. Their aprons would be dark and clean, their forearms shaved, their eyes trained to read lamplight on water. Mine would be red-rimmed from crawl spaces and smoke. But they would listen when I said a hidden channel was taking more silt than it should, or that the western seep had warmed, or that the basin’s outflow had developed a second pulse beneath the first.

Disputes might bring me there too.

A household claims its neighbor fouled the shared run. A slaughter crew denies sending bone chips into a narrow mouth. A plasterer says the water rose because the under-stone shifted, not because he rinsed too early. Then I would be asked to speak, not as judge but as witness of passage.

I would say what I heard, what I found, what the marks showed.

The basin people would respect that because truth here is often physical. It has weight, residue, direction. It catches on screens.

If I had a private reverence, it would not be for the gods of grand balance, though I would honor them in the way everyone does. Mine would be for openings.

Mouths, drains, nostrils, kiln vents, animal throats, door gaps, plug-holes, the space between two stones that is either harmless or fatal depending on what passes through it. An opening is a promise and a danger. It lets the city breathe; it lets rot enter. It relieves pressure; it loses heat. It carries away blood; it may return fever.

I would keep small markers near the important mouths: a shell disc, a bit of black stone, a bone wedge carved with three lines. Not charms, I would say if teased. Reminders. Though if one went missing, I would not sleep well.

My closest companion might not be a person but an animal: the lame roof dog, narrow-headed and scarred, who follows me along lanes but refuses crawl spaces. She would know which households drop scraps and which children throw stones. She would bark at strangers and at one particular water wheel for reasons of her own. In the wet dusk she would lie with her chin on my ankle while I cleaned hooks. I would talk to her more softly than I talk to people, because dogs hear under the city too.

There might also be someone I love among the night firers.

That seems likely. Someone whose world is not water but heat in wet air. A kiln-watcher with red eyes, burn scars along the wrist, hair singed short at the temples, hands always smelling faintly of ash and clay vitrified just enough. We would meet at bad hours: me coming up from a blockage, them stepping back from a firehole, both of us salt-crusted and exhausted. Our courtship would be practical. Shared food. Mended straps. A warning about a cracked flue. A hand on the back when crossing a slick threshold. Praise so plain it nearly hurts:

“Your gate held.”

“Your firing took evenly.”

If we quarreled, it would be about risk. Fire people trust transformation; drain people distrust surprise. They would say, “Nothing hardens if you fear heat.” I would say, “Nothing survives if you ignore where runoff goes.” Both true. Both insufficient.

Where would I find myself in this world?

In the moment before trouble becomes disaster.

That is the place that fits me: the tense interval between first wrong sound and public alarm. The city is made of repetition, but repetition is never perfect. Somewhere, always, a seam is tiring. A screen is loading. A beast is limping. A vat is souring in the wrong way. A roof prop is taking more than its share. Most citizens participate in continuance by doing the necessary motions at the necessary time. I would be one of those whose life is shaped around noticing when the motion changes.

Not heroic. Preventive.

The kind of person whose best work leaves no story.

If I do my work well, the lane does not flood. The slaughter room drains. The fever does not enter from the black channel. The central basin remains level. No one praises what did not happen, except perhaps the old, who understand avoided failure as a form of construction.

Yet there would be one day, because every such city requires one day, when the sound changes beneath everything.

Not a dramatic crack. Not a roar. A lack.

I would be walking the back run by the hoof-washing shed with a hook over my shoulder and the lame dog behind me, when I would stop so suddenly she bumps my calf. Around me the city would continue: buckets, hooves, voices, wheels, rain beginning to thicken the air. But under the paving, one channel would have gone smooth. Too smooth. A drain that should knock and chatter would be sliding silent, full-bellied.

I would put my palm to the stone.

Warm. Pressed. Carrying more than it should.

Then I would run.

Not gracefully. Basin-running: short steps, low center, one hand out, shoulder through hanging hides, over a trough, under a rack of drying brick, shouting as I go.

“Shut the east wash! Close slaughter! Lift the child gate! Knock three to the lower crew!”

Some would obey before understanding, because the city survives by trust in competent alarm. A boy would strike a pipe rhythm. A woman would swing a plug beam into place. A turn-beast handler would curse and halt his animal mid-circle. Water would seek all the habits denied it. Somewhere below, a hidden blockage would swell against old stone.

By the time we open it, the lane may already be breathing foul water at its seams. People will arrive with poles, wedges, hide sheets, buckets, lime. My kiln-watcher, if I have one, may come with ash and heat-stiffened brick. My mother may shout from a bench that we are all standing in the wrong place. The apprentices will be frightened and proud. The dog will bark from a roof.

We will work until hands lose feeling.

We will cut, lever, sluice, haul up black ropes of waste, broken tile, bones, a whole collapsed mat of hair and hide strips and mineral fat. Drain crabs will scatter in offended armies. Someone will vomit. Someone will laugh. Someone will start the rhythm knocks again so the lower gate crews know when to open. The water will buck, then cough, then pass.

And when it passes, the whole lane will change pitch.

No trumpet. No miracle. Just the return of rough movement under stone.

I would lean there soaked to the waist, forehead against a wall sweating with condensation, and feel the city resume itself through my bones.

Someone would say, “It held.”

Someone else, perhaps one of my apprentices, would answer, “No. We held it.”

Both true.

That is who I would be: a keeper of passage in a city of maintained beginnings. A compact, damp, loud-when-needed, quiet-when-listening citizen of the under-stone runs. Not builder, not ruler, not priest exactly, but kin to all three. One who tends the mouths by which the city rids itself of excess and therefore remains alive.

I would believe, with the certainty of palms and soles, that permanence is not a state. It is a chore repeated before failure.

And at night, when lamps tremble behind hide screens and wheels turn in the dark and water falls only to be lifted again, I would sleep above a drain I trust, with the city’s endless wet thunder below me like a second heart.
