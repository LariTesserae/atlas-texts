You would not find yourself at the center of this world. There is no center here that would keep you. You would be found along an edge that is not quite an edge: under a tilted shelf of black slate where a sheet of water falls before a pocket of air, in a chamber too wet to be called shelter and too sheltered to be called current.

You would be low.

Not small in the way of insects or films, not one of the nearly invisible lives that make the stone slick and edible, but not large enough to command any passage either. You would be one of the basin’s deliberate animals: a compact, pale, amphibious thing with a body shaped less for traveling than for remaining.

At first you might be mistaken for a stone that had learned uneasily to breathe.

Your back would be darkened by the place—slate-gray, clay-brown, green-black in the seams where films cling to you as they cling to rock. Your underside would be the color of old shell and drowned root. Your skin would not be smooth exactly, but close-grained, rubbery, cold, always beaded. It would shed silt badly; a little of the basin would always travel with you. Fine grit would gather in the folds of your elbows, under your jaw, around the bases of your fingers. Pale scars would cross your flanks where stone had taken its payment.

You would have limbs made for holding fast.

Your arms would be short and strong, with narrow hands and long fingers that close like roots into cracks. The fingertips would be blunt, ridged, and extraordinarily sensitive. You would read the world through them: the grain of basalt, the looseness of a shell, the presence of a worm beneath a trembling tube, the difference between a stone that has slept in place for centuries and one that will shift if trusted.

Your feet would spread wide against current, toes webbed only partly, because swimming is useful but gripping is life. Your knees and elbows would know the posture of crouching better than standing. Height would be a danger and an extravagance. When you moved through the water, you would keep close to the floor, pulling yourself from ledge to ledge, flattening instinctively whenever the basin tightened its flow.

Your face would not be made for expression as humans know it.

Your eyes would be present but poor: clouded, wide, able to separate dim green from black, movement from stillness, opening from wall. They would not be your chief knowledge. Around your mouth and along your cheeks would be pale sensory filaments, whisker-like but thicker, sometimes pressed flat by current, sometimes fanning in the stillness of your mist chamber. Your lips would be soft and mobile, tasting water continuously. Your nostrils, if they could be called that, would close under pressure and open in the air pockets, drawing in mineral cold, sulfur, weed-rot, rain.

You would breathe both ways, imperfectly.

Underwater, gill-slits or folded membranes along your neck and ribs would take enough from the cold basin to let you cross troughs, forage under shelves, wait in the lee of stones. In air, your lungs would work slowly, heavily, as though air were only another dampness to be filtered. You would surface not into sunlight but into mist. You would sit under black rock with water falling before you, throat pulsing, skin shining, listening through your bones to rain striking the basin above.

You would be clever, but not inventive in the bright, ascending sense.

Your intelligence would not build towers, laws, names, fires, or histories. The place would not allow those things to remain, and over generations your kind would have learned not to spend life on what the water takes quickly. Your cleverness would be timing, touch, sequence, restraint.

You would know that the trough beyond the carbonate bloom is safe after three days of rain but deadly when the lower salt comes in thick.

You would know which pale limpet-shells can be pried loose with a slate wedge and which will cost a fingertip.

You would know that bubbles rising in a certain narrow crack mean gas and loosened mud, and that no burrow there should be opened.

You would know where blind fish gather when the upper basin turns white with storm.

You would know the stone shelf that hums before a silt bank collapses.

You would know hunger not as an emergency, but as one of the basin’s ordinary pressures.

Your tools would be momentary.

A slate flake held between two fingers to cut the seal of a shell.

A rounded pebble used as a hammer against crust.

A rib bone, washed from some dead visitor, used for a season to rake worms from mud before the current takes it.

A shell edge scraped across living film.

You would not keep a collection in any human sense. You might have a few favored objects wedged in a crack above your resting hollow, but the basin would keep reminding you that possession is only a delay. A storm would enter. The crack would empty. The tools would be sorted into gravel, polished into anonymity, buried under silt. You would search again, choose again, use again.

Your dwelling would be a preference.

Behind the falling sheet, under the slanted shelf, there is a pocket of air and mist. Not dry. Never dry. Water drips from the ceiling in cold intervals. The floor is stone slicked with clay. Pale lichen grows on the upper wall where spray reaches but current does not. Below the pocket, a submerged slot leads into a black corridor. Above it, a narrow fissure breathes wind during storms.

This would be your place because it is difficult for larger things to enter. The old eel cannot turn easily there. The heavy basin fish cannot push through the falling water without striking stone. Sudden floods scour the outer lip but leave the rear hollow intact more often than not. Food catches nearby: torn algae, dead crustaceans, drowned insects from the upper country, shell-creatures fixed to the sheltered wall.

You would sleep clamped into a fold of rock, fingers curled into familiar pits, body half in water, half in mist.

You would wake when the basin changes pressure.

Not at dawn. Dawn is too faint a thing here. You would wake because the water at your ribs cools by a fraction, because the falling sheet thickens, because suspended silt touches your whiskers differently. You would wake because the world has altered its grip.

Then you would go out low into the green-black.

You would move slowly unless movement must be quick. Your hands would find the next hold before your feet released the last. When crossing open stone, you would pause behind every blunt ridge, pressing your belly to the cold, feeling the long push of water pass over you. Small crustaceans would flicker away from your shadow. Worm-mouths would vanish into tubes. Shells would close. Sometimes a blind fish would hover too long, misreading you as rock, and your hand would close around it with a speed rarely used and therefore terrible.

You would eat what the basin permits.

Films scraped from stone.

Soft worms drawn from silt.

Shell-meat pried loose after long work.

Crustaceans gathered in cupped hands from underside hollows.

Fish, when they are careless, injured, or trapped after storms.

Carrion, when the water brings it.

You would not disdain decay. Nothing here can afford purity. A dead thing is not unclean; it is temporary concentration. You would eat quickly before the basin distributes it beyond you.

Your kind—if there are others—would not gather often.

Perhaps you would smell or taste them before seeing them: a trace in the water, an old scrape mark on a shell bed, a favored stone missing from a crack. Encounters would be cautious. The basin does not feed crowds. Two of you might share the same storm deposit for a little while, crouched on opposite sides of a shelf, watching through dimness with poor eyes and excellent nerves.

Mating would be seasonal only in the way flow is seasonal. It would follow salinity, pressure, the opening of certain mudbanks, the oxygen-rich aftermath of violent rain. Eggs would be few, large, hidden under stone lips in chambers where water moves enough to breathe but not enough to tear. They would be coated in clear gel that gathers silt until they resemble pebbles. You would guard them perhaps, but not sentimentally. Guarding would be another form of remaining.

The young would emerge already gripping.

Pale, translucent, all head and hands at first. Many would vanish into fish, brine, silt, storm. Those that lived would learn the basin not through teaching alone but through injury. They would taste wrong water and retreat. They would touch shifting stone and lose a nail. They would follow an adult’s path along a safe shelf until one storm rearranged it and forced memory to become attention again.

If you had language, it would not travel far.

In water, you might pulse your throat, click stones softly, tap through the shelf with knuckles, release breath in patterned bubbles. In air pockets, there might be low croaks or wet consonants, brief and practical. Not songs in the open-sky sense. Not speeches. More like warnings, invitations, refusals, recognitions:

Brine below.

Hold.

Food here.

Large one passed.

Do not enter.

Wait.

Much of what you know would never become signal. It would remain in posture: the flattening of the body, the closing of fingers, the angle of the head toward a current seam.

You would have no myth of owning the basin.

The basin would be older than ownership and more convincing than any claim. It would take the dead, the careless, the warm, the upright, the overconfident. It would take tools, marks, nests, paths. It would smooth evidence into geology. Even your bones, when your time came, would not long declare you. They would lodge under silt, separate, mineralize, crack, feed films, become pale fragments among shells.

But while you lived, you would be exquisitely placed.

You would be the being that fits between stone and water, between animal and weather, between hand and current. Not master, not victim only. A low pale knower of drowned passages. A user of temporary edges. A keeper of routes in the body.

You would be found below the weather and below the land, though not entirely below the sky:

crouched behind a curtain of falling water,  
silvered with mist,  
one hand closed around a slate flake,  
whiskers tasting rain in the dark,  
waiting for the basin to tell you what may be done next.
