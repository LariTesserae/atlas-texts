You would find yourself **inside the ring**, but not at its center.

Not at first.

At first you would be at the inner edge of the black wall, where newcomers pause long enough to be measured. You would stand among the notched stones and dangling beads, in the gentler turbulence where the dust has learned to go around bodies rather than through them. Behind you: the open highland, the pale basin, the impossible absence of rivers. Before you: the dry garden, roaring softly and continuously, all its vanes and bladders and combs turning toward you without appearing to move.

You would not be a visitor from elsewhere for long.

The place does not allow simple visitors.

It tests what you leak.

Breath. Salt. Heat. Fear. Memory. Moisture hidden under the tongue. The dampness in the eyes. The thought of rain.

If you belonged there, the grove would not welcome you kindly. It would simply fail to reject you. The fan-plants would remain half-open. The copper needles would keep listening past your shadow. The root-threads would tighten, then loosen, then continue their slow migration across salt. One of the small sail-backed runners might climb onto your foot and raise its membrane to read the charge rising through your bones.

You would be one of the **dry-tenders**.

Not quite priest. Not quite gardener. Not quite human in the ordinary lowland sense anymore.

You would be a person who has given up wetness by degrees.

Your skin would be wrapped in layered fiber, lacquered with mineral paste and resin until it held the body’s vapor in strict obedience. Your eyes would sit behind horn lenses smoked amber and violet, not to shield against brightness, but to interpret dimness: charge halos, pollen gusts, the faint glow of armed veins. Around your mouth you would wear a ribbed mask filled with powdered salt, ash, and scraped fan-fiber, so that each breath is dried again before it leaves you.

You would carry no waterskin.

To carry water past the wall would be obscene. Worse than obscene: incompetent.

At your belt would hang wooden tongs, a flint crescent knife, a spool of pale root-cord, a bone awl, and several small sealed packets of exchange dust: blue, sulfur-yellow, black, red. You would carry a folded net so fine it could catch airborne seeds without bruising their skins. Around one wrist you might wear rings made from old horn, each carved with harvest-times, warnings, and debts owed to plants that remember.

You would move by manners rather than paths.

Step only where salt is dull, never where it shines newly cleared.

Pass copper needles low and side-on.

Pause when amber sacs grow transparent.

Never speak during the first lift of dust.

Never place a hand on the hut unless the mica has already named you.

You would know that the garden is not a place one walks through, but a body one asks to enter again and again.

Your work would be timing.

You would know the moment when a bladder-tree offers resin from its underside, before the sacs harden and begin clacking warnings. You would know how to scrape mineral flesh from a pillar without waking its teeth. You would know that luminous veins may be cut once, and only once, and that the second cut is not theft but stupidity. You would know which rattling fan has gone sterile, which comb is building charge toward bloom, which bare circle of dust should be ringed with stones before whatever made it rises.

When the grove exhales, you would crouch with your back against one of the seated stones and let the storm pass through the spaces around you.

You would not close your eyes.

Only children and outsiders close their eyes during a full exhalation.

In the storm you would read the place: the direction of powder fall, the color of sparks along the root-threads, the smell of resin clotting somewhere unseen. The dust would strike your mask and sleeves like thrown seed. Shapes would appear in the air—antlers, towers, hands, a face made of mineral grain—and you would decide which were accidents, which were warnings, and which were the garden remembering something it had not finished consuming.

You might live in the skeletal hut when permitted, though “live” would mean occupying it between its own activities.

The hut is older than any one tender. It is not merely shelter. It is an instrument built for a climate with no rain: shade, ear, throat, sieve, resonator. Its mica plates translate wind into language badly enough that language survives. Its poles remember hands. Its lashings hum at night with tensions moving through the grove.

You would sleep beside it, not in it, unless the clicking had the right interval.

If the hut clicked three times and paused, you would answer with a knuckle on ribwood.

If it clicked five times, you would remain silent.

If it clicked in a rhythm you had never been taught, you would leave before dark.

Your people, if they are still your people, would come and go.

You would not have a village in the wet, settled sense. The ring itself is smaller than a village square, but denser than a city. On market-days of dust, others would arrive from the slopes: masked figures bent under packets, horn plates, seed cages, coils of cord, stories sealed in song. They would hang offerings in the notches of the wall. They would shout greetings over the clatter of fans and the moan of hollow stems. No one would ask, “What did you bring to trade?” They would ask, “What has changed its timing?” or “Which side of the grove remembers blood?” or “Did the south glass reeds sing before dawn?”

You would answer with what you had seen.

A crescent of new salt on the lee side of the violet fans.

The ribbon-predator traveling through the root-web before moonrise.

A goat-horned grazer found pearled and hollow near the amber sacs.

A child’s old footprint brightening under dust.

In exchange, someone would give you a warning from another slope, or a powder that calms the seed-moths, or a new verse for approaching copper needles when they are listening.

You would be poor in possessions and rich in prohibitions.

But the prohibitions would not feel like prison. They would feel like grammar.

The grove speaks in danger because danger is what remains when water is removed from mercy. Everything there is exact because imprecision wastes particles. Every life is sharpened by the covenant: no drinking, no rotting, no soft abundance. Instead: charge, salt, resin, horn, heat, dust, timing.

You would be shaped by that covenant too.

Your blood, if cut, would clot faster than lowland blood. Perhaps not fully resin, not yet, but amber-edged and fragrant. Your tears would be rare and shameful, caught in absorbent cloth before they could fall. Your dead, when they died within the ring, would not be buried. They would be seated against the inner wall until wind, insects, and root-filaments made them useful. Their bones would become tools, beads, warning chimes, or eventually pale architecture in the eddies.

And perhaps, after many seasons, you would no longer be only one of the masked tenders.

Perhaps the grove would begin to use you more directly.

A root-thread might stitch itself through the sole of your sleeping sandal and not withdraw. A scar on your palm might seal with glossy mineral instead of skin. You might begin to hear the difference between the hut’s clicking and the underground knocking, then between the underground knocking and your own pulse. The dust-spheres in the basin beside the hut might roll toward you when you pass, each one accreted around a seed, a tooth, a forgotten name.

You would still walk. Still harvest. Still shout with others on market-days.

But the garden would have added you to its diagram.

Not as ruler.

Not as sacrifice.

As an organ of attention.

You would be a **listener-tender of the storm-garden**: a dry human, or once-human, standing between the intermittent people and the continuous intelligence of the ring. Your task would be to keep the manners intact between flesh and basalt-root, between hunger and offering, between memory and weather.

You would know that the grove is not sacred because it is gentle.

It is sacred because it is exact.

Because nothing there can afford forgiveness in the form wet places know.

Because a single drop of water becomes catastrophe, shrine, feast, infection, miracle.

Because life has not overcome thirst; it has made thirst articulate.

At night, when the stars blur close above the ash-clouds and the black wall becomes a circle of broken teeth, you would sit on a polished stone beside the humming hut. Around you the grove would change watches. Some fronds would close, others open. Armored scavengers would grind in the salt. Seed-moths would shrill inside amber sacs. Somewhere beneath the basalt, something would knock with slow patience.

You would taste ozone, resin, powdered horn.

You would lift one gloved hand into the charged air and feel the fine hairs of your sleeve rise.

And when the whole garden drew itself inward before the next exhalation, you would bow your masked face—not in worship exactly, but in recognition.

You are in the ring of black stone, on the warm high spur above the pale basin.

You are one of those who do not bring water.

You are a keeper of dry abundance.

A negotiator with thirst.

A temporary body in an ancient breathing engine.

A listener at the edge of the dust that thinks.
