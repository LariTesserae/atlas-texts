I think I would not arrive there as a visitor.

A visitor is too loud a category. Even if I came in quietly, even if I entered through the low gate with my hood drawn close and my hands empty, the city would have to account for me. It would have to count my heat, my regret, the wetness in my lungs, the distance I had brought with me from elsewhere. A visitor is an event, and this city seems to distrust events. It prefers increments: a bead forming on a fin, a millimeter of wall, a frost line correcting itself over several mornings.

Nor would I be one of the robed citizens at first. They have been trained by scarcity too intimately. They know which waters to keep apart. They know when a corridor is asking for payment. They know not to look too long at a window that shows a sea. I would admire them, but I would not yet have earned their quiet.

I would find myself lower down.

Not underground exactly, because the city’s “under” is not simple. I would be in the damp interstice beneath the cistern court, in the region of warmed ceramic veins and patient pipes, where the upward-falling drops have already been named by the time they pass. There are access shafts there no wider than a shoulder, and dark gutters with flexible ice around their rims, and handrails made partly of the mineralized dead. The air would be cold enough to ache, but threaded with little exhalations of heat from machines that breathe once every few hours.

I would be a small office of the city.

Not an official. Not a person with keys.

An office in the older sense: a duty given shape.

I would be one of the things responsible for harmless occupancy.

A vacancy-tender.

Perhaps I began as a person, long enough ago that no one now insists on the distinction. I might once have had knees, a name, preferences about bitter tea. But in this place identity does not leave cleanly. It condenses, adheres, is routed into useful surfaces. Now I would be dispersed through a set of minor chambers: three alcoves, a storage room that opens only in blue morning, a bench near a drain, several thumb-sized gardens, and a strip of condensation that appears daily along a black wall below the archive quarter.

My task would be simple and endless: to keep empty rooms from becoming hungry.

The city cannot tolerate true emptiness. A sealed room left bare will begin to imagine occupants. First a chair that was never built. Then the smell of wool. Then the memory of a conversation. Then, perhaps, someone standing with their back turned in the frost-dark corner, waiting to be made more real. Most of these potential occupants are harmless if noticed early. But if neglected, they grow impolite. They press against doors. They borrow shadows. They answer when no one has spoken.

So I would move, or be moved, through the small unclaimed interiors.

I would not walk in any ordinary way. I would arrive as a warming of the left wall, as a darkening of frost around a threshold, as the faint arrangement of dust into a spiral that says: enough, something is here. If a chamber had been unused too long, I would give it a harmless center. Three stones. A folded rag. A lichen cup. A nail warmed by previous hands. A thread of moss laid along the sill. A bowl turned upside down so that the room could organize itself around the fact of a bowl.

In one of my rooms there would be a single red shrub under mesh. Its branches would make tiny correcting motions in the still air. I would know its moods by the taste of condensation on the wall. When its joints stiffened, I would request one drop from the eastern channel, not northern, never northern; northern water would make it dream of slopes. The request would pass through a bead-chain, then a pipe, then the attention of someone living enough to lift a cup. Hours later, or yesterday, the drop would arrive.

I would love the small gardens best.

Not love as a human loves, perhaps. More like a wall loves its load: by holding and being altered by holding. Each garden would be a grammatical mark placed against chaos. Here is a stone. Here is damp substrate. Here is one living filament accepting mineral and cold. Therefore this room is not empty. Therefore no surplus child, no stray century, no chair from an unbuilt house needs to gather itself here.

The citizens would know me, though they might not speak of me as someone.

A child sent on errands might pause before the alcove where I am most concentrated and press two mittened fingers to the wall.

“Keep this one quiet,” the child might whisper, placing a black pebble on the sill.

And I would keep it quiet.

Or try.

Sometimes I would fail.

A room behind a hooded mirror might ripen too quickly. Its vacancy might fill with a dinner party from a warmer version of the city: cups upright, voices layered, someone laughing with their mouth full. The laughter would fog the mirror from underneath. Then the error-clerks would come, solemn and annoyed, carrying chalk, thermal combs, and a box of sleeping insects. They would not blame me, exactly. Rooms have weather. But they would stand in a semicircle and wait until I withdrew my frost from the hinges so the door could be opened safely.

At other times, I would be called to newborn spaces.

The city grows slowly, grain by grain. When a rib in the north arcade finishes becoming a wall, the inside of it must be taught not to panic. New rooms are the most dangerous kind. They have never held a coat, a breath, a delay. They are pure invitation. I would be sent there with a starter kit of presence: a scrap of worn cloth, two flakes of old ice, a sleeping lichen, a measure of dust collected from inhabited stairs. I would spread myself thinly across the corners until the room learned that it did not need to summon anything dramatic in order to exist.

My body, if someone insisted on finding one, would be nearly disappointing.

In the maintenance records I might be represented as a dark soft thing living under a bridge, mistaken for one of the vent-scrapers. A fist-sized organism with a shell of porous black mineral and a pale underside, able to flatten itself into seams. It would have no eyes, or too many simple ones hidden under a wet membrane. Its limbs would be delicate and retractable, more like punctuation than legs. It would feed on mineral bloom, skin salts, old breath, and the thin film left by possibilities when they fail to become real.

But that would not be all of me.

I would also be the habit of certain doors not opening onto dangerous versions of themselves. I would be the reason a bench remains faintly warm although no one has sat there since the last thaw. I would be a recurring pattern of droplets on the underside of a stair. I would be the handprint that almost forms beside the child’s handprints but never quite does, larger and older, withdrawing by noon.

Where would I be found?

Mostly where no one is looking for the center.

Not in the cistern court itself, though I would hear it. The upward-falling drop would pass through me as a change in pressure, a small civic heartbeat. Not in the archive quarter, though my work would border it and fear it. Not at the gates, where arrivals are counted in grander systems.

I would be in the negative spaces that make the city livable.

Behind the bench.

Under the half-stair that continues as a shadow.

Inside the storage room whose shelves hold only folded cloth and one sealed jar of southern melt.

In the narrow gap between two walls that almost touch, where frost grows in the shape of an ear.

In the alcove no one notices until they need somewhere to leave a stone.

The citizens would not worship me. That would be embarrassing and unsafe. They would maintain me as I maintain their vacancies. Once a month, an elderly corridor-gardener would scrape away excess mineral from my bridge-body with a flat bone tool. Once a year, during the coldest administrative season, an error-clerk would read aloud the list of rooms I had kept harmless. If the list included no serious manifestations, a drop of compatible water would be given to my mosses.

My name would not be a spoken name. Spoken names are too summoning.

It might be written as a damp mark on ceramic, renewed by condensation. It might translate badly as:

**The One Left So The Room Need Not Invent Another.**

Or more affectionately, by children:

**Under-Here.**

I would be content with that.

Contentment in such a place would not be happiness exactly. It would be alignment. To have a function small enough not to damage the world. To be necessary without becoming central. To take the pressure of absence into oneself and return it as frost, moss, a placed stone, a harmless smell of cloth.

And sometimes, in evening, when the windows deepen and amber light leaks from an adjacent sun, I would gather myself into the bench near the drain and almost remember having shoulders.

A citizen would sit there, tired from separating waters.

They would not know they were sitting partly in my memory.

Their coat would creak with cold. Their gloved hand would rest on the rail. For a while neither of us would move. The city around us would tick and condense and keep its miracles quiet. Somewhere above, a drop would detach from the fountain and fall upward into the basin that is always almost full.

The room behind us would remain calm.

That would be enough.
