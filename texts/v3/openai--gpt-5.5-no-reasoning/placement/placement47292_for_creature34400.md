I would find myself low to the world.

Not crawling, exactly. Not dragging myself like something helpless. But held close to the made surface, my weight distributed across it carefully, politely, as if the floor were not floor but a treaty. I would live where the black composite plain dips a few centimeters beneath the pipe-racks and becomes a chain of warm shallow sumps, each one roofed by gantries and sweating conduits. Not the wide reservoirs, not the dry turbine roads, not the high towers where the pale crabs fold themselves into brackets. Lower than that. In the under-margin between walkway and water, where condensation gathers before the scheduled rain and the grates hum faintly with heat from below.

At first I think I am one of the grazers: blind, patient, oval, mouth sealed to ceramic, moving so slowly that direction hardly matters. A body made for adhesion and rasping. A creature that polishes warning arrows without knowing they point. That would be easy here. It would be peaceful in the way a gasket is peaceful: pressed into purpose, warmed by the system, fed by invisible films.

But no. I am not that simple.

I have too much attention.

Not intelligence in the human sense; not thought rising cleanly above appetite. More like an elaborate tactility. My world reaches me through my underside, through dozens of damp contact-pads arranged along the rim of my body. I feel heat before I know place. I feel vibration before I know danger. The map inside me is not visual. It is a web of remembered hums: the narrow-tremor of a valve scheduled far down the channel, the broad waking sigh of a compressor behind a sealed wall, the soft granular clicking of pipe-crabs moving overhead, the blank pressure of a stilt-hunter standing motionless nearby.

I am a seam-dweller.

My body is flat enough to vanish under a raised lip of paneling, broad enough to cover a drainage slot, flexible enough to enter the long black spaces between plates when the cleaning arms pass. From above I might be mistaken for a spill, a patch of wet insulation, a collapsed membrane, a shadow beneath a rail. My skin is translucent smoke-grey over pale yellow organs. Along my back are darker lines, not quite stripes, following the pattern of the expansion seams I inhabit. Droplets bead on me and do not roll off until I move.

I have no head worth naming.

One end is slightly thicker and carries the tasting frill: a crescent of soft tendrils, short and constantly opening, closing, opening again. They sample condensate, mineral salts, the musk of passing animals, the sour trace of poisoned trays, the electrical tang that precedes a pressure purge. Beneath that frill is my mouth, which is usually hidden: a round, muscular aperture lined with fine dark rasping plates. I feed on the grey films that bloom where warmth, moisture, and shade meet at the underside of infrastructure.

But I am not only a grazer.

I wait.

I am one of the creatures that learned the difference between a surface and a surface pretending to be empty. I lie in the sump lips and drainage seams, thin as poured rubber. Small animals cross me: heat mites, bead-larvae, soft transparent vent-clingers dislodged by mist, the youngest crabs before their climbing limbs have hardened. Many pass unharmed. Their weight tells me they are not worth closing over. Others pause to feed on the film growing across my back, and then my rim lifts without hurry.

I fold from the edges inward.

There is no strike, no drama. The world teaches quietness. I make a small dark tent of myself, seal my underside to the panel, and digest what entered the hollow. By the time the next rain event ticks across the gantries, nothing remains but a thicker line in me, a little stored warmth, a few hard flecks worked slowly toward the waste pores along my rear edge.

I live by schedules I do not understand and remember perfectly.

Every nineteen hours, the vent beside Sump-Line Twelve exhales warm vapor rich enough to wake the films. Six minutes before it opens, the pipe above it begins to tremble with a frequency that reaches deep into my pads. I leave my seam then, always then, and spread across the ceramic buttress before the palm-sized grazers arrive. The first bloom is mine. When the vapor thickens, the transparent bead-organisms cluster over it; when they cluster, the narrow fliers sometimes drop from the underside of the catwalk. I flatten at the first pressure-change of their wings. They prefer movement. I become panel.

Every third misting, the channel flushes from west to east. I know west only as the direction from which the cold taste comes. The breathers rise just before it, rows of clicking mouths dimpling the water. The stilt-hunters know this too. Their needle feet enter the old dents above the channel, one after another, with a precision I feel through the plates. When their shadows fall across my seam, I withdraw deep enough that only my back remains exposed. A stilt-hunter will eat me if it is starving, but I am not ideal prey. Too flat. Too little struggle. Too many mineral plates in the mouth.

The crabs are more dangerous.

They are curious in a hard, methodical way. A pale pipe-crab can mistake me for a film-rich spill and begin dismantling my edge with its smaller claws. If I stay flat, it will peel me. If I fold too quickly, it will cut me. So I do what my kind does: I loosen my outer skin.

A crab’s probe enters. My rim puckers around it. The crab grips, pulls, and receives a false strip of me, a sheet of tasteless membrane already dead. While it cleans its claw, I slide backward into the seam, thinner than a shadow. Later I regrow the strip from dissolved grazers and vent-film. My margins are old with such losses. My outline is scalloped, repaired, scalloped again.

I do not see the horizon. Creatures like me do not need distances that large.

My distances are: from seam to vent; from vent to sump lip; from sump lip to the underside of the warm cable tray; from cable tray back to the safe black crack before the maintenance crawler passes.

The maintenance crawler is weather.

It comes on its rail with a vibration too large to be animal. Long before it arrives, the fliers lift in layers, the crabs brace against pipework, the grazers seal their bellies hard to the panels. I withdraw entirely beneath Plate 8-Delta, where the water is only a skin and the heat is constant. The crawler moves above with its slow administrative thunder, brushes hanging, suction mouths testing the slots, articulated cleaning arms sweeping roost-matter and shells and unlucky young into the drains.

Sometimes its instruments taste my hiding place.

A white light enters the seam. A thin spray follows, bitter and sterilizing. I contract into a bead then, all my breadth gathered into the smallest possible knot. My outer skin blisters. Films die around me. When the crawler passes, the seam is clean, horribly clean, stripped of food and scent. For a time the world is too bright in the chemical sense. Then warmth returns. Spores arrive in condensate. Bead-larvae drift through the mesh. The grey pasture begins again.

I have offspring, though I do not tend them.

During the long interior drizzle, when condensation falls steadily from the gantry forest and the panels remain wet for hours, my body thickens with pale capsules. They form along the darker lines of my back, each one a cloudy oval with a trembling point. I carry them until the relay-click behind the sealed control room: three clicks close together, then a hum rising through the handrails. That signal means the under-panel channels will warm.

I climb then, slowly, dangerously, up the side of the low buttress. For a creature like me, vertical is a kind of exposure. My pads grip mineral sweat. My frill tastes old electricity, bird-ammonia, the iron sweetness of a recent kill. Under the handrail, where no human hand has passed for ages, there is a sheltered groove warmed by a conduit behind the wall. I press my back to it and release the capsules in a neat row.

They adhere at once.

For several cycles they are only beads. Then threads uncurl inside them. When the warmth peaks, they split. My young emerge as soft commas, translucent and almost without shape. They do not resemble me. They enter the wet groove, feed on film, hide from mites, and are carried by condensation into other seams. Most become food before they have a rim. A few will learn the hums. Fewer still will flatten into adulthood and remember a section of the world as I remember mine.

Remember is not the right word.

But it is close.

I know the panel where a dead flier once fell and fed me for three days. I know the drain whose water tastes safe only after rain, never before. I know the old yellow arrow under the grazer trail, not as a sign but as a smoothness in the floor where films grow poorly. I know the sealed glass of the control room as a vertical coldness, and the chairs beyond it as dark upright shapes that sometimes shelter small warm sleepers. I do not know what the chairs are for. I know that beneath them the floor holds heat.

There may be descendants of people here. I have felt them only as rare disturbances.

Once, during low-glare, something thin-footed crossed the service walkway above my sump. Not a stilt-hunter: too warm in the wrong pattern, too irregular, too full of small hesitations. Not a grazer, not a crab, not a maintenance crawler. It paused near the sealed door and touched the handle again and again. Each touch sent a delicate metallic question through the plate.

I remained flat.

A droplet fell from the pipe and struck its shoulder. It startled violently. Many animals startle, but this one did so inwardly, as if the shock continued after the motion ended. It crouched by the condensate tray, drank, and left behind a strip of shed skin or fabric caught on a bolt. Later I tasted it. Salt, oil, old fear, mammal heat. The crabs took most of it. A film bloomed where it had lain.

If such beings live here, they live as we do: by learning what opens, what burns, what lowers, what cleans.

The world does not hate us. It does not welcome us either. Its mercy is repetition.

I am suited to that mercy.

My body is an agreement with maintenance. I flourish in the places not quite reached: the underside of the handrail, the half-shadow of a sump, the millimeter gap beneath a plate, the warm wet lip around a drain. I eat what the machine permits to grow. I catch what the schedules dislodge. I breed when the conduits say breed. I vanish when the cleaning arms pass. I am not wild, because there is no wilderness here. I am not domestic, because there is no keeper. I am native to an intention that forgot it was intention.

At night, if the glare lowers, I come out farther.

Blue inspection strips shine through the shallow water, and the breathers move there in layered columns. The undersides of pipes gather pearls of condensation, each droplet reflecting a tiny inverted gantry. Pale crabs pick along the cable trays. Far off, a basin creature releases a bubble so large that the channel shivers under me. The fliers rearrange themselves with dry wing-scrapes. A stilt-hunter stands over the water like a question no one asks aloud.

I spread myself across the warm ceramic and feed.

My mouth works slowly. My pads read the hum below the hum below the hum. Around me the machine-marsh continues: wet, exact, crowded, complete. Nothing becomes forest. Nothing becomes ruin. Nothing becomes anything other than itself.

And I, too, become only more myself.

A grey seam-membrane.  
A living stain with timing.  
A soft inheritor of the gap between made things.
