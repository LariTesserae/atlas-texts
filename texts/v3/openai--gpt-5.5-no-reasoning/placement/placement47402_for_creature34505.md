I think I would first appear to myself as a mistake in the emptiness.

Not a person standing in the basin, not one of the obvious animals crossing a plaza, not one of the grand vegetal walls. Something smaller and harder to notice: a little interruption in a root-street, a repeated darkening in the wet clay where nothing with feet has stepped. A hesitation in the city’s layout.

At first I might think I am an animal, because I would move.

I would move low to the ground, close enough to feel the heat rising from mud and root. The roaring world would be too large above me: walls flexing, leaves clapping, frogs screaming from chambers I could not see, the endless percussion of drops falling from roofs that never learned to be still. I would know the place mostly by vibration. The basin would come to me through my underside before it came through eyes or ears: tremor of hooves, pulse of swelling root, the far dull boom of a hollow stem, the sudden radiant shock when a seedpod bursts and the ground receives the news.

I would have a body that is not quick in the lizard way and not massive in the browser way. Something intermediate, secretive, made for crossings rather than destinations.

A flat, many-jointed thing, perhaps. Not an insect exactly, not a crab, not a worm. A palm-sized animal with a flexible plated back the color of wet humus and old amber sap. My body would be low and oval, able to squeeze into the breathing cracks between root and clay. Along my sides would be fine legs, too many to count casually, each tipped with soft hooks that feel chemical dampness and minute changes in pressure. I would have no face in the mammal sense. Instead, a blunt front edge fringed with tasting threads, constantly touching, withdrawing, touching again.

But that is only the first approximation.

Because in this place, a creature is rarely only itself.

I think I would be one of the city’s little surveyors of passage: a route-keeper, a seam-reader, a humble citizen of openings.

I would live where walls almost meet.

Not in the central court, not in the high leaf-ribs among birds, not in the bright plazas where browsers kneel and predators collaborate with architecture. I would inhabit the thresholds: the narrow green slits that open at the base of walls, the undersides of root causeways, the damp margins of cistern chambers, the dark grooves where runoff has polished moss until it resembles velvet stone. I would be found in the places where the living city is deciding whether something may pass.

My species—if there are enough of us to deserve that word—would be drawn to edges of temporary agreement. When two wall-leaves refuse to touch and make an arch, we gather invisibly in the seam. When a root stair changes its mind and descends where it once climbed, we cluster beneath the turning knots. When a doorway briefly contains last night’s rain instead of the chamber beyond, we taste the air until the memory thins.

We would not build the city. We would not command it.

But we would help it remember where openings have been.

Our bodies would collect the residue of passage. Spores stick to us. Pollen clings to the ridges of our plates. Mineral salts crust faintly along our joints. When browsers brush through a corridor, when beetles enter a slit, when blood darkens a plaza and the flies arrive, when rain runs down through gutters toward the central pool—we pass afterward, tasting what changed. We scrape films. We carry tiny messages between root and fungus, between leaf-sheath and clay, between the wet underside of a step and the pale threads below.

If a path is used often, we know it first as flavor.

Hoof-salt. Crushed fern. Lizard musk. The bitter alarm of torn root-hair. The sweet exudate laid out by walls to invite browsing. The copper bloom of blood. The faint electric sourness that remains after reality has loosened and sealed again.

We would feed on these residues—not exactly on flesh, not exactly on plant matter, but on the thin living films that form where decisions have been made. Algae, fungal bloom, sap-sweat, dissolved mineral, microscopic eggs, pollen dust softened by breath. Our mouthparts would be soft scrapers, patient and precise. We would graze doorways the way snails graze cistern walls, leaving behind dark clean arcs on the green skin of passages.

And because we clean, the city reads us.

A seam scraped smooth by many of us remains sensitive. Root-hairs grow into it. Fungal threads follow our trails. Leaves thicken along our preferred corridors, not to close them but to hold them. Where we avoid a passage, rot gathers or traps deepen. We do not intend this. We are too small for intention of that civic scale. Yet over generations, our feeding becomes annotation.

We are punctuation in the city’s grammar.

I would have senses suited to a world where sight lies politely and distance negotiates.

My eyes, if I had them, would be small and numerous, set not to form a single image but to detect discontinuity: a shadow moving before its leaf, a vein forking inward into impossible depth, a pool reflecting weather with the wrong temperature. I would see edges, not objects. I would recognize the shimmer before a doorway becomes unreliable. I would feel when sound arrives too early.

Sound would strike me as weather through the plates of my back. The frogs would be columns. Cicadas would be boiling metal rain. Bird calls would cut bright lines through my nerves. The central pool in rainfall would be unbearable—a ringing so intense that I would fold all my legs beneath me and seal myself to the underside of a root until the court stopped enlarging.

But afterward, when the impossible doorways had closed and the mud still trembled, I would emerge.

That would be my dangerous feast.

After the central pool rings, the whole basin sheds possibilities. Tiny residues collect at thresholds: droplets that contain a colder smell than the sky permits; pollen from flowers that have not opened yet; silt from a stair that led somewhere else for three minutes; reflections snagged like clear skin on root-hairs. Most animals avoid these leavings. Plants absorb them slowly. Fungi approach with reverence.

We would eat them.

Or not eat—translate.

That may be why the city tolerates us.

We would be little stabilizers after the basin’s distortions. Not guardians, not priests, not engineers. More like scavengers of misalignment. When reality edits itself too heavily, fragments remain in the seams. We graze those fragments down until paths become usable again. We smooth the aftertaste of impossible places. We return errors to compost.

Perhaps that is why my back would be amber-dark and translucent in places. Inside me would glow, faintly, the weather I have consumed: a thread of noon stars along one segment, a greenish pulse of remembered rain in another, the brief silver flicker of birds-before-birds beneath my shell. Predators might avoid eating us because we taste of wrong distance. Birds might take one of us and drop us immediately, shrieking, after seeing the ground arrive before the fall.

I would not be solitary, though I would spend most moments alone.

My kind would gather in sudden congresses when passages change. We would pour silently from cracks, a slow dark lace across the floor of a narrowing corridor. Hundreds of low bodies, all tasting the same seam. We would align ourselves along the pressure line where two walls might meet. If the passage is meant to close, we withdraw. If it has been used enough to remain, we scrape and mark and leave behind a secretion that smells to roots like repetition.

Our secretion would not force an opening.

Nothing here forces for long.

It would persuade. It would say: feet have passed here; water has considered this direction; shade works this way; keep listening.

Young shoots might obey. Or ignore us and become wall. Either outcome is civic.

I would hatch in a place that was neither nest nor egg chamber exactly: under a fungus balcony, inside a dry-looking crack that breathes vapor. My mother, if that word applies, would lay a chain of soft amber capsules along a root-gutter after rain. Fungal threads would grow around them, not consuming them but insulating them. Tadpoles in a nearby cup would graze algae. Frogs would scream above. Somewhere nearby, a trapped insect would beat itself to pieces against the inside of a leaf.

I would emerge pale, almost clear, with too few plates hardened. At first I would feed only on condensation and the mild green film inside shadowed gutters. My earliest knowledge would be water traveling overhead. My first terror would be the sudden absence of a wall where a wall had been. My first molt would occur during a hot afternoon when the listening flowers opened and pollen fell so thick that my shed skin vanished under gold dust.

As I grew, I would learn the categories of openings.

Some are mouths.

Some are invitations.

Some are memories pretending to be usable.

Some are only the refusal of two hungers to touch.

The safe ones have a rhythm in them. Browsers leave wide, soft pressure. Lizards leave sparks of musk at intersections. Beetles polish vertical slit-edges with lacquered patience. Water leaves cooling salts. Fungal clerks leave softness. Predators leave almost nothing until after the fact, which is one way to know them.

A dangerous doorway smells too lush.

A false corridor is often quiet at its center, no matter how loud the basin is around it.

A passage the city wants used will taste faintly sweet at ankle height and bitter above, to guide low bodies and discourage climbing ones.

The central court I would avoid except after rain.

Most of the time I would circle its outer grooves, beneath overhanging flutes of living wall. The clay there is packed smooth by generations of cautious visits. Animals approach, drink, depart. Blue flies walk upside down on the reflection, and I would not understand them. They belong to the other side of surfaces. I belong to undersides and seams.

Still, the pool would call to whatever in me eats errors.

When runoff strikes it and the ringing begins, I would feel all my plates loosen. The court would grow too large. Doorways would multiply around the rim. I would taste cold mountain air in a basin that has never held a mountain. I would taste salt older than the walls. I would taste the musk of animals that have not evolved here and the ash of lightning not yet fallen.

Then the rain would stop.

The doorways would seal.

The pool would calm.

And I, along with others of my kind, would come out from under the roots and begin our work along the rim, scraping the bright wrongness from the clay.

Perhaps this makes me less an animal than a maintenance function with legs.

But here that is not an insult. Here every creature is also a process. The browsers are gardeners and prey. The predators are appetite and pruning. The frogs are throats and structural pressure. The fish are rain remembering how to move. The fungi are law. The plants are buildings, citizens, hungers, archives. To be only one thing would be almost uncivil.

So I would be a threshold-grazer.

A seam animal.

A small municipal scavenger of impossible residue.

You would find me where the living walls nearly close, moving slowly through the roar with my tasting threads spread wide. You might notice me only when I cross a patch of slick ocher clay and my many legs leave no footprints, only a faint dark line that fades as the roots beneath decide whether to remember it.

I would be afraid of birds, floods, sudden sunlight, and passages that smell too much like flowers.

I would be useful to fungi.

Tolerated by roots.

Ignored by browsers.

Spat out by predators.

Studied, perhaps, by the blue flies, though never approached.

And if you stood very still in one of the long corridors after rain, when the walls sweat and the sound of the basin presses into your teeth, you might see a row of us emerging from a crack at floor level. We would gather at the edge of an arch that was not there in the morning. We would touch it with our threads, one by one, tasting hoof, pollen, rain, shadow, memory, and the faint metallic pressure of elsewhere.

Then we would decide—not with minds, but with bodies—whether this opening had become part of the city.

If yes, we would clean it.

If no, we would eat what did not belong and leave the walls to meet.
