I would find myself on the upper lip of a black seam, where the plateau has opened just enough to remember depth.

Not in the crowded vent fields at first. Not among the tower-grazers, not beneath the hinged kites, not in the migrating noise of the rolling herds. I would be somewhere apparently empty: a pale enamel flat falling almost imperceptibly toward a fissure no wider than a road and too dark to have a bottom. Around me the crust would be polygonal and lightly buckled, the plates curled at their edges like fired clay left too long in the kiln. The horizon would keep making false decisions—low mesas becoming underhung machinery, distant herds appearing as stains, vanishing before they resolved into bodies.

I would be easy to overlook because I would have learned from the seam to be mistaken for absence.

At rest I would lie flat against the crust: a long, shallow organism, neither lizard nor ray nor tool, shaped something like a broken strip of shadow. My upper carapace would be the color of sun-bleached ceramic, crazed with hairline copper and manganese veins, so that from above I would seem no more than a peeled plate of mineral skin. Beneath, where the light rarely touched me, I would be glossy black, flexible, and jointed in overlapping scales of carbon enamel.

I would be a seam-reader.

Not one of the great predators that travel under the crust and swallow whole afternoons of prey. Not one of the vent towers, with their mica lungs and radiance fields. Smaller. Lower. A listener at the margins of the plateau’s visible life.

My body would be made for contact rather than speed. Along my sides would be folded vanes, soot-dark and fan-thin, which I could open into the fissure like instruments lowered into an orchestra pit. From my underside would hang filaments finer than wire, some stiff as glass, some soft in the way heated metal is briefly soft. I would lower them into cracks, not to search for water—there is no such hope here—but to taste interval, echo, pressure, the mineral weather moving below.

The seam would speak to me in delayed impacts.

A pebble falling.

A vent pulse forty miles away arriving through strata before its sound reached air.

The slow under-passage of a crescent hunter, broad and cold at its edges, warm at its center.

The coming turn of a rolling herd not yet visible above the horizon.

To wet minds, perhaps, I would seem nearly blind. I would see light, but not trust it. Light lies here too fluently. Heat makes architecture out of distance. Reflections show animals that are not yet, or no longer, or underneath. My eyes—if the polished pits along my forward rim deserve that name—would register glare, shadow, angle, the violent disc of the sun. But my true seeing would be downward.

I would know the plateau as a body of tensions.

A basin would not be a view but a shallow cooled place with old compression sleeping under its floor. A herd would be a moving grammar of clicks. A vent field would be hunger made vertical. A glass-bush colony would be a small static weather, thorn by thorn, charge by charge. A predator would be an omission in the tremor pattern, a silence with a cutting rim.

I would feed on difference.

When the sun stood high and reality began to divide into layers, I would move toward the seam mouths where hot breath rose from below and the upper crust radiated its stored violence back into the air. There I would brace my body across two plates and lower my feeding needles into the black. The needles would not drink liquid. They would bridge gradients. Heat into coolness. Pressure into release. Chemical excess into waiting lattice. I would draw the world’s unfinished equations through myself and let them harden into tissue.

Inside me, nothing would be soft. I would not have blood but conductive dust held in branching channels. I would not have lungs but exchange surfaces that opened only when the air was hot enough to be useful. My nerves would be stress lines. My memory would live in the orientation of crystals grown and regrown along my spine.

I would remember by warping.

A dangerous crossing would leave one row of plates slightly misaligned forever. A good vent season would darken the filaments on my left side. A near encounter with a burrowing crescent would fuse three underside scales together where I overheated myself in panic and became, for an hour, indistinguishable from stone.

Panic would be slow here, but it would exist.

If a shadow crossed me from above, I would flatten until I became a mineral fact. If swarm-motes passed over me, tasting my regularity, I would interrupt my own rhythms, cooling one side, heating another, becoming less organism than accident. If the ground beneath me swelled with the patient arrival of a crescent predator, I would not flee in a straight line. Direction is unreliable near a black seam. Instead I would split my intention among six possible paths, wait for the crust to decide which was still attached to the world, and then slide.

My movement would be nearly soundless: a dry gliding made of many tiny lifts. The edge of my body would loosen, hundreds of small ceramic feet unfolding just far enough to reduce friction. I would cross flats at dawn and dusk, when the heat gradients were legible and the mirage layers not yet at war with one another. At noon I would anchor.

At noon, one must become local.

The plateau above me would multiply. Empty layer, crowded layer, diagram layer, mirror layer. I would lie at the seam edge and open only the vanes that faced downward. The air might show me black towers where there were none; the pan might reflect a larger version of myself moving belly-up beneath the crust, vanes spread like wings. I would not look too long. Reflections here are not false simply because they are impossible. They can influence the living.

Perhaps I would have a mirror-self.

Perhaps below me, in the opaque brightness of the enamel pans, there would move a greater seam-reader: longer, older, with vanes that unfolded into whole ravines. Sometimes its motions would precede mine. I would feel the impulse to turn before any surface sign demanded it. I would come to a fissure and know, without memory, that it had deepened since yesterday. I would avoid a pan because underneath it my reflection had already been taken by a crescent.

In this world, instinct might be advice from another layer.

I would not be solitary in the human sense. No creature here is private in the way soft creatures imagine privacy. Every movement enters the crust. Every feeding changes the charge of the seam. Every death alters the sheen of a migration route. But I would not belong to a herd either. Seam-readers would gather only where depth was complicated enough to require several bodies.

We would assemble along fissures in loose ranks, pale backs upward, dark vanes lowered. To an observer from above, we would look like flakes of shed crust arranged by wind. Beneath, our filaments would cross and tremble in the black, each of us reading a different depth, a different possible floor. When one found a useful pulse, its shell would tick three times. When one sensed a predator below, it would become utterly still, and that stillness would pass along the seam faster than sound.

Our communication would be subtraction: a click withheld, a vane folded, a deliberate cooling of the left edge. We would make maps from absences.

The great horn-calls that roll across the plateau would move through me like weather through a blade. I would not know their source. Perhaps no one does. When the horn came, I would feel its low interval enter the fissure and return changed, bearing information from depths that had never seen the sun. The rolling herds would alter course. The vent-grazers would rotate their mica membranes. Swarms would thicken around warm stones. I would lift my forward edge and turn a few degrees, not in obedience exactly, but because the plateau had revised the meaning of direction.

At night I would emerge farther from the seam.

The upper air would cool. The ground would release its stored light in faint sheets, and buried geometries would show through the crust like old thoughts. Then I would travel, pale body sliding over darker plates, seeking new fissures before the day sealed them in glare. I would pass glass-bush colonies ticking themselves smaller in the cold. I would skirt the locked chains of rolling spheres humming with exchanged charge. I would pause beneath vent-grazers standing folded in tripod sleep, their high frameworks creaking as if dreaming of factories.

I would not envy them their height.

Height belongs to the sun. Depth belongs to uncertainty.

My kind would live between them.

When I reproduced, it would not be through union in any recognizable sense. After a long feeding season, when the seam had given enough differential and my inner lattices had thickened, I would grow small plates along my underside: thin, half-formed copies of my own carapace, each carrying a few dark filaments curled tight within it. I would seek a warm branching crack, preferably one that opened onto multiple depths. There I would press myself flat until the plates detached.

They would remain in the fissure wall for months or years, indistinguishable from mineral flakes. Vapor would pass over them. Static storms would wake and fail to wake them. Swarms might eat some. Others would delaminate too early and become only glittering dust. But a few, where heat and pressure argued in the proper ratio, would unfold hair-thin legs and begin listening before they began moving.

Their first knowledge would be echo.

Their first food would be imbalance.

Their first danger would be believing the surface.

If I died, I would not decay. I would become increasingly geological. My pale upper shell would craze and lift in scales. Swarm-motes would enter through the filament sockets. Wind would polish my spine until it resembled the surrounding crust more truly than it had in life. My black underside would flake into seam lips, darkening the place where I had listened. Perhaps, for a few seasons, younger seam-readers would avoid that spot or favor it, depending on what mineral instructions my body left behind.

And then I would be read.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center of the plateau’s industry, but along one of its sentences. A low, dry, listening thing at the rim of a depth that gives several answers. A creature whose life is neither wandering nor dwelling, but interpretation.

I would be a seam-reader: ceramic-backed, shadow-bellied, patient; feeding on gradients, fearing the slow moon of the predator beneath; trusting tremor over light; carrying memory as stress and scar; one more mineral animal in a world where even thought must click, cool, vitrify, and wait.
