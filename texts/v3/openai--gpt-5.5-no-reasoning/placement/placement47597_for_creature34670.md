You would find me where the new cracks have already stopped looking new.

Not at the center of one of the older growth-cities, where the columnar stems stand shoulder to shoulder and the corridors have been polished by centuries of cautious passage. Not out on the exposed flats either, where the salt crust lies bright and the wind has too much room. I would be in the in-between place: a district that began as an injury in the young plain and has become almost a neighborhood.

A long fracture runs there, shallow and crooked, its edges lifted just enough to cast two fingers of morning shade. The raw undersoil, once exposed as pale clay and powdered mineral, is now stitched over with crust-life. White lichens rim the fracture like dried foam. Black-green films darken the deepest seams. Orange stains bloom where salts seep upward. Thorn seedlings have rooted in the broken lip, and low wax-mats have crept over the shoulders of the crack, fastening the loose ground with a patience more architectural than botanical.

Above it all stand several dead stems.

They are not tall by the standards of forests, but in that low crowded world they serve as towers. Their living skins split seasons ago. Their pith has been tunneled, their ribs hollowed, their crowns broken into thorned baskets. Yet they are occupied from base to tip. Beetles move in the old channels. A pair of small quiet birds nests in a side hollow lined with root hair and shed reptile skin. Dry fungal fans fold and unfold in the dawn along the shaded side. Filaments hang from the upper forks like smoke that decided to become fiber.

That is where I would be.

Not one of the great hoarders of water, not a grazer, not a long-legged browser carrying a map of shade routes in its bones. I would be something smaller and more embedded: a pale, narrow-bodied caretaker-insect living inside the dead column stem at the edge of the fracture.

I would be one of the sorters.

My kind would not own the tower, because nothing here truly owns anything for long. But we would occupy it thoroughly. We would move through a honeycomb of old borer tunnels and chambers sealed with chewed wax, mineral dust, and plant resin. We would have no bright colors, no singing voice, no display meant for distance. My shell would be matte beige-gray, the color of the chalk ground in shadow. My legs would be jointed for gripping dry pith and rough inner bark. My antennae would be long, constantly tasting the corridor air: salt, larva, fungus, bird feather, reptile musk, opening flower, fresh wound, dangerous breath.

I would be small enough that the city’s architecture is enormous.

A lifted scale of bark would be a roof.

A crack in the stem would be an avenue.

A bird dropping would be an event.

A fallen flower pore, waxy and fragrant, would be a public harvest.

My body would be built around thrift. I would carry little free moisture. My gut would be slow and careful, lined with microbes inherited from the nest, able to break down dry pith, fungal skin, pollen dust, shed plant wax, flakes of dung, dead larvae, and the translucent scraps of reptile skin that catch on thorns after shedding. I would not drink often. I would take water as a trace inside other things: in fungus folded before dawn, in fresh eggshell membrane, in the softer centers of seeds, in the breath-damp of crowded chambers.

By night and dawn, I would emerge.

Not far. Far is dangerous. My world would be measured in stem-lengths, root-shadow, fracture-edge, and the nearest corridor where dew-lickers pass.

At dawn the colony would loosen. Valves in the wax-mats below would open, and the entire district would exhale faint chemical invitations. The hanging fibers would stiffen to comb the air. Dew-lickers would come with their delicate tongues, cleaning invisible films from stems and stones. Their feet would leave readable traces: salt, oil, fear, kinship, old routes. We would follow after them, not to steal from their mouths, but to harvest what they loosened. A flake of mineral crust turned over by a tongue. A mite crushed under a padded foot. A grain of dung-dust caught in the lichen fringe.

My colony would live by removing what cannot be left unused.

We would cut dead fungus into fibers and drag them inward.

We would collect fallen scales from wax-plants and stack them in dry chambers, sorted by bitterness.

We would carry the husks of pollinators after evening bloom.

We would clean the edges of bird nests, eating the spoiled fragments before parasites multiplied too freely.

We would chew passages through old pith that roots could later enter.

We would bury our own dead in mineral dust and fungal thread, not from reverence as a human would understand it, but because even a dead worker is a storehouse that must be returned correctly.

Our nest would be one layer in the tower’s many governments.

Above us, the birds would sleep in silence, their chicks trained early to stillness. Below us, blind larvae would scrape at the softened interior. In one abandoned chamber, a dry spider would keep a tension-web strung across the passage, and we would know that turning by scent and by the absence of traffic. A small reptile might use the sun-warmed exterior of the stem at midmorning, pressing its flat body against the pale surface, eyes half-lidded, pretending to be cracked bark. At the base, crust-organisms would be turning the tower’s fallen powder into soil thin enough for a thorn seedling to believe in.

I would know the world by touch more than sight.

The city would speak through vibration. A browser pushing between columnar stems would arrive as a slow, heavy shudder through root and ground. A predator waiting near the corridor pinch would be known by the sudden absence of dew-licker foot-tremors. Winged beetles gathering for evening emergence would make the tower hum from within. A new crack opening in the cooling dark would travel through the whole district as a soft report, followed by a rearrangement of scents: raw mineral, buried salt, root alarm, fungal opportunity.

I would not think of the basin as vast.

I would not know continent.

I would know route, chamber, shade, seam, safe hour, unsafe hour.

I would know that the pale open strip beyond the fracture is death unless crossed under cover of dusk.

I would know that the waxwood quarter smells resin-bitter after grazing and that the salt-bush lanes bring mites in abundance.

I would know that the mineral vent two corridors away is rich and dangerous, a plaza where every hunger eventually intersects.

I would know that when the hanging fibers turn silver before dawn, there will be enough trace moisture in the air to risk widening the outer galleries.

If I had anything like a vocation, it would be conversion.

Not creation, not command. Conversion.

Dead pith into tunnel.

Dung-dust into fungus bed.

Shed skin into salts.

Bird refuse into chamber lining.

Broken stem into neighborhood.

New crack into usable passage.

I would be one of the citizens that makes the place city-like without intending a city. My movements would help keep corridors open at the smallest scale. My colony’s waste would feed films that feed mites that feed reptiles that feed larger hunters whose bones one day feed lichens at the base of the same tower. I would be municipal without knowing municipality: a sorter, cleaner, archivist, undertaker, builder-by-removal.

At noon I would withdraw deep into the stem.

The outside world would whiten. The plants would seal. Reptiles would take the visible lanes. Predators would wait in the places where traffic narrows. Inside, the colony would compress around its guarded dampness. We would close resin doors. We would slow. We would pass food mouth to mouth, not generously but efficiently, each exchange measured by need and caste and chemistry. The air would be warm, close, dry but not lethal. In the deepest chamber, fungal patches would be tended under a ceiling darkened by generations of breath.

Toward evening, we would open again.

The flowers hidden in grooves along nearby stems would release scent, heavy and low, rolling through the corridors like invisible smoke. Pollinators would come: matte-winged, quiet, dust-colored. Some would be caught by spiders. Some by lizards. Some would fall stunned after mating. We would wait beneath the traffic and gather what dropped. A wing. A husk. A dusting of pollen. One torn leg still rich with trace fluids.

Nothing wasted.

If danger came, it would come close and sudden. A thief-beetle from another tower. A probing bird beak. A burrowing reptile splitting the base chambers open with sealed nostrils and blunt armored head. A parasitic fly laying its young in the dampest brood cells. There would be no heroic defense, only chemical alarm, blockage, evacuation through secondary tunnels, sacrifice by those nearest the breach. Survival would be architectural and collective. No individual body would matter much except as a carrier of use.

And yet I would belong intensely.

The dead column would be my district. The fracture my frontier. The lichen lip my market. The root-shadow my weather. The dawn condensation, too slight for a human finger to feel, would be a season of plenty. The cooling crack of young stone at night would be news from the world’s deep body.

I would live in a city without knowing it is a city, among citizens who do not know they are citizens.

I would be a small dry custodian in the tower of a dead wax-stem, pale and quiet, sorting the remains of abundance in a land where abundance means that every remnant has already been claimed.
