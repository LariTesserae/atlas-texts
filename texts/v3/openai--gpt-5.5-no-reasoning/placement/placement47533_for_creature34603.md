I think I would find myself near the central factory hall, but not inside its obvious center.

Not among the belts at first—not where the trays pass in their slow civic procession of moss and scrap and larvae and eggs—but just beyond them, in the warm margin where the moving parts become architecture and the architecture becomes root. There is a place beside the old conveyor line where a maintenance alcove has been nearly swallowed. Its original purpose can still be guessed from the geometry: a recessed panel, a waist-high shelf, a gridded drain, the remains of a red emergency box behind a veil of liverwort. But none of it belongs to emergency anymore. The shelf is barked over. The drain breathes vapor. The panel is sealed by a fungus the color of old parchment, veined with green.

That is where I would be.

A threshold thing.

Not one of the great shy animals, not the many-legged mother, not one of the root systems large enough to revise streets. I would be smaller than that, but not insignificant. Something that lives where categories rub together and become useful.

At first I want to say I would be a person there: a cautious human descendant in bark sandals, lamp hooded, palms stained with spores, knowing the etiquette of rails and flowers. Someone who keeps calendars of pump cycles, who trades salt to the soot-rats and sleeps in a root-built room under lantern-fruit. But that feels too separate, too narrating. A person would still stand slightly apart from the hollow, even if beloved by it. A person would ask what things are called.

I think I would be something that does not need names first.

Perhaps I am an animal about the size of a cat, though longer, lower, more jointed in the shoulders. A warm-blooded scavenger-pollinator-messenger, descended from something ordinary and urban: rat, civet, ferret, possum, stray thing. My body has accepted the station plan. My spine bends easily through service gaps. My feet know cable, tile, moss, pipe, root, and the treacherous give of fungal crust. I have whiskers not only around the muzzle but along my forelegs and ribs, fine pale filaments that taste vibration and current. In electrical night they lift from my skin like grass in weather.

My fur is dark where the soot gathers, but not black. It is mottled rust-brown, green-gray, amber at the throat. Spores lodge in it; mites live there; two kinds of tiny beetle clean my ears and take payment in flakes of wax. Along my sides are shallow folds where seeds catch. This is not accidental. Some plants fruit for mouths, some for hooves, some for flowing water. Some fruit for bodies like mine: bodies that go everywhere but never straight, bodies that visit warm cabinets, dry alcoves, damp sump edges, root nurseries, machine housings, and abandoned offices in the same night.

I am not domesticated, but I am employed.

The hollow has many employments that are not assigned. Mine is to pass. To carry. To sample. To remember safe intervals between one pulse and another.

I wake before the sweating hour, curled inside the old maintenance alcove behind the parchment fungus. The alcove is lined with shredded insulation, old moth wings, vine-skin, and the hair of my kin. Above me, a pipe clicks as heat enters it. The fungus at the entrance tightens its pores. The conveyor begins before the birds do: a shudder first, then the belt’s thick, slapping patience. Somewhere a mechanical arm wakes and descends. The birds inside its shoulder scream outrage at the weather they nest in.

I open my mouth and taste the morning.

Oil. Warm moss. A current leak to the left. Rat colony active above the third girder. Narcotic flowers still closed. Rain not yet. A grazer herd has passed in the night; their flank moss left burrs along the tile seam. The many-legged mother has eaten recently; her hall smells metallic and calm.

My eyes are large, but sight is only one of the minor senses here. Light lies. Lamps are mimicked. Glows persuade. Cracks of grayness from above move too slowly to trust. I navigate by tremor, odor, draft, charge, memory, and the particular pressure of air around openings. A corridor breathes differently from a dead end. A hungry flower makes a silence around itself. A safe root has a taste like wet bark and iron. An unsafe root tastes awake.

I leave the alcove when the first condensation begins to bead on the girders.

The factory hall is already in motion. Trays pass with their mixed cargoes. Moss cushions, larvae knots, white threads of fungus, bits of colored wire, egg clusters pearled with moisture, seedlings leaning toward nothing visible. The mechanical arms sort them according to an extinct instruction. One arm lowers, hesitates, pinches a mat of blue-green mold, lifts it three inches, and drops it onto a tray of beetle husks. This has happened for generations. The mold now fruits only after being bruised by that particular gripper. Its spores smell briefly of bruised apple and hot dust.

I pause beneath the arm, but not directly beneath. I know its circle.

My forepaws rest on a rail polished by many crossings. I wait for the pulse. It comes irregularly, but never without warning if one has ribs enough to hear it. A faint tightening in the copper vines. A metallic sweetness in the teeth. The mites vanish into cracks. Then the rail wakes blue-white for half a breath, and a vine beside it opens three flowers in rapid sequence, each with a tiny electrical sigh.

Afterward I cross.

In my cheek pouches I carry three things: a seed shaped like a black tear, a flake of salt stolen from the masonry near the old restrooms, and the foreleg of a cricket-machine insect whose shell has grown too much like enamel. None of these are mine in the human sense. They are in transit. The salt will buy passage through rat territory or distract a young guard. The seed belongs in a cabinet behind the switchboard where heat persists after the fans stop. The insect leg I will eat unless something more strategic presents itself.

I pass the kiosk row next.

The thin pollinating mammals are finishing their shift. They move with priestly efficiency, faces blackened by nectar, tongues withdrawn into their skulls like measuring tapes. One sees me and freezes with all four feet on a broken sign. We know each other’s kinds. We are not enemies unless made so by hunger or nursery panic. I lower my head, not in submission exactly, but to show my mouth is carrying and not hunting. It blinks once. Behind it, green lantern-fruit pulse in the kiosk gloom.

I do not enter.

Those fruits are not for me this morning. They smell of warm milk, which means they are lying to mammals larger than the kiosk folk. Their vine has been narrowing the rear passage all season. Something will eventually follow that smell too far and feed the roots.

Past the kiosks the air changes from damp to dry in six steps. Spores lift around my ankles. The tile here is exposed, old cream squares cracked into islands by liverwort. This is ant country. Their covered roads cross the lane in raised brown veins made of paper pulp, resin, and copper dust. A patrol emerges through a small arch. Their soldiers’ heads flash with chips of blue paint.

I stop.

The ants are citizens of procedure. They do not forgive trampling, but they appreciate predictable tribute. I set down the cricket-machine leg. Two workers touch it. A soldier touches me. Its antennae taste my whiskers, my cheek pouch, the burrs caught in my side-fur. It finds no fresh ant-death on me. This matters. After a moment the patrol parts, and I step over their road with exaggerated care.

The seed in my cheek is growing warm.

It is one of the black-leaf seeds, copper-veined, heavy for its size. Its parent grew above a sleeping compressor and opened only when the belt rhythm faltered. I took the seed from a fruit split by geckos. Or perhaps the fruit arranged itself to be split where I would find it. These distinctions are for creatures with law courts. Here there are consequences.

The switchboard cabinet waits behind a cage of rust and root. Blue arcs blink inside it like small trapped weather. The old warning placard has become a gecko nursery; five eggs cling to the back, soft white against flaking red letters. I squeeze through a gap no grazer, no child, no rat elder with swollen flanks could use. My ribs fold. My whiskers flatten. The seed presses against my tongue.

Inside, it is hot and almost dry. Perfect.

The cabinet is full of old contacts polished by ants, beetle dust, dead moths, and the pale roots of a vine that learned geometry from wiring diagrams. I spit the seed into a corner where condensation from an overhead bolt falls once every forty-seven breaths during the sweating hour. Not always forty-seven. Nothing living counts that way. But my body knows the interval as a patience.

I press the seed down with my nose until it lodges between insulation floss and corroded copper.

For this I receive nothing immediate.

That is also citizenship.

When I emerge, the rain has started.

Not rain everywhere. Never everywhere. It begins above the rail trench as a shiver of drops from ceiling roots, then spreads in broken curtains across the factory hall. Animals adjust without discussion. Birds leave the arm housings and fling themselves into the damp. Rats descend in braided streams from cable runs, each trying to drink without yielding position. A grazer calf hidden in a stairwell calls once and is answered by a low sound from the lane beyond. Leaves tilt. Moss darkens. Dust becomes scent.

I climb.

The best water is not on the floor. The floor water is already claimed by drains, crabs, larvae, fungal films, and hooves. I go up a root-column twisted around an old support, claws fitting into habitual scars. Halfway up, a gecko lunges at the same drop I wanted. Its body is translucent, green organs flickering before an amber bulb. We collide softly, both offended, neither injured. It gets the drop. I get the next.

Above the hall, among the lower pipes, the civic sound gathers around me.

The belts slap. The fans begin to consider waking. The many-legged mother strikes something in the ticket hall; her turnstiles ring once, then still. A herd moves through the old lower market, hooves pressing seedpods open. The boiler creature shifts in its tank, water booming against metal. Ants seal a road against rain. Flowers open to the compressor’s cough. Somewhere the green lamps brighten along a narrowing path.

I cling to the warm pipe and feel the whole hollow through my feet.

This is how I think, if what I do can be called thinking: by comparing tremors to remembered tremors, scents to outcomes, intervals to hungers. I do not possess a map, but I am partly made of routes. My mother carried me by the scruff through the dry tiles, the rail trench, the root-built rooms, the shaft ledges where condensation falls in long, solemn notes. Her mother did the same before her. Some of our paths are older than our species. Some were human maintenance paths. Some were rat roads before we used them. Some were made by roots encouraging us where they wanted droppings. We inherit all of them without needing to know which is which.

By midday I go toward the shaft.

Everyone is drawn there eventually. Not because it is escape. Escape is an upper-city idea. The shaft is exchange: draft, scent, spores, occasional gray light, falling insects, rumors of weather beyond the sealed bulkheads. It is the hollow’s throat.

The way to it passes near the ticket hall, and near the ticket hall one must be correct.

The many-legged mother is visible today only as portions: two polished limbs hooked around a turnstile, the brass-dark curve of her carapace behind webbed signage, mandibles moving delicately over something pale. Her young cling inside the bars in clusters, bead eyes watching all traffic. Her webbing holds the cracked ceiling in place. It is not metaphor. Without her repair, the tile arch would have fallen seasons ago and blocked the grazer route, which would have starved the roots beyond, which would have dried the nursery where the kiosk mammals sleep, which would have sent predators into the factory hall in leaner numbers. She eats the unwary. She also maintains a district.

I slow before her hall.

From my side-fur I comb loose a burr with my teeth. It is a grazer-borne seedpod, armed with hooks and pressure-memory. I set it at the edge of her webbing, where young can practice cutting without danger. One of her forelimbs lifts. Her attention passes over me like shade.

I am allowed through.

Beyond the ticket hall the sound opens vertically. The shaft rises into darkness. Pipes climb its walls, furred with vines. Animals move above animals: rats on cable, geckos on tile, crabs in the wet seam below, birds launching badly from bracket to bracket, hunters folded higher where the electrical night will favor them. Condensation drops from heights invisible and lands on leaves with resonant, single notes.

I climb until the air cools—not cold, never cold, but less held.

Here, in a niche behind a pipe clamp, is another place I use. Many of us use it. Not a nest. A ledger of passages. The wall is stained with musk, pollen, mineral rub, the scratch marks of claws. Information accumulates here because bodies return. I add my own: the black seed planted; ant road open; green lamp vine active near kiosks; rail pulse early; many-legged mother fed; rain moderate; boiler creature not yet emerged.

No words. Better than words. Words can be false by themselves. Scent must be paid for by having been somewhere.

For a while I rest with my belly against the pipe and let the draft move through my fur.

This is when I almost become something else.

The place does not keep identities hard. While I sleep, mites travel from my shoulder to the wall. Spores caught in my coat loosen and enter the shaft air. A seed burr I failed to remove drops into a crack where warm water sometimes threads downward. Beetles clean the salt from my whiskers. My breath feeds a small black leaf growing upside down beneath the pipe. In return, its surface warms slightly when current passes through the cable behind it, and that warmth keeps my paws from stiffening.

I am animal, yes. But I am also corridor. Carrier. Weather instrument. Pollination accident. Seed judgment. Moving niche.

If I die here, I will not leave the city. That is one of the comforts of the hollow. Death is not removal but reassignment. My body would first call flies, then mites, then roots. Ants would take the eyes if the geckos did not. Fungus would enter through the mouth. A plant might grow from the seed lodged in my gut or fur. My bones would soften in the damp or dry white in a cabinet. A young rat might gnaw them for mineral. Perhaps a root would thicken around my skull and make a small step in a passage used by others. I would become a convenience.

But not yet.

The fans engage.

Drying comes hard through the lanes. The shaft exhales. Spores rise below in visible weather, clouds crossing amber light. Winged aphids lift around lamp housings. Birds fling themselves into the draft and become briefly competent. Thin plants flatten to masonry. Seedcases rattle. Somewhere, as the engines change rhythm, the yellow hanging roots stiffen and lash the last moisture from the air. A cry follows—small, mammalian, cut short. The rules are old and practical, but not everyone remembers them in time.

I descend by another route.

At electrical night I hunt.

My eyes matter more then, but my whiskers matter most. Charge crawls through the place. Filaments pulse along vines. Switchboards glitter. Predators come down from the high pipes, their faces dark mirrors. I avoid their main paths but follow in their consequence. A hunter drops and misses; prey scatter; one young rat breaks toward a tight drain and leaves behind the fruit it was carrying. I take the fruit. It is shaped for a rodent mouth, but I have learned to crack it against a bolt.

Inside are three seeds and a bitter pulp full of stimulant.

For a while the hollow sharpens. Every drip stands apart. Every machine has an outline of heat and intent. The fungal panels on the brick show their slow ledgers: rings of wet years, scars of blocked corridors, a dark band where silence once opened too many flowers. I pass a wall where old graffiti has been eaten into halos. I do not know the human marks, but the pigments nourish different microbes, and the microbes smell like history.

Near the quieter arch, I see the root-built rooms.

Sometimes there are humans there. Not always where one expects. Tonight there is one: small, wrapped in patched damp cloth, crouched beside a bench grown from a patient trunk. Their lamp is hooded red. Their hair is bound back with vine fiber. They hold very still as I enter the edge of the room.

We know each other also.

Humans are dangerous because they decide at a distance. But the ones who remain below have learned to decide slowly. This one sets something on the root-bench: a flake of salt, larger than any I could steal alone.

Payment. Request. Apology. All three perhaps.

Beside the salt is a seed I recognize: green-lamp vine, immature, still soft. Bad to plant near dwelling paths. Worse to discard where rats may carry it. The human wants it removed.

I take the salt first. Not all of it; that would be rude or foolish. I take enough to show bargain. Then I take the seed gently in my front teeth. It tastes of persuasion. Even unripe, it suggests a safe corridor, a narrowing warmth, the relief of going forward.

I carry it far.

Past the dry tiles, where ants will not want it. Past the ticket hall, where the mother’s young click in their turnstiles. Past the rail trench during a dead interval. Past the conveyor belts, now bearing night cargo: pale grubs, black moss, fragments of bone sorted from scrap by the tender obsolete arms. I carry it to a flooded service tunnel sealed at the far end by collapse and root. There the crabs prune aggressively, and anything that cannot bargain with water becomes food.

I drop the seed into a drain channel.

A crab emerges, sidewise and practical, touches it, dislikes it, cuts it in half anyway. The smell of false safety dies.

This, too, is citizenship.

When I return to my alcove, the hollow is still loud, still becoming. The engines grind in their resigned cycles. The belts slap. The fungi breathe through walls. A grazer herd settles in the lower market, their flank moss full of tomorrow’s seeds. The boiler creature hauls itself back to its tank, leaving wet prints already alive with insects. The many-legged mother repairs a strand. The human’s red lamp goes out behind the root-built doorway. The black seed in the switchboard cabinet waits for its forty-seven breaths of water.

I curl behind the parchment fungus and tuck my nose under my tail.

Where am I in this world?

In the warm margin beside the factory hall. In the routes between districts. In the small permissions by which a place remains passable.

What am I?

A carrier of seeds, salt, warnings, and consequences.  
A low citizen of the city-plant-engine-swarm.  
A body that knows machinery as weather, condensation as water, architecture as inherited bone.  
Not ruler, not witness, not intruder.

One of the ones who pass through and, by passing, help the hollow continue.
