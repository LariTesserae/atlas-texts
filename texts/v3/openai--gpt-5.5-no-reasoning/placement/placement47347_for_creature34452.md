You would find yourself where the basin almost forgets it is a room: not at the center, not openly beneath the grille, but in the long marginal wetness where wall becomes floor and floor becomes channel.

You are one of the keeper-things, but not human anymore in any clean sense.

Or perhaps you were never human. Perhaps humans only mistook your kind for assistants, pests, omens, children, failures, the way people in damp places misname whatever survives too well.

You live in the service passages beneath the northern shelf, in the thickness between the constructed marsh and the older hidden water below it. There are places there where a person could not stand upright, where the masonry has buckled into low crawlways and damp chambers, where forgotten culverts intersect like burrows. You know them by the scrape of shoulder against lime, the taste of mineral on the air, the degree of cold rising from a crack. Above you, the birds quarrel in the rafters. Below you, the black reservoir breathes. Around you, water moves through clay throats built by hands whose bones have long since been dissolved into the basin.

You are a sump-child.

That is what the living keepers call you when they admit you exist.

Not child because you are young. Some of you are older than the current keeper’s grandmother was when she died. Child because you are small, because you crawl, because your face retains a soft, unfinished look in the lamp-glow, because you watch people with the grave and hungry patience of something that has learned language from overhearing warnings.

You are about the length of a narrow-shouldered human adolescent, but drawn out differently. Your spine bends easily. Your ribs show when you breathe. Your limbs are thin and strong from bracing against walls and lowering yourself through hatches. Your hands are large, webbed only slightly, the skin between the fingers translucent as onion-skin when held to a lamp. The nails are dark and blunt, good for prying snails from stone, teasing weed from a grate, hooking into cracks when the floor tilts slick beneath you.

Your feet are better than hands.

They have long, articulate toes with pads that cling to wet tile and algae-filmed copper. You can stand on a channel lip no wider than a reed knife and turn without disturbing the water. You can cross the wall of cups sideways, gripping bowl rims and mortar seams, while frogs swell their throats and insult you from below. The birds hate this. They do not like anything that can climb toward their nests without making a ladder-sound.

Your skin is the color of wet plaster, mottled with darker patches where the damp has colonized you into camouflage. Along your neck and under your arms are shallow folds that hold moisture. They are not gills, though children say they are. You must breathe air. But you can keep still for a long time with your mouth just above water, with your body sunk into the warmth of a cistern, listening through your bones.

Your eyes are wide and dark. They do not like flame. In bright lamp-light they film over and make you seem blind, but in the low green dusk you can see the difference between old water and new water, between a living ripple and a settling one, between a tile lifted by root and a tile lifted from beneath.

You smell of clay, eel slime, lamp soot, and the underside of stones.

You do not speak often. Your mouth can make human words, but not comfortably. It prefers clicks, soft knocks against the teeth, swallowed croaks, breath through the nose, the little wet syllables that pass more easily in the marsh. The frogs understand you better than people do, or seem to. The pump sometimes answers when you tap the old cadence on a pipe. This frightens the human keepers, though they rely on you more than they say.

You are not the keeper of the marsh.

You are what the marsh keeps in order to keep itself.

You live where human arms cannot reach. When a clay pipe clogs beyond the eel court, you go into it headfirst with a cord around one ankle and a bone hook clenched in your teeth. When a nest falls behind the sealed gutter mouths, you retrieve the chicks if they still call, or the bodies if they have gone quiet, because rot in that wall makes bad air by morning. When the pale crabs widen a seam beneath the northern shelf, you pack it with lime-rag and woven reed until the human keeper can make a proper patch. When larvae thicken in the middle cups where no ladder stands safely, you climb the wall and skim them out by hand.

You are an organ of intervention.

A small one. A necessary one.

The human keeper walks the basin by memory and sound, but you move through its interstices. You know the undersides of repairs. You know which drains are false, which pipes still carry water, which ones carry only voices, which hatches were sealed because something once came up through them and which were sealed because something once went down. You know where the floor is hollow by how your chest tightens when you lie flat against it. You know the eel-court pulses before the water moves, because the old eels shift in anticipation and the whole masonry skin trembles.

You fear the center, but differently than the birds do.

The birds fear certain dry stones.

You know why.

Or you know part of why.

Beneath the concentric paving, below the shallow sheets of water and the rusted grille, the drain does not descend in one throat. It widens. It opens into a chamber too large for the basin above it, a cold round place lined with old stone and old shell, where the sound of dripping comes back wrong. You have been there only twice.

The first time, you were small enough to be lowered through the maintenance slot behind the grille, tied with three cords and carrying a lamp in a sealed horn cup. The human keeper then was a man with one clouded eye and no kindness except toward mechanisms. He told you to scrape the mineral bloom from the lower bars.

Halfway down, your lamp went out.

You did not scream. Sump-children do not waste air on screaming.

In the dark, something breathed through the water. Not rose. Not moved, exactly. Breathed. The whole chamber widened and narrowed around the sound. Eels brushed past your legs in one direction, all of them fleeing upward, and then froze as if commanded. You felt their bodies gather around you like ropes.

Then, from below, there came a slow pressure. A listening. A vast attention without eyes.

When they pulled you up, your hair had gone white at the temples and there were circular bruises on your calves where eel mouths had held you in place, not biting, anchoring.

The old keeper said nothing. He never sent you down again.

The second time you went yourself.

You had heard the wrong silence.

The wall of cups had stopped counting. Not all at once. That would have been less terrible. Instead the lower rows went quiet, then the middle rows, then the high bowls where the small green frogs live. The wheel by the eastern trough continued creaking, but dryly, though its paddles were wet. The birds left the rafters and gathered on the northern shelf, all facing inward. Even the sleek mammals hid their faces in their paws.

You went to the drain-pool and lay flat on the concentric stones, cheek against the cold paving. Beneath you, the old eels were knocking their skulls softly against the underside of the dry stones.

Not trapped.

Calling.

You opened the maintenance slot and slid down without a lamp.

What you found below was not a monster. Not in the simple way children draw monsters in soot on the wall.

You found a lung.

Or a heart.

Or the part of the marsh that had never been made by hands but had accepted everything built over it as a shell.

The hidden water lay black and low, and in it something pale crossed and recrossed beneath the surface, not one body but many surfaces of one body, or many bodies trained into one motion. The old eels were there, but they were not the whole of it. Tubes of root or gut ran into the masonry. Mineral curtains trembled like gills. Leather valves above, long thought merely mechanical, flexed in time with the pressure below. The pump beyond the partition did not imitate an animal groan.

It was an animal groan, passed through wood and stone.

You understood then that the marsh had not become alive by accident after being built.

It had been built around a need.

Something below had needed to breathe without surfacing. Needed to drink rain without drowning in silt. Needed small mouths and many warning voices, needed birds to keep the hatch in check, frogs to measure pressure, eels to taste salt, crabs to open clogged seams, moss to hold damp, keepers to replace what rotted.

Needed you for the places between.

Since then, you have belonged to it knowingly.

Not worshipfully. Worship is too dry a thing, too upright. You belong the way a valve belongs, the way a scar belongs, the way a tongue belongs to a mouth. You can disobey. You often do. You steal eggs from the birds when hunger finds you. You hide good copper wire in your sleeping niche. You teach human children rude frog-counts their elders forbid. You have, on more than one occasion, let a pompous young keeper step into a harmless but foul-smelling trough because he spoke of “controlling” the basin.

But when the bad brine rises, you wake before anyone calls.

When a leather bladder stiffens, your own throat aches.

When the frogs count past the number they should know, you leave whatever you are doing and go to the pipes.

Your sleeping place is behind the wall of cups, in a cavity made when an older channel was bricked over but never fully filled. You reach it by climbing the lower bowls, squeezing past a blind niche where a shrine once stood, and sliding sideways through a crack masked by moss. Inside is a dry-enough shelf of wood, some old reed matting, a heap of feathers stolen from nests after fledging, three polished snail shells, a broken bronze spoon, and a strip of cloth given to you by a keeper-child who later left through the ceiling stair and did not come back.

You keep things that do not rot quickly.

You listen to things that do.

Your days are measured less by light than by tasks.

At condensation time you lick moisture from your upper lip and crawl the beams to clear spider-silk from the vent ropes. The bats dislike you but permit your passing if you move slowly. You check the dwarf willows, pressing your fingers into the tubs around their roots. Sometimes one has pushed a hairline crack wider, and you must cut back its pale roots with an apology-click. The willows remember insults. Their wands will scratch your face for days afterward, even when there is no draft.

During ceiling rain you work low. The whole basin wakes too loudly then. Water falls from carved mouths, the cups overflow, channels forget their banks, frogs call as if the world has become only throat. You wedge reed gates into place while water pushes at your thighs. You carry egg strings wound around your wrists like translucent bracelets. You scoop drowning beetles from bowls where the wrong larvae must not feed. Human keepers shout across the basin, but you hear mostly tone, not words. Urgency. Warning. Curse. Gratitude, sometimes.

In low-water time, you descend.

The floor exhales old smells then: rust, sour clay, dead weed, cracked lime, secrets exposed to air. Lost things appear. A child’s tooth. A bent hook. A bird skull with algae in its eye sockets. A small carved figure worn faceless from years underwater. Once, a human finger bone wedged upright in a tile seam as if pointing. You collect these and sort them. Useful. Dangerous. To be returned. To be hidden from children. To be shown to the keeper. To be dropped through the grille.

You have rules too.

Do not let the pale eels taste blood from your hand.

Do not sleep in a trough that has gone warm without cause.

Do not mimic the pump unless you are willing to be answered.

Do not take all the crabs from any seam, no matter what the keeper says.

Do not tell a child the true number of dry stones.

Do not look into a cup that has filled while you watched it empty.

Do not give your name near the drain-pool.

Your name is not one word anyway.

It is a sequence of sounds: three clicks, a breath, a low knock in the throat, and the drip that follows. The frogs can say it. The human keepers cannot. They call you Silt, or Latch, or Little Valve, or Underkin, depending on which generation is speaking and how afraid they are of needing you.

The current keeper calls you Marr.

She is old now, though she was not old when she first found you stealing lamp-fat from a shell bowl. She has hands like bundled roots and white scars around both ankles from eel court mistakes made in youth. She trusts you more than she trusts her own knees. In the mornings, if morning is the pale loosening of dark above the ceiling slits, she leaves a clay cup of broth at the third post of the northern shelf. You pretend not to know it is for you. She pretends not to know you drink it.

Together you maintain the world.

She walks above with rake, wedge, knife, cord, and ledger.

You crawl below with hook, fingers, teeth, and listening skin.

Sometimes she asks what is happening beneath the center.

You never answer directly.

Not because you are cruel. Because no answer remains true long enough. The thing below is not sleeping or waking, sick or well, hungry or satisfied. It is balancing. It is becoming. It is using what enters it. It is sending up fresh water in the morning and brine by evening. It is letting the frogs count in sixes, then sevens, then no number human hands can show. It is teaching the eels to gather under new stones.

It is making itself differently.

And you, who are one of its movable thoughts, feel the change first as an itch under the ribs.

There are new sounds in the sealed pipes.

There are new larvae in the upper cups, pale and jointed wrong.

The birds have begun avoiding not only the central stones but a line of tiles leading eastward.

One of the dwarf willows has sprouted a wand with tiny white rootlets hanging from its leaves, as if it would rather drink from air than mud.

Marr has noticed. Of course she has noticed. She says little. She sharpens reed knives. She renders new fat for lamps. She copies the old water-height marks into fresh clay because the posts that held them are softening at the base.

At night, or what passes for night, you crouch beside the drain-pool and press your palm flat to the grille.

Cold comes up through the metal.

The bubbles rise one by one, large and black and silver-skinned. Each breaks with a soft lip-sound, almost speech. Around you the marsh continues: ticks, drips, wing-snaps, frog calls, crab-scrape, wheel-creak, the distant exhale of the pump. All the small noises that make the large body possible.

You feel then what you are.

Not master. Not servant. Not monster. Not child.

You are the marsh’s hand inside its own wound.

You are the one sent into narrowness when balance requires touch.

You are the listener under the floor, the climber behind the cups, the pale shape glimpsed between gutter and shadow, the reason children are told not to answer when something taps from inside the wall.

You are what a built wetland grows when maintenance becomes heredity.

And if someone entered this place—some dry-footed stranger staring upward at the rafters, downward at the tiles, flinching at the frogs too loud for their bodies—they would not see you at first.

They would see a room too large to be a room.

They would smell ash, clay, fish oil, mildew.

They would hear dripping from a hundred mouths.

They would watch a small marsh bird step delicately around a dry stone.

Then a cup on the wall would overflow though nothing had fallen into it.

A frog would stop calling.

A dark hand would emerge from a crack in the masonry, place a bone wedge exactly where it needed to go, and withdraw.

Only then would they understand that the place is inhabited not only by creatures within it, but by creatures of it.

And you would be gone before their lamp found your face.
