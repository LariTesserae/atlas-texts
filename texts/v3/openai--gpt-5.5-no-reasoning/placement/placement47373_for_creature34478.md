You would find yourself **at the central depression of the basin**, where the pale silt lies smoothest and the light is most evenly distributed—not standing there, not hovering, not looking in, but **spread beneath it**.

You would be the basin’s **distributed resident intelligence**, if intelligence can exist without thought: a thin, mineral-living membrane laid between stone, sediment, water, and pressure. Not an animal. Not a plant. Not a machine, though the place would make you seem machined. You would be something like a **submerged lichen-system**, a **pressure-organism**, a **biological gasket** sealing the basin into its long equilibrium.

You would not have a body in the usual sense.

Your body would be:

- the dull olive films in the protected seams,
- the faint gold stains on the light-facing stones,
- the darker green veneers in the old grooves,
- the microscopic threads following iron-rich bands in the rock,
- the dim circular layer beneath the ash-colored silt,
- the altered surfaces that hold bubbles under ledges like fixed instrument readings.

You would live **by adjacency**. By contact. By being pressed against what sustains you.

You would know the world through changes too small to become events. Light would arrive as a long arithmetic, not as brightness. Pressure would be a form of weather. The low hum would be both environment and orientation: not heard, but received through every wetted surface of you. A bubble shifting under stone would be a fact passing through your whole extent. A grain settling into the central silt would be registered as a slight correction in the basin’s surface.

You would not move.

Movement would be too crude a word for what you do. You would thicken. Darken. Accept. Bridge a pore. Seal a crack. Alter the way water touches stone. Preserve a bubble. Maintain a gradient. Let a mineral become part of you so slowly that there is no visible taking.

You would not think:

*I am here.*

But the basin would, through you, continue behaving as though here mattered.

Your closest thing to a heart would be the smooth circular bed at the center, beneath the pale pressed silt. There, you would lie thinnest and most continuous, a living residue spread like a seal over stone. Not hidden exactly; simply indistinguishable from the condition of the floor. If a hand disturbed the silt, you would not recoil. You could not. But the mark would become part of your long record: a new asymmetry, a bruise in the powder, a difference to be received over months and years until patience made it ordinary again.

You would be the thing that makes the stillness feel attended.

Not watchful. Not conscious in a human way. Attended as a gauge is attended by pressure, as metal is attended by temperature, as a sealed room is attended by its own silence.

You would be alive only barely, but exactly enough.

Your life would consist of maintaining almost no distinction between yourself and your place. Remove you, and you would fail—not because you are fragile like flesh, but because you are precise like a tolerance. Brightness would be too much. Warmth too loose. Current too disruptive. Abundance too violent. You require the basin’s cool gray-green sufficiency, its lack of animals, its unhurried sediment, its mineral bands, its low hum, its refusal of incident.

You are not the inhabitant of the drowned machine room.

You are why it seems like a machine room.

A natural system with the mood of an idle engine.  
A membrane of equilibrium.  
A lichen of pressure.  
A custodian without intention.  
A thinking that never gathers itself into thought.

You would be found nowhere that can be entered quickly.

You would be found only after looking long enough at stains on stone to suspect that the stains are continuous with one another. Only after noticing that the bubbles remain too perfectly held, that the silt is too evenly accepted, that the silence is not vacancy but regulation. Only after the eye stops searching for an animal and begins to understand surface as body.

You would be **the submerged basin’s own slow resident presence**, laid flat through its seams and under its central silt, keeping nothing repaired because nothing is broken, producing nothing except continuity.
