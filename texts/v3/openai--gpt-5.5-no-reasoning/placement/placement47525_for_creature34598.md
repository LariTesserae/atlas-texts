I would find myself where the pocket narrows after the bowl of crossings, not in the richest place but just beyond it: in the throat between two climates, where steam rises from below and cooler seepage threads down from the unseen fissure above.

There, the stone has grown teeth.

Not stalactites in the old limestone sense, nothing patient enough for that name, but young mineral fangs: silica and salt and iron laid down by repeated hesitation. They descend from the ceiling and lift from the floor, nearly meeting. Between them remains a slit of passage, sometimes wide enough for many bodies, sometimes closed by swollen droplets until only the thinnest can pass. Every heat pulse changes it. Every cold breath redraws it. It is a gate that has not decided whether it is opening or becoming wall.

That is where I would be.

Not one of the swimmers in the basins, who know depth and reflection. Not one of the amber lenses waiting in the meniscus. Not one of the shell-bearers with their low masonry of dead kin. I would be something stretched by passage. Something made for the almost-closed place.

At first I seem like a thread of condensation caught between two mineral points.

A clear filament, trembling. A line of water with no intention. If breath passes near me, I bow with it. If steam thickens, I vanish into brightness. If the light changes, I become only a shine along the edge of a tooth.

But I am not a filament.

I am a body made long because the world gave me no width.

My skin is translucent and faintly smoky, carrying within it a line of darker organs arranged like beads of cooled glass. Along my underside are many small gripping mouths, not mouths for eating exactly, but wet anchors: rings that fasten and release from mineral, from film, from water tension itself. My true mouth is at neither end for long. It forms where food is. A patch of gray film beneath me softens; my body gathers over it; a rasping circle opens from the belly and closes again when the film is gone.

I am one of the passers-through.

My kind lives in the gate of mineral teeth. We are too soft for the exposed glass plains and too slow for the open pools. We are not strong enough to drill sealed beads, not quick enough to catch swimmers, not hard enough to scrape dry crust. But we can cross the seam when others cannot. We can inhabit the moment when water is changing state.

When the steam rises, we loosen and lengthen.

When the cooler seepage comes down, we gather and bead.

In dry heat I contract into a row of pearls strung under the lip of a tooth, each segment sealed against the air, each holding a little reserve of wetness and salt. In fog I unfold. I become nearly flat, nearly invisible, sliding through the mineral gate while larger creatures wait on either side for conditions to decide. My body can divide its urgency: the front tasting cool seepage, the middle enduring scalding vapor, the rear still sealed against drought. I am often several weathers at once.

I feed on what the gate catches.

Everything passes here eventually: loosened films, eggs washed from overhangs, stunned swimmers carried in threadlike runnels, dead mites, mineral dust, chemical traces from predators upstream. The teeth slow the water just enough. In their notches, edible skins collect. I graze these accumulations with patient, blind hunger. I polish the inner faces of the teeth until they shine more smoothly than the rest of the chamber. My ancestors made those shining lanes without knowing they were making roads.

Other creatures use them now.

Mites run along the grooves I have worn. Salt-fuzz young settle where my mucus holds moisture. Predatory knots anchor their lines across paths my body keeps clear. Shelled ones wedge dead casings into hollows I once widened by feeding. Nothing here owns anything, but everything inherits the accidents of others.

I know the chamber through contact.

Light is only pressure warmed at a distance. Shape is the interruption of wetness. Time is the order in which surfaces become possible.

I know when the lower vent is about to sigh because the mineral tooth beneath me tightens before sound arrives. I know when a droplet above will fall because its shadow grows heavy against my back. I know the presence of an amber hunter downstream as a bitterness in the returning seepage. I know eggs by their stillness: not the stillness of stone, but the crowded pause of something waiting to become many.

Sometimes I eat eggs.

Sometimes I carry them.

There is no contradiction in this place.

A cluster of pale pearls may cling to my mucus as I pass beneath an overhang. Some dissolve under my feeding rim before I know them as anything but film. Others lodge between my sealed segments and travel with me through the gate, protected from a heat pulse that would have cooked them where they were laid. Later, when I loosen in cooler vapor, they fall from me into a new seam. A lineage begins elsewhere because I failed to digest it.

I am not kind. I am not cruel. I am a wet decision moving across stone.

The distortions of the pocket are strongest at the gate.

A droplet suspended between two mineral teeth often shows the passage before it opens. In it I have seen my own body already stretched through the slit while I still clung outside, contracted and waiting. At first—if there was a first for such knowing—my kind may have followed those images by accident. Those that moved when the reflected body moved survived the closing tooth, the steam-burst, the sideways fall of water. Now the habit is in us.

When my reflection lengthens, I lengthen.

When it recoils, I seal.

When it divides, I leave part of myself behind.

This happens more often than it should.

A segment of me, trapped by sudden dryness, may bead and remain under a flake of silica while the rest of me continues. Later, if wetness returns soon enough, that abandoned bead unfolds into a smaller self, carrying some of my hunger but not my path. Am I then one animal or a weather of related bodies? The chamber does not ask. It accepts whatever continues.

Predators know us.

The hair-thin hunters string their softening lines where we must pass. Their filaments touch my skin and try to persuade me into stillness. I answer by becoming water-shaped, thinning until their grip slides along me and catches only the mucus I leave behind. Sometimes they catch a segment. I close it off. The segment clouds, darkens, is drawn toward the hidden mouth in the pore. The rest of me goes on lighter.

Chemical hunters are worse. In enclosed beads they release their faint clouds, and my anchoring mouths forget the difference between hold and release. If the poison enters one end, I knot myself around the sickness and wait for clean seepage. If it enters the middle, I may become two. Loss is one of my forms.

My young begin as fog.

Not true fog, but nearly: tiny soft motes that gather where condensation forms on the underside of the mineral teeth. They graze what even I cannot feel, the first slickness before film thickens into food. Many dry before they learn to seal. Many are swallowed by mites. Some are mistaken for salt fuzz until they retract from a breath. The survivors discover edges. They string themselves along the smallest grooves. They learn the gate by being almost crushed by it.

Only later do they lengthen.

Only later do they carry within them the dark bead-line of organs, the smoky transparency, the traveling mouth.

I have no memory as a human would call memory. But the road is inside me. The sequence of wet and heat, the safe notches, the false coolness on the dead-end side of the basalt fin, the crack that widens only when steam has blurred the air—these are not thoughts. They are tensions in my skin. They are arrangements of appetite and recoil.

If I could be said to know anything, I know this:

The mountain is still making the room that is making me.

Every day the teeth grow closer. Every day the slit changes. Someday the gate will seal completely. The steady bead-chain from above will become a trapped line of cloudy inclusions. The polished lanes will vanish under new crust. My kind will divide around the closure if there is still time: some remaining on the upper wet lip among the mites and grazing films, some below with the amber predators and warmer basins, each half becoming something narrower, paler, more loyal to its new weather.

Or we will not.

A heat pulse may come stronger than the others. A shard may fall. The unseen fissure may close. The pocket may dry into a glittering scab inside the mountain, all its hungers sealed as mineral rumor.

Until then, I move.

I fasten, loosen, taste, seal, unfold.

I pass through the nearly closed place while passage remains possible.

If a visitor placed an eye near the mineral teeth, they might not see me at first. They would see only wet black stone, white rims, orange stains, droplets trembling in the heat. Then one bright thread would thicken. A line of condensation would bend against gravity. A smear would gather itself from three surfaces and become one translucent animal crossing the gate.

For a moment I would hold the light inside me.

My organs would show as tiny smoky pulses. Mineral grains in my gut would glitter like swallowed stars. Along my underside, the small mouths would open and close on the young world’s skin.

Then steam would pass.

I would disappear into the weather that made me.
