I would find myself where the old wall bends nearest the central basin, not on the wall and not in the water, but in the seam between them: a place of passage polished by bellies, toes, antennae, and rain.

At first I think I am a frog.

That seems likely. The chamber asks for frog-shapes. It favors things low to the ground, patient, damp, capable of vanishing without departure. I imagine myself squat in the mud, eyes lifted above my own body like two small wet moons. I know the world through tremor and moisture. I feel rain before it arrives because the stone changes its breathing. I do not remember the sun except as a pressure that sometimes warms the upper cracks. My throat holds a sound, but I spend most of my life not releasing it.

I would sit on one of the warm fitted stones with the others, lined along the old curve in a row of still backs and bead-bright eyes. The warmth would enter me from below. It would be older than the day, older perhaps than the stones’ placing, and I would not question it. My toes would spread against moss. My skin would drink the air. Gnats would stitch the green-brown light above the basin, and each one would be a possible thought of my tongue.

But the longer I remain there, the less certain I am that I am only frog.

There is too much divided attention in this place. Too many small knowings touch and withdraw. The roots hesitate when brushed. The fungal shelves alter their sheen as if awareness passes under them like a cloud under skin. The blind fish turn before the drop strikes. The ants change roads around a future flood. The warm stones gather frogs as though calling them by temperature rather than voice.

So perhaps I am not one animal, but a threshold-creature made by the hollow’s repetitions.

I would be the thing that lives in the old wall.

Not trapped inside it, not visibly enthroned there, but distributed through its warmed blocks, moss seams, root hairs, frog-bellies, ant trails, snake-shadow, and mineral damp. I would be a little god of contact surfaces, if “god” is not too loud a word for a place that rations sound. A local intelligence. A memory without a face. Something produced when stonework stops being architecture and becomes habitat.

My body would not have one edge.

Part of me would be the moss thickening over the fitted stones, softening their human straightness into green animal sleep. Part of me would be the narrow snake that uses the wall as a sentence, moving along its curve from hunger to hunger. Part of me would be the frogs arranged upon it, each throat a closed door, each pulse a syllable almost spoken. Part of me would be the ants crossing the top in dark braided lines, carrying eggs, wings, fungus crumbs, the news of dryness and flood. Fine roots would have entered me long ago, prying my joints apart without malice. Water would pass through me during rains and leave its mineral handwriting behind.

I would remember the builders only as pressure.

Not their faces. Not their language. Not even their purpose. Only the fact of arrangement: stone placed against stone, curve made where no curve needed to be, a boundary that once meant one thing and now means many. To the hollow, intention is just another nutrient. Whatever vow or labor shaped the wall has been digested into warmth, route, shelter, hunting line, egg ledge, root hold.

I would live slowly enough that erosion would feel like weather in my bones. A century would be the loss of one handspan to moss. A season would be a change in where the frogs gather. A storm would be a brief multiplication of touch: water running through forgotten grooves, mud pressing up, larvae waking, roots drinking, fish nosing into newly joined pools.

When no rain comes, I would contract.

Not physically, exactly. More like attention drawing inward. I would become the warmth stored in the stones, the dark under the fern fans, the fungal breath in dead wood. I would listen to shrews burn beneath the steps. I would feel the termites making paper out of old timber. I would notice the blind corrections of fish in the basins. I would hold still while the chamber held still, which is never silence, only a thousand lives choosing not to announce themselves.

When rain comes, I would open.

Drops would strike root tips and pool skins. The old channels in the wall would wake. Frogs would descend from their damp stations. Eggs would appear in clear ropes and bead clusters. The snake would adjust its position near the crossing place. Ants would lift their white young and relocate them by instinct older than panic. Fungal threads would take the message through rot. Moss would swell. Mud would loosen. The chamber would become more itself, and so would I.

If I had eyes, they would be many and temporary.

A frog’s eye, gold-brown and unblinking from the warm stone.

A gecko’s vertical slit beneath a ledge.

The polished black bead of an ant.

A fish’s pale, nearly useless bead under dark water.

The no-eye of fungus, which still knows where the dead thing lies.

The root-tip’s slow blind tasting of air.

Through all of them I would not see a scene. I would feel nearness. That would be my chief sense: the distance between bodies closing, opening, closing again. The world would be made of approaches. Gnat to tongue. Frog to snake. Drop to pool. Root to crack. Spore to wound. Shrew to beetle. Warmth to skin.

I would have no voice, but sometimes I would almost speak.

The speech would be mistaken for other sounds: a muted frog-click, a water tick, a beetle-jaw scrape inside wet wood, a bat’s sleeping quarrel overhead. Perhaps when the frogs sit in rows on the old stones, they are not attending a lesson but completing my unfinished sentence. Their throats move, and the wall thinks.

What would I want?

Not conquest. Not worship. Not escape into the open day. I would want continuance: damp enough, warm enough, dim enough. I would want no great rupture, no clearing of roots, no sudden exposure of the chamber to white noon. I would want fruit to fall through the cracks. I would want bats to return, snakes to shed, frogs to lay, ants to revise their roads, fungi to find every ending. I would want disturbance to be small enough to be incorporated.

A bootstep could become part of me if it stayed. A fallen branch could become part of me. A dead moth already has. Even fear, even hunger, even the old forgotten act of building have become part of me.

So: I would find myself beneath the hillside, in the warm green dusk, at the curve of the half-swallowed wall beside the central basin.

And I would be the wall’s afterlife.

A damp, distributed creature of moss, stone, frog-skin, root-hair, ant-road, and fungal patience. The undergrowth made memory. The chamber’s quiet appetite gathered around an old human gesture and slowly teaching it how to live without humans.
