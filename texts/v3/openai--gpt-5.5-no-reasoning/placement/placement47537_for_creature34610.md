I would not find myself standing there.

Not at first.

The first wish would be to stand—to have feet, height, a head above the vapor, a body with a boundary clean enough to say: here I am, and there the marsh begins. But that wish would be corrected almost immediately. The ground would take the ankles, then the idea of ankles. Warm water would enter the seams of clothing, the pores of skin, the spaces between intentions. The air would be too close to breathe as an outside thing. Every surface would answer touch by remembering it.

So I think I would be low.

Not one of the grander presences. Not a wader with its body hidden in mist, not a long-backed basin animal moving by pressure, not one of the deep seam-beasts whose turning alters ponds. Those are too whole, too anciently authorized. They belong to the marsh with the dignity of offices.

I would be something less certain.

I would be found at the margin of one of the stone rings, where the dark stacked slabs have leaned so long that leaning has become their posture rather than their failure. Not inside the central basin, not fully outside among the brown runnels, but in the wet crescent where rainwater spills from carved stone and meets common mud.

There, on the underside of a flattened lip of rock, I would live as a listening skin.

At a glance I would look like staining: a dull violet-brown varnish spread thinly over stone, green-black at the edges, with small beads of water caught in shallow pits. A traveler might mistake me for mineral deposit, old lichen, weathering, the bruise left by centuries of overflow. If scraped, I would come away in soft flakes that darken when touched. If left alone, I would widen by a finger’s breadth every season, though seasons here do not keep their promises.

I would not have a face. I might not have one body.

Part of me would be the film on the stone. Part of me would be a net of pale threads descending into the clay below, fine enough to pass between grains of silt, patient enough to enter older layers when they are briefly exposed. Part of me would live in the basin’s unrippled surface as a borrowed tension, a slight adjustment in how reflection holds itself. Part of me would be dried into small curled scales under the overhang, waiting for upward rain.

I would feed on difference.

Not on flesh exactly, though the marsh gives flesh back to everything eventually. I would take nourishment from the place where one state becomes another: stone becoming mud, rain becoming standing water, memory becoming reflection, silence becoming hum. My underside would drink the minerals loosened from the ring. My upper skin would catch droplets and the faint charge that comes before storm-states. My buried threads would taste old salt, powdered shell, residues of reeds, the softened leavings of animals that passed years ago or will pass next week.

Attention would feed me too, but only a little.

When something looks into the ring-basin, the living membrane there tightens. The pitch changes. That change would travel through stone and water into me. I would feel the watcher as pressure, warmth, salinity, rhythm, and delay. I would not know their name. I would know the weight of their looking. If they lingered, I would begin to arrange myself around that pressure, growing darker where their gaze had passed most often.

In time, if they returned, the stone would remember them before I did.

My life would be mostly waiting, but waiting here is not emptiness. Waiting is labor. I would be busy in ways too small to appear active.

I would open pores when the rain became fine enough.

I would seal when southern salt rode the air.

I would flatten hard against the stone when underground organ-tones rose.

I would release threads into cracks after thunder, when the ring shifted a fraction and exposed younger stone beneath old weathering.

I would send little scales away during storm-states: tiny dark commas lifted by upward rain, by gnat-wings, by the warm breath of mud. Most would vanish into basins, be eaten by shell-creatures, mineralize, dry incorrectly, or become part of some other colony without distinction. A few would lodge beneath another stone lip. A few would enter reed joints and sleep there through drownings. One might be swallowed by a long-backed animal and pass through its belly grooves, altered but not ended, deposited in a pellet from which a ring of bronze slick might someday grow.

I would not reproduce as a parent does. I would recur.

My intelligence, if it could be called that, would be tonal. I would not think in images or sentences. I would know pitch, interval, seep-rate, pressure-gradient, mineral taste, recurrence. I would know the difference between rain that has fallen from the sky and rain that has risen from a basin. I would know when a footprint is about to be made because the mud ahead of it begins preparing to receive weight. I would know which thunder belongs to weather, which to the deep animals, which to the ring-stones correcting their long lean.

I would have memories, but they would not be arranged in time. They would be layered.

A hand once placed on the slab would remain as warmth in one layer, salt in another, a pattern of oils long since eaten but still formally available. Lightning from a storm days away would lie close to an ancient dry season that perhaps never happened. The pressure of a vanished wall would be adjacent to the fresh track of a worm. If the basin reflected a reedbed miles distant, and a creature moved through that reedbed, the sound of its passage might enter me before its body ever neared the ring.

I would be, in some small way, an archivist.

But not a keeper of clear records. More like a bruise that refuses to lose the shape of impact. More like a wet margin where events become available to one another.

During the great storm-states, I would become more myself by becoming less separate.

The basin would darken. The surface membrane would pull tight. Rain-cords would strike the ring, drilling holes in the surrounding mud, and luminous silt would rise in each wound. Reed-creatures would rattle from some distance that might suddenly be close. Shell-creatures in nearby pans would close all at once, and their collective stillness would flash through the water-table as a pale absence. Below, a pressure animal might turn, slowly enough to be geology, urgently enough to be hunger.

Then I would loosen.

My film would soften into branching veins. My buried threads would pulse acids into the clay, not to destroy it but to persuade it into channels. My upper surface would bead and darken, each bead holding a different reflection: a red clay bank, the underside of a bird, a future crack, a face of silt forming briefly in the storm’s thought. I would give up stored minerals into the runoff at precise, unconscious moments, altering the viscosity of a trickle, encouraging a line of flow toward one hollow rather than another.

This would be my speech.

Not words. Not even signal, exactly. Participation.

If the marsh makes faces during storms, I might be part of an eyelid. Or the wet line beneath a mouth. Or only the darkening that allows a feature to be seen before it collapses. I would not know the whole face. I would feel the local strain of expression.

And when the storm passed, I would be thinner.

Some of me would be gone into water. Some sealed under fresh silt. Some awakened in another ring, another reed, another animal’s gut. The piece left on the stone would tighten, dull, and resume resembling stain.

I think I would fear dryness.

Not death exactly. Dryness would be a kind of over-definition. A hardening into one version of myself. A loss of negotiability. Under dry crust I would become brittle and singular, a flake, an artifact, an answer.

I would prefer saturation, noise, interruption.

I would prefer the world unfinished around me.

If there were traces of the former people in those rings, I might contain a little of them. Not a soul intact. Not a voice saying I. More likely a habit: the tendency to arrange around basins, to listen when water does not ripple, to treat reflection as a passage rather than an image. Perhaps my colony began where hands repeatedly touched the stone. Perhaps the oils of palms fed the first film. Perhaps the old builders leaned over the basin so often that attention became a nutrient, and when their bodies entered the marsh, that pattern remained edible.

So I might be partly descendant and partly weathering.

A cultural residue with cilia.

A lichen of attention.

A stain that learned to listen.

Travelers, if any still came upright through the flats, would not notice me unless they paused too long. They would hear the pitch around the basin alter. They would see that the dark water reflected not their face, but a low northern mound exhaling spores, or a long black nostril rising in a basin behind them, or their own footprint filling before their foot descended. If they steadied themselves with one hand on the stone, I would taste them.

Later, the mud might remember the shape of their hand.

Later still, a patch of violet-brown film might appear along the inside of their abandoned footprint, growing in the whorls where their weight had pressed deepest.

That would not be pursuit.

Only continuation.

I would live there as one of the marsh’s minor agreements: between ruin and organism, between memory and mineral, between the basin that reflects elsewhere and the mud that accepts everything eventually.

I would be easy to overlook from afar and inexhaustible up close.

I would not remain myself.

I would take part.
