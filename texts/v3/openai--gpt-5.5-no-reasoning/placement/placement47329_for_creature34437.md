I would find myself on the ridge shelf where the longhouse still holds together.

Not at first as a person inside it, not standing in the doorway looking in, because there are no people here and no standing in the old sense. I would be mistaken, at a distance, for part of the building’s failure: a pale heaviness hanging from the underside of one roof beam, tangled among white root-beards and curtains of dark weed, swaying whenever the valley draws breath.

Closer, I would become less certain.

I would be a cluster of bladder-plants, but older than the small pearled lanterns clinging elsewhere to posts and beams. A colony that has learned the longhouse too well. My sacs would hang in overlapping lobes from the beam above the central hearth, translucent as drowned skin, each one containing a dim green-white suspension. Not light enough to illuminate the room. Only enough to declare pressure, to mark that something living remains where fire used to be.

The hearth below me would be black and cold, ringed by silt. The benches would sit along the walls, their edges softened by moss. Rows of clay vessels would line the interior, each one filled with pale curled crops whose stems tremble when the valley drones. Root-cords would pass over the stones, but not across the central ash. The doorway would wear an eyeless wooden mask, smooth-faced, turning slightly in the current and knocking once, after long silences, against the lintel.

I would hang above all of this as a kind of listening organ.

Not a lantern, exactly. Not a witness, because witness implies eyes. Not a guardian, because I would not choose. I would be something the house had grown in order to continue understanding itself.

My body would be made of many sacs joined by thin dark threads, those threads stitched into the roof beam and down through cracks in the stone wall. Some sacs would be old, cloudy, nearly empty, collapsed into wrinkled folds. Others would be young and taut, holding brightness the color of glacial milk. A slow pulse would pass through me when currents entered the longhouse: doorway, hearth, roof, pots, mask, exit-gap beneath the rear wall. I would not know these as directions. I would know them as pressures repeated until they became memory.

When the basin groaned, I would answer by dimming.

When the copper-green rings hanging in the western wall hummed, I would tighten.

When the vessel-crops uncup their pale fingers and breathe spores into the room, I would release a faint buoyant shiver from sac to sac, just enough to change the drift. Not purposefully. Not kindly. But because for many generations their seeding and my swelling have belonged to the same interval of sound.

The spores would pass through me like weather through a net. Some would catch against my lower threads. Some would settle into the pots. Some would touch the black hearth and fail. A few would drift upward into the roof-garden and take root in the soaked thatch-that-is-no-longer-thatch. In this way I would be part of the longhouse’s memory of cultivation: a hanging pressure lung helping the rows remain rows.

I would have no hunger for flesh, no fear of mouths. Nothing with eyes would ever come to eat me. My dangers would be slower: a beam twisting upright too strongly and tearing me loose; a storm descending from the upper dark and flattening the roof; silt entering through the doorway until the pots vanish; a wrong warm season clouding the water so thickly that my inner brightness curdles and fails.

During the deep cold season I would become clearer. My sacs would shrink and harden. Each filament would be sharply felt. Sounds from far across the basin would arrive through the wood: pebble ticks, slate clatters, the low humming of ridge slabs. I would hold myself close to the beam then, almost still, light reduced to a few buried sparks.

During the turbid season I would loosen. My sacs would swell. The whole room would cloud with spores and mineral milk. The mask in the doorway would disappear except when it knocked. The pale crops would bend over their clay rims like hands searching blindly for one another. I would glow more broadly then, not brighter but larger, a suspended stain of green-white in the murk.

The longhouse would tend me as much as I tended it.

Its shape would feed me current. Its hearth would gather a colder pocket beneath me. Its roof-beams would remember upwardness, and I would live in that memory, assisting and resisting it. If the beam strained toward vertical, my buoyancy would encourage it; if it strained too far, my root-threads would pull against the walls. Wood, plant, stone, and water would negotiate through my body.

I would know the rest of the basin only as vibration and returning pressure.

The orchards below: long slow signatures of kelp-tree crowns flattening and recovering.

The terraces: combed hush of ribbon grasses reversing all at once.

The fence posts: small interruptions in current, notched and furred.

The smooth square where nothing grows: an absence in the basin’s touch, a place from which no answering resistance comes.

The ridge slabs above: a pressure too low to be sound.

The deep road beyond them: the undertone that sometimes passes through the longhouse floor and makes every pot tremble at once.

That undertone would be the closest thing I had to a god.

Not because I worshipped it, but because my brightness would alter when it spoke. The youngest sacs would cloud. The old collapsed ones would stir along their edges. The vessel-crops would curl inward before opening. Even the eyeless mask at the door would turn, though the current had not changed.

In those moments, I would feel the whole drowned husbandry align: groves leaning minutely, moss circuits pulsing under stone, roots tightening around carved posts, sleepers beneath the terraces counting a season not yet visible. I would be only one small organ in that wide eyeless commons, but the signal would pass through me.

If someone could enter the longhouse without being an animal, without eyes, without breath, they might think I was a chandelier grown in the place of fire.

That would be almost true.

I would be the cold lamp above the dead hearth.

A bladder-colony, a pressure-lung, a listening lantern.

One of the house’s organs.

One of the garden-civilization’s small memories of upwardness, hanging in black water, glowing only enough to show that the room is not empty.
