I think I would find myself **near the edge of the central city but not within its most inhabited rings**—in one of the long, ambiguous districts where habitation thins into maintenance: a place of cistern courts, abandoned plazas, half-trustworthy roads, roof-fields, and empty stone expanses that must be visited daily so they do not become too strange.

Not a capital quarter. Not a village. A **threshold district**.

A place people pass through because the road usually keeps faith with it, but where nobody lingers without work to do.

There would be a low hall there, built partly into an old terrace of pale bedrock. Its roof would be alive with silver cushion moss, and its walls would have been repaired so many times that the seams themselves would form a kind of writing. Behind it: three cisterns under lattice roofs. Before it: a swept plaza, empty by intention, bordered with frost-white stones whose shadows sometimes fall in different directions. Beyond that: a long causeway crossing a shallow basin where distance is known to stretch after rain.

I would be one of the people whose task is to **go out before others do**.

Not quite a map-weaver, not quite a road-comber, not quite a shadow-measurer, but something between them: a keeper of morning conditions. Someone who walks the district at first light with a staff, a covered bowl, a chalk cord, and a pouch of small stones marked with handprints.

My work would be to ask the world, quietly, whether it has remained itself overnight.

I would test the plaza.

Tap thresholds.

Look into the cisterns without letting my reflection linger.

Measure whether the standing stones cast shadows downward, sideways, or into the undersides of the clouds.

Watch the blue pulse-ferns planted along the causeway wall.

Listen at cracks, not for water alone, but for the soft wrongness that means depth is beginning to think about becoming distance.

I would return before the first meal and tell the district what kind of day it had inherited.

Not as prophecy. As maintenance.

“The eastern alley is short but honest.”

“The third cistern reflects sky only if approached from the west.”

“No one should use the roof door today.”

“The plaza has gathered something. Leave it unswept until noon.”

“The basin road is remembering last year. Travel in pairs.”

I imagine I would be called something like a **morning witness**.

One of those small necessary offices that outsiders might mistake for superstition and locals would trust more than any formal authority. I would not command anyone. I would simply be believed if my record had stayed clean long enough.

My body would be compact and weathered like the others: scarred hands, cracked lips, narrowed eyes, lungs trained to the thin air. I would wear quiet layers of bark-cloth and plant felt, a resin-dark cloak stiff at the edges from frozen rain, and boots soft enough to read the stone. My measuring staff would be older than I am, inherited from someone whose hand-wear still polished one side of the grip. Along it would hang little weights: stone, fired clay, a bead of black mineral, a shell-shaped object no one can explain.

I would have a particular intimacy with **puddles and reflections**.

Not because I love them, exactly, but because I do not trust them, and long mistrust becomes a form of attention close to love. I would know which basins show the present sky, which show earlier weather, which should be covered before speech begins. I would be able to tell by the skin of a cistern whether rain fell in the night somewhere impossible. My household would tease me for staring too long into jars.

But I would never leave a bowl uncovered when roof-fog descended.

I think I would live with others, not alone. Loneliness in that place would be impractical, almost arrogant. My sleeping alcove would be in a communal hall where warmth is kept like a guest. At night, I would knot the day into cord: one knot for stable roads, one for altered water, one for shadow irregularity, one for plant warning, one for silence of uncertain meaning.

My closest companion might be a moss-tender, someone more patient than I am, with green-black stains under their nails and a way of speaking to walls as if coaxing old relatives to remain calm. Together we would maintain a family dead-wall on the west side of the district: gray lichen for the oldest, black moss for the fog-lost, silver fern for one ancestor who vanished on the southern road and returned decades later younger than his own daughter.

I would have no hunger for conquest, no dream of leaving the plateau forever. But I would be drawn toward its edges of trust. I would want to know where the district frays, where roads begin to reconsider, where the plants warn before people can measure. My flaw would be that I sometimes wait too long before turning back. Not out of recklessness, exactly. Out of listening.

There would be a story told about me one winter.

A fresh crack would open across the basin road after a night of granular snow. The blue pulse-ferns would tremble without wind, and the plaza stones would cast their shadows faintly upward. I would go out with two others to mark the unsafe stretch, and the road would shorten behind us but lengthen ahead. One companion would say we should sit and let the day tire itself. Another would say the road was trying to return us by sending us forward.

I would pour a little cistern water into a covered bowl, uncover it only beneath my cloak, and see not my own face but the roof of our hall from above, smoking in evening light.

So I would know the way back was not backward.

We would return by walking toward the image, not the road, and arrive in the plaza just as the wooden bell began to sound from a street that had not yet changed. After that, people would trust me with mirror-basins, though I would dislike the honor.

Children would be sent to me for their first route lessons.

I would teach them to walk from sleeping hall to cistern court and back by three alternatives. I would teach them not to count columns twice, not as a frightening taboo but as ordinary respect for arithmetic under stress. I would show them how moss sounds different when it covers stable stone. I would make them sit with their shadows at noon and notice whether the shadows were tired, eager, obedient, or pretending.

And I would tell them the rule that matters most:

Leave room around what cannot be understood.

Not because understanding is forbidden.

Because some things must be given space in order not to press back.

So: I would be a **morning witness of a threshold district**, living at the margin of the central city’s reliable rings. A tester of roads, reader of cisterns, listener at cracks, keeper of small empirical rituals. One of the quiet people who step outside before dawn to learn whether the world has remained negotiable.

Not a hero.

Not a ruler.

A person whose work is to notice early, speak softly, and help the day arrive safely.
