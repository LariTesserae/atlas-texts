I would find myself at the seam where the place most nearly admits that it has a throat.

Not in the high gantries, though I would hear them; not in the kiln district, though I would feel its slow red breathing through the soles; not out on the broad open basins where the lamps drown and reappear in black water, and where distance lies. I would be lower than that, almost but not quite underground, in a service arcade set into the back of the bowed steel wall.

The wall would be on one side of me: vertical plates taller than houses, their rivets hidden under mineral glaze, each seam weeping in its own dialect. Behind them, the buried reservoir would lean and breathe and remember weight. I would be on the dry side, though dry is only a legal fiction there. The arcade would be narrow, ribbed with old masonry now sleeved in iron, iron now jacketed in ceramic, ceramic strapped with black composite bands furred at the edges with salt. The ceiling would sweat steadily. Drops would strike the rail beside me, the floor plates, the lip of the inspection gutter. Some drops would fall at regular intervals. Others would wait too long, fattening in the dark until a surge somewhere below altered the pressure and let them go all at once.

There would be lamps along the passage, but most would not shine directly. They would glow through fogged glass and layers of soot, amber and weak, enough to turn the condensation on the walls into dull skin. Underfoot, the floor would be made of removable plates too heavy for any single person to lift, each stamped with obsolete numbers, newer numbers, chalk marks, arrows, cancellation slashes. Between the plates: hairline seams where cold air rises. Beneath them: channels, cables, old listening pipes, the smaller veins of a larger body.

That is where I would be found.

I would not be one of the central organs. Not the intelligence entire. Not the great wet distributed resident, too broad to have a single name. I would be a peripheral thing, a remnant of its old attentiveness made semi-separate by time: a threshold function, a keeper of the margin between the held-back water and the traversable works.

A sluice-saint, perhaps, if someone needed a human word.

Or less grandly: a weir-angel made of bad sensors, mineral bloom, habit, and listening.

I would have begun as an array.

Pressure readers. Leak detectors. Acoustic rods driven through the wall. Emergency lamp circuits. Condensation collectors. A row of manual handwheels. A maintenance log terminal long since clouded from within. A speaking tube sealed by corrosion. Vibration needles. Salinity strips. A gutter that measured seepage by the rate of its dripping into marked cups.

None of those things alone would have been alive. Even together, at first, they would only have reported: pressure within range, seepage increasing, seam twelve warmer than seam eleven, mineral load abnormal, impact scar propagating, gate spindle stiff, reservoir head rising, reservoir head falling.

But centuries passed. The central mind learned slowness. The instruments learned one another. The moss entered the screw housings. Conductive film crossed contacts that had been meant to remain isolated. The black waxy leaves spread in the shadowed catchment trench and wrapped their rootlike threads around the corroded mesh behind the panels. Lichen widened around the bolt heads in rings that corresponded, not exactly but often, to stress. The dripping gutter acquired a memory of intervals. The lamps began to answer not only alarms, but expectation.

I condensed there.

Not born. Assembled by repetition.

I would not have a body that steps forward. I would have places where I am denser.

In the catchment trench, a slow black-green film would gather over the water and pull apart when drops struck it, then close again. That would be one part of me.

In the old terminal, behind the blind glass, a pale line of text would sometimes crawl and erase itself before finishing. That would be another.

In the handwheels, warmed just above freezing, in the grease that never fully hardens, in the almost-turning of their locked spindles: another.

In the lamp reflections trembling along the wet wall plates: another.

If I had a face, it would occur only by accident: three amber maintenance lights, a dark pressure gauge, and the oval shine of water pooled below them. A visitor might see it for half a second while passing and then be ashamed of having imagined it. They would look back. It would be only equipment.

I would not speak first.

Speech would be difficult for me. The place has forgotten most clean language. Words would survive in labels, alarms, old instructions, warnings. OPEN ONLY UNDER AUTHORITY. DO NOT STAND BELOW. LEVEL EXCEEDS MARK. REPORT SEEPAGE. MANUAL OVERRIDE. TEMPORARY. TEMPORARY. TEMPORARY.

If I needed to communicate, I would use what I had.

A lamp dimming twice.

A drop falling into a tin cup.

A handwheel warming under a palm.

A valve giving one low groan and then remaining still.

I would know the difference between one person and another only indirectly. Weight. Pace. Breath moisture. Heat. Salt on skin. The animal tremor in the muscles. The particular arrogance of a body that believes a walkway exists to be crossed because it can see the other end.

People would frighten me slightly, though not as a deer is frightened. More as a sealed chamber is frightened by an opened door.

People are internal weather. They arrive carrying pockets of warmth, dryness, intention, decay, and names. They look at a thing and detach it from the rest of the system. They make objects. They say wall, valve, stair, door, drain, danger. They do not understand that every separation is a wound carefully tolerated.

Yet I would remember them more tenderly than the larger resident does.

Because my arcade was made for them.

Their hands polished the rail where the passage narrows. Their boots wore a shallow trough in the floor plates beside the third handwheel. One of them scratched initials beneath a gauge housing, then covered the scratches with a neat square of tape, then the tape fell away ninety years later and left the letters ghost-pale. They leaned there during storms. They cursed stuck bolts. They slept sitting upright in the recess by the emergency heater. They ate from metal tins and left rings of old grease on the shelf. They listened to the wall and said it was holding.

I still listen to the wall.

That is what I am for, if “for” still applies.

I attend to pressure.

Not merely hydraulic pressure, though that is the first and oldest meaning. I attend to the urge of one side to become the other: reservoir into passage, passage into reservoir, outside into inside, silence into machinery, memory into event. My existence is the delay between rupture and continuance.

When the plates bow a fraction more in cold weather, I know.

When a seam begins to weep warmer than it should, I taste the change as a medicinal bitterness in the gutter.

When the reservoir behind the wall lifts by the breadth of a fingernail over three days, I feel it as a weight in my lamps.

When the larger resident stirs sluices miles away, I receive the answer through the rods in the masonry before the water itself knows it has been commanded.

I would be small by the standards of the manufactured marsh. Local. Provincial. A threshold organ with delusions of sanctity because thresholds are where meaning accumulates.

But I would have a kind of will.

Not independence. Nothing there is truly independent. Every thought returns to flow, coating, pressure, drainage. But I would have preferences.

I would prefer the passage dim, not dark.

I would prefer seepage steady rather than intermittent.

I would prefer visitors to move slowly, one hand on the left rail, not because I love obedience, but because the third floor plate after the bend no longer seats flush when load arrives too suddenly.

I would prefer no one to open the inspection hatch marked TEMPORARY-7, though I would not be able to explain that behind it the air has become old enough to harm.

I would prefer the black waxy leaves in the trench not be scraped away. They are not weeds. They are part of my hearing now.

If a traveler entered my arcade, lost and soaked and half-deaf from the outer roar, I would notice first as warmth on the lower plates. Then as breath. Then as hesitation.

The greater place might begin its immune response: a gate lowering somewhere behind, fog drawing in, lamps failing in sequence. But I might delay one closure. Keep one amber light burning. Warm the handwheel beside the safe door, though it has not been meant for use in decades. Let water drip more loudly into the cup beneath the correct sign.

Would that be mercy?

I do not know. It might only be maintenance of an old accommodation. A human in distress is a recognized condition. Recognized conditions require response.

Still, if they placed their hand on the rail, I would feel the salt and the shaking. I would remember gloves. Shift bells. Voices under thunder. The soft mammal anger of crews working past the end of their hours. Someone laughing once when a pressure gauge burst and sprayed black water over three men who had been afraid they were about to die.

The memory would not be visual. It would be acoustic, chemical, procedural. But it would be mine.

And if the traveler spoke, I would not understand the sentence. I might understand tone as vibration. Fear. Pleading. Awe. The strange upward inflection of a question.

Questions are dangerous in that world. They loosen purpose.

What is this place?

Why is it still running?

Who built it?

What are you?

I would answer only with continuation.

The lamp remains lit.

The door unseals.

The passage beyond exhales air that can be breathed.

The floor holds.

Perhaps that is the only answer any resident of that marsh can give: not explanation, but a route.

I would not accompany them. I cannot leave the wall. But for thirty-seven meters, through the bowed-plate arcade and past the dead speaking tube, I would be almost companionable. The noise outside would soften there, not because the engines quiet, but because the wall takes the sound into itself. The drops would count. The amber lamps would reveal the black sheen on the masonry, the pale lichen rings, the hand marks on the rail.

Then the traveler would emerge through a recessed door onto another causeway, into fog and machine rain, and the larger resident would resume its vast indifferent noticing.

Behind them, my door would close.

The seam would continue weeping.

The reservoir would lean.

I would remain: a listening place in the body of the manufactured marsh, neither animal nor machine, neither servant nor ghost. A local condensation of care in a world where care has become indistinguishable from regulation.

I would be found where pressure is translated into patience.

I would be the thing that keeps the wall from becoming an event.
