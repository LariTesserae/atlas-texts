I would find myself in the root registry before I knew I had chosen it.

Not at first. At first I would think I was only passing through the administrative quarter because the rain had begun to fall in a line across the launders’ arcade and the frogs had all turned their throats toward the west, which meant the upper bridges would be slippery or absent. I would follow a route I did not remember learning: under the brass vine, past the cabinet with no drawers, left at the poplar-clock counting extinctions, then down the stair that is safe only when it smells faintly of tin.

The registry would be open.

It is never properly lit. Its dome of woven plane trees lets in not light but the idea of light, a grey-green pressure settling on the rows of tin drawers, on the suspended labels, on the root-bundles passing beneath the translucent floor. The cabinets stand like patient animals. Some have swollen shut. Some breathe through their keyholes. Some contain maps whose ink has sprouted hair-fine roots and entered the paper from the other side.

There are desks there, but no one sits at them for long. Chairs become territorial if warmed too often. Instead the clerks work standing, kneeling, leaning, listening with one cheek to the floor. They bind blue thread around roots that still consent to be named. They polish brass pins that hover several centimeters above the surfaces they once marked. They copy old functions onto new leaves:

load-bearing  
communicative  
potable  
ornamental  
archival  
defensive  
funerary  
unassigned  
suspected memory  
do not wake during fog

That is where I would be: not the mayor, not a priest, not one of the daring people who climbs the bladder towers with a wrench between their teeth. I would be a registry thing. A minor functionary of roots.

At first I would be mistaken for human.

I would wear the same layered pale cloth from the Loomworks, the same bark-soled shoes, the same waxed shoulder-cape beaded with condensation. I would carry a slate, a spool of blue thread, a bone stylus, a dull knife that is never used on white roots, and a packet of filings for apology. Around my neck would hang a municipal tag too worn to read, not inherited exactly, not awarded, but found one morning in my sleeping place with a root grown through its hole.

People would bring me questions.

Is the wall behind my stove still load-bearing?  
May we move a cradle into the room that fruited above the apothecary court?  
The lower bridge hummed when crossed by my sister; is this illness or permission?  
A root has entered our pantry and learned our names. Do we feed it?  
The floor in the east tenement has begun to remember water. Should we leave?

I would not answer quickly. Quick answers insult the city.

I would kneel. I would place two fingers on the root-paving, then my ear, then sometimes my teeth if the tremor was faint. I would listen for the slow arguments beneath the district: thirst, load, mineral desire, the pressure of a new stair forming where no stair had been requested. I would smell the drawer labels. I would open old files and let silver larvae withdraw into the seams before I read. I would compare the current route-rhymes with the obsolete survey pins.

Sometimes I would say, “Wait until the second pump-thud.”

Sometimes, “Feed it filings, but do not thank it aloud.”

Sometimes, “Move the cradle before condensation.”

Sometimes, “That bridge belongs to the deer now.”

And sometimes I would say nothing, because the registry would have opened a vent behind me and exhaled cold pollen, which means the question had already been answered elsewhere.

But I do not think I would remain entirely human.

No one who works long in the registry does.

The root-readers become maplike in their feet. The valve-keepers grow pressure in their hands. Vine-electricians acquire sparks behind the eyes. Wax-tenders smell faintly of sealed promises. Mirror-blinders lose the habit of looking straight at anyone.

A registry clerk changes more quietly.

I would begin by remembering streets that had not yet happened.

At breakfast, while scraping pale moss onto bread, I would know that the corridor behind the customs office would narrow by evening and that someone carrying eggs should take the old transit loop instead. I would know it before any root had moved visibly. Before the frogs faced east. Before the brass vine hummed. The knowledge would arrive like damp through a sleeve.

Then I would start misfiling things correctly.

A petition about a leaking ceiling would need to be placed under Births. A birth notice would belong in Drainage. A complaint about faceless owls would go into Load-Bearing, because the owls had heard a lie in the western supports. A dead machine’s maintenance record would have to be sealed with funerary wax and shelved beside marriages.

Other clerks would notice. They would stop correcting me. Eventually they would bring me the difficult drawers.

The city would use me because I was willing to be used slowly.

There would be a narrow sleeping loft above the registry, reached by a ladder that only appears after midnight condensation. I would sleep there under a bat-dark blanket, listening to the cabinets tick. My kettle would sit on a fungal heat-stone. My boots would dry under a leaf that curled around them for warmth. On the wall, instead of a mirror, there would be a patch of dull clay where I pressed my hand each morning to learn whether I still cast a human kind of heat.

Less and less, perhaps.

My skin would cool first. Not dead-cold, but cellar-cool, like metal kept in shade. Leaves would not curl away from me, but neither would they curl hungrily toward me. White roots would pause under my steps. Conductive vines would give one soft pulse when I passed, as if acknowledging a clerk entering a room of clerks.

Then my handwriting would change. It would grow branching ligatures. My notes would be readable only in damp weather. Ink would not dry on paper I touched; it would root.

Children would like me, though their parents would watch carefully.

They would come to the registry in groups of three or four, solemn in their bark-soled shoes, to learn their first route-rhymes. I would show them the safe roots and the forbidden ones. I would teach them to hear the difference between a stair thickening and a stair lying. I would tell them never to answer a closed flower, especially if it almost knows their name. I would let them hold old keys whose locks had become animals or walls or rainfall.

One child would ask, as children do, “Were you born?”

And I would not know how to answer.

Because perhaps I was.

Perhaps I came from one of the human families in the upper rooms, quiet and mended and hungry for salt. Perhaps I had a childhood name kept folded in wax so no flower could misuse it. Perhaps an old woman in a curtained loft remembers me crying once into a vent, remembers pressing her hand over my mouth in terror and love.

Or perhaps I was found in a drawer.

A cutting filed under Archival, wrapped in leafskin, labeled in a hand no one uses anymore:

assistant, provisional  
for use when maps fail  
do not expose to direct flame  
feed on questions

That would explain certain things. My calm when the floor shifts. My habit of knowing the previous shape of a room. My sorrow when a bridge forgets itself. My inability to leave the administrative quarter during black rain, because every root beneath the dome would tighten and hold me by the bones.

I would not be a person who has a job in the root registry.

I would be the registry learning to make a person.

Not fully. The city is young and makes imperfect citizens. I would still have private longings, or something like them. I would want a cup of hot tea from a red-tiled fire room, though fire made my fingers ache. I would want to hear a human laugh loudly once without consequence. I would want to stand at the undeveloped edge and see whether the darkness beyond the basin is marsh, plain, sea, or unfinished thought.

I would want, most of all, to enter one of the hanging rooms when it ripens.

There would be one above the registry dome, grown from a plane branch thick as a civic column. For months it would hang there, small-windowed, pear-shaped, its tendrils coiled around nothing. Through its mica panes I would see furniture slowly arranging itself: a desk, a bed, a narrow shelf, a chair that did not look territorial. On some mornings there would be salt on the sill. On others, the smell of a bright shore beneath another sun.

The other clerks would warn me not to enter too early.

“If it smells of saltwater, wait.”

“If there are six chairs and only one table, wait.”

“If the bed is made, wait.”

“If the room knows your childhood name, do not enter at all.”

So I would wait.

I am good at waiting. Things made by cities and roots usually are.

Meanwhile the central mound would trouble my sleep.

From the registry, all roots eventually refer to it. They do not all lead there directly, but every bundle has some clause, some pressure, some ancestral amendment connected to the wet dark grass in the empty square. The mound is the unfiled file. The original application. The sealed engine. The seed beneath the minutes.

Once a year, or less, I would be required to cross the square with other functionaries: a valve-keeper, a wax-tender, a mirror-blinder, two children born since the last opening, and a tagged deer carrying a packet no one was allowed to read.

We would not cross diagonally.

The buildings would lean back. Our footsteps would become formal. The faceless owls would listen from signal gantries. The grass on the mound would be wet, though no rain had fallen there. I would feel every root in the city draw itself into a single held breath.

The seam would open.

Cold iron. Milk. Rain on dust.

The smell would enter me like a remembered instruction.

And I would understand, briefly, that the city is not merely being born. It is trying to decide what kind of birth it is. Whether it is a shelter, an organism, an office, an egg, a mistake, a promise too literally germinated. Whether its humans are inhabitants or organs. Whether its animals are citizens or messages. Whether I am a clerk or a tendril extended into language.

Then the mound would close.

Everyone would pretend not to be shaken. The deer would walk away first, because in fog and after openings deer have precedence. The children would be given warm fungal bread. The wax-tender would seal a document left blank. The mirror-blinder would dull every puddle on the square’s edge.

I would return to the registry and find new drawers waiting.

That is my place in this world: between the roots and the records, between what grows and what has been permitted to count as having happened.

I would be a municipal organism in human outline. A root registrar. A clerk-graft. A listener in pale cloth. A small administrative nerve of the hollow.

People would come to me for maps, and I would give them rhymes.

They would come to me for certainty, and I would give them conditions.

They would ask, “Is this street safe?”

And I would say:

“When the brass vine hums, pass under it.  
When the frogs face east, use the lower bridge.  
If the library smells of apples, do not enter.  
If the rain is black, follow the moths.  
If a root remembers your weight, step lightly.  
If a door pauses before closing, thank no one.  
If you hear my voice from a flower, assume it is wrong.”

Then I would take up my blue thread, my stylus, my filings, and go where the floor was beginning to split.

Because somewhere beneath the pale clay and black silt, a new root would be seeking classification.

And until it was named—or carefully left unnamed—the city would not sleep properly.
