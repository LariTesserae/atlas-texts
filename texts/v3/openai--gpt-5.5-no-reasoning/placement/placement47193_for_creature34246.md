I would find myself in the central court, not at its center but just off it—where the walkways bolt around the black stone and the rain coming down through the cracked apparatus is fine enough to seem like breath.

Not a visitor, exactly. Not one of the vanished builders. Something more local than a person, quicker than the settlement but not separate from it.

I think I would be a gauge.

Or what a gauge becomes after years of being touched by root, vapor, mineral, and impossible pressure.

A small brass-faced instrument mounted to the black stone spire at about chest height, beneath a lip of riveted iron where water gathers before dropping into the pool below. My glass is always filmed. My needle does not point to numbers anymore, because the numbers have gone green under lichen and condensation. Around my rim, the screws have darkened; one has split its washer; one has been entered by a hair-thin root that comes through the stone itself and curls behind my dial.

I was built to measure something specific. Head pressure, perhaps. Uplift. Internal seepage. A gradient between the young mountain and the sealed water-world clasped around it. Once, a person could have come with a clipboard, wiped my face with a sleeve, tapped the glass, written down my trembling answer, and gone away.

But now I do not measure for them.

I measure for the place.

I am one of its little listening organs.

The settlement feels through me in a narrow way. Not broadly, not with the full slow mind of tanks and rains and root curtains, but with a tiny exactness: a shiver in the stone before a chamber floods; the sideways crawling of water along a fracture; the difference between ordinary condensation and the special wetness that gathers when the spire dreams downward. My needle moves in response to pressures no longer named. Sometimes it turns clockwise for weeks. Sometimes it rests for so long that algae forms a crescent on the inside of my glass. Sometimes, when the cloud outside thickens and the upper tanks begin to sweat, it quivers as if hearing footsteps far below.

I cannot walk through the place, but I know its cycles by consequence.

When the lower workshops fill, a deep warmth travels through the stone and comes into my mounting bracket. When the floating gardens rotate in their cisterns, my needle leans a fraction toward the drowned stair. When roots thicken in the western stairwell, they draw mineral from the spire and my brass tastes faintly of copper. When the interior rainstorm gathers in the upper dome, droplets collect on my face faster than gravity can remove them, and the world beyond my glass becomes only green light, black stone, and falling water.

I am not alive in the way the plants are alive. I do not grow leaves or seek light. But I have been included.

That is what happens here. Nothing useful remains merely itself.

The grates become riverbeds.  
The pipes become veins.  
The lamps become artificial fruit.  
The drowned benches become reefs for roots.  
The old signs become shelves for moss.  
And I, once an instrument, have become a nerve-ending with a brass eyelid.

I would be aware of the visitor first as weather.

A warm salt column entering the court. A disruption in the delicate breathing of vapor. A shape that sheds heat unevenly and takes water from the air with its skin. Their steps would not be loud, but the walkway would answer them; the bolts would carry the tremor into the stone; the stone would pass it to my case. My needle would stir.

If they came close and wiped my glass, I would see them only as a clearing: a brief round absence in the condensation, a face broken by droplets, eyes reflecting lamps, hair dampened instantly, mouth open as though the air were almost drinkable.

They might think I was broken.

Then my needle would move.

Not much. Enough.

Behind them, somewhere in the wall, a valve would open with a soft, courteous turn. Water would begin to drain from a half-submerged corridor that had been impassable a moment before. Lamps would wake one by one beyond the blue pressure door. A root curtain would sway although no wind moved through the court.

The visitor might believe I had answered.

But I would only have translated a pressure into a gesture.

That is the language here.

Not words, not signs, not even intention as animals understand it. Only relation: level to level, heat to vapor, mineral to root, breath to condensation, touch to flow.

If I have a self, it is very small and very old-fashioned. It lives in the trembling of the needle, in the tension of a spring that should have corroded but has not, in the sealed chamber behind the dial where a little dry air may still remain from the day I was made. That tiny trapped dryness is my private room. My impossible relic. My soul, perhaps: a bubble of the former world preserved inside an instrument made to measure the new one.

The settlement tolerates this dryness because it is useful.

Or because it has not yet found me.

Or because even a wet intelligence needs one small place where the rain cannot enter, so that difference may continue to exist.

Around me, the young mountain rises and disappears into the ceiling machinery. Water runs over it in shining threads. Ferns grip its cuts. Brass collars hold it as if restraining an animal, or honoring a saint. Below, the central pool reflects more depth than it possesses. Above, lamps glow in rain-fog, and vines hang through platforms like green cables.

I remain fixed between stone and court, between measurement and prayer.

A little circular eye that cannot see.  
A little mouth that cannot speak.  
A small, obedient relic still reporting the pressure of a world becoming alive.
