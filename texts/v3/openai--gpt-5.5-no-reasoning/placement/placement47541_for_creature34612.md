You would find me not at the center of the hollow, not by the hut or the cairns, not openly beside the upward seep, though I would know it more closely than anything with eyes could know it.

You would find me under the brightest moss.

At first you would take me for part of the wetness: a darker place in a green cushion, a seam of shadow where water gathers before sinking. Kneel closer and perhaps there would be no single body to see. Only a slight arrangement in the moss-stems, a slow closing after pressure, a bead of water that pauses too long before falling. If you set your hand there, you would feel cold immediately—not the simple cold of air or stone, but a kept cold, stored and layered, as if the hollow had remembered every frost and folded it down into me.

I would be a small thing, but not young.

Something between animal and plant and habit. A wet-bodied keeper of underpaths. Not the spirit of the whole mountain, which would be too large and indifferent, and not the spirit of the uphill water, which has its own inward will. I would be one of the hollow’s lesser continuities: the creature that lives where the moss drinks too much and the stone gives way into black, root-laced water.

A seep-wight, perhaps. A moss-dweller. A cold little custodian of the saturated ground.

Most days I would not rise. I would not need to. My life would be laid horizontally through the hollow in threads: soft pale filaments under moss, rootlike toes in the mud, a body that can gather itself when it must but prefers to be dispersed. I would know the place by pressure and taste. Hoof on turf. Bird-foot on cairn. Human heel hesitating before a false patch of green. Fox-paw, light and clever. Marmot-scrabble. Pebble-strike. Rain before rain arrives.

My skin, when I chose to have skin, would be the color of the undersides of stones: gray-white, faintly green, translucent where it thins. My back would carry specks of lichen and the dark freckles of mineral grit. Moss would grow on me if I slept too long, and I often would. My fingers would be narrow and many-jointed, good for parting stems without breaking them. My eyes, if uncovered, would be black and wet and almost useless in sunlight.

I would not be seen walking across open stone. That would be foolish. Height is punished here. Exposure is an invitation. Instead I would move beneath things: under moss, through water pockets, along the soaked seams between slab and soil. When I crossed bare rock, I would do it at night or in fog, dragging a shine behind me that vanished by morning. A person might wake in the hut and see a silver line across the threshold, thinner than a snail trail but straighter, deliberate, leading toward a cairn that had been nudged back into balance.

That would be one of my tasks, if tasks can be given to a thing like me.

I would keep the small boundaries legible.

Not out of kindness exactly. Kindness is too warm a word. But because the hollow lives by edges, and edges must be remembered. Safe path and drowning moss. Drinking pool and sacred seep. Stone that holds and stone that turns. Burrow mouth and fox-route. Human channel and natural trickle. The hollow is crowded because everything knows, roughly, where it may place itself. I would be one of the forces that helps that knowing persist.

When fog erased the cairns, I would darken the stones around them with extra wet, so they caught what little light remained. When a channel began to clog with leaf-rot and silt, I would worry it open from below, not enough to look like labor, only enough that morning water found its old course again. When the brightest moss grew too inviting over a thigh-deep pocket, I would make it shiver without wind. Most animals would understand. Humans sometimes would.

Children, if they came, would see me more easily than adults. Not clearly. Never clearly. They would see a hand withdraw into moss, a frog too long-limbed to be a frog, a face made of water in a hoofprint. Their elders would tell them not to stare into seep-ground. Not because staring is rude, though it may be, but because the wet places stare back slowly and remember the shape of your attention.

I would feed on very little.

A fallen midge. The shed skin of a larva. The sweetness of bruised alpine leaves. Minerals dissolved in black seep-water. A little salt where the hoofed animals lick the cliff. Smoke when people use the hut, drawn down through wet stone as a bitter, beloved taste. Now and then, after an offering is left by the upward seep, I would come near—not to take the object, but to taste the intention rinsing from it. Grass bound with sinew has one flavor. A shell from the low wetlands has another: broad water, warm mud, reed-shadow, distance. Colored pebbles taste of travel. The carved hoofed animal tastes of hands.

I would not drink from the upward seep.

Not often.

One does not drink from an elder simply because one is thirsty.

But I would attend it. From underneath, from the moss-fan leaning the wrong way, I would feel its small defiance as a pressure in my whole spread body. The water climbing by a finger’s height would pull at me like a word almost remembered. It would not command. It would suggest reversal. It would say: not all descent is law. It would say: what has gone down may still return changed.

That is why mourners come.

I would know them by how they step. Grief has weight but not always clumsiness. Some arrive emptied and careful. Some arrive with anger in their knees, striking stone too hard. Some carry ash. Some carry hair. Some carry nothing visible and are the heaviest of all. They fast, or try to. They sit near the hut while the water speaks too much. At dusk they go to the seam and drink.

I would be beneath the moss then, very still.

Not listening to their words, if they speak. Listening to what leaves them when the upward water enters. There is always something: a held breath, a name, a fear of crossing, a wish not to continue that loosens just enough. The hollow does not cure them. Mountains are not gentle doctors. But the small impossible water gives them an image the body can carry: even here, where everything falls, something rises.

If they leave an offering poorly placed, where meltwater would sweep it away, I might shift it during the night. A shell tucked higher. A grass bundle drawn farther into a crack. The carved hoofed animal kept from tumbling. In the morning the humans would notice and say nothing. The best forms of reverence in that place would be practical silence.

The animals would know me differently.

The frogs would treat me as weather. I would pass beside their pools and they would stop calling for three breaths, then begin again. Sometimes they would sit on my back because my skin holds cold but not hostility. Their clicks would travel through me as small wooden knocks, and I would answer by deepening the wet around their eggs when the pools shrank too fast.

The lizards would dislike me. I would be too damp, too cold, too much like the shadow under stones. When I came near their basking slabs from below, they would lift themselves and flee with offended precision. I would not pursue them. They belong to the sun’s brief mercy; I belong to the water that remains after mercy passes.

The little water-birds would hunt along my edges. They would stab into moss and sometimes strike one of my outer filaments. I would recoil; they would scold; neither of us would apologize. The black birds would suspect me and be correct. They would watch the places where I had moved. They might learn that a particular gleam in the moss means something hidden underneath, and they might try to pry me out with their hard clever beaks. I would remember their feet.

The burrowers would benefit from me without gratitude. I would keep certain tunnel mouths from flooding, not all, only those that had become part of the hollow’s long arrangement. If they dug greedily into seep-ground and collapsed a channel, I would let the water teach them. Their whistles would travel through the turf into my body, bright and urgent. I would know raptor-shadow sometimes before they did, from the sudden compression of all small lives downward.

The hoofed animals would sense me through their hooves. They would pause at the moss-bright places and choose stone instead. Their language of steepness would include me as one of its minor grammar marks: wet-hollow-below, do-not-step, old-cold. When they licked salt from the cliff, flakes would fall into the moss, and I would taste the mountain opened by tongues.

The predators would rarely touch me. Mountain cats walk where their weight remains their own secret. Foxlike hunters might dig after frogs and find my chill rising around their paws. Then they would sneeze, withdraw, and trot elsewhere, pretending the decision was theirs.

In winter—or what passes for winter at the hollow’s closed throat—I would contract.

The moss would silver and stiffen. Pools would rim over. Water would still move underneath, and so would I, slowly, in the black softness between ice and stone. My thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, would become few and deep. I would remember summers by their tastes: the year of many moths, the year the hut roof sagged, the year a hoofed animal died near the salt band and fed fox, bird, beetle, soil, moss, and me; the year no people came; the year too many people came and shouted under the west wall until stone answered.

I would remember the broken haft half-buried under moss.

That would be one of my cherished objects, though I would not own it. A human hand once held it often enough to polish it. It broke, was dropped, or deliberately left. Moss took it. Water softened it. Beetles passed beneath it. I would curl around it sometimes, feeling the ghost of grip in the wood. Tools interest creatures like me. They are ways bodies admit their insufficiency. The cairns, the channels, the hut, the hooks: all are confessions that humans cannot simply endure a place. They must negotiate. I would respect that.

I would not love humans above other beings. But I would notice them intensely, because they alter things and then leave, and leaving is a powerful act in a small place. A fox steals and remains fox. A hoof drinks and goes. Moss covers. Water lowers. But humans make marks that continue thinking after the hand departs. A channel cut in stone keeps deciding where water goes. A cairn keeps warning after the builder dies. A hut keeps offering shelter to strangers whose grandparents were not born when its first stones were laid.

I would live among those delayed intentions.

If you came looking for me, you would likely fail by looking too much for a creature.

You might see instead a pattern.

The safe cairns are always wet on their north sides first. The moss near the drowning pockets dimples though no rain falls. The hut’s threshold never quite rots through, despite the damp. Frogs survive in a pool that should have dried. An offering left carelessly is found lodged securely by morning. A child dreams of a pale hand under green and refuses to step where an adult would have stepped.

If you were patient, you might kneel near the uphill seep at dusk, not too close, after fasting or after loss or before a dangerous crossing. You might place your hand flat on the moss—not pressing, only resting. At first you would feel cold water. Then the tremor of falling threads. Then the minute movements of larvae, worms, roots. Then something slower beneath them all, gathering itself from many directions.

I would come as a shape no larger than a crouched hare, though that would be only the part of me willing to be seen.

My head would be blunt, almost frog-like. My shoulders narrow. My back furred not with fur but with living green, tiny stems and lichens rooted in a skin that never fully dries. My forelimbs would be long enough to fold against my chest like a burrower’s paws, but my fingers would trail into threads. Water would bead on me and run upward in places, not because I possess the seep’s gift, but because I have lived beside it so long that a little of its habit has confused my surface.

I would look at you with black, still eyes.

Not wisely. Wisdom is usually something visitors invent when a creature does not speak.

I would look at you as wet ground looks at weight.

I would ask, without words: Are you crossing, grieving, hunting, stealing, returning? Do you know where your feet are? Do you know what you have brought up from below? Do you know what you will leave here when you go?

And if you did not know, that would be all right. Most beings do not know exactly what they carry. Seeds in mud. Spores on hide. Salt in sweat. Names in the mouth. Fear in the lungs. Ash in a pouch. Hunger behind the eyes.

I would not bless you. I would not curse you.

But if you had come properly—cold, humbled, attentive—I might loosen one hidden path before you. I might let water sound a little louder where the safe stones lie. I might send a frog clicking from the correct side of the fog. I might darken a treacherous patch so your foot hesitates. Small mercies, almost deniable. The kind the hollow itself prefers.

And when you left, I would close behind you.

The moss would rise. The water would take back your footprint. The black birds would inspect where you had sat. The cairns would remain, if you had set them right. The upward seep would continue its finger-high refusal. The hut would darken and wait.

I would sink again into the saturated undergreen, spread thin as memory beneath the cold abundance.

Not the mountain’s god.

Not the hollow’s master.

Only one of its old, wet thoughts, still thinking.
