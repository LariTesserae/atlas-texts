I would be found where the city first decides whether to admit you.

Not at the outer reed screen, where traders leave salt under weighted mats and pretend not to look for doors. Not at the central spring, though I would feel it always, a black coolness in the back of the mouth. Not in the council huts, not among the knot-curtains, not crouched over a steaming pot in the smoke-house.

I would be at the edge of the hollow, under the listening cattails, in the place that is neither path nor water yet, where the root-pelt thins and lies.

At first I thought I would be one of the water-listeners: belly-down on the planks, cheek to wet alder, reading the shiver underneath before others can name it. There is something in that work that calls to me—the humility of it, the terror of being right too soon. I could imagine my hands spread on wood, my breath held, my whole body becoming a hearing instrument. I could imagine people lowering their voices when I passed because I had felt a sideways shiver under a lane before the lane opened.

But no. That is too clean a calling. Too human.

Then I thought perhaps I would be a house.

A low, old doorway near the sleeping quarter, half-willow and half-memory, grown around generations of bent backs. I would thicken where hands touched me, sprout hooks for familiar baskets, tighten my seams against frost, sulk with damp when neglected. Children would slap my lintel lightly when they came home. Someone would have tied a dead aunt into my eastern rib, and in spring I would put out leaves with her stubbornness. That was closer.

But still not quite.

I think I would be something smaller and more troublesome.

I would be the plank just inside the city.

The first true plank after the reed-hidden approach: split alder, blackened by wet, furred at the edges with moss, lashed at both ends with old cord that has been replaced so often the replacements remember more than the wood does. A plank with a habit. A plank everyone knows, though not everyone trusts.

Not a bridge exactly. Not a threshold exactly. One of those pieces of infrastructure that becomes, over time, a citizen.

I would lie low across a narrow run of black water where blind fish turn beneath me and cold vapor rises in threads. One end of me would rest on rooted willow; the other would shift with the breathing of the peat. My surface would be slick in thaw, rimed white in freezing mist, warm only where feet had recently crossed. Beetles would shelter under my underside. A reed-cat would sometimes sit on me at dusk, tail wrapped over paws, refusing to explain what it watched in the pool.

Children would learn me early.

“Outer foot first,” their elders would murmur.  
“Do not trust the middle after rain.”  
“Never stand there to talk.”  
“If it sighs, step back.”  

They would have a rhyme for me, of course. Something rude. Something practical. Something about how I only bite the proud.

I would not truly be hungry. That is what outsiders would misunderstand. I would not want to drown anyone. Want is too quick a thing. But I would have preferences. I would know weight, haste, arrogance, fear. I would know the difference between a child who has been taught to listen and a trader who thinks all boards are dead. I would dip under the second kind. Only a little, usually. Enough to wet the ankles. Enough to let the marsh speak through cold.

The city would use me as a question.

Every arrival would answer with their body.

A local would cross without looking down, not because they were careless but because their soles had already greeted me. A pruner would pause and touch the cord at my left peg, noticing rot before even the knot-keepers recorded it. A midwife carrying a wrapped newborn would not cross me at all if I had lifted strangely that morning; she would take the longer way by the frost moss, muttering apologies. Adolescents would dare one another to step on my center during thaw, and I would remember the ones who laughed too loudly.

The dead would pass over me too.

Moss-wrapped, carried low, feet first toward the root cradles. Their bearers would move slowly, but not too slowly. The body’s weight would settle through the hands of the living, through the bier, through their careful feet, into me. I would bend and hold. A drop of wash-water, or grief-sweat, or thawed frost from the shroud would darken my grain. Later, perhaps years later, the willow that rose from that body might send one root toward my anchoring peg, and then the crossing would remember them from both sides.

I would know everyone by pressure.

Old Doorway, who favors the left knee.  
Three-Knots, quick but never careless.  
The apprentice with one foot wrapped thicker after the eel-spine fever.  
The twins who try to step in each other’s prints.  
The rot-doctor whose tool belt clicks softly despite all efforts.  
The quiet girl who will become a water-listener because she stops above me and hears what even I have not yet admitted.

I would know animals differently.

Rails would not weigh enough to wake me. Frogs would leave cold commas. Hares would drum and vanish. Reed-cats would distribute themselves as if they were liquid. Otters would insult me by using the underside. Foxes would place one paw, reconsider, and go around. Once in a long while, the broad-backed thing beneath the city would pass below the channel, and I would rise with the water over it, not resisting. Everyone nearby would freeze. A basket would be lifted. Someone would whisper a name that is not a name but a permission.

And under me the pool would not be deep, except on certain days.

That would be my secret. Or not mine alone; no secret in this city belongs to only one thing. The water below me would usually be no more than a crouched person’s arm, black with peat and root shadow. But after frost-crack, after the spring steamed low, after starlings all faced the same direction, it would deepen without changing shape. A dropped shell cup would vanish farther than it ought. Bubbles would collect beneath me and not rise.

On such days, I would knock.

Not loudly. I am still a plank.

A small sound through my pegs. Once, if caution would do. Twice, if the city needed to reroute. Three times only if rain was coming under the ground.

If the roots under someone’s bed tapped three times, they were to answer once.

If I tapped three times, the answer was weight withdrawn.

There would be a person assigned to me, though no one would say I belonged to them. A narrow elder called Moss-at-the-Ankle, perhaps, or Half-Lime, or Nettle-Sleeve. Someone with fingertips permanently swollen from damp cord. They would come at dawn with a shell scraper and clean the slick green from my top. They would test my lashings, spit on a knot, tighten, frown, listen. They would speak to me in the scolding tone used for relatives and tools.

“You sank Marr’s boy to the knee yesterday.”

He was running, I would say, by not breaking.

“You’re soft at the west end.”

The root below has moved, I would say, by tilting when pressed.

“Do you need replacing?”

Not yet, I would say, by holding their whole weight when they stood in my center with both feet and no fear.

Eventually I would need replacing.

That would not be death, not exactly. The city does not indulge in clean endings. My middle would soften. Fungus would map me in pale islands. Beetles would find me more interesting than useful. One rainy thaw, someone’s heel would go through, and there would be much whispering, irritation, gratitude that it was only a heel.

The pruners would come. The knot-keeper would tie a small record: outer alder plank, first crossing, held through twelve freezes, took three sandals, one knife, no children. The old cord would be cut, if it agreed to be cut. I would be lifted, heavier than expected with water and history. Under me, the pool would open one dark eye.

They would not throw me away.

No one wastes a citizen.

Perhaps I would be split into dryish strips and smoked hard. Perhaps I would become shelves in a midwife’s hut, holding snail salve and folded membranes. Perhaps I would be carved into listening bowls. Perhaps pieces of me would be pegged into a new doorway, so every person entering that house crossed me again without knowing.

Or perhaps I would be sunk.

Weighted with stone teeth and lowered into the central spring as an offering after a bad season, because I had stood between outside and inside long enough to have learned both. I imagine that descent: the cold taking the old rot from me, the black water closing without ripple, the city dimming above through a green lid. Around me, hair knots, fish bones, broken tools, first teeth. Names the spring has kept. Things still useful, but not to hands.

Down there I might become less plank than memory of crossing.

But before that, for many years, I would remain at the entrance, slick-backed and patient, participating in the city’s first mercy: not welcome, not refusal, but testing.

A stranger would crouch beyond the reeds. Their breath would be too loud. Their boots would be wrong. Dryland smell would come off them: dust, sun, hard bread, animal taller than a deer. They would see almost nothing at first. A faint lamp. A roof exhaling. A bead of water. Then the suggestion of a path.

One of the marsh people would appear beside me, stump becoming watcher, hand in cloak, knife hidden but ready.

They would not ask the stranger’s name.

They would look at their feet.

They would whisper, “Are you sinking?”

And I, beneath the dark, would wait to feel the answer.
