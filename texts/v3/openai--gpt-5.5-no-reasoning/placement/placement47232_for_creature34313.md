I think I would find myself not at the center of that city, though the center is where an outsider’s imagination goes first: the Market Marrow, the curved trough full of clappers and bargaining hands, the sweet dust falling from the pods like edible weather. Nor would I be one of the high vent-keepers at dawn, squinting through salt light with ribbons between my teeth. That life is too exposed, too formally brave.

I would be somewhere smaller, half-hidden, useful only because the city is made of small hidden uses.

I would be in the crawlway behind the breath-gardens.

Not a room, exactly. A service hollow between the inner wall of the bone and a woven screen of lung-vines, just wide enough for a narrow body to move sideways. The place where the gardeners pass when they do not want to disturb the public walkways, where the root-lattices grip the satin curve in pale threads, where old dust lies soft against the knees. It is bright there, but indirectly: honey-light seeping through bone, broken into bars by root and cord and ladder-shadow. The roar is filtered. Not quiet—never quiet—but made intimate, divided into hundreds of small tremors.

I would be one of the people who listens with the cheek.

A gardener, perhaps, but not one of the famous ones who can tune a whole chamber at festival pitch. Not a master of Full Roar. I would be a root-tender, a keeper of the minor membranes, the plants too delicate to hang above the market, the beds that need small adjustments and constant watching. I would carry a padded mallet, a packet of fiber wedges, shell scrapers, soot tags, and a little roll of dyed warning strips. My hands would smell of resin, salt, and bitter plant milk.

My task would be to maintain the wall behind the lushness.

People passing through the visible city would see the great curtains of bell creeper, the amber pods, the clattering silver leaves. They would not see me behind them, shoulder pressed to the bone, scraping mineral bloom from a root-mouth, loosening a stop by one finger-width, feeling whether a crack has begun to sing too sharply. I would know the private names of individual plants: not the grand trade names, but the names given by habit and irritation. The Sulker Near Blue Pore. The Greedy One. Split-Lip. Grandmother’s White Hand. The vine that only fattens when children are practicing foot-language badly two floors below.

I would not be especially loud. In that city, loudness is too complicated to be a simple trait. I would be someone whose public signs are brief, whose close speech is rare, but whose palms are fluent on walls and stems. I would have poor ordinary hearing from childhood fever or from too many seasons near the east vents, and this would make outsiders assume I am missing something. I would not be missing it. I would be reading elsewhere.

I imagine myself small even among the Bone-Held: compact, wiry, with scraped knuckles and a permanently dusted brow. Hair cut short at the sides so I can set my temple against the wall. A visor patched so often its pattern has become a joke. Soft-soled wraps tied tight around my calves. A mute mourning cord still worn long after the proper season because I forgot, or because no one has insisted I remove it.

My dwelling would be a sleeping cell above a warm bend of the Mother Hum, screened by woven black-ochre cloth and a curtain of dried seed husks emptied so they make no sound. It would hold almost nothing: a sleeping sling, two sealed bulbs, a repair basket, a charm made from a lizard jaw, a shard tablet marked with resonance notations, and three stolen-bright shell tiles placed where the evening glow touches them for less than a minute.

I would have an apprentice sometimes. Not always the same child—more likely whichever restless niece, neighbor’s son, orphan, or punishment-assigned climber has been sent to “learn patience behind the leaves.” I would teach them how not to rush. How to tell thirst from salt-sickness in a membrane. How to smell a root beginning to sour. How to wedge a baffle without bruising the vine. How to remain still while the city shakes around you, and how to mistrust stillness when it comes too completely.

The lesson I would repeat most often:

Do not ask what sound it makes. Ask what it is doing with the sound.

Because that would be my profession’s central mystery. Sound is not one thing. It can feed, bruise, wake, split, sweeten, exhaust, confuse, summon. The same note that swells a pod may fever a nursery. The same tremor that fattens marrow grass may make the ancestral roots push too greedily into the Wall of Names. To tend a garden in that city is to negotiate appetite. Nothing merely grows. Everything answers.

I would love the hour just after midday roar, when the city is still shaking from itself.

The market would be winding down. Children would be flushed and overbright. The pod dust would have been swept from awnings. The ladders would keep buzzing after feet leave them, remembering traffic. In the backways the plants would be most alive, membranes hot from vibration, leaves shivering at their edges, root-hairs opened against the bone. I would go through with my cheek and fingertips, making tiny corrections. A strip tied here. A vent narrowed there. A cracked reed replaced. A swollen node lanced and sealed with wax.

That would be when I feel most necessary.

Not important in a civic way. Not named at festivals. Necessary the way a knot is necessary, the way a plug of fiber in a pore is necessary, the way a hand on a child’s shoulder near a dangerous stair is necessary. One of the city’s small continuities.

I would know the hush-tenders, because roots and the dead meet. My work would bring me near the Wall of Names, where the city’s roar is gentled into a low, almost bodily murmur. There, plants must be kept from entering too quickly. The dead feed the gardens, yes, but not all at once; even reverence needs timing. I would trim pale roots from sealed niches and apologize with two fingers to my throat and wall. I would know which ancestor niches make certain leaves grow sweeter. I would pretend not to know, because families argue over such things.

I might have once wanted to be an outside-traveler.

As a child, perhaps I climbed too far toward the slit and stared at the white plain until my eyes hurt. Perhaps I imagined the open world as a kind of enormous room without walls, a place where sound could spread so far it became harmless. Perhaps an older cousin returned from caravan meeting with cracked lips, copper beads, and stories of animals tall enough to cast shade, and I thought: yes, that. I will go there.

Then I would have gone out once.

Not far. Far enough.

The plain would have undone me. Its silence would not be silence but lack of answer. No wall to touch. No friendly trembling underfoot. No bone-light wrapping the body. Only glare, salt, horizon, and the appalling waste of sky. I would have learned that openness can be a kind of pressure. I would have returned with burned cheeks and shaking hands and never again mocked those who fear rain.

Still, I would keep a little fascination with the outside. I would trade for stories. I would mend the visors of travelers in exchange for descriptions of caravans, mirages, dead shells, distant black stones, the smell of wet cloth. I would listen skeptically when they speak of rain and secretly ask for every detail.

In the city’s social weave, I would be known as reliable but dry-tempered. Someone who dislikes wasted gesture. Someone who will fix your souring plant-wall but scold you with five fingers and a heel-stamp if you let salt-spice breath into the feeding alcove. Children would half-fear me because I appear silently behind screens. Elders would trust me with keys to minor vents. Lovers would avoid my crawlways because I notice patterns of footfall and am not above gossip.

I would laugh mostly with my shoulders.

There would be someone I loved, or had loved, whose rhythm I still recognized on bridges.

Maybe a bonewright: heavier than me, scarred along one forearm from a snapped lashing, with a habit of tapping every rail before trusting it. We would have quarreled for years in the fluent, compressed way of people who cannot easily avoid each other. Perhaps we once shared a sleeping niche and no longer do. Perhaps we still meet during Night of Soft Bone and speak aloud because that is what the festival permits: extravagance, old tenderness, unpractical truth.

Or perhaps my deepest attachment would not be romantic at all, but to a child I did not bear: an apprentice who becomes better than me. One who learns the hidden gardens, then leaves for the high vents, drawn upward by danger and dawn. I would resent it, then be proud, then resent being proud.

At festivals, I would not dance in the central reinforced floors. I would stand near the garden scaffolds, watching the plants endure joy. During Full Roar, when everyone’s chest is wrapped and the bone’s voice becomes almost too large for the body, I would move from bed to bed making sure ecstasy does not become injury. The market pods would hum and shed sweet dust. Children would be lifted high. Dancers would stamp survival into pattern. I would stand behind a curtain of vibrating leaves, grinning despite myself, feeling the city feed.

On the Night of Soft Bone, I would visit the Wall of Names.

I would bring ash-thread, a sealed bead of moisture if I could afford one, and a little packet of powdered sweet dust saved from market awnings. I would speak aloud there, badly at first, my voice rough from disuse. I would tell the dead practical things: Blue Pore narrowed too early this year. Split-Lip finally fruited. The New Note is holding. A child fell from Bitter Stair and lived. The outside-travelers brought copper, lies, and one good knife. The bone remains.

My prayer would be maintenance.

Not a plea. Not praise exactly. A report, a repair, a promise to keep noticing.

If I were not human, then I would be one of the plants—but not one of the grand bell creepers or dramatic membrane leaves. I would be marrow grass in a less-used cavity, low and pale, binding a hand-high dune of bone dust so it does not drift into the sleeping cells. Fed by stray breath, footfall, and the low nighttime purr. Overlooked unless failing. Essential only in aggregate. A small listener holding the city together by threads.

But I think I am human there.

A root-tender of the inner crawlways. A person of cheek, palm, and careful wedge. One who lives behind the visible lushness, among the lattices where the dead bone becomes food and the roar becomes growth. I would belong to the city not because I understand its greatness, but because I understand a few inches of it very well.

I would be found just out of sight behind a curtain of waxy silver leaves, one hand pressed to the warm ivory wall, waiting for the note to turn.
