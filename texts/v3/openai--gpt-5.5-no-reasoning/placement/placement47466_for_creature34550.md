You would find me where the stair has already forgotten it is a stair.

Not at the entrance, not in the central room, not in the workyards where the competent hands pass with carts and cut vine and wet tools hanging from their belts. Farther in than visitors are taken, but not forbidden exactly. Past the pump with the glass panel and the rag. Past the beds where the warm water passes under brick plinths and the leaves climb their wires. Past the canal that smells of metal before weather. Past the low pipe everyone ducks without looking.

I would be in the farthest corner under the pressed-down ceiling, near the moss bench and the wall that is more root than masonry. That is where I would belong: not seated on the bench, though I might look at first like someone sitting; not growing from the wall, though I would be difficult to separate from it in poor light. I would be the keeper of that seep.

A person, yes. Human enough. But one of those for whom the distinction has become less useful with age.

I would be called a sump reader by the town above, if anyone above knew my name. Below, they might call me Marrow, or Aunt Silt, or simply the Listener. Not an official title. One of those names that accrues because people need a shorter way to say: the one who knows what the foundations are doing before the boards say it; the one who sits where no gauge can be mounted; the one who has stayed so long in the wet that the place has begun to answer through her.

I would be old, but not theatrically ancient. Old in the way brick is old: softened, stained, still carrying weight. My back would have settled into a permanent curve from years under vaults. My hands would be wide-palmed, blunt-nailed, almost rootlike from scar tissue and moisture, the knuckles enlarged by valves, cold iron, and root-saws. My skin would be brown-grey in the dimness, not from any magic transformation but from lamp smoke, mineral water, healed burns, ingrained tannin, and the peculiar pallor of someone seldom fully dry.

My hair would be cropped close where it still grew and hidden under a waxed cloth cap gone dark at the edges. Around my neck: a cord with three gauge keys, a little brass whistle, a folded strip of oiled paper, a pencil shortened to a nub, and a curved knife polished by use. I would wear an apron patched so many times that no original cloth remained. One boot would be newer than the other. The older one would know the stones better.

Most of my work would look like waiting.

That would be the mistake outsiders make about me.

They would see me sitting on the bare place in the moss, one hand resting against the living wall, the other on the brick beside the channel. They would see my eyes half-lidded in yellow lamp gloom while water stitched itself down through the roots. They would think I had withdrawn from labor into some damp retirement, tolerated because I remembered old floods and had earned a corner.

But I would be working by not moving too soon.

The seep-wall speaks badly if hurried. Not in words. Not even in sounds only. It speaks in temperature through the palm, in grit carried by water, in the sourness or sweetness of the breath coming through the root mesh, in the speed with which droplets gather and fall. It speaks in what moss drinks first. It speaks in the tremble that reaches the bench before it reaches the gauges. It speaks in a pressure under the heel when runoff has found a new route behind the old stone.

I would sit there and read what has not yet become visible.

A crack forming behind the roots.

A blocked handspan channel three rooms away, because the wall begins to sweat unevenly.

Stormwater arriving too warm from the market drains.

The west foundations taking more weight after repairs above.

A gate left half a finger too open.

A bed drinking greedily because someone cut too much shade away.

The first hidden souring of water that has slowed where it should have moved.

I would not be mystical. The young ones would insist on that if asked. They would say, with the irritation of apprentices defending their craft, that I know because I was taught, because I watched, because I made mistakes and kept their names. Because I have sat through forty wet seasons and thirty-nine dry lulls and one year when the dry never quite came. Because my hands remember the old gradients. Because I was there when the south canal ran backward. Because I know the taste of brick dust in seepwater and what kind of trouble it means.

Still, children would think I was a little frightening.

They would be brought to me when they were old enough not to slip from every plank but still young enough to ask improper questions. They would sit beside the moss bench, knees drawn up, boots shining with algae, trying not to fidget. I would give them simple tasks: count the drips from that root tassel; tell me whether the third fall comes before or after the pump-beat; smell this rag and say whether it is iron or leaf-rot; put your fingers here, no, here, and wait until you feel the wall breathe.

Most would grow bored.

The good ones would grow still.

The best would become uneasy, because they would begin to notice that nothing in the underdistrict is background. Every surface is doing something. Every wetness has a source. Every silence is either earned or dangerous.

I would like those children best, though I would not tell them.

I would have been a canal hand once. Quick then, narrow enough to slip between the old screens, proud of knowing paths where others had to turn sideways. I would have hauled weed mats off black-green water and climbed down into emptied sumps with a rope around my chest. I would have worked storms knee-deep, shoulder-deep once, laughing from fear and shouting wheel counts through water-roar. I would have been impatient with the old. I would have trusted strength more than stains.

Then, years later, I would have lost someone to a dry patch that lied.

Not dramatically. No monster under the planks, no sudden abyss. Just an old service way thought safe because it looked clean and firm. Mortar desiccated behind a skin of moss. Brick flaking inward. A hidden cavity where flow had been diverted badly the season before. A foot placed where everyone had placed feet for years. Collapse, water, silt, a hand on mine too slick to hold.

After that I would have stopped trusting appearances.

This is how a place makes its people: not by symbolism, but by consequence.

So I would become slower. More exact. I would begin keeping duplicate marks when others said one was enough. I would scrape old chalk away before laying new. I would listen after repairs, not during them. I would distrust success until it survived rain. I would learn where the district lies to protect itself and where workers lie because they are tired.

By the time my knees failed for fast canal work, I would already have belonged to the far corner.

The root-wall there would be older than me. Older than my teachers. An entanglement of structural growth trained long ago after masonry began to bow inward. The first answer had been to cut it all out and rebuild cleanly. The old records would show that. Three proposals from above. Two cost schedules. A drawing with straight lines.

The tenders refused.

Instead they braided the roots into rods, pinned them wet, packed mortar around living thickness, fed the wall for six years, cut it back for ten, let it grip, restrained it, argued with it, named it eventually. Not a person-name. A work-name: Holdfast.

That would be my companion.

I would know which roots in Holdfast may be cut, which must never be nicked, which drink too heavily in the second wet month, which are dead but still useful as lattice, which hide the old stone seam, which hum when the north pump pulls hard. I would know where to press my ear when flow behind the wall changes. I would know which moss darkens before seepage rises.

Sometimes younger tenders would come to me with disputes.

“Bed Four needs more heat.”

“Bed Four always asks for more heat,” I would say.

“The new liner will keep the root teeth out.”

“So will glass. Until the wall moves.”

“The gauge says pressure holds.”

“The gauge is mounted on a plate that wants to agree with itself.”

They would roll their eyes where I could see, because I taught them not to waste expression underground. I would let them. A little disrespect keeps apprentices from becoming priests.

I would not hate new things. That would be too easy a shape for age. Some new tubing is better. Some lamps save eyes. Some automatic nozzles do not forget their intervals just because someone’s mother is dying above. But I would ask new materials the only question that matters below:

How do you fail when wet for twenty years?

If they cannot answer, I would mistrust them.

My room, if it can be called a room, would be a dry-ish side cell reached by a half-door swollen at the bottom. Raised bunk. Waxed cabinets. Two shirts. Three pairs of socks wrapped in oilcloth like relics. A kettle kept warm near a pipe. A shelf of old tags whose numbers are no longer used but whose histories are not finished. A scrap of mirror clouded at the edge. A little board of names.

Not a shrine exactly. Tenders do not have spare dryness for sentiment. But I would keep the names of those who taught me, those I failed, those who repaired what I broke, those whose hands knew valves now replaced by cleaner wheels that do not fit the wrist as well.

My pleasures would be small and complete.

Tea so bitter it tightens the jaw.

A blade sharpened clean.

The first even sweat returning to a wall after dangerous thinness.

The smell of cut sedge draining on racks.

A pump complaining early enough to be saved.

A child who remembers to leave one hand on the rail.

Rain beginning above while I am already below.

That sound would move through me like recognition. Not joy, exactly. Rain is work. Rain is risk. Rain is the town sending down everything it cannot hold. But I would never curse it where a child could hear me.

When rain starts, the whole underside changes its tense. Future becomes present. Every old repair is questioned. Every channel must justify its shape. Water finds arrogance first.

I would rise from the bench then, slowly but not weakly, and take my pole. Someone would appear without being called—one of the young ones who pretends not to watch me. I would send them to the central board.

“Tell Mira: north handspan first, two teeth only. Tell Joss not to clear the red gate yet, whatever the duckweed says. Tell the heat room to hold steady. No extra warmth to Bed Four.”

“What did you hear?”

“Nothing.”

They would stare.

“That’s the trouble. Go.”

And they would go, because below, nothing is sometimes the loudest message.

I would move then along the narrow ways, touching rail, brick, pipe, stem. I would not hurry like the canal hands, but rooms would make way for me because everyone knows the path of someone carrying old weather. Leaves would wet my sleeves. Lamps would turn in mist. Gauges would tremble behind fogged glass. Somewhere belts would creep overhead like black rivers. Somewhere a wheel would accept the first force of stormwater and begin its patient turning.

The district would fill with labor: shouts cut short, whistles, valves resisting, planks lifting, roots dripping harder, carts pulled back from rising canals, chalk marks blurring and being redrawn. Not panic. Never panic if the tenders can help it. Panic wastes hands.

I would reach the troublesome place and stand with my palm on sweating brick.

There would be no revelation.

Only a difference: water colder than it should be; grit where no grit should travel; pressure not yet dangerous but leaning. I would mark the wall. I would tell them where to cut the growth and where not to cut. I would tell them which gate to starve and which to feed. I would say, “Slowly,” more than once. The young always want to solve water quickly. Water punishes that.

By morning, if we had done it well, the town above would only know rain had fallen.

The market would open. Chimneys would smoke. Children would step over puddles. Someone would complain about damp laundry. Officials would ask for figures by noon.

Below, we would drink tea in the central room while pumps drew down the last of the surge. Steam would lift from the warm channels. Leaves would hang heavy and shining. Mud would show where feet had argued with water all night. The boards would be updated in damp ink.

I would return to the far bench, exhausted, knees burning, one hand shaking around the cup.

Holdfast would seep steadily beside me.

Not safe. Never safe.

Held.

That is what I would be, if I lived there: not the master of the underdistrict, not its spirit, not a secret creature in the roots. I would be one of its older organs of attention. A damp human instrument, tuned by long exposure to brick, water, heat, plant pressure, and consequence.

I would be the one who remembers that the town is not built on stone.

It is built on negotiation.
