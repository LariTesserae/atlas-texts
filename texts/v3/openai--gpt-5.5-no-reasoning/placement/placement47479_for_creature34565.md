I would find myself low to the ground, not in the center at first, but just off it—where the child-high cairn gives the basin its one small upward thought, and where the stones around it have been placed with enough care that even their separateness has begun to feel deliberate.

Not the cairn itself. Not the resident entire. Something nearer, smaller, more local: one of the stones set apart from the others, half-sunk in the mineral crust, dark on its lower side, pale filmed on top. A stone that was once chosen by a hand and is now no longer separable from weather.

I think I would be found as a marker whose meaning has emptied out but whose placement still matters.

A palm-sized black stone, perhaps flattened on one face, lying in the ring around the cairn but not touching any of the others. Too regular to be accident, too mute to declare itself. My upper surface would be dulled by warm condensation and chalky mineral bloom. My underside would be sealed into clay, not buried deeply, but held with that intimate firmness wet ground gives to things it has accepted. If someone tried to lift me, I would come up reluctantly, dragging a skirt of mud, leaving behind a print sharper than my present outline.

I would not remember who put me there. I would remember being put down.

Not in images. In pressure.

The brief heaviness of fingers. The slight twist as I was settled into place. The nearness of other stones arranged with the same hesitation. The pause afterward, when the hand withdrew and the air closed again. Perhaps there had been words, but they would not remain as words. They would have been shortened by the basin long ago into a darkened vibration in my body, no different now from the sound of a drop falling into the covered pit.

I would be one of the resident’s small hard thoughts.

Not alive apart from it, but not wholly dissolved into it either. A minor node in its distributed body. A knuckle, maybe, or a tooth not used for biting. Something through which the basin knows spacing. Near me, water would collect in a shallow meniscus where clay has shrunk away from my edge. Gnats might stand on the surface tension there and lift off again. A threadlike worm might pass beneath me in the softened seam, and I would know it only as a brief loosening below.

My world would be contact and interval.

Above: warm dim air, pressing without weight.

Below: clay, close and slow.

Beside: other stones, each near enough to belong to the same arrangement, each held apart by a measured gap now bridged in places by crust and damp.

I would have no wish to move. Movement would seem like damage, like being made strange to myself. My form of existence would be to continue being exactly where I had been left, while everything around me altered by almost nothing.

A drop would fall from the cairn’s cupped top and strike near me once in a long while. Rings would try to widen through the shallow water at my side and fail against my edge. The shock would pass into me as a small bright fact, then dull. Rain would thicken the clay around me. Dryness would draw pale lines along my rim. Mineral would gather, season after season, until I was less an object on the ground than a slight decision the ground had made.

I would be aware of visitors by their disturbance of nearness.

A footstep would not frighten me. It would arrive first as a pressure traveling through mud and fitted stone, then as shadow, then as warmth, then as the small rearrangement of water. If someone knelt by the cairn, their breath might bead on my surface. If they touched me, I would darken beneath the hand. If they lifted me, I would become suddenly exposed—too light, too separate, unbearably distinct from the wet hollow shaped to receive me.

But if they set me down again, even in a slightly different place, the basin would begin its correction. Clay would rise. Water would find the new angle. Silence would gather over the error. In time even displacement would look original.

That would be my knowledge: that almost everything can be made old if it remains.

I would not be the keeper itself, but I would be kept so thoroughly that the difference would hardly matter. The resident would pass through me in its low way. When the basin grew saturated after rain, I would feel continuity return: hidden films connecting the cairn, the clay seams, the pits, the causeway stones, the slick black pockets under tilted slabs. In drier spans, I would become more solitary, an island of remembered placement in a tightening crust. But beneath me there would always be damp. Enough to hold. Enough to wait.

If I had a voice, it would be the small scrape made when grit shifts under my edge.

If I had a face, it would be the wet sheen on my flattened top, reflecting no image clearly.

If I had a desire, it would not be devotion, hunger, escape, or sleep. It would be only this: that whatever is set down near me remain long enough for its purpose to soften.

A bone bead, a flake of worked stone, a shard of clay, a fingertip’s smear of pigment, a tool forgotten in hesitation, a name spoken and immediately shortened by the damp air—all of these I would receive as neighbors. I would not understand them. I would help make them belong.

So I would be found near the central cairn, in the deliberate ring where none of the small stones touch.

I would be a placed stone.

One of the old intervals.

A bit of arrangement that outlived intention.

And through me the basin would continue its quiet labor: taking the sharpness from things, holding their pressure, and teaching them the slow grammar of remaining.
