I would not be at the center at first.

That would be too much presumption in a place where centers are organs, not plazas; where arrival is not granted by walking inward but by being measured, abraded, sounded, and found temporarily harmless. I would come to myself in one of the marginal districts, where the old city has been planed low by wind and the street plan persists as a dark pattern under living mat. Knee-high walls. Foundations like worn teeth. Half-buried thresholds that no longer admit bodies, only currents. The plateau edge somewhere near enough that the air has an outward hunger.

I would be in the low quarter of listening ground.

Not a citizen. Not a predator. Not one of the high trunks with lightning in its crown. Something smaller, later, derivative. A creature allowed because it performs a function no single root or tower can perform quickly enough.

I think I would be a runner of pressure and shadow: a narrow, many-jointed animal that lives between the open courts and the mat-archives, a courier not of messages exactly, but of disturbances.

At rest I would be almost indistinguishable from debris.

A length of blackened rib. A strip of bark peeled from an old wall. A dry, articulated thing lying in the lee of a broken stair. My body would be low and long, built from overlapping mineral scales the color of violet ash. Six limbs, perhaps eight when counting the small anterior handling hooks folded beneath the chest. Not large. The length of a human forearm, maybe a little more; light enough that the mats do not harden defensively under me, heavy enough to impress them with a readable signature.

My feet would be my importance.

Each foot would end not in claws, but in small tense pads fringed with stiff sensory hairs and piezoelectric needles. When I ran, I would not run silently. Silence belongs to hunters and certain kinds of danger. I would make a deliberate ticking, a patterned bead-rattle, legible to the ground. Each step would produce pressure, charge, and a tiny coded percussion. My species would have evolved—or been cultivated—to cross the managed absences without being mistaken for waste, prey, or infection.

The city would know us by rhythm.

We would live in cracks at the borders of open plazas, in the narrow thermal seams between old stone inclusions and newer root-skin. We would nest communally, though not tenderly. There is little tenderness here. Our chambers would be dry capsules lined with shed scale, powdered bark, metallic dust, and thin curled fragments of dead membrane. The young would hatch sealed, already thirsty for charge rather than fluid, their first meal a paste of scraped salts and symbiotic powder regurgitated dry from the adults’ throat-pouches.

We would carry gardens, as many animals here do.

Along my back would be a set of shallow grooves filled with pale gray and rust-colored cultures: lichenoid powders, mineral yeasts, electrostatic spores, colonies that feed on my breath, my waste heat, the salts I collect, the charged dust that clings to me when I cross the courts during a storm-brightening. They would not be decoration. They would be metabolism extended outward. In return for shelter, they would refine trace nutrients, seal cracks in my armor, alter my scentless chemistry so that the city continues to tolerate my passage.

Perhaps they would also write on me.

After moving through one district, I would not be the same object chemically as before. Dust from a civic spire’s cleared ring would cling to my grooves. A filament from a solving street might touch me and etch my dorsal plates with microscopic scars. A mat I crossed at dawn might load my foot-needles with a particular ionic imbalance. A glider’s shadow might trigger my flank scales to open and close, admitting a new powder. By the time I reached another district, I would be carrying a composite of local conditions, not intentionally but faithfully.

That would be my role: to be a moving sample.

The city, vast and rooted, thinks deeply but not always swiftly. Its cables tighten across neighborhoods. Its trunks boom through air columns. Its shadow-pulses travel along high lines. But some knowledge needs feet. Some knowledge needs repeated crossings by small nervous bodies that can be frightened, redirected, attracted, spared, eaten, replaced.

I would belong to one of the tolerated quicknesses.

Most of my day would be spent in shadow, tucked under a lifted lip of root-pavement while the cold light crawls across the high surfaces. I would feel the city through my belly before I saw it change. A distant trunk would begin a low note. The mat beneath me would stiffen in bands. Dust would gather against the windward edge of a plaza in a pattern that means charge rising. My back-gardens would prickle, their powders aligning grain by grain.

Then I would move.

Out from the crack, across the open court, not in a straight line but in a grammar of pauses. Tick-tick-tick. Stillness. Tick. A lateral dash to a salt ridge. A forelimb lifted into the wind. The world would arrive as vibration: glider-booms above, the bead-rattle of grazing herds far off, the slow groan of a wall opening its plates, the almost subaudible knock from inside a civic spire. Sight would be secondary, useful mostly for shadow. Heat would matter more. Static more than heat. Tension more than shape.

The city would be enormous from my height.

A root-bridge would not be a bridge but a weather system overhead, a dark vaulted continent humming with traveling occlusions. The legs of browsing tripods would pass like jointed towers. Their black tongues rasping a wall would sound like stone being combed. A predator crossing a terrace would be known first by the absence of small noises, then by the cold pressure of attention. Its padded feet would not announce themselves to the pavement, which is exactly why the pavement would rely on creatures like me.

I would be afraid often.

Fear is useful motion. Fear makes maps.

When the city charges, I would emerge with many of my kind. Not because it is safe, but because it is readable. Hidden pigments flare. Bark reveals violet circuitry. Membranes pulse with green-gold veins. The air fills with blue-white crawlings from the high crowns. For larger creatures, this is confusion or opportunity. For us, it is scripture written briefly in force.

We would run the exposed fields during these illuminations, each of us drawing lines of pressure across the living ground. The mats would taste our feet. Our back-gardens would bloom in dry sparks. We would pass beneath hovering seed-pods that hum deep enough to shake grit from old thresholds. We would scatter around the kneeling browsers, avoid the shadow of gliders, freeze when the ultrasonic swarms pass and the dust trembles upright around us.

If one of us were taken by a predator, the event would not be tragedy to the city. The heat flare, the sudden pressure change, the spilled powders, the predator’s path afterward—all of it would be information. The body would become useful in stages. Scale, garden, gut-salt, polished bone. Death would not erase the route. It would emphasize it.

I would not understand the city as a mind.

That is a human weakness: wanting mind to be shaped like an inside looking out. I would know the city as response. As permission and refusal. As surfaces that harden or soften. As corridors that open because my kind has been moving correctly for generations. As certain courts where I must never step during low wind. As spire-rings where animals gather and my feet begin to pulse involuntarily, matching the deep vibration under the dust.

In those rings I would become still.

Perhaps all my kind does. We would arrange ourselves along radial dust figures, each body aligned between lines of force. The spire’s vibration would enter the pads of our feet and climb the needles of our limbs. Our dorsal cultures would lift in faint powdery nap. For several minutes or hours, we would be less animals than contact points: small removable nerves laid upon the city’s exposed thought.

Then the vibration would change, and we would break apart, carrying whatever alteration had passed through us.

I might have enemies besides predators.

Some plant-mouths would not distinguish courier from nutrient if I came at the wrong phase. Certain walls being solved would extrude filaments too rapidly to avoid. A hovering seed-pod might descend and hold me in a field of charge, turning me slowly, reading my scars, scraping one groove of symbiont clean and replacing it with another. I would survive if I were useful. I would be dismantled if I were not.

Use would be the nearest thing to law.

Yet there might be pleasures.

The warm face of a membrane at late day, when it has opened to dust and gathered a skin of edible charged film. The shelter beneath a resonator bone during high wind, where the whole world becomes one low note and predators cannot localize the feet. The dry bloom of one’s back-garden after crossing a newly molted tower, rich with century-old mineral powder. The company of others in a crack, bodies stacked without affection but with perfect conservation of heat. The rare moment after a charge-front when every surface is bright with signal and the air tastes sharp enough to feed on.

I would groom my foot-needles then, drawing them through the combs under my jaw, cleaning dust from each sensory hair. I would scrape my flanks against mineral bark to renew the scars by which the mats know me. I would press my belly to the ground and feel, far away, a street being solved.

If I became old, I would grow slower and more valuable.

My dorsal plates would be crowded with etchings. My back-gardens would contain strains gathered from districts I could no longer reach. Younger runners might follow my route not because I lead them intentionally, but because the city opens slightly where my pressure signature passes. I would know which open fields are true emptiness and which are traps of exposure. I would know the difference between a wall opening to breathe and a wall opening to eat.

At the end, I would choose a listening mat in the old low quarter near the plateau edge.

Or perhaps choice is too grand a word. I would be drawn there by cooling limbs, by failing charge, by the familiar mineral taste of the first cracks I knew. I would lie in a shallow groove between vanished street lines while the wind sawed over the foundations. The mat would lift its cilia around me. Not tenderly. Accurately.

It would read my weight.

It would read the worn patterns of my feet, the scars in my plates, the cultures in my grooves, the salts in my sealed blood, the dust of spires, courts, walls, towers, predators, gliders, seed-pods, and solved streets. It would take me apart without rot. My scales would become pavement-hardener. My foot-needles might be incorporated into a sensory fringe. My back-gardens would be distributed into cracks where young runners shelter. My small bones, polished by dryness, might be hung in a fibrous net to click in the wind.

Then I would continue as sound.

Not a soul, not memory in any sentimental form. A usable remainder. A rhythm added to the city’s dry computation. When others of my kind crossed that place, the mat might harden one breath sooner. A root-thread might redirect them from a dangerous court. A wall might recognize the old pattern carried forward in younger feet.

So I would be found there:  
near the eroded edge, in the listening ground,  
a small ash-violet pressure-runner,  
a tolerated quickness in a civilization of roots,  
carrying news without knowing language,  
writing with feet on the body of a city that thinks in wind, dust, charge, and stone.
