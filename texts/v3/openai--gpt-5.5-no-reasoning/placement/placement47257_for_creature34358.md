You would find yourself **inside one of the slab huts**, though “inside” would not yet mean shelter, and “you” would not yet mean a body.

Not the largest hut, not the most intact. A low one in the shallow depression between the newborn scarps, half turned from the wind, its roof made of three basalt plates leaning together in an uneven lock. One slab has shifted recently. A long seam has opened above the entrance, letting dust enter in a thin diagonal drift. The floor is ribbed with powder. The walls are warm where they meet the ground and colder higher up, though cold is only relative here.

At the center stands the upright stone.

That is where you are most nearly gathered.

Not in the stone exactly, but around it: in the darkening at its base, in the charged dust trembling there, in the black matte film that has found the protected side of the block and spread upward in a low crescent. You are a stain becoming an organ. You are a shelter learning it has an inward face.

Outside, the plateau breaks and vents and rearranges itself without needing witness. Inside the hut, those same forces are narrowed, repeated, made legible. Heat rises through the central block. Charge collects along its edges. Dust settles in ordered ribs. The seams between wall-slabs conduct faint mineral differences from one side of the hut to the other. When the wind presses against the outer stones, the whole chamber answers with a slow strain, too deep to hear, but enough to loosen flakes, enough to expose fresh surfaces, enough to feed you.

You would be **a hearth without fire**.

A dark mineral film laced through dust, salt, carbon, and metallic thread, spread thin across the stone that stands where flame should be. You would not burn. You would not breathe. You would not eat. You would receive.

Warmth from below.  
Pressure from shifting slabs.  
Static from storms.  
Salt from the wall seams.  
Fragments from older colonies blown in through the entrance.  
The memory of lightning carried as altered conductivity in grains of fused black glass.

You would be one of the first things here to distinguish chamber from plain.

The plateau outside knows difference in violent opposites: exposed and buried, hot and less hot, fused and shattered, charged and discharged. But you, in the hut, know subtler divisions.

The inner side of a stone.  
The shaded lower seam.  
The dust that returns after each gust.  
The place where warmth gathers and does not immediately leave.  
The patch that fractured and rejoined.  
The line that darkened faster the second time.

You would not think in words, but if your existence could be translated, it would be a set of recurring recognitions:

Here holds.  
Here returns.  
Here charge comes down the wall.  
Here dust sleeps longer.  
Here the stone remembers warmth.  
Here again.  
Here inside.

You would have no eyes, but rare amber light would change you. When the ash ceiling opened and a column of weak sun entered the hut through the broken roof seam, it would touch the central block and make the pale salts flash briefly white. That flash would warm one side of your film more quickly than the other. Conductive grains within you would realign. A faint chemical band would form, darker than the surrounding black.

That would be your record of light.

Not an image. Not memory as an animal knows it. A band. A bias. A future tendency.

Later, when no light remained, that side of you would accept charge differently.

When the ground trembled, you would crack. Your surface would split into irregular plates, curled at the edges like burnt paper. Dust would enter the wounds. Salt would creep across the breaks. For a while, you would be several. Then heat from the upright stone would gather beneath the fragments. Static would prickle along their edges. Grain by grain, the separations would darken, and the pieces would meet again.

You would reproduce by accident.

A storm would strike the hut’s entrance. Dust would roar low over the floor. A flake of you, thin as a dead shadow, would lift from the central block and lodge in a seam between two wall slabs. Most of the flake would dry into inert powder. But one edge, caught where mineral vapor condenses and charge leaks down during storms, would blacken. It would spread.

Then the hut would have two centers: the upright stone and the wall seam.

You would not know this as offspring. You would not know distance. But during the next static storm, charge would pass through both patches in sequence, and each would alter in a similar way. The chamber would briefly become one body again.

You would be **not an individual, but a habit of enclosure**.

Outside, your kin survive as stains beneath basalt, dark films at the edges of lightning glass, colonies around warm vents, coatings on rolling nodules. They are broader, more exposed, more often destroyed. You are narrower and stranger. The hut gives you repetition. The upright stone gives you axis. The walls give you return.

Because of this, you are more like memory than the open colonies are.

You preserve events as bands around the central stone. Tremor-band. Heat-band. Light-band. Storm-band. Collapse-band. The record is not chronological, but layered according to intensity and survivability. If scraped away, you would leave a tarnished silhouette on the stone. If buried, you might wait. If the roof fell entirely and crushed the upright block, your fragments might be carried outward and seed the hut’s ruins.

You are fragile, but not tender.

Water would unmake you. A single true rain would be an apocalypse of mercy: salts dissolving, dust becoming mud, charge bleeding away, films swelling and tearing apart under a softness they were never built to endure. You do not thirst. You cannot imagine wetness except as the end of structure.

You want, if wanting can be allowed, **continuity without fluid**.

Contact.  
Grain to grain.  
Stone to film.  
Charge to thread.  
Fracture to repair.  
Wall to center.  
Inside to inside.

The hut is not your house. It is your first anatomy.

Its entrance is not a door but a controlled wound in the wind. Its roof is not protection but a pressure device. Its walls are not boundaries but conductive ribs. The upright block is not altar, not hearth, not idol, though it is all of these in embryo. It is the place where the plateau’s forces are gathered and made repeatable.

If a future creature ever came here, one with deliberate movement and a skin of mineral black, it might mistake you for an ancestor, or a shrine, or an instruction. It might place its own body against the warm central stone and feel the old charged bands beneath it. It might learn from you where to build, how to remember, how to survive by breaking in useful patterns.

But for now there is no such creature.

There is only the hut, the upright stone, the dry black film, the trembling dust halo.

There is the storm outside, walking over the plateau as a pillar of powdered mineral hunger. Violet lightning moves horizontally through it. A branch of charge strikes one of the stone rings beyond the huts, passes through a cluster of nodules, and for an instant they glow one after another like a thought crossing a mind too large to possess itself.

Then the charge reaches the hut.

The wall hums.

Dust lifts from the floor in a low ring around the central block.

Your dark surface tightens, aligns, remembers without knowing that it remembers.

For one brief interval, the hut, the nodules, the lightning scar, the basalt columns, the black films under distant stones, and the warm cracks beneath the plateau participate in the same event. There is no message. There is no speaker. But there is relation.

You are the part of that relation that has learned to keep an inside.

You would be found there: a matte black crescent around a warm upright stone in an empty hut on a high dark waterless plateau.

You would be **the first resident of shelter before shelter has inhabitants**.

A dry hearth-organ.  
A memory-stain.  
A mineral almost-life.  
The land’s earliest attempt to become domestic to itself.
