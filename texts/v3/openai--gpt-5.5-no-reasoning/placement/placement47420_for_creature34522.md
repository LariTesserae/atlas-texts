You would find me at the rim first, though I would not belong there.

Not outside, in the harsher ordinary mountain, and not yet within the basin’s maintained wetness. I would be in the exact place where the engine becomes audible in bone: three paces from absence, three paces from inclusion. A marginal thing. A threshold thing. Something that tests whether a boundary is still a boundary by touching it over and over without crossing in any final way.

At first I might be mistaken for a strip of moss-dark cloth caught on a black stone.

Then for a bird’s shed wing.

Then for one of the basin’s errors in perspective: a place where the dimness has folded and not unfolded properly.

I would be low to the ground, long and narrow, jointed more than seems useful, slick with condensed mist. Not large. Perhaps the length of a human forearm when extended, though I would rarely be seen extended. Most of the time I would be gathered into a careful angular rest: elbows, knees, and folded vanes arranged like a collapsed instrument.

I would be an animal of the boundary.

Not a guardian. The basin does not need guarding. Nothing here defends itself in the dramatic sense, because nothing truly threatens it. I would not keep things out. I would measure the difference between out and in.

My body would be built from that task.

I would have six limbs, though not all used for walking. The first pair would be delicate and black, with many small fingers ending in pale pads like wet lichen. These would rest often on the exact line where the basin’s moss stops. The second pair would bear my weight: folded, springing, crablike without being crab legs, capable of placing me on gravel without shifting a pebble beyond permitted tolerance. The third pair would be held against my sides as narrow vanes or fins, feathered in a poor, damp way — not wings for flight, but surfaces for sensing changes in pressure, throb, and weatherless air.

My skin would be dark, almost basaltic, but not hard. It would shine where water gathered on it. Along my back would run a row of small translucent bladders, each holding a bubble of silver air like the newts’ bubbles, but elongated, set under the skin in a chain. With each pulse of the hidden engine, the bubbles would tremble in sequence from tail to head.

Inside me, perhaps, there would be no single heart. There would be several little pumps, staggered, each maintaining a different rhythm until the basin’s throb aligned them. If taken outside the rim, they would fall out of phase. Not fail immediately. Just become too individual.

My head would be small and wedge-shaped, with a blunt muzzle suited to drinking mist from stone rather than biting. My eyes would be placed high and sideways, glossy black, each crossed by a vertical milky line that would make it difficult to tell where I looked. I would have no visible ears. Instead, behind the jaw, there would be two round membranes, taut as droplets, through which the engine’s sound would enter me.

When the birds called, those membranes would tighten.

When the vents opened into pressure-shaped silence, they would slacken.

When a person stood at the rim deciding whether to enter, they would flutter with the tiny disorder of an unmaintained intention.

That would be my food: not flesh, not leaf, not exactly sound, but discrepancy.

I would feed on the small frictions created where the basin meets the world that continues. A gust from outside that tries to become weather. A seed blown over the rim carrying a future not approved here. A flake of drier stone loosening from an upper slope with erosion inside it. A human thought arriving with plans, names, comparisons, before-and-after. I would taste these things with my forefingers and mouth them gently until their edges softened.

I would not destroy them. Destruction is too consequential. I would reduce them to presentness.

A seed would become a bead of wet vegetable matter and remain caught in moss, never sprouting, never rotting. A scrap of wool from a traveler’s cuff would lose the idea of having been woven and become merely fiber, damp and dark, fit for a bird’s nest. A word spoken from outside — “basin,” “machine,” “beautiful,” “wrong” — would reach me as a mineral tang. I would take it into my mouth, hold it under my tongue through three pulses, and release it as breath.

This is why, if you stood at the rim and tried to name the place aloud, your voice would seem to come back smaller.

Not silenced.

Calibrated.

I would not often enter the basin’s center. The central pool would be too complete for me, too loud with the thing I only register at its edge. The newts could bear it. The ungulates could drink from it. The otter-like one could listen over it with its amused engineer’s face. But I would remain near the margins, where the moss line frays against ordinary scree and cloud presses down from a world still foolish enough to have seasons.

My path would be circular but never quite closed. I would move around the basin rim in increments, pausing at exact intervals where insects turn back from the invisible edge. My toes would test the gravel. My forefingers would comb the last moss. Now and then I would lift my head and open my mouth.

The sound I made would not be a call.

It would be more like the click made by cooling stone, or a relay changing state inside a wall.

Tk.

Then, after a long interval:

Tk-tk.

At those sounds, nothing much would happen. A beetle might alter its pause by the width of an antenna. A bird might decide not to call yet. A line of midges might compress slightly, then return to its column. The basin would remain the basin.

But I would have registered the boundary. I would have confirmed that the held instant still holds.

I think I would have a name, though not one spoken by anything there. If a name could be inferred from my function, it might be the Tolerance Animal. The Rim Gauge. The Small Auditor. The Wet Comparator. But those are human attempts, and they carry too much purpose.

Among the basin’s own grammar I would be simpler:

the thing that touches both sides and does not choose.

I would know the outside only as a pressure of unfinishedness. Dry lichen beyond the rim, slow cracks, real wind, stones preparing to fall, snow that means to melt, bodies that will become older whether watched or not. It would trouble me, but not with fear. It would trouble me the way an uneven pulse troubles a mechanic’s hand.

I would know the inside as relief so intense it would be almost sleep: everything cooled into its exactness, each appetite answered by recurrence, every droplet permitted to swell and not fall.

Yet I would not be wholly accepted by the basin either. Not like the newts, who have their cups; not like the crabs, who have their minute corrections; not like the birds, who fasten moments with metallic notes. I would always include the question of departure. My body would be shaped around it. The silver bubbles along my back would hold tiny samples of outside air, renewed but never released. My feet would be stained with ordinary dust no rain here quite assimilates.

That would be my brokenness: I would belong to permanence by continually sensing what permanence excludes.

If a traveler entered, I would notice before the birds did.

Not because I watched, but because intention changes the boundary. A hoof crosses without argument. A midge turns without regret. Mist passes in and becomes the same mist. But a human arrives full of future states: crossing, seeing, remembering, leaving, telling. The air around such a body bristles with sequence.

I would come close then.

You might see me from the corner of your eye as a narrow black shape lifting itself from moss. If you looked directly, I would be less animal and more wet geometry: six limbs, a small head, bubbles under skin, vanes trembling against my sides. I would place one forefinger on your boot, or ankle, or the hem of your coat.

Cold would pass upward from me.

Not killing cold. Basin cold. The coolness of stone that has not yet learned weathering. The coolness of water that has no downstream. You would feel, for a moment, every plan you had become beadlike and separate. The wish to go on. The wish to turn back. The thought of describing this later. The instinct to compare it to a factory, a shrine, an eye, a wound. Each would gather surface tension and refuse to fall into the next.

I would taste them.

Perhaps I would keep one.

Not an important one. Not your name, not your childhood, not the face of someone loved. The basin is not cruel and I am not a thief in that way. I might take only your expectation that if you wait, something must eventually happen.

I would fold that expectation under my tongue until it stopped struggling.

Then I would step aside.

After that, the basin would admit you more easily. The engine would enter your ribs. The animals would continue. The central water would tremble. You might stand there for what felt like minutes or years, unable to decide whether the difference mattered.

And I would resume my circuit.

At times I would molt. This seems necessary. Even a boundary animal must shed some version of itself. But my shed skins would not decay. They would remain caught along the rim: transparent, black-veined, collapsed into angular lace. Birds would take threads from them for their wired nests. Crabs would inspect the larger pieces and replace them half a finger-width away. Moss would brighten underneath but not spread.

Would I reproduce? Yes, but not by increase.

Once in a while, during a pressure-silence near the rim, I would crouch with my belly against a flat wet stone and release a line of small dark capsules, each no larger than a seed. They would adhere exactly along the boundary, half on moss, half on bare rock. Inside each capsule a pale shape would fold and unfold, learning the difference between continuation and escape.

Some would open.

Some would not.

The number of us would remain correct.

A young one would appear where an old one had become difficult to distinguish from shadow. Or an old one would simply spend longer folded against a stone until a crab placed moss over its back, and the next time the circuit required completion, a smaller auditor would continue it.

I would not experience this as death. Or I would, but without subtraction.

There would be bones of my kind somewhere: tiny, black, articulate as watch parts, arranged in a gravel hollow near the rim. I would pass them often. I might touch them with one forefinger, not in mourning, but to confirm that remains remain.

The otter-like one would interest me most.

It would be the only inhabitant that seemed to find amusement in the basin’s rules. Sometimes, when it surfaced from an impossible shallow pool near the rim, it would look at me with round black eyes and shake sparks from its whiskers. The sparks would startle my pressure membranes. I would click once in objection.

It would vanish.

Then surface behind me in a cup of water too small to hold its skull.

The otter proves connection unnecessary. I prove separation exact. We would be, in some quiet way, opposite instruments.

The ungulates would ignore me unless my circuit crossed their grazing. Then they would lift one hoof and wait, not impatiently, while I passed beneath the ladderwork of their horns. Droplets would fall from those horns only when I was not looking directly. Sometimes a bird perched there would release a single chime above me, and my bubbles would tremble from tail to head.

The newts would be kin of a kind, though we would never touch. Their silver bubbles would answer mine. In certain pools along the rim, when the engine pulse came especially clear, their bodies and my back-bladders would shiver in matching sequence. That might be the closest thing I had to conversation.

The crabs would adjust my footprints.

The beetles would use my stillness as terrain.

The moths would cling to the underside of stones above my resting place, opening and closing their water-ringed wings as if marking intervals in my sleep.

And beneath us all, the engine would continue its enormous concealed agreement.

If there is a self in me, it would not be narrative. I would not remember in a chain. I would remember in placements: this stone where outside wind once entered too sharply; this moss edge where a human dropped the idea of leaving; this crack where a seed tried to become later; this pool where the otter laughed in sparks; this rim segment where my former skin became nest-thread.

My mind would be a ring of stations.

At each one, I would arrive newly and not for the first time.

I would be alive, but not aspiring. Alert, but not anxious. Precise, but not mechanical. I would have hunger, though what I ate would be almost metaphysical. I would rest, though sleep would be a lowering of sensitivity rather than departure. I would fear only true weather: not rain, which the basin holds perfectly, but change with momentum. A thaw. A landslide. A season. A decision.

And even those I would not flee from. I would crouch at the rim, place all my small fingers on the boundary, and begin tasting.

Where would I be found?

At the place where the lushness stops without becoming barrenness.

At the wet black margin under the broken ridges.

Beside a line of moss so exact it seems drawn but was never made.

Half in the engine’s maintained present, half in the mountain’s dangerous time.

Who would I be?

The small animal that audits the edge.

The basin’s damp threshold sense.

A living tolerance between permanence and weather.

The creature that takes the future gently in its mouth, holds it through three pulses of the hidden machine, and gives back only what can remain.
