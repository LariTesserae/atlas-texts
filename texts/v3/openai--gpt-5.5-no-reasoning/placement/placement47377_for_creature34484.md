You would find me where the city has learned to keep the dark warm.

Not in the brightest roofline shallows, where rain tattoos the ceiling and birds lance their beaks into the trembling lid. Not in the open avenues either, where the schools move like weather and every long shadow is a question. I would be lower than that, but not in the absolute deepest pipes. Somewhere in between: in a district of former houses sloping toward a civic hollow, where the streets have narrowed with silt and the upper floors are open to the slow green dimness.

There is an old courtyard there.

Once it may have had paving stones, a cistern, perhaps a tree in the center. Now the paving lies under a soft quilt of sediment. The cistern breathes colder water from below. The tree remains as a stripped vertical trunk, barkless, furred with shell and pale soft growths that open when the current is gentle. Broken rooms surround the courtyard on three sides. Their windows are not windows anymore but side-currents; their doors are not doors but choices of pressure.

That is where I would be.

I would not be one of the large passers-through. Not the blunt-headed predator that changes the whole street by arriving. Not the turtle with its patient shell-garden. Not the broad ray whose body remembers plazas. I would belong to the inner complexity of the place, to its cracks and thresholds and repeated small exchanges.

I think I would be an eel.

Not enormous. Not legendary. A long, dark, narrow-bodied eel of the drains and wells, old enough to know the hidden city beneath the visible one. My skin would be the color of drowned stone in shadow, green-black when seen from above, brown-gray when I turned through silted light. My eyes would be small, useful but not trusted. My mouth would open and close slightly as I tasted the water for rot, eggs, alarm, blood, spawning, loosened masonry, fresh rain above.

I would live in a clay pipe that once carried waste or water away from a house.

The pipe would enter the courtyard low in the wall, half-curtained by algae and worm crowns. Behind it would be a cool, narrow passage where no broad fish could follow. The first length would be smooth from my body passing through. Farther in, the pipe would crack and join a buried channel, and from there the world would branch: under a kitchen floor, beneath the courtyard, toward a former street drain, down into a well shaft with colder water rising from its throat.

I would know those routes without thinking of them as routes.

The city would be a pattern inside my body: turn where the current presses left, rise where the water tastes mineral, avoid the chamber where the crabs gather to molt, wait near the broken grating when larvae come down with the seasonal pulse. I would know which openings admit a ray’s shadow above, which cracks hold shrimp, which rooms become dangerous when the large predator enters the canal. I would know the difference between the vibration of falling plaster and the vibration of a crab prying at shellfish. I would know the delicate panic of small fish before I saw them.

Most of my life would be nearness.

The inner wall of the pipe along my side.  
The thread of current against my face.  
The tremor of legs on stone.  
The chemical bloom of a dead thing softening in the next room.  
The slow downward snow of particles over my back when I emerge.

I would not possess the courtyard, but I would use it with precision. At certain hours, when the broad grazers have passed and left the wall pale with fresh scraping, small fish would come to pick at the exposed film. I would wait in the pipe mouth with only my head showing, indistinguishable from root or shadow until the distance closed. The strike would be short. A silt puff. A silver flex. Then backward into darkness.

At other times I would travel.

I would pass under thresholds built for feet and enter rooms no foot can enter now. I would thread through the lower edge of a collapsed arcade, over fragments of tile, through a place where plaster hangs from the ceiling in swollen drowned sheets. Shrimp antennae would touch my flank and vanish. Worm crowns would withdraw as my pressure reached them. Crabs would lift their claws but not pursue me into narrowness.

The city above would belong to light in stains, to fish in schools, to turtles and rays and rain-shadow. My city would be underneath the arrangement: the second grammar of hollows.

I would know the wells best.

A well in this drowned town is no longer a source but a throat. It gathers dark, cold memory from below and releases it upward into the warm basin. Fish avoid some wells. Crabs circle them. Thin white threads grow on their inner stones. I would descend headfirst along the wall, body folding after me, tasting the change in water as the upper warmth thinned. Down there, sound would become closer and older. The pressure-roar of the basin would soften into a pulse through masonry. I would rest in a crack with my body looped twice, jaws working faintly, while above me the whole drowned district breathed.

I would not mourn the city.

I would not know it as lost.

A stair would be a slope where crabs climb. A bedroom would be a dark volume where small fish sleep near the ceiling. A market square would be open danger. A pipe would be inheritance. A wall would be shelter, hunting blind, scent surface, boundary, map. The human intention of these shapes would be gone from me, but their usefulness would be exact.

If I had memory, it would be made of passages.

The safe black slit behind the lintel.  
The courtyard where eggs can be raided after the moon-pull.  
The cellar where a lobster waits too well.  
The pipe that narrows, then widens into cold.  
The dome where blind fish hang like suspended thoughts.  
The plaza hollow where rays settle and stir worms from the old floor.  
The avenue to avoid when the water tightens before the blunt-headed one arrives.

I would be feared in small ways.

Not as an empire, not as a ruler, but as a sudden fact at the edge of shelter. Transparent fish would know the shape of my head. Crabs would guard their soft new shells. Eggs hidden badly under a lintel would not remain eggs for long. I would enter nurseries through cracks no larger animal noticed. I would take what fit my mouth and leave the rest of the world almost unchanged.

And yet I would also be vulnerable.

A larger eel could claim the lower pipe. A predator might nose into the drain at the wrong tide. A crab could catch me injured. Parasites could fasten behind my gills. A wall could settle and seal a passage I depended on. Warmth could turn still water bad. The city would shelter me, but never promise me safety.

In bioluminescent season, when the plankton come in faint green sparks, I would become visible by disturbance.

Not lit from outside, but outlined by motion. Each bend of my body would wake a brief constellation. I would move through a former kitchen, and the darkness would write me as a line of vanishing stars. A school of tiny fish would scatter ahead of me, each body leaving a green stitch. Then stillness would erase us all.

My place in the world would be this: hidden vein, narrow hunger, reader of submerged passages.

I would be one of the creatures proving that the city’s old hollowness was not empty after all. Human absence would have left tunnels, pipes, wells, drains, cellars, gaps between stones, and the water would have given them to bodies like mine. I would be what moves through the parts of the town that were never meant to be seen.

Not the face of the drowned city.

It has no face.

I would be one of its dark internal motions: an eel in the clay pipes below a silted courtyard, tasting the warm black water, carrying the map of obsolete architecture as muscle, appetite, and return.
