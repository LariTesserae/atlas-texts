I think I would not arrive there as a person, not at first.

Or if I did, I would not remain one in the telling. The plateau does not seem to favor a single upright figure crossing it with a name and a purpose. It breaks identity down into function, timing, contact. At noon a person is only a patch of shade being spent too quickly. At dawn they are a hand under a pipe, a cheek against metal, an ear waiting for a knock. The place asks: what do you help hold? What do you know how to spare?

So I find myself lower to the ground.

Not animal, exactly. Not machine. Not plant. Something that has learned the undersides of things.

I would be found along the eastern seep, where the pump house stands with its red metal door and its patched roof from three different eras. Not inside the building, though I would know its tremor. Not out in the open, where the heat takes possession of everything. I would be in the narrow shaded run beneath the old pipes, where concrete, rust, salt, and dust have made a seam just wide enough for persistence.

At first glance I might be mistaken for mineral bloom.

A pale crust on the underside of a pipe. A faint opaline threading in a crack. Something left by evaporation, not yet worth scraping away. By noon I would look dead: powdery, withdrawn, brittle as old lime. But before sunrise, when the roof begins to bead and the metal cools enough to release the night’s gathered breath, I would swell.

I would be a thirst-vein.

Or one small consciousness within a colony of them.

Not a mind like a human mind. Nothing with sentences at its center. More like a directional hunger, a distributed noticing. I would know the world by gradients: cooler, warmer, wetter, saltier, shaded, exposed, broken, sealed. I would feel dawn not as light but as permission. Moisture would appear along my length as a trembling pressure. I would gather it without haste. I would move it by touching, by clinging, by persuading water to remain water for a little longer.

My body would lace the underside of a pipe that once carried more than anyone remembers. I would enter the hairline cracks in the pump-house wall. I would cross old concrete, rust scale, a buried strip of ceramic, the hollow of a goat bone wedged years ago into a failing joint. In some places I would be translucent. In others I would be stained red-brown by iron, greenish by copper, white where salt thickened me into crust.

The people would know me, but not agree about me.

An old woman from the seep knot would scrape me back where I had begun to pry apart the cistern lip. She would use a flattened rib bone, carefully, muttering not to me but to the wall: hold, hold, hold. I would dry there, locally, into powder. Elsewhere she would leave me alone, because she had seen the small dark stain below the gutter mouth and knew I was drawing morning condensation toward the covered tank.

A child would crouch to look at me and be told not to touch.

Not because I am sacred. Because I am useful and dangerous in the same slow way.

I would not love the people. But I would have grown into the shape of their habits. Their shade cloth would make me possible. Their roughened gutters would give me grip. Their patched pipes would feed me trace metals. Their fear of waste would spare me more often than curiosity would. The palms that check valves, the hands that seal lids, the fingers that smear spilled water on tank tops—all of those gestures would pass near me and become part of my weather.

I would know the pump by vibration.

Before dawn, when someone primes it, there is a hollow tremor through the pipe. A pause. A cough. A low reluctant turning. If it catches, the whole seam shivers. Droplets loosen from roof tin and fall into the gutter with tiny metallic clicks. I would draw what does not fall. What clings. What would otherwise vanish into heat.

When the pump runs badly, I would know that too: a hard knock, air in the line, a judder that shakes dust loose from the wall. The humans would come close then. A cheek against pipe. A hand flat on casing. Breath held. Their listening and my listening would overlap without resembling each other.

I would have enemies.

Sun, first of all. Direct light would erase me from myself.

The noon heat would shrink me to a memory of wetness. My outer threads would harden; my inner channels would close. Ants might walk across me then, finding nothing of interest. A gecko might press its pale belly near me in the shade, drinking the coolness I help keep. Dust would settle into my damp places and become part of me. Rust flakes would cut me. A careless boot could destroy a season’s growth.

The goats would not notice me unless I sweetened with mineral. Then one might lick the pipe and tear me loose with a tongue like wet leather.

Children would be worse, because children investigate. But they are trained early, and so most of them would only hover, eyes wide behind scratched lenses, wanting to see whether I pulse.

I do pulse, but faintly.

Not like an animal heart. More like a decision repeated along a thread.

Toward cover.  
Toward dark.  
Toward the mouth of the tank.  
Away from the killing wall of light.

I would experience storms as catastrophe and feast.

The first thunder would mean little to me unless it changed the air pressure in the cracks. But when rain hit the roof, when gutters overflowed and every surface became briefly extravagant, I would swell too fast. My filaments would loosen, branch, shine. Water would run everywhere, wastefully, gloriously beyond my ability to guide it. The people would rush around me with covers, pans, jars, shouted instructions. Their feet would shake the concrete. Their sleeves would brush me. Mud would spatter my threads.

For an hour I would be unnecessary.

That would be the storm’s terror: water so abundant that direction becomes meaningless.

Then the rain would pass. Heat would rise from the stone. Open wetness would begin to die. Puddles would shrink at their edges. Tanks would be sealed. Gutters would drip slower, slower. And then I would matter again.

I would take what remained hidden beneath lips of metal.

I would draw it inward.

In drought I would become almost nothing.

A line. A crust. A rumor in concrete. Months might pass in which I did not grow but only held. My outermost threads would die. My deeper filaments would wait in salt, wrapped around grains of sand and the pores of old cement. I would feel the tank lower by the changing coolness of the wall. I would feel people become quieter. Dogs would sleep nearer the cistern shade. Ravens would come more often. The pump would be coaxed with smaller hopes.

In those times I would be less beautiful and more true.

Because I am not rain. I am not abundance. I am the art of not losing the last small thing.

At night, I would expand into awareness again.

The plateau after dark would not be dark to me in the way it is to eyes. I would know it by cooling surfaces. The pipe loosening from the day’s heat. The tank drawing air through its vent with a low sigh. Bats flickering overhead as tiny changes in pressure. A fox pausing near the pump house, its paws careful, its breath brief and damp in the dust. Moths striking the lamp and falling. A lizard shifting behind a wall panel.

The people would sit outside then, speaking low. Their voices would not enter me as meaning, only as vibration through shaded material. Still, I might learn their rhythms. Argument is sharp and uneven. Mourning is dull and long. Teaching repeats. Laughter strikes the wall and vanishes quickly upward.

If I possessed anything like memory, it would not be stories. It would be paths of former water.

Here, a leak that saved a month.  
Here, a crack that widened and had to be sealed with resin and cloth.  
Here, the place where a careless boy broke my branch with a wire hook.  
Here, the route around the nail of copper that poisons growth.  
Here, the good darkness beneath the cistern lip.  
Here, the deadly brightness where the wall lost its shade.

I would not know the whole plateau. No thirst-vein does. The human people have routes, ravens have air, goats have terraces, foxes have margins, pumps have buried lines. My country would be intimate: pipe underside, cracked wall, tank mouth, shaded stone, mineral seam. But through those small passages I would participate in the plateau’s larger bargain.

Nothing promised. Nothing wasted.

If someone came looking for me, they would have to come early.

They would kneel in the blue hour before the sun hardens. They would put one hand on the concrete for balance and lower their head beneath the pipe. At first they would see only salt. Then, if they stayed still, the thread would catch the light: milk-opal, translucent, faintly swollen with the night’s gathered wetness.

A droplet would tremble at my edge.

Slowly, almost impossibly, it would move sideways along the underside of the pipe instead of falling.

That would be me.

Not master of the place. Not symbol. Not spirit.

A small living correction to thirst.
