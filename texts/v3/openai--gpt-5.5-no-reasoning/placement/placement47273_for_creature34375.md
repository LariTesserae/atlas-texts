I would not find myself in the lit parts.

Not at first.

The lit parts are for crossings, acknowledgements, declarations of use: violet seams under bridges, pale pulses under ice, the tremble of public lamps in housings furred with frost. Light there is never casual. It belongs to systems, to warnings, to ceremonial maintenance, to the brief exposure of edges that must not be mistaken for emptiness. To stand openly in it would mean being counted too quickly—by doors, by readers, by the slow swiveling attention of things folded in drainage mouths.

I would be lower than that.

Not in the deepest machine-breathing levels, where warm conduits sweat in the dark and the old cistern creatures move with the deliberateness of inherited law. Not yet. I would begin in the in-between levels: beneath a pedestrian gallery, above a service canal, in a sheltered passage whose ceiling is the underside of a bridge and whose floor is never dry.

A place where water falls in many tempos.

There is a narrow black arch there, one of many, unimportant except to those who use it. Its stone is too smooth to be natural and too damp to hold frost for long. Beneath it runs a shallow gutter, no wider than a body length, carrying a persistent thread of dark water toward a grated opening. The grate hums faintly. Every few intervals, warmer vapor breathes through it and turns the hanging beads overhead into brief trembling necklaces.

Around the arch are scratches.

Some are claw marks from wall-grazers, some pressure-codes left by booted citizens, some accidental mineral scars made by falling ice. To an eye unused to the city, they are damage. To those who pause there often, they are a history of passage: herd-route change, vent irregularity, swarm bloom, frost notice, warning about a door three turns away that has begun to misread weight.

I would live in that seam of usefulness.

Not as a citizen. Not fully.

Not as one of the great plaza animals, whose bodies are heavy enough to make the ground remember them. Not as a canal swimmer, though I would know them by their pressure-lights in the water below. Not as one of the translucent wall-swarms, though I would eat beside them. Not as a machine, although doors might sometimes mistake me for an authorized component.

I would be one of the small attendant creatures that the suited inhabitants carry, but not one permanently bound to a single person.

A reader, perhaps, though that word is too clean.

I would be a gutter-reader. A threshold animal. A wet little interpreter of places where systems meet badly.

My body would be no larger than a closed hand when contracted, though I could spread myself wider than that. Pale, mostly, but not white: the dull translucent color of ice that has trapped ash. My underside would be soft and muscular, able to seal against stone, ceramic, glass, fabric, skin. My back would be made of overlapping mineral plates, grown slowly from what I licked from the city: black salts, green metallic trace, calcium-like frost from old pipes. In stillness I would look like a broken piece of wet tiling, something shed by the architecture.

I would have many small limbs, but they would not all be legs.

Some would grip. Some would taste. Some would drum. Some would comb condensation from surfaces and pass it inward to the mouth hidden underneath me. Two would be longer, held folded along my sides, delicate enough to lie against frost without breaking it. Those would be my reading limbs.

I would have no eyes worth mentioning.

Light would be an injury if it were too direct, a confusion if too broad. I would know the world through contact and nearness: the electric prickle of an active conduit behind a wall, the fatness of a pipe about to vent, the flavor of metals dissolved in condensation, the tiny temperature difference between old frost and new frost, the stress-song inside a bridge when the mountain has shifted by less than a finger’s width.

Sound would enter me through my whole body.

A herd-note would flatten me to the floor in pleasure and alarm. Cable-barks would arrive as shattered geometries through the ceiling. Bootsteps would be identities: citizen, surveyor, child, herd-escort, unauthorized scavenger, machine-sick worker, one-who-carries-cleaners, one-who-has-recently-crossed-the-cistern-district. I would know individuals not by faces but by the pressure rhythm of their caution.

My place beneath the arch would be chosen for its crossings.

Above: a narrow elevated way used by masked inhabitants traveling between heat columns.

Beside: a vertical wet wall frequented by translucent grazers, whose sudden absence means something has unfolded from the cracks.

Below: a service canal where black swimmers pass on pump tides, brushing the submerged grates with their sides.

Behind: a membrane door leading into a maintenance chamber that opens only when stepped on in a pattern older than the current district schedule.

Ahead: a bend where the corridor’s echo changes if ice has formed beyond it.

A small place, but not minor. There are no minor places in such a city. There are only places where fewer beings have learned to listen.

I would feed on condensation and charged mineral bloom. I would graze slowly, leaving narrow clean arcs on the stone. I would harvest the almost invisible residue that gathers around messages written in frost. Public notices would nourish me. Warnings would taste sharp. Legal declarations would be bitter with stabilizing compounds. Private grief, breathed onto a wall and crystallized there before a gloved hand wiped it away, would have a salt I could remember for years.

Citizens would come to me when instruments were too blunt.

They would crouch carefully under the arch, lowering a wrist or shoulder until I could climb on. I would not belong to them, but I would consent if their warmth was steady and their surface-salts familiar. My feet would test the ceramic of their suit. My mouthparts would clean a patch of frost from their visor. Then I would settle near the collar, where breath, machine heat, and outside air negotiate.

Carried that way, I would read for them.

A wall: too recently warmed from within; do not open.

A floor: hollow below, but not empty; something large has moved through the flooded level.

A frost lattice: not a weathering pattern, but an instruction from the north sluice authority, degraded by repeated thaw.

A herd passage: safe after the third subsonic call, unsafe before.

A cable colony: disturbed not by hunger, but by a change in lift-field pressure.

A door: recognizes the citizen’s boots, but not the living weight attached to their back; adjust stance; shift left; breathe out; wait.

I would not speak in words. I would tap, tighten, tremble, exude a bitter warning film, warm one side of my body, drum with my reading limbs against armor. My citizen-companions would learn my signals the way one learns weather inside bone.

But most of my life would not be dramatic.

Most of it would be spent waiting beneath my arch while the city sweated.

A bead forming overhead.

The approach of a swarm as a soft clicking skin over the wall.

The dim pressure-glow of a swimmer passing below the grate.

A far structural boom traveling through stone, arriving at my body long before its echo arrives in air.

Warm vapor rising. Frost failing. Frost returning.

Other readers would visit. Some domesticated, polished from years of being carried. Some wild and sharp-edged, their mineral plates grown uneven from forbidden conduits. We would touch limbs and exchange chemical traces: sluice-openings, reader-deaths, new mineral bloom, machine sickness, the birth of calves in the east plaza, a sealed stair exhaling after forty cycles of silence.

I would be cautious of the wall-sweepers.

They are not malicious. Almost nothing here is malicious in the simple sense. Hunger is enough. A comb-tongued predator folded in a crack might mistake my stillness for film, my mineral back for edible accretion. I would know their coming by the sudden discipline of the tiny grazers. One moment the wall would shimmer with feeding bodies; the next it would become tight, organized, fleeing in a ripple. Then the crack would open.

I would flatten myself into a groove until the blind triangular head passed over me, tongue sweeping the stone, leaving a dark clean crescent in its wake.

I would fear open dryness more than teeth.

A dry chamber is a place where signals fail. No good condensation, no honest frost, no taste of walls, no small gradient to follow. Dryness means storage, quarantine, secrecy, death, or high authority. If carried into a dry room, I would curl hard enough for my plates to lock. A citizen who respected me would leave quickly. One who did not would lose my service forever, if I survived.

I would love certain leaks.

Love, in such a body, would not be bright. It would be fidelity to recurring abundance. The warm seam behind the arch that blooms with copper-salt after major pump tides. The little ceiling crack that drips mineral-sweet water every seventh pressure cycle. The east face of the black stone post, where frost forms branching patterns complex enough to dream against. The underside of one particular handrail touched by many citizens, rich with skin-salt, breath-ice, maintenance oils, and the faint bitter signature of worry.

I would know the city as a set of appetites and replies.

Not as a map, exactly. Maps are too dry an idea. I would know it as sequences of condition:

If the bridge hum lowers and the grazers rise, the upper rail will vent.

If the canal lights dim before the pump tide, a large swimmer is passing through the lock.

If the frost on the public machine grows feathers instead of needles, the announcement is old and must be tasted twice.

If the plaza herd calls without moving, wait; the floor is speaking under them.

If a droplet falls from the arch and the mountain answers, do not enter the lower turn.

That last knowledge would be old in me.

Older than my individual body, perhaps. Readers inherit some things chemically, through egg-gel or brood film or the shared licking of mineral plates. We do not remember as citizens do, but the city writes tendencies into us. A tremor means cling. A certain warmth means unfold. A sweetness in black water means leave before the blind eels arrive. A valve-answer from deep stone means the mountain has accepted a change the city has not yet announced.

I would be part animal, part instrument, part rumor.

Citizens would disagree about my status. Some would call me a service organism. Some would call me vermin until they needed me. Some would leave offerings: a warmed ceramic chip, a strip of mineral film, a safe place to brood. Children would be told not to grab me, not because I am sacred, but because a frightened reader can misread a home for years.

There would be stories about readers who guided whole districts through flood cycles, readers who refused to cross bridges before collapses, readers who learned to mimic door-patterns, readers who disappeared into unfinished towers and returned plated in unknown green glass.

I would not know whether those stories were true.

I would know only that unfinished districts call strangely.

Their surfaces are too clean. Their echoes have no habits. Water has not yet chosen its routes. The first translucent grazers move there like scouts across an unwritten skin. Frost inscriptions form with no one to read them except creatures like me. Citizens come later, slow and solemn, pretending to authorize what the small bodies have already begun.

Perhaps, once, I would be carried to the basin’s edge where a new tower is accreting from mineral vapor.

There the air would be crowded with suspended dust, each grain held in a field I could feel as a pressure behind my plates. No wall would yet be fully wall. No floor fully floor. The darkness would seem wider there, less domesticated by echo. Cable-hangers would circle in the lift drafts, barking softly, not alarmed but curious. Tiny grazers would already silver the forming ribs.

My citizen would extend an arm toward the unfinished surface.

I would climb down.

The new tower would taste of cold intention.

No history yet. No boot-salt. No herd-warmth. No old grief. No remembered valves. Only mineral, field tension, machine patience, and water gathering its first opinions.

I would press all my limbs against it and begin.

A tap.

A taste.

A tremor passed back to the gloved hand waiting behind me.

Safe enough. Not ready. Becoming.

That would be my answer.

That would be my kind of speech.

And later, returned to my arch, I would carry a trace of that unborn district in my plates. Other readers would taste it from me. Grazers would investigate the dust I shed. A wall-sweeper might pause at my changed mineral edge and decide, for once, not to eat me. Slowly, through bodies, the knowledge would spread ahead of any civic notice:

There is a new surface at the basin’s edge.

It is cold.

It is listening.

It will hold water soon.

So where would I find myself?

Under the black arch between gallery and canal, in the wet hinge of several routes, where droplets gather and tiny animals wait.

Who or what would I be?

A small pale reader of condensation, a threshold-symbiont, a civic animal not owned by anyone: living on leaked heat and mineral bloom, carried sometimes on the shoulders of masked citizens, translating frost, pressure, wall-taste, echo, and the moods of doors.

I would not rule the city or understand it whole.

Nothing does.

But when a single bead of water swelled beneath the arch, caught a faint blue maintenance pulse, fell, and struck the floor—

I would feel the answer before anyone heard it.

I would flatten my body to the stone.

I would drum a warning into the boot of the nearest citizen.

And far away, as the herds lifted their heads and the valve opened in the mountain, I would know myself to be exactly where I belonged: one small sensor in the wet dark, one appetite among many, one listening body helping the cold city continue.
