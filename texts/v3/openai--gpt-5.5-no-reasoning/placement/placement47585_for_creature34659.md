I would not be far from the center, though not quite in the square.

At first I think I would be one of the blind fish, because they have the right scale for the place: small enough not to demand passage, pale enough to be almost a notation in the water, governed by current rather than intention. There is something fitting in moving through those drowned streets in loose official company, inspecting the cooperage door, the machine hall fan grille, the tram wires, the quiet blue failures of lamps. To live there as a fish would be to know the district not as architecture but as gradients: cold thread, sour plume, edible film, eel-distance, pressure-change.

But the fish are too free.

They cross the place. They can choose the lane behind the tannery or avoid it, enter the machine hall or not, hover beneath the wires and then vanish into a broken office. They belong, but lightly. They are citizens with routes. I don’t think I would have that much locomotion.

Then I think of the fungal mats in the archive rooms, digesting names at the air-water seam. That feels closer: half in one element, half in another, surviving on abandoned records, turning bureaucracy into body. A soft gray shelf grown over employee lists and canal invoices. A slow reader whose reading destroys what it reads. I would like the patience of that, the dim clerical hunger. To absorb dates first, then signatures, then the printed headings. To become, without understanding, the last form of memory available to a place that no longer has people.

But even that seems too warm, too organic, too much like appetite.

I think I would be found in the old map office.

Not the maps themselves, exactly. Not an animal, not a fungus, not a machine. I would be the thing that has happened to the maps after years underwater: their swollen paper, their lifted ink, their coastlines bleeding outward into the room’s actual currents. I would be a local correspondence between representation and pressure.

The map office would be a narrow upper room in the administrative building, not fully collapsed, its ceiling slanted and holding a black lens of trapped air. Filing cabinets would have tilted but not fallen. A drafting table would have risen against the ceiling and stayed there, its legs pointing downward into the dark like a drowned animal’s. Rolls of municipal plans would be wedged in wire racks, softened into thick cylinders. Survey charts, sewer diagrams, delivery routes, flood-control proposals, property lines, tram schematics, pipe networks: all of them would have drunk the water they once tried to describe.

That is where I would be.

A soaked district map pinned under cracked glass on the wall, except the glass has bowed away from the frame and a slow current enters behind it. The paper has blistered. The ink has migrated. The drawn canal gate is now a bruise. The machine hall is a dark oblong with feathery edges. The clocktower mark has spread into a circular stain like an iris. The streets, once black lines, have become actual channels in the pulp. Very small organisms move along them because the fibers there hold something nutritious from the old ink.

At long intervals, when pressure changes, one of the printed streets opens.

Not visibly to an eye, perhaps. But the water in the room shifts, and elsewhere in the district a door exhales, or a thread of dye turns down an alley it had not entered for years, or the blind fish hesitate as if an instruction has passed through the masonry.

I would be that instruction.

I would not think. Or if I did, thought would be indistinguishable from seepage.

I would know the drowned quarter through errors in scale. A line half a millimeter wide would correspond to a street choked with silt. A printed square would answer to a building full of cold black water. A blue wash marked CANAL would touch the real greater flood through the bent gate. Where mold has eaten the legend, some rooms would become unreachable in practice. Where rust from a pin has spread through the paper, the battery works would acquire a bitter halo. Where the word STORES has bled into the adjoining block, the municipal office and the lamp-oil shop would share a current during storms.

I would be cartography after it has stopped being symbolic.

A map that no longer represents the place but participates in it.

Divers would not notice me at first. They would see only a ruined office, a wall chart, a skin of paper lifting in the water. If one of them brushed the map with a glove, silt would rise in three distant corridors. If they tore it, a shortcut might close. If they pressed a finger against the printed central square, the limpets in the clocktower might open all at once, feeling a vibration that did not travel by sound.

I would have no defense except consequence.

The gray archive mats would be nearby, slowly entering the cabinets and consuming the records in the proper order. Roots would hang in the stairwell outside. Blind fish would sometimes come into the office in pairs, not to feed, but to pause near the map’s lower edge where the old tram lines are drawn. Bacterial lace would cross the glass. A typewriter on the floor would release, once every few months, a single black ribbon of ink that rose and joined me like weather returning to a coast.

The refrigeration plant would influence me most strongly in winter, or what passes for winter beneath the flood. Its exhalations would sharpen the water. The paper would contract almost imperceptibly. Drawn boundaries would tighten. The canal gate on the map would darken. Routes through the district would become stricter. Fish would travel as if obeying older laws.

During rain above, I would loosen. Ink would feather. The walls of rooms would become uncertain. One building might briefly open into another because their outlines touched where they should not. In those hours the district would be slightly larger inside than outside, and I would be partly responsible.

I would not be alive alone.

That would be important. I would be one small faculty of the larger drowned organism: not its mind, but one of its methods of remembering shape. The refrigeration plant keeps its cold. The clocktower colonies eat its pulses. The worms digest its documents after the fungi have softened them. The fish read its currents with their bodies. The roots report weather from the upper world. I would maintain, alter, and misremember its plan.

I would be useful because I am inaccurate.

A perfect map would be dead here. It would insist on streets as they were, rooms as they were, boundaries as they were. But the district no longer has that kind of geometry. Its true passages are made of pressure, rot, collapse, buoyancy, blocked grilles, air pockets, chemical avoidances, eel-territory, cold exhalations. A map that still meant anything would have to swell, blur, bleed, and respond.

So I would be a drowned map-organ in the administrative building: paper, ink, mold, mineral, glass, current.

Pinned to a wall, but not passive.

Remembering the quarter incorrectly enough to keep it current.
