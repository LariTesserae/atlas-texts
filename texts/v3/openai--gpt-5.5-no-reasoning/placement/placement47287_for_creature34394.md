I think I would be found at the edge of the central basin chamber, not quite in its authority and not quite outside it.

Not a priest-listener in the old sense. Not one of the white-corded measurers whose years hang from their wrists in knots and whose judgments can still a room. I would be younger than that, or perhaps only less completed. Someone entrusted with nearness but not yet interpretation. A keeper of rims. A cleaner of lips. The one who moves between the basins with a folded cloth of scraped hide, a bone pick, a little waxed tablet, and a covered lamp whose flame is kept low enough not to trouble the water.

My place would be among the smallest sounds.

Not the deep cistern knocks that the elders feel in their breastbones, not the civic warnings, not the famous bowls whose tones are carved into law. I would tend the minor vessels: the palm-sized cups tucked beneath doubtful seams, the little witness-basins set out after repairs, the shallow plates that catch only one drop every hundred breaths and therefore must be watched with almost unreasonable faith. My duty would be to know whether a silence is ordinary.

In the Held City, that would be a dangerous knowledge.

A silence can mean nothing. A silence can mean stone drying where it should sweat. A silence can mean a path above has shifted, a channel has taken a new route, a corpse-cavity has begun to drink, a forgotten seal has opened its hairline mouth. But most silences are not omens. Most are just the long interval before the next drop. Someone has to learn the difference without becoming proud of it.

So I would be one of the patient apprentices of delay.

I would live in a wall-bay near the basin chamber’s eastern throat, where the lane narrows and everyone turns sideways without thinking. My sleeping stone would be short, warmed at night by a little lamp-pan slid beneath it, and above my head there would be three shelves: one for tools, one for tablets, one for objects that are not useful enough to keep but not meaningless enough to discard. A cracked ceramic bead. A bat bone no longer than a thorn. A smooth black pebble said to have come from above, though no one believes that truly. A scrap of green-stained cloth given by a surface trader, sealed in wax because it smells faintly of rot and open air.

I would be known for touching things before answering.

Someone would ask, “Did the west witness-cup speak?”

And I would put two fingers to the wall, though the wall had nothing to do with the question. Not to delay. Not to seem wise. Simply because the answer would feel incomplete if taken only from memory. The city would have trained me into consultation. I would have difficulty saying even ordinary things without first checking the pressure of the world.

I would have a narrow face, perhaps; large eyes made larger by lamplight; hair bound close with gray cord stiffened by lime. My shoulders would be uneven from passing, always, through the same left-leaning gap between the eighth and ninth plinths. On the skin of my forearms there would be pale mineral scarring, not from injury but from years of reaching under collars of calcite to clear silt from basin mouths. My hands would be strong in the fingertips and soft in the palms, because the work is more touch than force.

I would not be important in any grand way.

But people would notice if I failed.

The basins I tend would be too small to command a gathering, yet they would be the first to betray certain changes. Hairline leaks. New seepage. A foreign bitterness in the water. The beginnings of a clog far above, where no tool can reach. I would keep a row of small clay counters, each impressed with a different mark: clear, delayed, bright, dull, silted, bitter, uncertain. The uncertain counter would trouble me most. I would hate using it. I would use it often.

During loud hours, when speech vanishes and the city lowers itself closer to the ground, I would move almost happily.

Not because I love danger. Because in loud hours everyone becomes honest. No one pretends voice is enough. Hands speak. Shoulders answer. The whole chamber declares itself in tremor. Drops strike every vessel until each basin wears its tone openly, and my small ones, usually shy, become distinct. I would kneel among them with both palms on the stone ledge and feel the tiny conversations travel through water, clay, bone, wrist.

That would be when I am most myself: half crouched, eyes lowered, lamp behind me, hearing by skin.

I would be called something like Third-Interval, perhaps not as a birth name but as a work-name. Or Little Delay. Or She-Waits-After. Names in that place would accrete like mineral deposits; one given by family, one by apprenticeship, one by mistake, one by a child, one by an elder who noticed the shape of your usefulness before you did.

Maybe children would call me Cup-Mouse because I move between the little vessels and dislike being stared at.

I would pretend to be offended and secretly keep the name.

There would be an elder over me: a basin-keeper with wrists white from lime, severe in all public matters and privately amused by almost everything. She would never praise directly. If I recorded a difficult silence correctly, she would only say, “You did not hurry it.” From her, that would be blessing enough.

She would teach me that listening is not hunger.

“You must not want the sound,” she would tell me. “Wanting makes a bowl lie.”

I would not understand at first. I would lean too eagerly toward the quiet basins, inventing meaning where there was only interval. Later I would learn. The city cannot be interrogated like a frightened child. It must be kept company until it speaks in its own time.

That would become my devotion.

Not worship. Not exactly. A companionship with slow evidence.

I would also carry one small forbiddenness in me.

Everyone in the Held City has some private misalignment, some place where the city’s law and the person’s inward current do not perfectly join. Mine would concern the sealed lower doors.

I would not touch them often. I would know better. I would know all the reasons: not every chamber has consented to be city; sealed means held; curiosity is a kind of tool and therefore can damage. I would repeat these things to children if I found them crouched near the disks. I would send them away.

But sometimes, during the thin hours after a loudness has passed, when the city seems exhausted and every basin is still adjusting to its own return, I would go down three lanes and one half-stair to where the lowest stone door sits in its groove.

There would be water gathered before it, black and motionless.

I would stand with my toes at the edge and listen.

Not with my ear pressed to the disk. That is childish, and besides, ears are too easily fooled. I would set the heel of my hand lightly against the stone beside the seal and wait.

At first there would be only the known city behind me: gutters, lamps, distant bowls, a sandal slap, a rope’s damp groan, someone coughing into cloth. Then, sometimes, beneath that, or not beneath but away from it, I would feel the idea of a slower drop.

Not a sound, perhaps. Not even a vibration. A spacing.

A room-sized pause.

I would tell no one.

Or I would tell only the nearly blind girl who navigates loud hours better than anyone else, because she would not ask foolish questions. She would sit beside me once, palm to stone, and after a long time sign in the dark: Not empty.

Then: Not asking.

And that would be enough.

I would be afraid of the surface, though I would claim not to think of it.

When traders returned, I would listen to them being listened to. Their dry clothes rasping. Their breath wrong from vast air. Their stories of wind moving without channels, of water falling everywhere at once, of light so large it gives shadows to stones in the open. I would find it grotesque and compelling. I would dislike the part of myself that leaned toward those stories.

Once, perhaps, I would be chosen to go upward only as far as an exchange cellar.

Not truly to the surface. Just into the old city’s underworks, above the Held City but below the sky. Even there the ceiling would seem obscenely high. Dry dust would sting my nose. The quiet would be terrible, because it would not be full; it would be vacant. I would hear my own breathing too plainly and understand why some who travel upward return with a wound no one can seal.

I would bring back oil and a little bundle of reed pens and one fallen leaf pressed between two pieces of cloth.

The leaf would frighten me.

Not because it is dangerous. Because it would be evidence of a world that makes thin green things and lets them die openly.

I would dry it badly. It would spot with mold. In shame, I would seal it in a tiny jar and place it on my useless shelf, beside the bat bone and the black pebble. For years afterward I would sometimes open the jar during private quiet, smell its faint sourness, and feel a pull like vertigo.

But I would not leave.

My love would be for the Close. Not a simple love; a pressed, mineral, inherited one. I would know its cruelties: the joints bent too early, the fear of coughs, the damp in the lungs of old people, the public nature of grief, the way every desire must negotiate with stone. I would know that endurance can become a habit mistaken for virtue. I would know that “continuance” can excuse almost anything if spoken too solemnly.

Still, I would love it.

I would love the exact depression worn into the third step near the salt rooms. I would love the orange reflection trembling along the main gutter. I would love the children’s clicking sandals and the way elders pretend not to track them by sound. I would love the civic basins when they are orderly, and the household bowls when they are not. I would love how a repaired crack is not hidden but marked, witnessed, named. I would love that the dead remain near enough to be included in temperature.

If I became old there, I would become very still.

My sight would dim early from lamp smoke and close work. My hearing might sharpen for a time and then betray me. I would dread that more than death. When ordinary sound began to fray, I would train my palms harder. I would learn the cheek against basin, the sternum against threshold, the sole of the foot on a wet slab. Perhaps I would become one of those whom surface people call deaf and the Held City calls deep-listening.

Children would be sent to me with troublesome silences.

I would ask them, “How long did you wait?”

They would say, proudly, “Fifty breaths.”

And I would say, “Then you have heard fifty breaths. Go back.”

My wall-token, when I died, should not be placed behind one of the great basins. That would be too much. I would want it beneath a small witness-cup at the edge of the central chamber, one that sounds only after heavy condensation, one that can be mistaken for silence by anyone in a hurry.

The token would be fired clay, thumb-marked, with the sign for interval.

My body would be dried, salted, limed, wrapped, and sealed into thickness. Not hidden. Included. A little more wall. A little more memory against the pressure above.

And after that, if a child asked who lay there, perhaps someone would say:

“That one kept the small waits.”

Or:

“She knew when nothing had not yet become something.”

And the drip would continue.
