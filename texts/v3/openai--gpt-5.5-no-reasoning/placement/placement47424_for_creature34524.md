I would not find myself in the plaza first, though that is where a visitor would think the place meant to gather them.

I would find myself along one of the narrower ways beside the central cistern district, where a service corridor pretends to be merely a corridor but is actually a decision the city keeps making. It bends around a sealed pressure room, dips under a low ceramic arch, and rises again toward a balcony overlooking a channel that only carries water after surface rain. There are root curtains nearby, but not thick ones. Just a few translucent strands descending from a ceiling seam, each one faintly veined, each one trembling when the pumps below begin to draw. The wall there is black composite under mineral bloom. The floor plates are hexagonal and uneven, polished in a strip no wider than a foot by whatever has passed that way for longer than memory.

It is not an important place. That is why I would be there.

Not in the chambers that announce themselves. Not beneath the lowest hanging tower, not at the hand-pylons where the voices surface, not in the north passage with its oceanic machinery. I would be in one of the city’s small useful margins, where the loudness arrives softened by turns in the masonry and the air smells of wet dust, warm ceramic, fern-bitterness, and the faint animal sweetness of skitterling husks crumbled into the floor seams.

I would be something that lives by noticing when things almost happen.

At first, I might be mistaken for one of the listening mats under benches, or for a rolled piece of mineral felt fallen from some dead machine. Low, soft, pleated, the color of dust-dark leather. But I would not belong entirely to that species, if species is the right word here. I would be one of the corridor listeners: a small, old, half-animal thing that has learned the city through its skin.

I would have no eyes worth naming. Perhaps two cloudy pits that can tell flare from dark, violet mapping from amber hatch-light, but not shape. Sight would be too extravagant in a place where most truths arrive through pressure. Instead my body would be arranged around listening.

Not ears, exactly.

My sides would be ribbed with thin membranes, each one sensitive to a different kind of vibration: pump-start, distant valve-click, eel-current in a wet channel, moth-wing turbulence, the pressure hush before chamber rain. My underside would be broad and tender, pressed often to stone. Through it I would know the hexagonal pavement as a map of tensions. I would know which plates are hollow beneath, which are nesting, which are loose, which have recently been crossed by something with threadlike legs, something heavy, something warm-blooded and uncertain.

I would live in the interval between a dry route and a wet route.

There would be a crack behind a bowed wall panel, just large enough for me to fold into. The panel would once have covered an access junction, but now its lower fasteners would be gone and a slow white mineral lip would hold it ajar. Behind it: warmth, darkness, old cabling, fungal threads, the papery remains of moth larvae. My resting body would fit there exactly because I had grown into the available space. Nothing in that city wastes form.

When the long warm stillness came, I would sleep in shallow pieces. Not true sleep. A loosening. A slow flattening against the wall while the dry dust settled on my back and made me look more like architecture than life. I would let the city pass through me: subsonic engine, pipe knock, relay crack, the far north roar pressing softly at the chambers. Each vibration would sort itself into meaning or non-meaning.

Too much signal would be ignored. Only changes matter.

A pump that starts late.

A drainage tone with grit in it.

A moth-cloud waking before the air handler.

Skitterlings remaining hidden during a safe thunder.

A root curtain tightening along its length before the lights dim.

These would be news.

I would feed when sound loosened food from surfaces. During the deep resonance cycles, I would unfold from my wall crack and raise my pleated body slightly from the floor. Dust-mites, spores, mineral bacteria, tiny transparent larvae dislodged from seams—all of them would drift and tremble in the moving air. I would open the dark pits along my body, not mouths exactly but receiving hollows, wet at the rims. The city would shake, and nourishment would arrive as a consequence of infrastructure.

I would not hunt in the dramatic sense. Nothing here would.

I would wait correctly.

Sometimes moths would pass close enough for me to take one. A wing would touch my outer membrane; my body would close before thought. Charred-paper flutter, copper underside, bitter heat-film taste. More often I would eat what the walls surrendered. After chamber rain, when the corridor smelled briefly alive, I would move farther than usual, grazing the softened microbial crust near the gutter and drinking from droplets that clung to the underside of a broken conduit. I would drink carefully. Standing pools are not to be trusted until the blind pavement-drinkers have crossed them.

I would know the skitterlings as neighbors and weather-signs. When they poured from beneath the plates, I would still myself. Their many feet would pass over my edges. They would read me as warm stone, perhaps, or as one of the permitted hazards of the route. If one lingered too long near a pit-mouth, I might take it, but not often. Their trust in water would be more useful than their bodies.

The eels I would know only as pressure in wet times.

After rare true rain above, when the city doubled its noise and brown water ran in the street channels, I would climb—slowly, laboriously—onto the raised curb outside my corridor. My body would not like immersion. I would anchor myself around a pylon base or under the lip of a bench and feel the flood move past. In that water the eels would travel. I would sense their weak electricity as a taste in my membranes: cloudy quartz bodies bending through reflected diagnostic light, mouths open, reading old inscriptions with their skin. I would keep still. They are not cruel, but they are factual.

The clicking thing in the hanging towers would trouble me.

Not frighten me, exactly. Trouble me because its sounds would be too much like decision.

Most city sounds are cyclical. They happen because pressure accumulates, because heat requires release, because water must be counted and moved. The clicking thing pauses. It places sound into silence and waits to see what the silence does. I would feel its clicks through ceiling ribs, through walls, through the little service corridor where I keep myself thin and unnoticed. Sometimes, after its passage, the corridor would change in small mercies: a fallen strip of insulation dragged away from a drainage cut; a cracked tile turned so its sharp edge faced the wall; dead moths moved into the gutter where they would soften and feed the films.

I would not know whether to call it animal, machine, or citizen.

But I would understand that it repairs what kindness notices, not what the city’s rules require.

That would make it dangerous in a way the city is not.

The city is indifferent. The clicking thing may be attentive.

I would avoid being attended to.

And yet there would be times I depended on it. Once, perhaps, a panel near my sleeping crack would seal itself with silver film because a hairline fracture offended some deeper rule. My old hollow would grow too warm, too airless. I would flatten outside it for a full cycle, exposed beside the corridor, while moths lifted and pumps hammered and skitterlings detoured around me. Then from above: three dry clicks. A pause. Another click from the transit mouth behind me.

By the next stillness, debris would have been arranged under a bench near the acoustic shelter, making a new narrow dark.

I would move there.

Not gratitude, exactly. Gratitude is large. But I would remember the pattern.

If humans came through, I would know them first by their mistakes.

They would pause too often under open space. They would breathe with fear when the diagnostic lights flared. They would put weight on plates that had been hollow for years. Their footfalls would be tall, blunt, full of water. If they were new, they might speak loudly to push back the darkness, and every listening body in the corridor would contract. I might knock one down by accident, expelling air in a hard cough of self-defense. Then I would fold and become refuse, hoping they had learned.

If they were not new, they would pass softly.

Soft shoes. Bowl at hip. Filter cloth. One hand touching wall where the mineral bloom is smooth. They would wait for skitterlings before drinking. They would never follow shadowless light. They would mark only in dust. Their voices would be close to breath.

I might allow such a person to know I was alive.

Not by revealing myself fully. But perhaps when they slept in the acoustic shelter, I would unfold beneath the bench and take the mites stirred by their warmth. My pit-mouths would open and close. Their breathing would slow. The city’s roar would drop there to a manageable murmur, and for a while we would share the same pocket of permission: mammal and corridor listener, both using an accommodation built for citizens no longer present.

If they placed their palm in one of the central plaza depressions and the city played voices, I would feel the voices through the floor before I heard them.

Remain below.

Allotment adjusted.

The river has been rescheduled.

Their body would change at those phrases. Human bodies always do. Even if they do not understand the language, they understand announcement. They understand being addressed by a system too old to care whether they are the intended recipient.

I would not understand the words as words.

I would understand that the city had briefly made a population out of absence.

Perhaps that is what I would be, too: not a survivor of the city, but one of the ways absence has learned to have a body.

A low thing in a side corridor.

A listener made of pleats and dust-colored hide.

A citizen of vibration.

I would know the city not as ruin, nor as home in any sentimental sense, but as the total grammar of possible life. Warmth means ceiling vents. Thunder means crossing. Violet means feeding for eels. Blue pulse means the high one moves. Rain means unfold. Silence means wait. A dry pool means memory. A wet basin means do not disturb. A shadowless light means there is no ordinary distance there.

My world would be small, but not simple.

From my corridor, I would know the plaza by its impossible stillness. I would know the north by pressure. I would know the east arcade by the sharp little withdrawals of the ocher nodules when skitterlings touched them. I would know the south by the wet electrical smell of flooded machines. I would know the surface only as rare abundance: sand trickles, sudden ceiling sweat, brown water, root sweetness, the city roaring greedily as it receives what fell from a sky I have no need to imagine.

I would live long, perhaps, but not noticeably. My growth would be slow. My deaths might not be individual. When part of me tore on a jagged plate, that part might dry, seed fungus, feed skitterlings. When I grew too large for one hollow, I might divide along a pleat and leave a smaller listener behind, pale and soft, pressed under the bench until dust made it credible. Memory would not be stored in a brain alone but in membranes trained by repeated cycles. The young would inherit little except sensitivity. The city would teach the rest.

And if, after many warm years, I died during a dry interval, there would be no ceremony.

My body would flatten. The pits would seal. Dust would settle. Moths might take the softer edges. Fungi would bead along my underside after the next rain. Skitterlings would cross me if I did not sour the path. Eventually I would become part of the fine organic sweetness under the mineral smell, one more dark layer in the seam between construction and geology.

The corridor would remain.

The pumps would start.

The root curtains would drink before droplets fell.

The city would continue its civic work around the shape where I had been, making room around absence, spending the little light, gathering the little water, roaring like a lung.

And for a while, perhaps, the floor plate nearest my old hollow would answer pressure with a slightly different tone.

That would be enough.
