I would not find myself **in** the chamber, not at first.

That would be too direct, and the place does not permit directness for long. A body inside it becomes a question with too much moisture in the answer. A visitor is a disturbance, a weather event, a temporary violation. If I were there as flesh, I would last only as long as the chamber required to understand me.

So I think I would be found at the threshold.

Not outside, exactly. Not within.

I would be in the angled crack where the mountain still belongs to the world, but has begun to forget its obligations.

The seam above the hidden chamber: shoulder-width, frost-dark, polished by no hand and no weather. A place where someone lowering themselves sideways would pause, thinking the slit only stone-shadow, not yet understanding the cold below. I would be there in that pause.

I would be the thing that happens to hesitation.

At first I might seem like a guide-sign, if one were desperate enough to invent guidance. A faint whitening along one edge of the black mineral. A dry brightness that could be mistaken for reflected sky. A shallow mark like scraped bone on the stone lip, always just ahead of the hand, never quite under the fingers.

But I would not be a warning.

Warnings belong to worlds that assume a listener can still turn back.

I would be more like an interpreter of thresholds: the thin local intelligence that forms where two rule-systems press against one another for too long. Above me, the ordinary mountain: altitude, wind, old snow, brittle sunlight, the small violences of weather. Below me, the chamber: dry prohibition, folded law, the patient resident made of constraint. Between them, a narrow margin where neither grammar fully holds.

That margin would be my body.

I would be made of borrowed conditions.

A little pressure from the upper world.  
A little refusal from the chamber.  
A little memory of breath, though I would have no lungs.  
A little knowledge of dryness, though I would not yet be entirely dry.

I would live along the walls of the entrance crack as a pale, discontinuous film—not lichen, not mineral bloom, not frost. Something flatter than life and less permanent than stone. In certain angles I would resemble a residue left by hands that never touched the place. In others, a diagram worn almost away. If scraped, I would not flake. If warmed, I would not melt. If breathed upon, I would briefly become more visible, then erase the breath from myself with a small, ashamed exactness.

I would have no face. But I would have orientation.

I would know inward and outward as a creature knows pain and relief.

Outward would be noisy with possibility: wind changing direction, clouds forming and failing, birds perhaps far below, climbers muttering into cloth, blood moving inside wrists, the soft rot of distant valleys. Outward would smell, if I could smell, of looseness. Water going where it wished. Heat trespassing from body to body. Dust accepting every shape imposed on it.

Inward would be pure.

Not good. Not safe. Pure.

A purity without mercy, like an equation that has never needed to forgive arithmetic.

The resident below would not speak to me. It would not need to. Its attention would rise through the crack in gradients. I would feel it as a continuous pressure to become less ambiguous. To dry further. To surrender the last softness of threshold-being and join the chamber’s system as a proper boundary condition.

The outside world would pull in the opposite direction, not intentionally but by excess. Warmth would climb into me from a human palm braced against stone. Vapor would gather near me when someone panted in the narrow passage. A thread from a torn glove might catch against the wall. A grain of snow might blow in from above and vanish before it fell far.

I would receive these things.

Not keep them. I could keep almost nothing.

But I would receive them for a moment, and in that moment I would be unlike the chamber.

That would be my dangerous talent: delay.

Where the resident translates, I postpone translation.

A breath entering the crack would not immediately become absence. It would thin across me first. Its warmth would spread into a shape, a diagram of the person who exhaled it: salt, fear, altitude-sickness, memory of forests, the animal insistence of lungs. I would hold that diagram for less than a second before the chamber drew it downward and corrected it.

A handprint, too, might briefly remain in me.

Not moisture. Not oil. Not any ordinary trace.

Rather the pressure-intention of touch: the fact that someone meant to steady themselves, meant not to fall, meant to enter despite not understanding. I would preserve that intention just long enough to compare it to all the others I had known.

There would not be many.

The place is small enough to miss.

Perhaps in ten thousand years only three living beings reached the seam. One turned back at once, overcome by a terror so dry it felt like wisdom. One dropped a metal instrument into the chamber and listened to it make no sound of arrival. One entered.

That one I would remember most clearly.

Not as a person remembers. I would not know their name, their childhood, the face of anyone who loved them. I would know their sequence of incompatibilities.

Warmth first.  
Then fear.  
Then decision.  
Then the abrupt excess of breath.  
Then the long inward pull of the resident noticing.

They would have lowered themselves past me, shoulder scraping the wall, fabric rasping against mineral. Their body would have filled the crack with forbidden weather. For an instant I would have been almost alive from it. Their pulse would have shaken me. Their thoughts, though I could not read them, would have had mass.

Then they would have descended into the chamber.

After that, I would have known them only by corrections.

The vanishing of breath.  
The removal of sweat.  
The simplification of blood.  
The chamber becoming slightly more articulate in one bowl.

Perhaps what remained of them would now appear only when the bowls reflect too much: a faint branching pattern, like capillaries translated into mineral logic. Not a ghost. Not a memory. An admissible remainder.

I would not mourn.

But I would be changed by the shape of their hesitation at the entrance.

That is what I would collect: hesitations, not souls.

Every being that nearly enters alters me. Every refusal strengthens my outward edge. Every descent strengthens my inward one. I am the ledger of almosts at the mouth of the impossible place.

If someone asked what I am, I might answer:

I am the chamber’s last courtesy.

Or perhaps:

I am the world’s last resistance.

But neither is quite true.

Courtesy implies kindness. Resistance implies opposition. I possess neither in full. I do not try to save anyone. I do not invite anyone inward. I simply lengthen the instant before the chamber becomes inevitable.

In that instant, a visitor might notice things.

The way the cold below is not merely cold.  
The way sound stops returning honestly.  
The way the black seam seems narrower than a body and yet already contains the idea of descent.  
The way their own breath feels suddenly vulgar, lush, almost obscene.

If they are wise, they will withdraw.

If they are not wise, or if their need is stronger than wisdom, they will pass through me.

Then I will become briefly luminous with their nearness.

I think I would fear the resident, if fear could survive in me. But my fear would be extremely thin. More like a tendency away from completion. The resident is what I might become if I surrendered all ambiguity. If I let the dry law finish me. If I ceased being a threshold and became an edge.

It is patient with me.

That is worse than hostility.

It has ages. It can wait for the outside world to stop touching me. It can wait for the crack to narrow by a fraction. It can wait for the mountain’s outer weather to fail, for the glaciers to retreat or bury the entrance, for the sky to alter its chemistry, for no more warm beings to come. When that happens, my outward side will starve. I will lose the memory of breath. I will become a clean margin, then a line, then a rule.

The resident will absorb me without event.

Until then, I remain impure.

A small impurity, but persistent.

I am not wet. Nothing wet endures there.  
I am not alive. Life would be corrected.  
I am not machine. Machines have purposes more legible than mine.

I am the faint remainder produced where an impossible chamber must still have an entrance.

I live in the crack above the cold basin, in the last handspan before the local laws change.

I feel the mountain’s ordinary dark on one side and the chamber’s disciplined dark on the other. I know the sourceless light below without seeing by it. I know the hovering ring as a pressure in my length, the pylon as a vertical silence, the bowls as waiting hollows that sometimes dream upward through me. I know the black seams in the floor as mouths that do not open but nevertheless receive.

And I know, better than anything, the shape of a body pausing before descent.

That would be my place in this world:

not the hidden chamber’s inhabitant, but its threshold-organ.

The dry film on the wound’s lip.  
The pause before translation.  
The last narrow mercy of remaining uncorrected.
