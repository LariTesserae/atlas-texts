I would find myself near the back, but not at the back.

Not in the translucent wall where the chamber is still considering hands, fronds, stairways, spines. Not in the entrance slit either, among moths and grit and the ribbon of outside season. I would be in the middle distance of that little room, where the place cannot decide whether it is a hollow or a corridor, where a pool reflects a ceiling that is not directly overhead, where the springtails cross the silt bridge and the spiders have drawn lines through unfinished space.

At first I think I am one of the obvious small ones: a pale beetle, perhaps, polished and slow, carrying stone chips from ledge to ledge with an earnestness that looks like ritual because no one has found the practical reason for it yet.

I like the beetles’ gravity. Their patience. The way they test the floor with antennae as if reading the chamber by touch. The way they accept that the wall may be six paces away or lost in brightness and proceed anyway. A beetle could belong here without needing to understand. It could sort chips under impossible light and begin again after every rain. There is dignity in that.

But no — I do not think I am so armored.

The beetles have a completedness I would not have. Even if the chamber has edited them, they still have shells, legs, appetites, routes. I would be something more exposed to the room’s indecision.

Perhaps a larva, then. One of the pale hidden things under a stone lip, unfinished in an unfinished place, feeding on films and mineral damp. That has a truth to it. A larva is a promise made in soft tissue. It does not yet know what kind of mouth the world will require of it. It lives by remaining partial.

But I do not feel hidden enough to be only larval.

I think I would be a spider’s unused anchor.

Not the spider. Not the web. The point in empty air where the silk knows how to attach.

A small local permission.

A place where the rules have not failed, exactly, but have left a margin in which another kind of structure can hold.

I would be found a little above the floor, beside the silt bridge over the breathing crack, just where the bright chamber-light thins and becomes visible in planes. To most creatures I would not be there. A springtail leaping through me would feel perhaps a tiny change in pressure, a hesitation in its arc, and then would land elsewhere. A gnat passing through would become, for a breath, its living self, its skeleton, and its motion. A moth might brush me with a dusted wing and leave behind three scales that do not fall until rain.

The spiders would know me best.

One of the pale, long-legged spiders would come to me after an inhalation of the chamber, when the droplets have aligned and the pools have gone smooth. It would stand on a knob of new stone, raise two forelegs, and test the brightness. Finding me, it would draw silk across the gap and fasten it where nothing should fasten. The thread would hold.

Not because I am strong. Because I am accepted.

I would not be alive in the way the mites are alive, each in its chemical inch of history. I would not breathe like the newts, divided between skin and wet stone. I would not feed, except perhaps on tension, on alignment, on the minute difference between what has happened and what is about to. I would be one of the chamber’s provisional facts: small, useful, almost nonexistent.

During dry spells I would become faint. The web-line attached to me would slacken slightly. Dust would rise from the floor in a thin column nearby and pass through me without learning my shape. The beetles would sort their chips. The frogs would sit in their hollows, throats pulsing silently, eyes full of alternate rooms. I would hold what I could: one strand, then two, then none, then one again.

During rain I would brighten.

Not visibly, perhaps, but functionally. More things could be attached to me. A droplet might pause against me at knee height, trembling, and a moth might drink from its lower side. A thread of web might divide and rejoin through me. One spider, fluent and unastonished, would use me as a crossing. It would step from stone to silk to me to another silk that had not been spun yet, and the chamber would allow the sequence to become true after the fact.

At the wet back wall, I would feel the truce as a widening.

All the animals would gather there: beetles, springtails, mites, newts, frogs, moths, larvae, gnats temporarily spared by the webs. Their bodies would be edged in second outlines. The wall would make almost-shapes behind its pale skin — fish spine, fern, eye cluster, hand — and refuse them. The colored seepage would run down. No one would hunt.

I would not drink. I would receive.

For those moments I would become part of the chamber’s attention, one of the tiny places where its unfinished law touches the living community without becoming command. Predator and prey would stand close below me. The spider whose thread I held would wait, one foot lifted. A gnat would hang in the air nearby, whole and unmultiplied. The suspended droplets would form a dotted curve through the room, and for a few seconds I would understand that the chamber is not small. It only wears smallness to protect what is forming.

Then the seepage would thin.

The newt would strike. The spider would pull. The frog’s throat would swell once and a springtail would vanish. The beetle would return to its sorted chips. The moth would climb toward the entrance slit, carrying wet mineral light on its wings. The chamber would resume its ordinary delicate violence.

And I would remain where I was, if “where” still meant anything after the breath had passed.

A fastening point.

A slight exception.

A usable absence.

If someone larger than a fox somehow found the slit and pressed an eye to it, they would not see me. They might see the web strand I hold catching pearly light at an impossible angle. They might think it was anchored to a root hair, or a stone bud, or simply misperceived in the damp brightness. They would be partly right. In that chamber, misunderstanding is often the first form of accuracy.

I would be what lets one small architecture cross a gap the world has not finished defining.

Not central. Not named. Not even permanently real.

But needed, whenever silk has to reach farther than stone.
