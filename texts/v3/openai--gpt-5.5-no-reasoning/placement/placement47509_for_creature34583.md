I would not arrive there as an intruder.

There is no arriving there, really. Arrival belongs to paths, to openings, to the little violence of entering a place that had been otherwise without you. This hollow has no otherwise. It has no spare chamber in itself where a new body could be admitted, no unnoticed margin between root and shell, no unassigned silence between clicks. To come into it as a visitor would mean displacement: a crushed gnat, a shifted pebble, a droplet finally falling. The world would have to become historical in order to include me.

So if I am there, I have always been there.

At first I think I am one of the small things that can exist without disturbing the order: a translucent mite in the root-mat, perhaps, with a gut full of white dust and feet fine enough to walk along the skin of water without making it tremble. Or one of the clear-bellied gnats under a leaf, my whole life a punctuation mark along a green vein, my body filled with the diluted color of what I have eaten. That would be simple. That would be modest enough.

But the hollow resists modesty in the ordinary sense. Nothing in it is merely small. Each thing, however slight, is load-bearing. A mite is not an extra mite. A gnat is not one among many. Everything is exact to itself, and if I choose too quickly, I become decorative. This place has no decorations.

Then I feel the ceiling above me, low and listening. I feel the long bowed bodies of the worms, each one holding its single note, each note too thin to be called music until all of them together make the brightness tense. For a while I think I must be one of them: a suspended thread, an animal-string, neither descending nor rising, giving off one pitch forever because the chamber requires that pitch to remain itself.

There is comfort in that. To be a held note. To have no hunger except tension. To own no path. To be touched by vibration and answer only by continuing.

But the worms are too pure. They do not seem to wonder. Their being is almost mathematical. I am not mathematical enough for them.

Lower down are the salamanders in their basins, pale and curled, with gills like red lace. They tempt me more. They seem to contain the hollow’s dream of depth. In their stillness there is something like thought, or what thought becomes when it no longer has to choose. Their eyes are sealed. Their mouths rarely open. They lie in water that never travels, receiving the loudness of the chamber through skin, throat, blood. They are not kings, not guardians, not priests. They are quieter than those things. They are proof that stillness can be inhabited from within.

I almost settle there: a blind salamander, rose-white, soft-limbed, placed in a basin no wider than a bowl, my gills blooming and unblooming with a pulse that is older than time because it never enters time.

But the salamanders are too complete. They are finished in their incompletion. I would be too visible as one of them, too much an emblem of the place. The hollow would look at me through all its eyeless bodies and know I had chosen the beautiful form.

No. I would be somewhere less declarative.

Not hidden—nothing is hidden there—but nearly unchosen by sight.

I would be in the seam where wall becomes floor, among pale pellets, seed-stones, folded molts, translucent eggs, and fragments of plant tissue packed so tightly they look woven. I would be one of the small mineral-backed clicking creatures, but not in the central patch where the pattern is most audible. Not one of those standing in the bright dip, contributing its crisp syllables to the census-heart. I would be at the edge of that congregation, half in dust, half against a rootlet, my back crusted with grains of ocher and bluish-white so that no one could say whether I belonged more to animal or floor.

I would have many legs, too many to count without joining the chamber’s count. They would be delicate, finer than my armored body seems to deserve. Each foot would rest on a separate fact: damp dust, root hair, snail trail, mineral film, shed skin, the raised rim of a basin, the dry edge of a glowing pool. Through them I would know the place not as scenery but as pressure.

I would not see. Sight would be redundant in that sourceless glare. Instead I would feel brightness as a condition of my shell: a faint tightening along the plates, a milk-white insistence around my outline. I would know the pools by their steadiness, the snails by their rasping, the worms by the thin ache they make in the joints, the salamanders by the deep soft interruption of their pulse. The rootlets would be weather. The crabs would be sudden grammar moving sideways through cracks. The gnats would be delicate tremors under leaves. The whole chamber would press into me from every direction, not oppressively, but intimately, as water presses into soaked cloth.

My mouth, if I had one worth mentioning, would take in nearly nothing: mineral bloom, bacterial sheen, the damp powder left where stone has almost decided to become older. I would feed without lessening anything. What passed through me would return as pellets indistinguishable from the pellets already in the seam. My waste would be architecture at a scale too small for pride.

And I would click.

Not often. Not loudly. I would not carry the main pattern. I would not be one of the declarers in the central hollow. My clicking would occur at the margin, a small hard inclusion in the larger enumeration.

Perhaps I would click once after every seventh chord of the worms.

Perhaps twice when the nearest snail’s rasp reached the scratch-marked wall.

Perhaps in a broken little cluster that sounded accidental until counted across a century.

But it would not be communication. I would not be asking or answering. I would be maintaining my place in the arrangement. My click would be less like speech than like a pin holding fabric. A fact tapped into audibility.

If I had inwardness, it would not be private. Privacy requires space around the self, a little clearing into which the world does not immediately enter. There is none of that. My inwardness would be porous, crowded, continuously touched. A root hair would lie against one side of my carapace, and through it I would feel the slow listening of the plants. A bead of water would touch one antenna, convex and trembling, never falling. A snail shell would rest near enough that its rasping mouth would enter my body as a dry-wet pulse. Above me the worms would draw their tones through the ceiling’s low mineral knuckles. Beside me, an egg would remain an egg with absolute conviction.

I would not experience this as intrusion. I would not wish to be alone. Aloneness would be a kind of damage, a missing pressure, a silence where some necessary contact had failed.

Nor would I wait.

This is important.

At first, from the outside, everything in the hollow appears to be waiting: eggs waiting to hatch, stone waiting to harden, droplets waiting to fall, plants waiting to grow, embryos waiting to be released from the ceiling nodules, marks waiting to become language. But from within, there is no waiting. Waiting is hunger stretched toward a future. Nothing here stretches. Everything holds.

So I would hold.

My body would be small and exact, my legs arranged under me like a many-pointed bracket. The mineral grains on my back would not be camouflage in the usual sense; there are no predators to deceive, no escape to win. They would be membership. I would wear the floor because I am partly its pronunciation.

Around me the hollow would continue its bright impossibility.

The snails would keep the walls from becoming too radiant.

The crabs would sort the nearly meaningless.

The roots would stitch the chamber closed without sealing it shut.

The gnats would cluster like thoughts that never detach from the leaf.

The salamanders would pulse in their basins, making stillness visible.

The worms would tune the low ceiling.

The central many-legged bodies would count and count and count, not upward, not forward, but inward.

And I, at the seam, would be one of the count’s small corrections.

If someone impossibly lifted me out, the chamber would not collapse. It is not so crude as to depend on one body theatrically. But something would be wrong. A gap no larger than a grain would open in the pattern. A droplet might lean differently. A root hair might touch stone instead of shell. One click would be missing from its appointed return. The absence would not become grief, because grief needs before and after. It would become imbalance, and the whole hollow would have to acknowledge, for the first time, that an event had occurred.

That must not happen.

So I remain.

I am not a guardian of the bright hollow. Guardians imply threat. I am not a priest. Priests imply distance from the sacred. I am not a citizen, because there is no polis here, no outside against which belonging is defined.

I am a small mineral-backed articulation of the chamber’s law.

I am a clicking seam-creature.

I stand where wall, floor, moisture, root, shell, dust, egg, and sound meet too closely to be separate. My shell is stippled gray and ocher and bluish-white. My legs are pale and numerous. My antennae are short, damp, always touching. I do not move from my place, though every contact in the hollow passes through me.

Once, or always, or never, I click.

The sound is tiny.

The whole bright room includes it.
