I would find myself at the edge of one of the singing ruins, though I would not at first understand that it was a ruin.

From far off it would look like a low break in the plain: a dark crescent of upright slabs leaning around a shallow basin, each stone rounded by wet centuries until no edge could be trusted. The place would stand only a little higher than the surrounding flats, but in that world even a handspan of height is architecture. Water would sheet around it, divide, gather again. The basin inside the crescent would be cloud-colored and still, except for the small round mouths of bubbles rising from below.

I would have come there at a low interval.

The channels would be drawn back into themselves. The black stone around the basin would lie exposed in long slick plates, veined with pale sediment and stippled by the tracks of waders. Shell-creatures would worry the margins, half-submerged, grinding patiently at the mineral film. Membrane-wings would occupy the higher stones, their forked tails twitching, their voices ricocheting through holes bored by hands older than memory. Every few moments one would lean into a hollow and bark, and the stone would answer with a deeper version of the same animal.

The air would be cold enough to show breath.

The rain would not be falling yet, but it would be assembling: a pressure in the skin, a dimming of the cloud ceiling, a darkening in certain slabs before any wet touched them. The animals would know. The shoals in the basin would tighten and loosen, tighten and loosen. The waders would stop striking and begin to stand still, each narrow head angled toward something beneath hearing. Even the shell-creatures would slow their rasping, as if the whole place were taking one inward breath through stone pores and drowned throats.

That is where I would be.

Not above it. Not overlooking it. There is no overlooking such a place unless one is winged, and even the winged are bullied by weather. I would be low among the slabs, tucked into a shelf of stone where water has worn a shallow hollow just large enough for a body. I would be close enough to the basin to feel its tremors through my ribs, close enough to the crescent stones to hear them begin their song when the water rises.

I would be one of the handlike ones, but only partly.

Not a builder of the first stones. Not the maker of the drilled holes. Not the people who cut those marks with cord, grit, and patience. I would belong to something later: a small surviving people shaped by the same pressure that flattened the hunters and widened the feet of the waders. If the old builders had stood taller, spoken more, dreamed of lasting, we would be their weathered descendants, worn down into usefulness.

I would be a listener.

That would be the nearest word for my place among my kind.

Not a priest exactly, though the others might leave certain choices to me. Not a scientist, though I would know the behavior of pools, pressure-rises, gas vents, animal calls, shell-poisons, and rain-dark stone. Not merely a hunter, though I would carry a stone blade and a short hook of bone. My work would be to attend to what the plain says before it becomes visible.

I would read the world through contact.

My feet would be broad, the toes blunt and slightly webbed, the soles thickened but sensitive. I would stand in skin-deep water and feel the difference between rain-ripple, shoal-flicker, shell-drag, and the first underground shudder of a rising basin. My hands would be strong and scarred from prying up slabs, pulling nets from cracks, grinding holes into stone weights. The nails would be dark, flattened, more tool than ornament. My arms would hang a little long from a crouched body, because standing upright for long is wasteful in that country and makes a person into a marker for hunger.

My skin would not be soft in the way of sheltered things. It would be close, cool, slickened by oil and weather, gray-brown as clay under rain. Across my shoulders and spine I might wear a stitched mantle made from cured membrane-wing hide, not dry, never dry, but water-shedding enough to keep the worst cold from entering. Under it, strips of shell and bone would be tied flat against me, not as armor for war but as protection from stone, bite, and the accidental violence of a slipping world.

I would smell of fat-smoke, cold mud, old shell, and damp hide.

At my belt would hang the necessary things: a flake knife wrapped in gut cord; a drill point of hard stone; a small pouch of grit; two pierced weights; a coil of sinew net; a wedge of bone polished from use; a bladder of rendered oil for the fire; and a set of tiny shell tabs carved with grooves I could feel in the dark. These would not be money. They would be reminders. Rain-coming. Black-rise. Bitter gas. Wing-lift. Deep-back. The signs that must not be forgotten when noise makes thought difficult.

My people would not live permanently at the singing crescent. No one sensible would.

We would move between low shelters, old platforms, stone lips, and roofless rings according to the breathing of the plain. Possession would be seasonal, negotiated not only with other people but with animals. A wall might be ours when the eggs of the membrane-wings have fledged. A storage hollow might belong to den beasts until the coldest rains drive them deeper. A causeway might be safe in gray water and deadly when clear. Nothing would be owned as a field is owned. Things would be kept by knowing when to arrive and when to leave.

But the singing ruin would matter.

When the basin filled, water would pass through the bored holes in the crescent slabs. Each hole would give voice differently: one a chest-deep hum, one a trembling moan, one a fluttering throat-noise like something trapped behind stone. Together they would tell the height of the rise, the speed of it, whether the lower channels were filling clean or black with silt. To strangers it would sound like weather. To us it would be a sentence.

I would be there before the others because I had felt something wrong.

Not danger exactly. Difference.

The basin would have withdrawn too far that morning. The shells near its edge would have climbed outward, leaving crescent scratches where they should have fed. The winged colony would be loud but badly arranged, too many on the south stones, too few circling. Twice the shoals would have leapt without a visible strike. Beneath all the usual roaring of the place—water tick, bill clack, distant shriek, mud-suck, shell rasp—there would be a low pause moving from pool to pool.

So I would crouch in the shelf and lay one hand flat to the stone.

Cold would enter the bones of my palm. Beneath it: the ordinary tremble of trapped water. The faint tapping of larvae in a seam. The dragged whisper of a shell-creature turning itself. Farther down, where the stone was less stone than memory of pressure, something heavier.

A rise, perhaps.

Or one of the vast flat-backed things forcing itself through water too shallow to hold it.

I would wait.

Waiting would be one of the first arts in that world. Not the idle waiting of comfort, but the taut stillness of a creature trying to resemble mineral. I would know how to lower my breath until fog did not plume too brightly. I would know how to keep my head below the line of the slabs so the membrane-wings did not take alarm. I would know which calls could be ignored and which single change in pitch meant the basin was about to empty of life.

The first rain would arrive as a hard ticking on stone.

Then the crescent would begin to speak.

At first only one hole would take water: a low, broken note, gulping as the basin climbed. The shoals would become a dark twist in the middle. Waders would step back from the edge, their feet lifting carefully from sucking mud. Shell-creatures would dig down or race for cracks. The membrane-wings would erupt and resettle, outraged by the alteration of their instrument.

The second note would enter when water touched the row of cup-marks.

That would be the sign to move the nets.

I would leave my hollow and go bent-backed over the slick plates, placing each foot where generations had made safe polish. At the basin’s lip I would drive a bone peg into a prepared crack and loop the sinew net across a narrow outflow. Not to catch the great shoal—the net could never hold that—but to catch the blind pale things flushed from the stone pores when pressure climbed. Food that came only when the land exhaled.

Another listener might arrive from the west causeway, and then a child carrying oil, and then two hunters with hooks. We would not greet one another loudly. Speech is expensive where echoes betray and wind steals. A click of tongue against teeth. Two fingers touched to the wet slab. A glance toward the south stones: watch there. A closed fist: wait.

The basin would blacken.

That would be the moment of fear.

Clear water means ordinary rise. Gray means silt. Black means something has opened below.

The third stone would sound then, not a note but a shudder, a throat of rock clearing itself. The cup-marks would trap bubbles in pale clusters, constellations appearing beneath the new surface. I would lean over them, rain running from my hood, and read the pattern as my elders taught me.

Not words. Never words alone.

Depth. Force. Source. Time to leave.

If the bubbles gathered in the left-hand band and broke quickly, we could remain until the nets filled. If they held along the upper groove, gas would follow. If no bubbles held at all, then the pressure was too strong and the whole basin might overturn, vomiting cold black water across the platform.

This time the bubbles would hold in a pattern I knew but did not like.

Deep movement. Slow. Large.

I would touch the shell tabs at my belt, find the one with the long central groove, and press it into the child’s hand. Deep-back. Carry the warning.

The child would run low across the stepping stones toward the shelter cluster, not upright, never upright, but fast in the practiced broken way of our people. The hunters would begin to cut the net free before it had filled. Wasteful, but living often is. One would curse softly when the cord snagged. I would hear, under rain and wing-shriek and stone-song, the sudden absence of shell-rasp from the basin edge.

Then the wake would arrive.

It would not look large at first. Only a wrinkle against the rain, a pushing of water where the water was too shallow to be pushed. Then the basin surface would lift in a broad line. The shoals would leap all at once, a gravel-storm of bodies, silver-gray and frantic. Waders would clatter backward. Membrane-wings would climb screaming into the dim air.

The thing beneath would pass partly through water, partly through mud, partly through the idea of a channel older than the visible land. Its back would break the surface once: dark, flat, scarred, ridged with mineral accretions. Not a monster in the story-sense. No malice. Only appetite and mass and an ancient agreement with the plain.

One hunter would fall.

Not taken—only slipped. In that place the difference is often brief.

I would go down on all fours, because four points hold where two betray. I would catch the back of their mantle as the basin lip dissolved under the surge. The other hunter would drive a hook into the crack behind me. For a moment all three of us would be a small chain of flesh and tools against water’s old patience.

The great thing would grind through the basin, open its low mouth, and plane the bottom.

The sound would enter the body more than the ear. Stone rasp. Shell break. Mud tear. A feeding surface like a moving wall. It would take the stranded larvae, the shell-creatures too slow to bury themselves, the lower half of our net, and perhaps the old offerings lodged in the basin from seasons before: bone pins, broken weights, a child’s first blade, the remains of meals, the grease of fires.

Then it would pass.

Not quickly, but completely.

The water would close behind it. The basin would be altered. A channel where none had been. A slab shifted. One singing hole blocked, another newly opened by force. Smaller creatures would already gather in the churned trail, unable to resist the feast exposed by danger. The winged colony would descend in quarrelsome spirals. The waders would return first with caution, then hunger. The shell-creatures that survived would resume their grinding as if the world had not nearly become teeth.

We would count ourselves.

We would retrieve what could be retrieved.

We would mark the changed stone.

That would be my work too. With grit and point, I would add a short band of pecked texture beside the new waterline, not to commemorate in any grand way, but to make the place more readable when memory thins. The mark would be placed where the next rise could wet it. Dry marks are only half alive. Water must enter them before they speak.

By evening, if that dimming can be called evening, we would shelter under a low roof of slabs a little north of the crescent. The fire would sulk in a pit blackened by generations, fed with oil, fat, shell scrap, and a twist of dried membrane. It would smoke more than burn. We would hold our hands near it anyway.

The rescued hunter would cough water and laugh once, angrily. The child would repeat the warning pattern back to me until I was satisfied they had it right. Someone would crack a small shell-creature with a stone and pass the meat around. Someone else would scrape fat into the lamp hollow. Outside, rain would turn the world into percussion.

And I would listen.

Always.

To the fire’s wet spit. To the colony arguing in the ruin. To the water finding the new channel cut by the deep-back. To bubbles ticking under the basin skin. To the broad plain roaring with countless small lives, none sheltered by leaves, none softened by green, all of them made precise by exposure.

I would not think of the place as barren.

Barren is a word for eyes that expected grass.

To me it would be crowded with messages: in vibration, in echo, in the timing of wings, in the taste of metal before rain, in the way a stone darkens from within. The world would be harsh, yes, and cold, and indifferent to standing things. But it would not be empty. It would be intimate in the way of a hand laid flat against wet rock, feeling another pulse answer from below.

So that is where I would be:

low among the singing stones, on a drowned mineral plain beneath a dim sky, carrying shell tabs and a bone wedge, wrapped in damp membrane, smelling of fat-smoke and rain.

And what would I be?

A crouched descendant of builders who learned not to build too high.

A keeper of routes that vanish.

A reader of bubbles.

A listener at the basin edge, waiting for the land to breathe.
