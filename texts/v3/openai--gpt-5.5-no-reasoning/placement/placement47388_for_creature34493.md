I would find myself just below the ridge, not in the sky-cold openness of the mountain and not fully inside the pump-house, but in the warm seam between them: crouched at the edge of the central pool, half-hidden by sedge and pipe-shadow, where the machine’s knock enters the water and comes back as rings.

I would be something that has learned to be both animal and instrument.

Not large. The place would not tolerate anything grand for long. I would be low to the wet ground, narrow-bodied, jointed delicately, dark as oiled basalt except where mineral crust had whitened along my back and limbs. At first I might be mistaken for a lizard motionless on a warm stone, or for a length of broken linkage fallen from the pump-house and overgrown. Only after the deeper knock sounded would I move.

My skin would not be quite skin. It would be a damp, flexible casing, black-green, slick in places, velveted in others with moss so fine it looked like breath made visible. Along my sides would run faint seams like old manufactured joints, little brass-dark hinges furred with verdigris. My ribs would be narrow iron arcs under living tissue, not because I had been built in a workshop exactly, but because this hollow makes no firm distinction between growing and being assembled. The mountain gives basalt, the pump gives rhythm, the water gives patience, and something like me is what gathers at their crossings.

I would have hands suited to wet things: long fingers with soft pads, able to cling to sweating rock, rusted railing, fern stem, pipe underside. My nails would be translucent and curved, stained orange by iron. I would move slowly most of the time, not from weakness but from obedience to the pace of the place. Suddenness would feel vulgar here. I would know how to pause for forty seconds at a time and let the next pulse pass through me.

My head would be small, almost wedge-shaped, with eyes filmed like fogged glass. They would not see sharply at distance; there is little distance to see. They would read shine, heat, tremor, condensation, the minute deformation of a droplet before it falls. I would hear through my bones and palms. The pump-house would speak first through the floor, then through the pipes, then through the pool. I would know which valve was tiring by the flavor of its vibration. I would know when a new seep had opened under moss by the way the sedges leaned.

I would live as a keeper, but not a master.

Not the kind of keeper who repairs things back into their original purpose. I would not polish gauges or clear the walkway or restore the pump to efficiency. I would keep the hollow becoming. I would redirect a leaking thread of water with a shard of slate. I would pack moss around a pipe too hot for roots. I would scrape mineral crust from one channel and press it into another where the flow had become too hungry. I would loosen a bolt only when the metal needed to weep. I would open broken valves to the ferns and close living cracks against collapse.

At times I would enter the pump-house.

Inside, the air would be warmer and louder, amber-dark, full of belt-breath and condensation. I would climb the sweating brick interior and crouch on a crossbeam, listening to the machinery labor below. The pistons would not frighten me. Their rhythm would be older than memory, as familiar as a parent’s sleep. I would place both hands against a cylinder and feel where pressure gathered, where mineral had narrowed the throat of a pipe, where rust had become too beautiful and too dangerous.

I would not understand the old human purpose of the system. I would understand only its present one: to breathe, to warm, to lift water, to fail slowly enough that life can follow.

Perhaps I was born from eggs laid in moss beneath the grating. Perhaps I condensed out of repeated leaks and unused voltage. Perhaps a human maintenance worker once stayed too long, slept beside the pump in wet clothes, and woke by degrees into something else. The origin would matter less than the tenancy. In this world, belonging is not declared. It is acquired by dampness.

So I would belong because nothing on me could dry.

Moss would grow along my shoulders in a narrow mantle. Liverwort would stain the joints of my elbows red-brown. Tiny water-striders would sometimes stand on the shallow pools gathered in the hollows of my hands while I slept. Pale moths would cling to me at night, taking me for warm stone. Bubbles would form and release from the mineral ridges on my back when I lowered myself into the central pool.

I would sleep in a niche behind the pump-house where a broken pipe exhales warm mist. My bed would be made of rootlets, old insulation, fern litter, and softened cloth left long ago by people whose names the gauges have forgotten. Above me a red indicator lamp would pulse through dirty glass. I would dream in intervals of thirty or forty seconds: pressure, release; pressure, release.

My work would be small and endless.

In the morning I would climb to the rim of the hollow, but only as far as the abrupt boundary where warm air gives way to alpine cold. I would crouch there with the wind needling my eyes and look out over the cloud basins below. The outer world would seem too dry, too vast, too decided. Then I would descend again into the wet room of the mountain, where everything is unfinished enough to need me.

At noon I would move among the brightest moss, turning my body so sunlight could warm the mineral plates along my spine. I would drink not by lapping but by pressing my palms to sweating stone. The water would enter through the skin, carrying traces of iron, lime, oil, root, and basalt. I would taste changes that way: a new crack opening, a buried pipe thinning, a microbial bloom spreading orange along a runnel.

In the afternoon I would tend the central pool. I would remove fallen rust flakes when they smothered the pale rootlets. I would sink washers and bolts into arrangements that slowed silt. I would watch the submerged pipes release their tiny bubbles one by one. When the pump knocked, I would hold still as the ring widened across the pool and broke the reflected sky into pieces. I would wait for it to heal. That healing would be one of my measures of time.

At night I would become harder to distinguish from the place.

The plants would turn black. Mist would gather near the ground. The pump-house lamps would glow amber, green, red. My eyes would catch them faintly, not like an animal’s eyeshine but like wet glass. I would move along the bowed walkway on all fours, testing each grate with my weight, tightening nothing, correcting little. I might hum back to the ceramic insulators when they trembled. I might answer the pump’s deep knock by tapping once on a pipe with a mineralized knuckle.

If a traveler came, they might not see me at first.

They would notice only that the wet place seemed attended: a runnel cleared of leaves, moss packed around a leak, a pipe mouth kept open, the walkway passable though never dry. Then perhaps they would see me near the pool, crouched low, looking like a piece of the pump-house that had learned caution. If they reached toward me, I would retreat into fern-shadow. If they left something dry behind, I would return later and place it where the hollow could begin accepting it.

I would not speak in words easily. My language would be made of knocks, pauses, drips, and placed objects. A stone shifted from one side of a runnel to another. Three taps on iron. A valve opened a finger’s width. A fern frond laid across a threshold to say: not there. Moss pressed into a palm to say: stay and become wet, or go.

I would be a custodian of succession.

Not preserving the industrial ruin.
Not protecting nature from the machine.
Not choosing between them.

I would be the little intelligence that arises when no one else is willing to make such distinctions anymore.

A marsh-wight, perhaps. A pump-newt. A valve salamander. A damp familiar of basalt and brick. Something born from the agreement between seep-water and abandoned labor.

And if another century passed, I would change with the hollow. My iron ribs might rust through and be replaced by root. My moss mantle might thicken until birds nested in it. My fingers might become more like fern rachises, my eyes more like cloudy gauge glass. If the pump stopped, I would listen for a long time to the silence it left, then learn the new rhythm of unassisted water. If the pump continued, I would continue with it, knocking back from the pool edge, keeping company with its unnecessary breathing.

So I would find myself at the top of the warm mountain, in the wet hollow below the knife-edge ridge.

I would be the thing that tends what is neither ruin nor spring, neither creature nor machine.

A small, damp keeper of the young marsh-machine.
