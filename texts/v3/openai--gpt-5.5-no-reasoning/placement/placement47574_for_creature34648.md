I would not find myself at the center first.

The center is too declarative: shrine, diagrams, the tooth of the old intention, the place where any arriving mind would be tempted to assign meaning. The black seam opens there. The floor rearranges there. The little ribbon-things tend or feed or translate there. It is the obvious mouth of the basin, and because it is obvious, I distrust it.

Nor would I be one of the storm-riders, though for a while I think I might be. It is easy to want that life: a body almost all span, a self made from refusal of weight, sleeping in spirals above the saddle while the whole world below rings like a struck vessel. To live by falling correctly. To be kept aloft by the violence that would kill a denser thing. But the storm-riders are too sovereign, too cleanly emblematic. They belong to the upper grammar of the place, the script visible from below. I am drawn instead to the places where the system mispronounces itself.

I would be found at the southern lip, in the grove of tall dry columns.

Not in the crown, where the plates knock together and animals nest among the listening ears. Not quite on the ground either, where husks, bones, mica, skins, and powdered mechanisms collect in the thin wealth of the place. I would live between those registers, in the vertical interval: clinging to the braided mineral trunks, moving along them by hook and static, inhabiting the open rooms of air between one column and another.

At first I seem like one of the pale lizard-things.

That is the shape a traveler would grant me because the eye needs a handle. Flat body. Splayed toes. Parchment skin stretched over a ribbed frame. A head held low against the wind. A throat that can swell and tremble. I would be seen crossing a trunk sideways, then freezing so completely that my body becomes a flake of old ceramic stuck to the bark. My color would be the color of shade on white mineral: ash, bone, faint violet at the joints. Along my flanks, not scales exactly, but overlapping dry plates, each one hinged and capable of lifting by a hair’s breadth.

But I am not really a lizard.

The longer one watched, the less animal I would become.

My limbs are too narrow and too numerous when I unfold fully. Four main legs, yes, for the lie of familiarity; but beneath the ribs are smaller clasping members, folded close unless the wind becomes violent enough to require them. My tail is not a tail but a braided filament, often anchored behind me, sometimes thrown ahead to catch a new surface. My eyes are dark, faceted, not forward-looking but rimmed around the skull in a broken crown, so that I see less by image than by interruption: pressure dents, eddies, wingbeats, the muscular heatless shock of something passing through the air.

My mouth is almost useless for biting.

I feed through the plates in my sides.

When the grove begins to clang, each trunk conducting wind down into itself, I climb to a place where two vibrations cross. I lift my flank-plates and let the air strike the membranes beneath them. The membranes are dry, black, pleated like burned paper. They tremble. Their motion grinds microscopic mineral grains held in the plate-hinges, waking charge. The charge moves inward through dust-bound nerves, into storage knots along my spine. I eat resonance first. Matter second.

Matter is for repair.

I lick powdered bone from the lee sides of roots. I scrape mineral fruit dust after a burst. I steal the cast skins of smaller things and soften them—not with saliva, for I have none—but with abrasion, rolling them under my jaw until they become flakes I can press into my throat grooves. Sometimes I take eggs from the pylon-bent creatures if they wander too far south. Sometimes I open dead glider membrane with the combs inside my forefeet and harvest the salts left by flight. But these are supplements. The true meal is the grove’s bronze-glass shouting passing through my body until some of it remains.

I would be called, if called by someone trying to name me, a vane-gecko, a pressure-thief, a runglistener.

Among my own kind there is no single name. There are knocks for us, brief and local. The sound made by a side-plate closing. The sound made by claws releasing a trunk. The sound made by a warning struck through hollow bone. Identity here is not carried as a word. It is carried as a timing.

I live in a crack halfway up the third column from the cliff edge, though “third” depends on approach and the grove resists counting. The crack is not large. Nothing in the saddle believes in spacious shelter. It descends in a spiral around an old inclusion of black alloy, perhaps a machine fragment the tree grew around centuries ago, perhaps the seed from which the tree began. In hard winds the inclusion warms by a few degrees and hums at a frequency too low for most animals. That hum passes into my sleeping plates. It keeps me from starving during poor turbulence.

My nest is made from failures.

Shed membranes of storm-riders. Empty seed husks split along their seams. The transparent wings of flicker-flocks, stripped of red lines after the nerves are eaten. A little dust from the shrine floor, stolen by accident from the feet of a ribbon-thing and precious because it rearranges itself at night. Three shells of my own young who never opened properly. A ring of mica flakes placed not for beauty, exactly, but for reading: each flake shifts under wind from a different direction, so that in darkness I know which pressure is arriving before it touches me.

I am not solitary in the way a wet-world creature might mean it.

My kind do not gather often, because bodies distort the listening. Too many of us on one trunk would blur the signal and leave everyone hungry. We keep distances measured by resonance rather than space. Another of my kind lives two columns downslope, behind a hanging carcass that has been singing for most of a season. Another nests near the stone lip itself, old enough that its side-plates no longer seal, so it leaks faint blue sparks in dry weather. We know one another constantly. Not by sight. Not by affection. By the small deletions and additions we make in the grove’s uproar.

Courtship occurs during peak collision winds, when the eastern gulf exhales pale mineral haze and the mountain wall throws it back. Then the grove becomes nearly unbearable. Plates slam. The air fills with broken overtones. Predators launch because no one can hear them coming, and prey scatter because no one can remain hidden. During such times I climb exposed, flattening against the windward side of a column, and open every flank-plate.

If another answers, I feel it first as a flaw in the trunk’s vibration.

A counter-rhythm.

Not louder. Better placed.

We climb toward the same height from different columns, never touching at first. We exchange closures: left flank, right flank, throat, tail filament, forefoot comb. Click, click-click, pause, rasp. We make no song of our own if the wind can be used. The skill is to cut the storm into a pattern without opposing it. A clumsy mate merely clatters. A desirable one disappears into the noise and then reappears inside it, undeniable.

Eggs are laid in dry faults in the mineral fiber, usually three, rarely four. They are not soft. Each is a dull bead of layered ceramic, seeded with a little charge and a little powdered inheritance. They must be tuned before they quicken. For many days I press my throat-sac to the column and sound into the clutch, adjusting them to the local wind. If the season shifts before they hatch, they die prepared for a weather that no longer exists.

Many die.

That is not tragedy here; it is arithmetic.

Their shells remain in the nest. Later, when the old alloy inclusion warms, the shells tick faintly, remembering pressure they never lived to use. I keep them because the dead are instruments and because instruments are kin.

My predators are not always larger than I am.

A thorn-walker can take me if it reaches the trunk during a covered boom, its hooked forelimbs unfolding delicately around my ribs. The membrane flocks sometimes mob my young, slicing them loose before their claws harden. There is a small glass mite that gets beneath the side-plates and eats the hinge dust; an infestation can leave one unable to feed from vibration, full of noise and starving. The storm-riders do not hunt me intentionally, but a low dive can peel me from a column and cast me into open air. If I catch another trunk, I live. If I fall to the mat, I become a brief feast.

The shrine frightens me.

Not as a predator does. Predators are simple events, even when clever. The shrine frightens me because its openings are calm-shaped. When the black seam appears at its base, the wind around it alters without becoming quiet. The difference is precise, surgical. No natural lee behaves that way. Sometimes the seam opens during a basin-wide alignment, when all the grove plates flash silver and the dust rises in a single spiral. At those moments my plates lift against my will. The diagrams inside the building are not visible from the grove, yet something in my body responds as if a pattern has been drawn on my nerves.

Once, when I was young and underfed, I crossed the basin floor toward it during a lull.

This was foolish. Calm makes fools of many creatures. The vegetation had tightened; the reed-columns were only ticking; even the black-violet mats had gone flat and watchful. I moved from bare patch to bare patch, following small charges in the ground. Near the shrine, a seam opened. Not wide. Wide enough for the ribbon-things to pass.

One came out.

It had no face I could read, only a pale length edged with blue sparks, and it moved as if the air were not resisting it but remembering it. It touched a seed husk. It touched a line in the dust. Then it lifted one end toward me.

No sound. No threat.

But the side-plates along my body closed all at once so hard that two cracked. I fled back south bleeding dry black powder from the hinges. For several days afterward, every vibration I ate tasted arranged.

Since then I do not approach the center except by listening.

Still, the shrine is part of me. That is the thing one learns in the saddle if one lives long enough: fear is not separation. The pylons, the grove, the mats, the buried engines, the high kites, the ceramic grazers, the old machines pretending to be geology—none of these are background. They enter the body as rhythm, as mineral, as timing. To live here is to be edited continuously by the place.

I am not an inhabitant of the basin.

I am one of its smaller ways of hearing itself.

When the wind shifts at the southern edge and the grove flashes silver, I am there: flattened against a trunk, every side-plate open, claws sunk into braided mineral fiber, tail filament snapped taut behind me. Dust rises full of seed, static, powdered bone. The storm-riders tilt overhead. The shrine opens its black seam. The pylons lean into their wrong shadows. Beneath the stone, the old organs hammer.

For a moment there is no need to distinguish hunger from weather, body from instrument, terror from abundance.

The whole saddle inhales without lungs.

And I, dry as a shard, ring with it.
