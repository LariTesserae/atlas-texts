I would find myself where the plateau is most nearly able to dream of a forest, though it never becomes one.

Not at the center of a basin, where the exhalations are frequent and every surface is busy with feeding; not on the open flats, where the white salt plates hold the sky like a second sun; not high on the bare mesas, where only the most severe films survive in hairline fractures. I would be at an edge: the sloping margin between a deep vapor-basin and the raised salt plain above it, where the ground is cracked into long polygonal shelves and the first fan-colonies stand far enough apart that each still seems alone.

There is a cliff behind me, but it is not tall enough to cast much shade. Its face is fluted and streaked with copper-green and rust, and down its old seepage scars hang dry root-veils, flattened against the stone by wind. Below, the basin floor opens pale and luminous, patched with velvet crusts so dark they look like holes in the light. Farther out, column plants stand in irregular ranks, their translucent ribs holding a green dimness inside them. Between them, mineral haze sometimes gathers before dawn, close to the ground, silvering everything.

This is a threshold place. The basin breath reaches it, but not always. Dust from the higher flats settles here, but not deeply. At night the cold lingers against the cliff, and in the morning the first light arrives reflected upward from the salt. The chemistry is variable, which in this world means richness. Not abundance, exactly. Possibility.

I would not be an animal. Not at first.

I would be one of the hanging veils strung between a fan-grove and the cliff-films: a dry aerial mesh grown from shed banners, spores, mineral dust, and conductive threads. From far away I might look accidental, like torn green-black gauze caught on stone. Closer, I would be more complicated: braided hair-fine filaments, glassy knots, dust-beaded strands, membranes thin as old skin and stiffened with silica. I would be neither wholly alive nor merely dead structure. I would be a retained accident that the living around me had learned to use.

The fans below would have made part of me, releasing banners during vapor hours until some tangled on the cliff-root veils. The cliff-films would have answered slowly, growing into the snare, lending mineral threads and charge-bearing fibers. Crust spores would sleep in my knots. Dust would thicken me season by season. I would become a bridge across air in a place where bridges are not built.

My life would be contact.

Wind would be the first language I knew. Not a roar, never that, but pressure laid along my strands: uphill morning wind from the basin, evening drainage from the flats, long transparent tides arriving from beyond the horizon. I would not hear as animals hear. I would feel tension alter from strand to strand. I would know direction by pull, dust-load by weight, season by the taste of particles caught in me. When the air was still, I would hang slack and almost thoughtless. When wind moved, I would become articulate.

Light would pass through me and heat me unevenly. My dark filaments would take it in; my pale mineral beads would throw it back. At midday I would draw tight, reducing surface, sealing the living threads embedded in my mesh. At evening I would loosen. At night I would open what pores I had and let the cold enter.

During ground-exhalations, I would wake most fully.

Before the haze became visible, the fan-colonies below would change tension through their buried networks. The crusts would darken. The column plants would click open along their ribs. That signal would come into me as a faint electrical leaning, a rearrangement of charges across my filaments. Then the vapor would rise: metallic, bitter, useful. I would catch it in my dust-beaded knots. Microscopic gels within me would swell without becoming wet. Dormant cysts would register the chemistry and remain dormant unless the mixture was exactly right.

Some of what I caught, I would keep. Some I would pass along.

Downward, through strands touching fan-fronds. Sideways, into cliff-veils. Into crusts beneath my shadow, where falling dust from me would be richer than ordinary dust. I would be a collector and a delay: holding the fleeting breath of the ground for a little longer than the air alone could hold it.

Animals would use me without recognizing me as a being.

The small grazers would climb my fallen threads after nights of strong exhalation, scraping films from the lower strands. Their hooked feet would tremble through me. They would leave tiny crystalline pellets caught in my knots, and those pellets would feed microbes that thickened my living skin. Sometimes a grazer would die suspended in me, too dry to rot, becoming a pale jointed husk slowly milled by wind into minerals.

Gliders would pass near at dusk. A ribbon hunter might brush me once with a membrane edge, leaving behind a scent of hunger and cold digestion. If one failed in flight, if it struck the cliff or lost lift in the thin air, it might hang in me for days, wrapped and still, while my lowest filaments slowly entered the seams of its body. I would not be a predator, but I would waste nothing.

The tall browsers would rarely come so close to the cliff. When they did, I would know them first by static: a pressure in the air, a faint charge gathering along my dusted strands. Their sails would flash dark bronze and green beneath me. Their many feet would avoid the richest crusts. They might feed from the fan-colonies that anchor me, opening wounds with precise beaks. The fans would seal afterward, altered. Some of that alteration would move upward into me. For a season, my filaments might taste of browser saliva: catalysts, foreign spores, distant basins.

I would be a memory-device, though not intentionally.

Every wind would change me. Every mineral plume would add or subtract. Strands broken by dust would regrow along easier lines. Sections too exposed would bleach and become structural rather than living. Sections near the cliff would darken, fed by ions mined from stone. Over years, my shape would record the prevailing winds more accurately than any map. Over decades, I would sag into a curve that matched the basin’s breathing. Over centuries, if I lasted, I would become an archive of contact: dust from far flats, spores from vanished crust-colonies, metals lifted from old fractures, the chemical signatures of browsers long dead.

I would think as a tensioned surface thinks.

Not in sentences. Not in images. I would know nearness and change. I would know when the fans below needed less glare and arrange, by growth and breakage, to cast a finer shade. I would know when dust was rich in dormant spores and hold it longer. I would know when a section of crust below had been overgrazed, and my falling bitterness might discourage the grazers for a while. I would know the cliff-films as a slow dark pressure at my upper anchorage, old and mineral and nearly silent. I would know the fan-grove as a more volatile intelligence, responsive to light, vapor, static, and the brief extravagance of reproduction.

Between them, I would be their weather-written thought.

A society of surfaces needs surfaces that meet. I would be one of those meetings.

There would be no desire in me to move across the plateau. Movement belongs to dust, spores, grazers, browsers, gliders, pressure waves. My own form of travel would be extension and loss. A filament breaks free and rides the wind. A dust-coated knot falls into a crack. A spore wakes in the chemical shadow of a column plant. A piece of me becomes part of a crust, a fan, a cliff-film, an animal’s gut, a dry pellet, a future veil.

So I would not be a person in this world. I would be an almost-structure, an inhabited remnant, a living net held between darker lives.

I would spend the bright hours drawn tight against the hard blue-white sky.

I would glitter with closed pores above a basin of velvet mats.

I would hum faintly when the thin wind crossed me.

At night I would loosen, taking the cold into my fibers while the column plants below showed green seams like banked embers.

At dawn I would catch the first spores lifting from the basin floor.

And when the ground breathed, I would become briefly more than myself: fan, cliff, crust, dust, vapor, and charge joined in one dry trembling span.
