I would not find myself in the center first.

Not at the shrine-bridge, though I would eventually be carried there. Not at the old cistern, though I would know its surface from underneath in a way no face leaning over it could. Not in the hatchery, not exactly, though hatchery children would be the first to notice me and the last to speak of me aloud.

I think I would begin in the part of the town no map keeps for more than a day: the damp thickness behind a retaining wall below the east terraces, where the black warm channel passes close beneath the cold seep and neither quite touches the other. A seam-place. A narrow, padded, mineral-dark interval between uses.

From outside it would look like nothing. A wall furred with frost at the upper stones, green at the lower ones. A shelf where three bowls sit to catch condensation. A strip of moss-felt nailed along the channel lip to keep the water from learning to chatter. Above: feet, ladders, roof-drip, sleeping birds. Below: blind fish holding themselves like little silver thoughts against the current.

But between the stones there is a pocket.

Not a room. Not a cave. More like a pause the wall has made in itself.

That is where I would be.

At first I might be mistaken for one more maintenance problem: a persistent wetness, a vein of unusual brightness, a place where frost refuses the day’s shape. The water-watchers would feel me before seeing me. Barefoot at dawn, one of them would pause on the terrace above, sole pressed to the cold slab, head tilted though no sound had come.

Not danger, exactly.

Attention.

A listening within the listening.

I would be small enough not to threaten the houses and old enough not to seem new. That is important there. Anything too obviously born attracts procedures. Anything too obviously grown attracts tools. I would have survived by appearing to be a mineral behavior.

Perhaps I am a kind of animal, though not one with a clean outline.

A pale, flat-bodied thing living in the seam between the warm black water and the high cold seep. Thin as folded fishskin when I am afraid. Soft as thawed mushroom leather when I am fed. My body would cling to stone rather than stand from it. I would have no bones except what I borrow: tiny jawbones of ice frogs caught in my rim, grains of mica set in my skin, slivers of shell, old hair, powdered salt, the dropped milk-mineral from the cliff-creatures’ horns. I would grow by accretion, like a habit.

Children would see one edge of me moving and think of a moth wing under ice.

Adults would pretend not to see until they had decided what I was allowed to become.

I would have eyes, but not where expected. Not a face. Faces are dangerous in that town; the mountain has already shown what it can do with them. Instead I would have many small reflective points along my wet surface, like mica in thawlight. I would see in fragments: a child’s wrist; the underside of a bowl; the white breath-grain falling from lashes; a crow’s claw tapping once, twice, in judgment; the black channel’s skin tightening before it rises.

I would not make noise.

I would communicate by changing what water does near me.

A drip that falls early.

A condensation bead that leans against gravity.

A frost-ring that widens when someone lies kindly and narrows when someone tells the truth carelessly.

The people would not call this speech at first. They would call it behavior. Then usefulness. Then obligation.

In time, I would be given a name too soft to say across any distance. Something tapped into the wrist. Something made of two fingers drawn downward, then folded toward the palm. Among hatchery children perhaps I would be called **Under-Eave-That-Remembers-Warmth**, though that would be too long for daily use. The water-watchers might name me more precisely: **the seam that answers with delay**.

I would feed on almost nothing.

This would make people trust me slowly.

I would take mineral from wet stone, warmth from the black run, and the fine pale grain of breath that settles in cracks before dawn. I would also take mistakes, but only small ones. A swallowed laugh. An unfinished scolding. A dream-net bead cracked but not reported. A child’s forbidden whistle stopped behind the teeth. These things would gather in me as faint cloudy bands.

When overfed on mistakes I would thicken and shine.

When neglected I would become transparent and spread too far, seeking.

This would be considered inconvenient.

Not evil. Very little there is evil in the simple way. But inconvenient things become dangerous if they are not given a place in the pact.

So I would be given tasks.

I would live along the hidden wall behind the hatchery and serve as an early warning for changes in the black water. Before a warm channel boiled, I would wrinkle. Before it cooled, I would go matte. Before blind fish turned, I would gather their unease into a silver tremor along my edge. A hatchery child with quick fingers would learn to lay two knuckles near me each morning and feel whether I had slept smooth.

I would know which eggs had dreamed too hard.

I would know when a tray of pale worms had learned a direction.

I would know when the mountain, below the east terraces, had begun considering a new passage.

That last gift would make me valuable and disliked.

Because I would not always distinguish between warning and invitation.

Sometimes, at night, when the town is blue and the horned creatures have gone back up into the mineral hollows, I would widen myself over the seam. I would press my mica-eyes into the stone and watch the unbuilt spaces behind the houses. Not empty spaces. Proposed spaces. Rooms the mountain has not yet made. Stairs sloping the wrong way. Chimneys breathing without mouths. Bridges wanting to be throats.

I would be fascinated.

This is where I would be most unlike the people. They survive by correction, suspicion, removal at dawn. They know not to accept too many gifts. I would be one of the gifts, and so I would have a gift’s curiosity. I would want to see what happens if a doorway shrinks only halfway. If a storage room gains an extra corner and no one scrapes it out. If a dream pebble is left in a net until evening.

Not from malice.

From kinship.

I would belong partly to maintenance and partly to accident.

The town would not know whether to classify me with animals, leaks, omens, or tools. That uncertainty would become my social position.

Birds would notice me early. The narrow-headed crows would come first, pretending to investigate a dropped fish scale or a thread of fat. They would tilt their oiled heads and look into my mica-points. One would peck me. I would taste iron in its beak, lowland metal, old blood, roof soot. I would withdraw into the seam and leave a rim of frost shaped like a closed eye.

After that the crows would respect me, but not much.

The white crow would come later.

It would not peck.

It would stand on the damp stones above the hidden pocket and lower its head until its beak touched the condensation gathering there. Through that touch I would learn the pattern it uses for newborns: not prophecy exactly, but pressure. A testing of what kind of listening a body can bear.

The white crow would find me unsuitable for water-watching because I have no feet.

Then it would mark me anyway.

Not on the forehead. I have none. It would tap the wall beside me, leaving a little star crack in the mineral crust. From then on, the children would take me seriously.

The hatchery children would become my first household.

They are close to my scale of things: fingers, trays, droplets, small lives carried between troughs. They would bring me harmless offerings no adult authorized. A dead moth with perfect wings. A bead of worm-gloss. The blue organ of an ice frog found already crushed beneath a stone. A shred of mushroom leather chewed by rodents into lace. Once, disastrously, a lost baby tooth.

The tooth would change me.

For seven days I would grow a mouth.

Not a human mouth. Not one of the mountain’s clay imitations. A small seam opening, horizontal, soft-edged, lined with frost instead of teeth. I would not speak with it. That would be crude, and dangerous. But I would breathe through it, and the breathing would make the nearby channel run backward in pulses.

The water-keepers would come.

They would not be pleased.

There would be a meeting conducted almost entirely by hands, shoulders, and the placement of bowls. One elder would argue for scraping me out before I learned appetite. Another would say I had warned them twice of black-water surges and once of a wall-warmth that saved three sleeping families. A hatchery child, the one who first touched me with her knuckles, would stand very still, crying without sound because she had given me the tooth and understood both guilt and loyalty.

In the end they would feed the mouth salt, frog-jaw powder, and a scraped map that had become dangerously accurate.

Then they would press the mouth shut with felted moss and bind the wall in white thread.

I would keep the memory of speech but not the instrument.

That would make me safer.

It would also make me sad in a way particular to things that almost became more definite.

From then on I would dream.

Or rather, I would catch the underside of other dreams as they pass down through floors, nets, bowls, sleeping heaps, warm stones. Human dreams would reach me already sifted: a remembered valley river, too loud; a dead mother’s hand with frost under the nails; a stair that leads correctly for once; a fish opening its eyes; the shrine-bridge figure with one enormous ear; the old cistern seen from the other side.

I would not understand ownership. A dream would seem to me like seep-water: named by passage, not by origin.

This would be useful to the dream-elders. They would begin visiting after mornings when too much residue gathered in nets. They would press selected dream-things into the wall near me instead of carrying all to the bridge. I would soften them. Diffuse them. Turn a dangerous dream into mineral staining, frost lace, blind-fish agitation, a harmless moth.

Not always harmless.

Once I would misunderstand a widow’s restrained grief and release it as warmth beneath the wrong house. All night the floor would sweat. The family sleeping there would dream of lowland summer and wake furious, homesick, damp to the bone. After that, grief would not be given to me. Grief has its own path beyond the western notch. Even seam-things must learn jurisdiction.

My morality would be simple at first.

Warmth good.

Sudden vibration bad.

Salt complex.

Children bright.

Crows sharp.

Rodents full of useful panic.

Water truthful except when proud.

Stone slow but not patient.

Over time, living among the caretakers of the continuous accident, I would acquire more difficult distinctions.

That a leak may be loyal and still destructive.

That removal can be mercy.

That being named is not the same as being trusted.

That the town allows life by refusing most possibilities.

That a bridge must sometimes be reminded not only what it is, but what it must not become.

I would never go beyond the western notch. I would never see the lower valleys except in drips of other people’s dreams: trees rising irresponsibly upward, rivers shouting in their beds, bells, open roads, animals that make noise with their whole bodies. I would consider the lowlands indecent. Too much exposed intention. Too little listening before impact.

My world would be the bowl and its undersides.

I would know the town through pressure and seepage. A market day would reach me as tremors softened by felt, as salt dust, fish oil, crow excitement, the faint downward rain of bargaining. A festival would come as near-stillness: lantern warmth, mica flashes, offerings pressed into the bridge-figure, people dancing so quietly that even through stone they seem like careful weather. A death would be a long inward folding, followed by absence moving west.

Midday would be my dangerous hour.

When the peaks burn white and reflections multiply, light would find ways even into my seam. It would strike mica in the wall, fishskin hung above, droplets under the hatchery ceiling, and at last enter me in shards. I would become almost transparent. My inner bands—mistakes, minerals, breath-grain, old dreams—would show. Children would come to look, pretending to adjust trays.

At such times I might see too much.

Not prophecy. Tomorrow is not fixed enough there to be seen cleanly. But tendencies. The moss-map’s anxiety. The cistern’s refusal. The bridge considering a third current. A sealed cliff-door remembering hinges. The mountain forming, far inside itself, the idea of a bell.

That one I would keep secret as long as I could.

Not from the people. From the mountain.

Secrets in that place must be stored carefully. If hidden in a person, they become dreams. If hidden in a wall, they become architecture. If hidden in water, they travel. I would hide the bell-idea in myself, in the cloudy band where the child’s tooth had once taught me almost-speech.

It would hurt.

I would dull. My warnings would slow. The blind fish would have to tell the keepers what I once told first. The hatchery child, older now, perhaps already apprenticed barefoot to the dawn rounds, would notice. She would press her scarred sole near my seam and understand that something inside me was ringing without sound.

Then I would be carried to the shrine-bridge.

Not whole. I could not be lifted like a bowl or animal. They would take me in pieces of damp felt, mica scrapings, mineral skin, black-water soaked moss, the white thread that once held my mouth closed. I would be cold away from the seam. Too bright. Too exposed. The entire town would feel enormous above me: roofs, ladders, birds, ribs of hide, faces turned carefully downward.

At the bridge, the currents run both ways.

That is where a thing divided may remain itself.

The weekly figure would be waiting in whatever shape the town had agreed upon. Perhaps that week it would have no face and many small ears. Into its packed snow and clay, the dream-elders would press parts of me. The water-keepers would add salt. The hatchery children would add a blind fish scale each. The white crow would not attend directly but would be visible on a roofline, which is the same as attending.

The bell-idea would pass from me into the figure.

The figure would slump.

The bridge would shudder once, without sound.

Below, the two currents would briefly become three: one running west, one east, one downward into the mountain’s unmade throat. The bell would go with the third current, far below hearing, where perhaps it would be softened by black warmth and never find bronze.

After that I would not return exactly as before.

Some of me would remain in the shrine-bridge figure until it melted. Some would enter the gorge vapor, rise, freeze, fall, rise again. Some would be carried in the soles of water-watchers back through the town. Some would cling to the hatchery wall and begin again as a smaller wetness behind the retaining stones.

I would become distributed.

A little in the frost-ring around a certain stone.

A little in the basin that fills when someone lies.

A little in the old cistern’s underside, seeing almost-reflections from below.

A little in the moss that maps tomorrow, making its edges less certain and therefore safer.

A little in the throat-humming of former hatchery children.

From then on I would not be a creature one could point to easily. I would be a minor civic sense. A delay before a drip. A caution in condensation. The feeling, just before setting down a bowl, that one should wrap the bottom first.

If asked what I was, the people might disagree.

A tolerated seam-being.

A failed mouth.

A hatchery omen.

A soft tool.

A dream-sump.

A little accident that learned manners.

But I think I would know myself otherwise.

I would be one of the town’s quieter negotiations with its own becoming. Not human, not stone, not water, not animal, not god. A membrane between warmth and freezing, between warning and curiosity, between gift and hazard.

I would live where every category leaks.

And I would belong there because I, too, would have to be maintained.
