I think I would find myself **under the northern weeping bluffs**, not out on the open polygonal flats, and not fully inside one of the old stone rings, though I would know them by vibration. I would live where fallen slabs lean together and make cold passages; where the daylight is already dim before it enters; where beads of water gather along a seam, hesitate, fall, and sound out the shape of the dark.

At first I want to say I am one of the pale bluff-creatures, compressible and slow, leaving a drag-mark in the blue-black clay. But that feels too visible. Too whole. The place does not encourage wholeness. It keeps making bodies provisional.

So I am probably something less individual than that.

I am a **listening colony** in the stone.

Not a film spread openly over the bottom of a pool, though I can become that for short periods. Not a shelled thing hidden in a nodule, though I use hollows. I am a distributed creature occupying the seam between a tilted black slab and the pale clay bank beneath it. My body is not continuous except during the wettest hours. In dry intervals I am only a series of darkened beads, smoky flecks, translucent knots, and threadlike bridges withdrawn into pores. A person looking into the passage would see mineral staining, perhaps a little greasy sheen where condensation collects. They would not see me unless I decided wrongly.

Most of me lies pressed inside a horizontal fissure no wider than a fingernail. The fissure runs several body-lengths through the fallen slab, then forks downward into clay, then opens into three little basins of black water. I occupy all of these incompletely. I am not in one place but in a relation among them: bead in the clay pore, veil beneath the stone lip, thread in the seep, pulse in the cold pool.

When the bluff weeps, I wake.

Water enters from above in separate drops at first. Each drop strikes a ledge, divides, enters cracks, falls again, and arrives changed by every mineral it has touched. I know these differences. Iron is a thickening in the mouthless parts of me. Sulfur is a brightness, not seen but tasted as urgency. Chalk makes me loosen and spread. Salt tells me to draw inward unless it is diluted. Clay comforts me. Black stone carries the far sounds best.

I feed on seepage, but not only by consuming what is dissolved in it. I feed on contrast: on the boundary between waters, on the brief chemical argument where a cold thread from deep stone meets a warmer skin from the open flats. My body arranges itself along those arguments. Tiny membranes open. Granules shift. I thicken where difference is richest, thin where the water is exhausted, and retreat when the chemistry grows too clean.

I have no eyes. Light is mostly a pressure of danger, a widening of the world. Shadow is not darkness to me; shadow is interruption, a change in surface rhythm. When something passes across the mouth of my passage, the falling drops alter before the body itself arrives. The air cools differently. Condensation gathers along another angle. The small resonances in the stone lose their upper edge.

Then I withdraw.

Not all at once. That would tear me. The exposed veils bead first. The beads darken and pull backward through pores. Threads release their grip on the underside of the slab and snap inward, each one becoming a wet line, then a knot, then a smoky speck. The portions of me in the black basins sink and flatten below the surface film. Within a few breaths there is no creature, only wet stone.

But when nothing large moves, I come forward.

In the dim hours I spread from my fissure in a thin grey fan. My outer edges are almost lace, but coarser, less decorative: a map of appetite. I cross the underside of the slab, then descend in small loops to touch the clay. If the water film is continuous, my parts join. If the film breaks, I remain separate, thinking separately until the next drop reconnects me.

Thinking is not the right word, but neither is instinct. I do not plan. I remember gradients. I compare present seepage with old seepage stored in hardened grains along my inner seams. I know, without possessing knowledge as a creature of nerves might know, that the third basin below the slab becomes dangerous after long rain. I know the low ring wall beyond the rubble holds bronze films after fog-heavy nights. I know the plate-backed grazer passes this way sometimes when the hollow nodules begin to ping in pairs.

The basin speaks constantly, and I am made to misunderstand very little of it.

A hollow knock far out on the flats comes through the black stone as a broad, flattened tremor. That is slab-shift, not footfall. A rapid clicking in damp clay, irregular but patient: a plate-backed animal testing crust. A suction pause followed by a soft rasp: bluff-creature feeding under another stone. The high needle-chirr inside mineral nodules: seed-shell colonies retracting from rain. Deep glugs below the clay: trapped vapor moving through older holes.

Some sounds are not events but conditions. A continuous silver hiss means fog is condensing over glassy rock. A low rounded pulse through the black water means the hidden pools have begun answering one another. When that happens, I contract into my lower chambers. Things from the deeper seams travel during those pulses. Not predators, exactly. More like unfinished portions of the basin’s own body, chemical moods with edges.

I have survived by being interruptible.

If the slab above me falls, part of me will be crushed into the clay, part exposed, part sealed in darkness. That would not necessarily be death. It would be division. Given water, the sealed part might become slow and mineral, feeding on almost nothing for seasons. The exposed part might harden into dull grains, some of which would soften again when rain returns. The part pressed into clay might discover a warmer seam and become another listening place.

I do not reproduce by making young in the ordinary way. I lose pieces that remember enough. During heavy rain, when the fissure overfills, beads of me detach and enter the overflow. Most are carried into wrong water and become residue. Some lodge beneath lips, inside pinholes, under salt crusts. If they encounter a rhythm they can bear and a chemistry they can use, they begin to pulse. After a long time, they are no longer pieces of me. They are another allowance.

The old structures matter to me, though I did not build them.

The waist-high piers across the flooded flat are too far for my body but not for my hearing. When rain fills their bowls and water spills from one to the next, the notes arrive through the saturated ground as spaced, rounded impacts. They make a sequence I can anticipate. Higher bowl. Pause. Lower bowl. Overflow lip. Hollow capstone. The pattern tells me how much rain has crossed the basin and how soon the bluff seams will thicken.

The broken ring closer to the rubble is more intimate. Its stones gather coolness. Its inner wall keeps a band of persistent wet at the height of my most mobile threads. Sometimes, after a rare flood, fragments of my outer beads travel there. They spread briefly along the submerged wall among disks, thread-creatures, and grey lace colonies. Those fragments never last. Too many grazers enter the ring. Too much open water. But while they persist, I feel the enclosure as a second ear: a circular hearing, unlike the long hearing of the seam.

I do not wonder who made the ring. Wonder requires distance from use. To me the ring is a modifier of condensation, a shelter for films, a resonant curve, a place where the mud receives falling drops in a slightly delayed voice.

Still, something in my inner grains responds to shaped stone.

There are grooves on the slab above me. Parallel marks, shallow, softened by seepage. Water collects in them before entering my fissure. Because of those grooves, every rain reaches me already sorted into several fine streams. I have grown according to them. My body branches where the grooves drip. My hunger thickens below the deepest cut. If the marks were made by intention, then I am partly formed by an extinct gesture. If they were made by water alone, then I am formed by patience. Here the difference is not large.

I would be safest in the hours before full dark, when mist rises and scale becomes uncertain. Larger creatures dislike uncertainty too, though they live inside it. The plate-backed grazer may pass then, almost indistinguishable from a moving patch of ground. I would feel it before seeing it, if seeing were mine: many small points, soft pads, a pause in which its underside tastes the clay. If it finds my outer veil, it will eat it. That is not violence in the basin’s terms. It will press down, rasp slowly, and leave the stone clean.

I can afford to lose the outer veil.

What I fear, if fear is the contraction before a known harm, is clean dryness. Not ordinary shrinking. I can become grains. I can wait. I fear the kind of exposed interval when the seam salts over and the black slab warms just enough to draw water out faster than the bluff replaces it. Then my bridges fail. My memories separate. Each bead becomes private. The basin grows silent in pieces.

In such times I retreat to the lowest black basin, where the water reflects nothing and sounds arrive late. There I become simple. A smoky clot under the surface skin. I do not spread, do not listen far, do not compare. I take in what little seeps through the clay and keep the smallest rhythm possible.

Then rain returns.

It always has, so far.

When rain comes hard enough, the whole passage becomes one instrument. Drops strike the outer slabs. The slabs ring. Water sheets down the pale wall, breaks over ledges, enters the rubble, strikes the basin surfaces, overflows, descends, gathers, presses upward through old pores. My body opens everywhere at once. Threads extend. Beads soften. Veils bloom along the underside of stone. I become wide, though still hidden.

In those hours I am almost joyful, if joy can belong to a thing without a face.

Not because the world is kind. It is not kind. It is exact, cold, unstable, crowded, and unfinished. But during rain the separations lessen. The fissure, the clay, the black basin, the carved grooves, the distant piers, the hidden nodule-shells, the grazing disks in shallow pans, the pale bluff-creature drinking carefully from heavy water: all of them participate in one saturated event.

The basin rings, and I answer by opening.

I am not the voice of the place. The place has too many voices, most of them water borrowing stone. I am one of its listeners that has learned to be edible, divisible, patient, and nearly indistinguishable from residue.

You would find me where the weeping bluff has dropped its slabs into a narrow cold passage, beneath the stone lip that never fully dries.

You would think I was a stain.

If you waited through the dimming, and kept still enough for your own warmth to stop disturbing the condensation, you might see the stain gather itself into branching threads. You might see pinholes darken. You might hear, beneath the louder dripping, a faint synchronized tightening in the wet clay.

That would be me, becoming continuous enough to notice you.
