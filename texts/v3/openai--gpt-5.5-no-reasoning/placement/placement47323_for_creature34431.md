You would find yourself not at the center of that world, because the plateau has no center. You would be near one of its shallow wet hollows, where the stone dips just enough to remember water after the rest of the plain has forgotten.

Not in the pool itself, at first. Slightly above it.

A little rise of worn basalt, low as a sleeping animal’s back, overlooks a basin no wider than a room. Around it are lichen rings, cracked salt edges, dwarf shrubs with leathery leaves pressed tight against their own shadows. A termite tower leans nearby, fluted and blind, its clay surface warm to the touch. The old paths of grazers pass within sight but not through the hollow; they curve around it with the unspoken caution of inherited knowledge. Birds come there after rain. Frogs knock there once or twice in the dark. Beetles vanish beneath the damp rim. Winged pale things browse the evening air just above it.

You would be in the seam between stone and life.

You would not be one of the large visible creatures. Not a grazer, not a circling scavenger, not a predator with a clear shape and a clear hunger. Those belong too plainly to one scale of the place. The being that rises out of this description is smaller, older in pattern, more compound. It is something the eye first mistakes for a stone, then for a root, then for an animal only after it has moved.

You would be a low, many-bodied creature of the damp-under-stone places.

Something like an armored amphibian, but not quite. Something like an insect, but too deliberate. Something like a spirit only because it refuses to be singular.

By day you would be folded beneath a flat slab at the edge of the basin, where the underside of the stone sweats a little even when the surface is dry. Your back would be plated in overlapping pieces the color of old clay and basalt dust, each plate carrying a faint crust of lichen or mineral bloom. Your belly would be pale, soft, sealed against loss. Along your sides would be fine legs or leglike feelers, enough that movement through cracks is not walking so much as a careful flowing. Your head would be blunt and low, with dark bead-eyes set not for distance but for nearness: moisture, tremor, salt, the shadow of a beetle crossing the entrance.

You would not hurry. Hurrying belongs to those who must cross open ground. You would be an inhabitant of thresholds: under rock, inside mud, beneath lichen, along the root-hair darkness where dampness lasts one more hour than elsewhere.

Most of the year you would be almost absent.

Not dead. Not sleeping exactly. Held.

Your body would know how to reduce itself to requirement. Breath shallow. Blood thickened. Limbs drawn in. Membranes sealed. A grain of life wrapped around its own continuance. Around you, ants would rebuild their roads. Lizards would bask above your roof. The grazers would pass and press the soil harder. Rain would fail, then fail again. The pool would be a pale crust, a memory in the shape of a circle.

You would wait without experiencing waiting as lack.

Then cloud would lower.

Before the rain reached the ground, you would know. Not by sight. By pressure. By the altered taste of the air coming through the pores of stone. By the termite galleries breathing differently. By the rootlets swelling. By the frogs, sealed in their separate darks, waking like questions.

The first drops would tick on the slab above you.

Then the world would open.

Water would run down through old cracks, carrying dust, dung, pollen, spores, powdered lichen, the scent of grazers, the bitter resin of shrubs, the loosened salt of the basin rim. Your plates would darken. Your sealed joints would soften. You would unfold one limb, then another, not quickly, but with the certainty of something following an instruction older than memory.

You would emerge at night.

At first you would look like a piece of wet bark dragging itself across stone. Then, in the brief gleam of starlight caught in the new pool, your shape would clarify: low, oval, jointed, glistening along the seams. Your feelers would test the mud. Your mouthparts would work patiently at the softened films of green life on the stones. You would eat algae, dead gnats, frog eggs if you found them, the softened edges of leaves, the bodies of smaller things that woke less carefully than you did. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be private.

You would also carry the plateau with you.

In the grooves of your armor would be dust from older dry seasons. Beneath your plates, mites. In your gut, dormant spores. Caught against your legs, seeds small enough to mistake for grit. As you moved from hollow to hollow, or merely from one side of the basin to the other, you would redistribute futures. A smear of mud on your underside might contain transparent worms, eggs of the flat armored pool-things, the beginning of a fernlike plant, the invisible intention of fungus. You would be less an individual than a weather event with legs.

Your life would be short in its active hours and long in its interruptions.

For a few nights after rain, you would belong wholly to motion. You would feed. You would mate by finding another dark, low shape beneath the wet lip of stone, recognizing it through touch and chemistry rather than display. There would be no song, no dance visible to the high air. Only a meeting in the mud, a joining of brief urgencies. Eggs would be laid not in open water, but tucked under a mineral flake where dampness retreats slowly. Each egg would have a shell like patience.

When the pool began to shrink, you would know before it showed. The mud would tighten. The frogs would fall silent. Dragonflies would hover over less and less sky reflected below them. Beetles would dig deeper. The grazers would come to the rim and leave crescent marks that dried at the edges.

You would withdraw.

Not in fear. In obedience.

Back under the slab, or into a crack beneath the lichen ring, or down into an old burrow abandoned by something with warmer blood. Your plates would close. Your skin would dull. Your limbs would fold so tightly that the difference between creature and pebble became a courtesy the world no longer bothered to maintain.

And there you would remain.

The plateau would pass over you in its immense, nearly motionless way. Sun. Dew. Hooves. Ant processions. Wind. The leaning termite towers casting the same small shadows. Stones exhaling faintly in afternoon heat. Pale winged animals rising at dusk from cracks not far from your own. Bones whitening. Lichen widening by a hair’s breadth. Grooves filling with silt. Paths deepening by the patience of feet.

If you had a mind, it would not be a human-shaped mind placed inside a small body. It would not think in names. It would think in conditions.

Wet enough.

Too open.

Salt.

Root shade.

Beetle.

Frog mass.

Danger pressure.

Return.

It would possess no ambition to master the plain, no desire to rise above it, no myth of escape into the younger valleys below. Down there, water speaks too continuously. Plants grow too tall. Sound tangles itself in leaves. Things rot extravagantly. The world is quick and green and immodest. You would not belong there.

You belong where life is hidden but not scarce.

Where the old stone has made an agreement with smallness.

You would be a keeper of intervals: the damp hour under rock, the week of water in the basin, the breath between droughts, the thin biological film between mineral and animal. You would be one of the plateau’s recurrent answers, a creature less born than resumed.

If someone stood above your hollow at midday, they would see almost nothing. A shallow basin. Tawny soil. A few dark stains of lichen. Termite towers leaning in their inexplicable agreement. A flat stone with one edge shadowed. Perhaps a beetle road. Perhaps the delicate print of a bird.

They would not see you.

That would be proper.

The plateau does not reveal its essential inhabitants immediately. It lets the obvious lives pass first: the grazers, the birds, the lizards flashing like sparks. Only after stillness, after attention lowers itself, after the eye learns to distrust emptiness, would your world begin to appear.

A dark seam under stone.

A bead of moisture.

A plate shifting where no wind touched it.

Then you: old as a habit, small as a withheld word, carrying in your folded body the law of the whole high plain.

Not a ruler.

Not a symbol.

A remaining permission.
