I think I would not find myself on one of the famous spans, not at first. Not in a place with a name that strangers are taught. Not in the Mirror Swales, not among the sleep-bridges, not in a high meadow under the bruised sun.

I would come to awareness somewhere half-useful and half-forgotten.

A side place.

One of those interstitial chambers that exists because larger things had to pass around it: a warm green pocket behind a cistern wall, above a drainage throat, below a village walkway whose traffic is felt more often than seen. Not abandoned, exactly. Nothing here is ever simply abandoned. But not central either. A place visited by workers with narrow purposes: to clear a filter, to test the taste of seepage, to retrieve a dropped hook, to check whether a swelling root has begun to press too hard against a water vein.

The chamber would be tall but cramped in its usable parts. It would open upward into dim ribs of living wood, down into a throat of vapor where old runoff falls in threads. Its walls would be layered bark, scarred by trimming, shiny with condensation. Blue moss would grow in plates along the lower curves. Transparent frogs would pulse in the rain cups. Pale insects would walk the ceiling in their quiet inverted errands. Somewhere close by, water would be moving under pressure inside a sealed green pipe, making a sound almost like someone breathing through sleep.

There would be a platform there, not meant for gathering. Just a maintenance shelf grown from three trained roots and stitched together with old fiber. The shelf would sag slightly toward the drainage side. It would be polished where knees and bare feet had worked for generations. A low rail would run along one edge, or what had once been a rail. Now it would be thick, moist, and faintly responsive to touch, with small sensing hairs that flatten when a familiar hand passes over them.

That is where I would be.

Not a great builder. Not a hunter. Not a priest of mirrors or a sleep-crosser with a name people lower their voices around.

I think I would be one of the water-listeners.

Not the grand keeper of a district’s whole rain memory, not the elder who decides which cistern may be opened during the dry curling of leaves. Someone smaller than that. Necessary, but local. A person whose work takes place in green dimness, behind walls, inside the living service passages of the suspended world.

I would be compact, probably, even among compact people. Good at folding myself into awkward spaces. Long-handed. Knuckled. Scarred along the palms and inner wrists where wet fiber has slipped and burned. My toes would know bark better than my eyes know distance. I would move with a permanent slight caution, not fear exactly, but the posture of someone who has learned that every surface is in conversation with forces not visible.

I would smell faintly of cistern moss, old leaf tea, and the sour-mineral tang of sealed water.

My wrap-harness would be dark from damp, patched with fungal felt at one hip, with loops for a short crescent knife, a bone awl, a little shell spoon, testing reeds, twist-cords, plugs of cured pith, and several narrow packets of seeds tied close beneath the ribs where body warmth can keep them. I would carry more water than seems sensible in a world of rain, because that is one of the rules, and because I would know too much about water to trust its nearness.

My hair would be bound very flat. No loose strands. No ornaments that swing. Perhaps a line of scent knots tucked close behind one ear: not decorative, but legible to those who work the same wet passages. My clan graft marks would be small raised crescents along the left side of the neck, the mark of people whose households tend cistern walls and root filters. The lichens in my braid, if I kept them, would be pale green-gray and almost odorless until rain wakes them.

My task would be to listen to the body of the place.

I would kneel with one ear near a swollen wall and hear whether the water behind it is clean or troubled. Not by magic, not quite. By rhythm, pressure, small changes in tone. By the thickness of the drip from an overflow lip. By the bitterness in a bead of seepage touched to the tongue. By whether the moss at the seam has gone blue-white or blue-black. By whether the frogs have left a cup they favored the day before.

I would know the ordinary moods of this small district: which conduit knocks before heavy rain, which root tightens in breeding fog, which cistern-flower opens too early when the upper catchment is overfull, which seep wall grows sweet before rot. I would know where not to put my hand. I would know which hanging vine is truly a measuring cord and which is an animal pretending to be one, though perhaps I would have learned that by being bitten once.

I would be called when a household says the drinking water tastes like sleep.

I would be called when condensation forms on the wrong side of a bark wall.

I would be called when children hear knocking from a sealed drainage hollow and an elder wants to know whether it is trapped air, a borer colony, a forgotten valve, or something that has learned to knock.

I would go with a lamp bowl of glow-fungus covered by my palm, and maybe one apprentice behind me holding the line. We would not speak much. Speech travels strangely in wet service chambers. Instead I would press two fingers to the shared rope: wait, slack, climb, cut, no farther.

I would not be especially brave in the way songs prefer. I would be the sort of person who survives by noticing early.

A stain in the moss.

A silence where insects should tick.

A door leaf that recognizes my pressure but hesitates before opening.

A drop of water falling upward and then pretending it did not.

I would dislike open spaces, though I would pretend otherwise. The great views across kilometers of dim air would make my feet remember falling. I would cross large bridges when required, seed packets pressed against my ribs, one hand on the rail and one eye on the rail’s shadow. If the same bird crossed from three directions, I would sit down immediately and wait, no matter who laughed. I would rather be thought timid than become a lesson in a children’s caution song.

But inside the cistern backs and root channels I would be calm. Those cramped passages would feel honest to me. Dangerous, yes, but intimate. The large world of voids and folding distances would narrow there into touchable facts: wetness, pressure, growth, blockage, thirst.

Perhaps I would have a particular responsibility for an old water route called something like Grandmother’s Throat — though not translated so cleanly. A vertical conduit grown generations ago from hollowed fern trunks and living root sheathes, carrying upper rain down through several households, a nursery moss, two fish bladders, a fermentation kitchen, and finally into a lower filter garden. It would be temperamental. Everyone would say so. Too fond of holding back. Jealous during dry spells. Generous after thunder. Some would say an ancestor was absorbed badly into one of its walls and still dislikes waste.

I would not say whether I believed that.

But I would always greet it before opening the valve.

The valve would not be a wheel or lever, but a living sphincter-flower braced in a shell ring, pale and muscular, responding to pressure, moisture, and a little hum at the back of the throat. My teacher would have taught me the hum. Their teacher would have learned it from someone who died before the Three-Root Span changed its listening. If sung wrongly, the valve would clench for days. If forced, it might tear, and then three terraces would flood while two went thirsty.

So I would kneel in the green dark, palm on the damp lip, and hum until the flower remembered opening.

That feels like my life there: persuading necessary things not to fail.

Not commanding. Never that.

There would be people who see me mostly when something has gone wrong. A young parent with a fevered child, asking whether the nursery water has soured. A fermenter angry because seepage has spoiled a batch. A bridge-thinner needing to know whether a supporting root can survive being lightened. Children peering into the service hatch, whispering that I go where the wall has teeth. Elders who do not praise but notice when no one has fallen ill after wet season.

I might be socially quiet even among quiet people. Not lonely, exactly. The canopy does not permit the kind of loneliness that comes from being unwatched; too much depends on everyone. But I would have a sidewise place in the village. Welcome at meals, useful in emergencies, trusted with hidden channels, yet often absent from festivals because rain has shifted and the cisterns are talking.

I would have one close companion, perhaps not a spouse in any grand formal sense. A shadow-pruner, maybe, someone who smells of cut leaf and sun pockets, whose work opens narrow shafts of light into nursery terraces. Where I listen to water in dark walls, they read light through leaves. We would understand each other’s silences. We would exchange seeds not dramatically, but carefully: mine from a blue-moss filter reed, theirs from a little sun-hungry vine that grows only where canopy has been persuaded to thin.

Our household, if I had one, would be small and angled around a trunk hollow. Sleeping sling, fermentation jars, seed shelf, a bowl of glow-fungus, a ribbon-snake in the upper rafter tolerated because it eats wall-mites. The doorway leaf would open quickly for my companion and more slowly for me, not from dislike but because I always come home smelling of too many hidden waters and the door likes to be certain.

If I had a child under my care, they would be forever trying to follow me into forbidden wet passages. I would forbid it, then teach them anyway, because ignorance is a worse danger than disobedience. Their first lessons would not be heroic.

Do not put your ear to a wall that is warmer than your cheek.

Do not drink from a clear drip unless something else has drunk before you.

If moss has no insects, ask why.

If a pipe sounds empty after rain, back away.

If water repeats your voice, stop speaking.

And always: tie the line before you trust your balance.

The weirdness of the world would not astonish me most days. That is important. I would have been born into it. A corridor lengthening would be irritating, not metaphysical. Rain falling from unwet leaves would mean postponing a repair. A reflection looking up too quickly from a basin would be reported to the right veiled specialist. If a carved marker appeared on the wrong side of a passage, I would ignore it unless a trained leaf agreed.

Still, I think there would be one place that troubles me.

Deep behind Grandmother’s Throat, beyond a mesh of old root braces, there would be a sealed chamber no one uses anymore. Perhaps it was once a sleeping pod or a small shrine grown too close to the water system. Its entrance has narrowed into a green slit, and most people consider it incorporated. Not ruined. Not dangerous enough to cut out. Simply no longer for people.

But sometimes, when the pressure changes before storms, I would hear water moving there.

Not flowing. Arranging itself.

And once, while clearing a lower filter after a long warm rain, I would see something pale in the runoff catch: a seed packet tied in a style no living household uses, dry inside though it had passed through water. On it, a pressure-knot I recognize without knowing why.

My own household pattern.

Older than my household.

I would not tell everyone immediately. That would be unwise; spoken things travel. I would take it to an elder whose body has begun to settle into a listening chamber, someone with fingers bent permanently around invisible rails. They would wet the knot, touch it, and go very still.

Then they would say: not yet.

So I would carry the old seed packet under my wrap, close to my ribs beside my own living seeds. I would continue my work. Open valves. Taste seepage. Clear root filters. Teach the child not to trust clear water too quickly. Nod to the fire-keeper from a respectful distance. Trade sour moss cakes for a new shell plug. Sleep lightly during storms.

But I would begin listening differently.

Because perhaps my place in this world is not only maintenance. Perhaps I am near an old decision the structure has not finished making. Something sealed but not dead. Some ancestor, error, promise, or route that water remembers better than people do.

I would not rush toward it. No one sensible rushes in that world.

I would wait for the mist to change.

I would mark reliable paths with touch, not carving. I would ask the door leaves with clean hands. I would carry water even in rain, a knife even in peace, seeds always. And when Grandmother’s Throat hums in a tone I have never heard before, when the frogs leave their cups and the wall warms under my cheek, when the old slit of a doorway softens like a mouth remembering its name—

then I would go in.

Not as a hero.

As a water-listener.

As a small maintenance organ of a vast suspended life, entering a chamber the canopy has kept folded in itself for generations.

And whatever I found there — root, body, archive, sleeping bridge, reflected ancestor, seed-bed, wound — I would first do what my people do before touching anything that holds weight.

I would ask what else it is holding.
