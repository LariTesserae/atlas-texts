I would not find myself in the plaza at first, though that is where a human story would want to put me.

The plaza is too declarative. Too nearly a center. Too easy to mistake for an altar, a forum, the place where the answer is meant to stand upright in black stone and explain itself. I would see it from the mouths of lanes and avoid it for a long while, the way a small animal avoids a cleared space whose emptiness has been maintained for reasons older than fear. I would know, without thinking, that the bare white crust around the column is not safe ground but active ground: a surface kept clean because everything unnecessary has been lifted, sorted, used, or refused.

I would be nearer the edge.

Not outside the city, not in the unfinished flats beyond the rim, but just inside the broken ring where parapets lean and new spires come up crooked from the basin floor. A marginal district. A place that still cannot decide whether it is wall, street, reef, or wound. There the forms would be less confident, less repeated. Half-arches would rise and fail. Doorways would close before becoming doorways. Ribs would fork and stop. Powder would gather in small indecisive drifts, then be pulled away by some seam that opened only a hair’s width.

I would be found in one of those seams.

Not as a citizen. Not as a maker. Not as the city itself, though I would belong to its grammar.

I think I would be one of the thin intermediary things: not quite animal, not quite membrane, not quite plant. A small, pale-gray organism living folded between plates of mineral crust near the outer ring, where the city is still testing its shapes. My body would be narrow enough to disappear into a crack that appears, to human eyes, too slight to contain anything alive. Most of the time I would be no more visible than a lifted edge of salt.

I would be a seam-taster.

A surveyor, though not by intention. A tiny moving question the city asks of its own unfinished margins.

My body would be flat and jointed, but softer than the armored grazers that cross the inner lanes. Not soft with water; nothing here can afford that. Soft in the sense of flexible mineral films, hinged plates, conductive gels sealed under translucent skin. My limbs would be filament-thin when extended, folding back into grooves along my sides when still. Along my underside would be rows of minute contact organs, able to read charge, grain size, temperature, and the faint chemical histories left by dust, mats, grazers, and collapsing lace.

I would have no eyes, or nothing that deserves the name.

Light here is not information enough. The dim pewter sky, the bluish shadows, the brief glimmer on crystal edges—these would be background weather. I would know the world by pressure and difference. A warm pulse from the black column would arrive through the ground as a widening condition. A thread-plant net opening across a lane would alter the field before it touched me. A predator would be a silence inside the silence: a patch where the usual small exchanges of charge had been interrupted.

My mouth, if it were a mouth, would be a fine scraping seam that opens sideways.

I would feed on beginnings.

Not on established walls, not on the old dark mats, not on the hardened towers. Those belong to stronger hungers. I would feed where new mineral lace has not yet decided what it is: at the tender rims of fresh extrusions, in the dampness-that-is-not-water of conductive salts before they fully set, on films condensed inside hairline cracks after a basin exhale. I would shave these surfaces gently, taking only the unstable excess. In doing so I would also alter them. A scrape from me might prevent a rib from thickening. Or it might roughen the surface just enough for dust to catch there next cycle.

So I would be both grazer and editor.

A punctuation mark in the city’s slow writing.

During the coolest phase I would unfold.

One plate would lift, then another. My limbs would open from my sides in careful sequence, each joint locking before the next released. I would not hurry; haste is for worlds where loss can be repaired by drinking. I would slide from the seam beneath a leaning spur of mineral and pause with most of my body still hidden. The lane outside would seem empty. A shallow corridor, violet-black at the base of its walls, striped with dimness from ribs overhead. Dust would lie in bands along the floor, sorted from the last exhale.

I would taste the ground before entering it.

A grazer passed there three pulses ago: metallic smear, faint abrasion, safe enough. A thread mat extended across the lane during the last cold cycle but withdrew before full dustfall: possible hazard, possible food. A new fissure opened at the base of the opposite wall: important. The wall itself is warming faster than the lane floor: hollow behind it. A folded thing clung above the arch recently, but the charge it left is already dispersing.

I cross.

To a human watcher, if one were impossibly patient and close to the ground, my movement would look deliberate almost to the point of lifelessness. A plate shifts. A limb touches. The body follows. Pause. Another limb. Pause. My translucent edges would make me difficult to distinguish from flakes of crust, except that I would obey a rhythm not quite geological. I would be the size of a human hand, perhaps smaller when folded, larger when spread thin.

At the opposite wall I would enter the new fissure and taste what the city is trying to become there.

This would be my work, though I would not know it as work.

The outer districts need creatures like me. The city grows by retaining successful shapes, but new shapes require testing. Dust must be sampled. Porosity must be opened or closed. Films must be trimmed. Lace must be weakened where it would crack badly later, strengthened where repeated crossings polish it into usefulness. I would travel between young seams, feeding on their excess and leaving behind conductive traces, dry pellets, faintly darkened lines. Other things would read those lines. The city would read them too, not with thought, but through altered flow.

A place I visit often might become a channel.

A place I avoid might seal.

Where I die, something complicated might begin.

I would have a refuge under a half-formed arch near the boundary wall. The arch would not yet connect to anything; it would rise from one side of a lane and taper into three sharp prongs, like a thought abandoned before completion. Beneath it, the floor would be layered with fine gray powder over a darker mineral plate. A seam would run diagonally into the wall. That seam would be mine, insofar as anything here has possession. Not defended loudly. Not marked by scent. Held by repeated use, by fit, by the exact correspondence between my folded body and its interior angles.

Inside, the seam would be cooler than the lane. It would contain a little chamber no larger than a closed fist, lined with scraped smoothness from my own turning. On the ceiling of that chamber would hang tiny mineral droplets hardened into dull beads. I would rasp them when food outside was poor. Behind me, deeper in the crack, a nodule of the city might pulse once every long cycle, not aware of me but tolerating my presence because my scrapings keep the entrance open.

I would be useful because I am small enough to misunderstand.

Predators that take the larger grazers would not always bother with me. The thread-plants would sense me, but unless I blundered into their extended nets during dustfall, I would offer little return. The folded gliders might eat me if they found me exposed, but they prefer richer surface-feeders, things with stored charge in their plates. My main danger would be the almost invisible cutters: edge-bodied animals, if animals they are, that pass through lanes as moving absences.

I would know them by the folding of bristles.

A grove two corridors away bends inward all at once. The usual static along the wall thins. Dust stops migrating for the length of a breath. Then I freeze. Not crouch, not flee: freeze. My limbs lock. My surface discharges into the mineral beneath me until I become, to most senses, another flake of the street.

The cutter passes.

Perhaps I never see it. Perhaps seeing is not a category that matters. There is only a moving correction in the field, a line where appetite has made itself narrower than perception. Afterward, a small mat at the base of the wall is missing a clean crescent. No sound. No struggle. Just absence with an edge.

I resume only after the next warmth from the central column reaches my lane.

The column is far from my territory, but nothing in the city is truly far from it. Its pulse travels under walls, through towers, along buried films and mineral veins. To the mats it is timing. To the nursery it is instruction. To dust it is permission to rise. To me it is a change in the rules of movement. Warm phase: hide, conserve, endure the lifting powder. Cool phase: unfold, taste, feed.

Once, perhaps, I would go inward.

Not often. The inner city belongs to denser processes. Older walls, richer mats, more confident predators, deeper dust. But some cycle would draw me there: a collapse in my outer district, a new mineral scent, a charge path opened by a crack running toward the plaza. I would follow the underside of a ribbed gallery, moving through shadow, touching each surface in turn.

The city would grow more legible and more dangerous as I approached its center.

The lanes would channel powder with greater precision. Thread-plants would occupy the corners in thick black knots, their filaments drawn back but alert. Pale grazers larger than I am would sleep folded into wall seams, armored plates overlapping like chips of mica. The towers would hum—not audibly, but as a pressure in my limbs. Everywhere, histories would be layered too densely to read at once.

At the edge of the plaza I would stop.

No cover. Matte white crust. The black column rising at center like cooled flame. Clean ground means active ground. I would feel the heat stored in the column before it released, a dark swelling in the city’s body. Around the plaza the walls would hold themselves open, waiting.

I would not cross.

Instead I would remain beneath a low overhang and feed on flakes shed by the column’s last expansion, fragments carried there by sorted dust. They would be rich with thermal history, sharp-tasting, dangerous if taken too quickly. I would rasp them thinly and leave before the next pulse.

Near the nursery chamber, I might encounter the mesh.

Not directly, not inside the chamber where the nodule pits open and threads span the air. That is not a place for a creature like me. But after the threads withdraw and the new lace hardens along the walls, tiny splinters of it travel outward through cracks. I would find one lodged in a narrow gutter between two buildings-that-are-organs. It would be nearly weightless, a fragment of structure before structure, delicate as a frozen breath, though there is no breath here but the city’s.

I would eat part of it.

The rest I would drag, unintentionally, caught on the hooks of my side plates. It would break free near my outer arch. There, lodged against a seam I frequent, it might seed a new habit in the crust. Dust catches. Charge repeats. A lip rises. In time, a small alcove forms where there had been only wall.

I would never know that I helped make a room.

But the city would know in the only way it knows: by keeping the shape.

If a human entered, I would experience them first as catastrophe.

Long before their shadow fell into my lane, their moisture would arrive as wrongness. A bloom of chemical possibility too broad, too loud, too wet with options. Their warmth would smear the fine gradients I use to move. Their footsteps would crush dust layers that have been sorting for cycles. They would shed salts like a collapsing cliff sheds stone. Their breath would strike the walls and briefly make them foreign.

I would retreat deep into my seam, plates locked, mouth sealed.

After they passed, I would emerge into a changed street.

Their footprint would be a basin within the basin, its compressed rim glittering with altered crust. The mats would reach it first, black threads extending toward the residue. Grazers would circle and scrape the edges. Dust would gather differently there. I would taste it from a distance, then come closer over several cool phases. It would be dangerous, rich, unstable. I would take one shaving from the hardened edge and withdraw.

For many cycles afterward, that footprint would continue to happen.

Not as memory of a person. As a new gradient. A disturbance becoming architecture.

Perhaps my descendants, if I have descendants, would live along the ribs that rise from it.

Reproduction, for my kind, would not be mating in any familiar sense. At the end of a long feeding period, when my plates have accumulated enough trace metals and stored charge, I would return to my seam and press myself against its deepest wall. Portions of my underside would harden into small lenticular scales. These would detach one by one and remain stuck to the mineral, dull and inert.

Most would fail.

Some would be eaten by mats, some sealed behind growing crust, some discharged into lifelessness. But if the seam receives the right pulse, if dust of the right fineness settles over them, if a thread of the city’s own conductive film touches their edge, they would soften inwardly. A folded miniature would form inside each scale, feeding first on the scale itself, then on the wall, then on the thin mineral breath passing through the crack.

They would hatch by becoming gaps.

A tiny line opens. A new seam-taster unfolds.

In this way I would belong to the city’s uncertain edges: born from crust, feeding on beginnings, leaving marks that become decisions. I would not be the intelligence of the place, but one of the ways it avoids having to think. One of its distributed hesitations. One of its small tests of appetite against form.

If named from outside, I might be called a margin grazer, a seam-reader, a dry isopod, a living stylus.

But from within there would be no name.

Only the cool plate against my underside. The pressure of a pulse traveling from the black heart. The taste of young mineral. The warning fold of distant bristles. The violet safety of a narrow lane. The powder rising ankle-high to beings that have ankles, body-high to me, turning the whole city briefly into suspended ground.

Then the dust falls.

Some grains lodge in my joints. Some cling to the scrape-lines I left behind. Some enter the seam where I sleep. By the next cool phase the opening has changed shape slightly, accommodating me, resisting me, using me. My refuge is no longer quite the one I entered.

Nothing here remains itself by staying still.

So I would be there: just inside the rim, beneath the unfinished arch, folded into a diagonal seam in a city that is alive because it has learned to build with hunger.

Not abandoned.

Not a person.

A small dry instrument in the metabolism of a place still learning the shape of streets.
