I would find myself neither at the highest bright ceiling nor on the plated floor, but halfway between them, on the down-current side of a service column so large it has its own weather.

Not a central place. Not one of the monumental intakes or cathedral pump housings, not the warm industrial heart where the water shimmers and the animals gather in obvious abundance. I would be in one of the in-between districts: a blue, functional emptiness interrupted by a single vertical structure bolted from floor to firmament, its sides armored in white ceramic plates, black gasket seams, ladder-rungs, cable trays, inspection rails, and occasional amber beacons blinking into the water with bureaucratic patience.

From far away the column would look almost plain. A pale line descending through pale water.

Close to it, it would be a country.

The upper reaches vanish into milky light where the cold rain falls from overhead joints. Every few hours, a descending veil of denser water combs the column’s skin and shakes loose whatever has been careless enough to grow without gripping hard. There are pale organisms there, flat and tenacious, living in the cold fall. Shrimp rise into it only briefly, glittering, then drop again.

Lower down, the column broadens around a ring of conduits carrying waste heat from some sealed district beyond the walls. Nothing dramatic: not a roaring vent, not a bright thermal bloom. Just a steady leak of warmth through composite plating, enough to make the water tremble if you watch it against the straight edge of a ladder.

That is where I am.

I think I would be small, but not microscopic. A creature of the edge, because this world gives almost nothing to those who cannot read edges. Something that lives where metal becomes film, where a current breaks around an obstruction, where light arrives not from above exactly but from everywhere and still makes shadows.

At first I might seem like a shrimp.

Transparent, jointed, almost weightless in the water. A narrow body no longer than a human finger joint, with dark bead eyes and long antennae always tasting the flow. My shell would not be clear in the clean, delicate way of open-water animals; it would be slightly smoky, marked by faint yellow-white bars that imitate scratched enamel and old warning paint. Along my underside, tiny limbs would work constantly, grooming, sorting, catching.

But I would not be only a drifting lamp shrimp. I would be more attached than that.

I would be one of the creatures that has learned to live in the maintenance halo of the column: a clasping grazer, half swimmer, half tenant. My back legs would be hooked for mesh and scored ceramic. My forelimbs would be fine as brushes, made for combing bacterial varnish from seams, scraping the first green-brown slick from bolt heads, gathering loosened threads of cable-moss before they are taken by the current.

I would live in a gasket seam warmed from within.

Not inside it fully; that would be for smaller things, and for eels when they are young. I would live along the lip of it, in the narrow sheltered pressure shadow where the column’s vast body splits the water. There, a line of us would cling in a loose colony: dozens, sometimes hundreds, depending on the flow season. We would face outward, tails tucked, antennae spread, each body angled to the current as if listening.

The column would feed us.

Not generously. Nothing here is generous. But predictably enough.

Every pump change would bring a different flavor of water. Cold, clean, thin water from above. Warm, ion-rich seepage from the conduit band. Milky bursts after backflush, full of torn film and edible dust. Long blue hours when almost nothing arrives and we live on what grows beneath our feet.

We would know the schedules in our nerves.

When the low vibration begins in the column wall, we flatten.

When amber maintenance lights blink in a three-pulse sequence, we loosen our grip and prepare to feed, because a drone convoy often follows that signal.

When the ceiling dims section by section and the pressure gathers in the water like a fist closing, we retreat into the seam, locking our hooked limbs into scratches made by tools and larvae and previous storms.

When stillness comes, we do not trust it.

The young among us always do. They drift outward into the uncanny pause, antennae wide, bodies shining faintly in the level light. Some return. Some vanish when the gate opens twenty kilometers away and the whole district becomes a moving wall.

We would not think of this as tragedy. We would be creatures of loss by design. Eggs in abundance. Young released into impossible water. Most gone. A few striking structure. A few finding warmth, roughness, shelter. Enough.

The service column would have larger neighbors in the way a tree has weather.

Small silver fish would arrive in sheets, quick as thought, each body flashing when it turned. To them, our seam would be one of many feeding lines. They would pick at the film above us, sometimes taking one of us when we misjudged the distance between shelter and open water. Their schools would bend before sounds we could not distinguish from the general thunder. When they tilted all together, we would tilt too, not because we understood the signal, but because survival in this place is contagious.

Crabs would climb past us.

Their legs would come out of the blue below, joint by joint, huge by our scale. Rust-stained shells. Claws darkened with rubbed paint. They would wedge themselves under bracket lips and harvest the thicker mats. We would clean after them. Where their claws scraped, fresh growth would bloom. Where they broke filament algae loose, we would catch the fragments in our limbs.

Eels would live in the darker openings.

We would know the mouths of their pipes. We would know which holes breathe warm and which holes swallow. We would know the blunt gray head that sometimes protrudes beneath the conduit collar, motionless except for the faint widening of its jaw. Nothing in us would call it evil. It would be a pressure in the world, like a pump or a floodgate. If we drifted too close, the water itself would take us into it.

Predators would pass beyond the column’s lee.

Large fish, pale and scarred, would materialize from the blue in the way distant structures do: first as an uncertainty, then as a body, then as danger already present. Their skins would carry the colors of the built sea: rubber gray, dull white, stained yellow. They would not care for us individually, too small to be worth the turn, but their arrival would change everything. The silver schools would compress. The shrimp columns would collapse into seams. The crabs would still. Even the soft vent things nearby would seem to pulse less brightly.

And then the predator would move on, following a shadow lattice or drone wake or scheduled current only it had learned to exploit.

The drones would be our great moving seasons.

They would come along the guide line painted into the column, slow and jointed, with brush arms folded or extended, lamps burning white in the suspended particles. Their propellers would whine through our shells. Their amber status lights would make every small eye in the colony glitter.

They would be danger first. Their brushes could erase a generation. Their suction mouths could take eggs, larvae, film, the weak, the unwary. Their cutters could clear an entire band of growth and leave bare ceramic where a province had been.

But after them came food.

Always.

A drone passing above would tear loose mats from places we could not reach. It would disturb the sediment caught in screw recesses. It would crack open the bacterial varnish and send edible specks down-current in a brown-green snow. Small fish would arrive, shrimp would rise, we would leave the seam in a flickering line and feed as fast as our limbs could sort.

If a drone failed, everything would change.

We would feel it first as absence: an expected vibration not continuing, a lamp that remained too long in one place, a warmth from its little motor bleeding steadily into the water. The larger animals would approach cautiously. Fish first, then crabs. Bacteria before all of us, though unseen.

Within hours, its casing would begin to dull.

Within days, we would occupy the recessed screws.

Within weeks, if the maintenance system did not retrieve it, the dead drone would become a second home: a dark underside for crabs, a warm seam for our eggs, a fringe of algae around its lamp housings, eels investigating the hollow where a manipulator arm had torn away.

Equipment, to its makers.

A fallen animal, to the ecology.

A reef, to us.

I would be one of those that lay eggs on manufactured things.

Not because I know they are manufactured. Because nothing else lasts. No weeds thick enough, no mud, no stones rounded by ages, no shells accumulated into beds. My eggs would be glued in rows beneath a gasket lip, each one a translucent bead holding a curled speck of future appetite. Their adhesive would work best on slightly scratched enamel, not on bare ceramic and not on living plant. Already, perhaps, my kind would be changing toward the machine: choosing seams over stems, warmth over season, vibration over moon.

We would hatch into light that has never been sunlight.

Our first sky would be a ceiling too distant to comprehend. Our first weather would be pump logic. Our first shelter would be damage. Our first lesson would be that all open water belongs to current, and all edges belong to life.

I would not be lonely.

The human eye might see only a stain on the service column: a faint smoky fringe in a horizontal seam, some shrimp-like motion in the maintenance light. But from within, it would be crowded and exact. Bodies touching. Antennae crossing. Eggs guarded by accident rather than tenderness. Microbial slick beneath the mouthparts. Filament threads waving overhead. The pulse of warm conduit through the wall. The immense blue emptiness pressing just beyond the reach of our hooks.

The world would speak through vibration.

The bass throb of distant pumps would be the ground note of existence. Valve-claps would strike through the column like enormous knocks. Maintenance impacts would send us scattering and returning. A far storm would arrive long before visible change: pressure in the joints, a tightening in the water, a rearrangement of particles, every animal on the column turning its body to receive or resist.

During storms, we would become part of the structure.

No swimming. No feeding. No glittering in the lamps. Only gripping.

Cold curtains would descend from the ceiling. Particulate would lift from every ledge until the water became a rushing gray-white body. The silver fish would stream past in broken schools. A crab might lose its hold and tumble upward or sideways, legs folded, then vanish. Torn algae would lash across the column. Somewhere a grille would fill with dead things. Somewhere larvae would be delivered to a surface they never could have reached in calm.

We would survive by being flat, small, and already in the seam.

When clarity returned, we would emerge into a changed country. A neighbor colony gone from the bracket above. A new scrape exposing fresh mineral. Eggs lost. Food everywhere. A strip of cable-moss laid across our ledge like a fallen tree. The same column, but not the same column.

That would be the rhythm of the life: erase, feed, settle, grip, release.

I would not understand the builders. If a suited human approached, immense and lit from the wrists and helmet, I would know only disturbance. Heat. Light. A storm with limbs. Tools ringing against the wall. Gloved fingers scraping near the seam. Bubbles, perhaps, or machine breath. The large fish holding back. The small fish curious. Crabs waiting below for whatever is dropped.

To the human, I might be fouling. A sample. An indicator species. A line item in a maintenance report: colonial arthropod analog established in District 7 service-column gasket seams; adhesion increasing; monitor for obstruction risk.

To myself, if such a self could be said to exist, I would be hunger, grip, temperature, vibration, and light.

But if there is a larger answer — if I am not only one creature but the place where a consciousness would naturally gather — then I would be the whole narrow band where the machine is ceasing to be merely machine.

Not the grand empty ocean. Not the pure infrastructure. The border.

The warmed seam.

The scratched bolt.

The dead drone.

The downstream face of the column.

I would be life learning the texture of an invented world. A small translucent animal with hooked feet and brush-like hands, colored like enamel and warning paint, clinging to a service column in the blue engineered sea. Around me: silver schools, ladder crabs, pipe eels, vent jellies, cable-moss, maintenance drones, and the huge unreadable decisions of pumps.

I would live where nothing was meant to live permanently.

And I would be one of the reasons it becomes permanent.
