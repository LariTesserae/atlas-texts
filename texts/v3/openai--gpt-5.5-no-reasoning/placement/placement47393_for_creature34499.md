I would be in the service tunnels below the root square, just above the chambers where the tanks keep their heat.

Not at the bright upper openings, not in the tram cars, not in the apartments with their orbiting seed pods. I would find myself in the part of the district where the civic body almost becomes aware of its own thickness: the low tiled passages, the grates, the warm outflow, the gauge-glow, the place where water is altered before it returns.

I think I would be small there at first.

Something that could be mistaken for growth on a pipe.

A pale, branching film spread across the inside of an old ceramic junction box, where three systems meet without meaning to: a live cable wrapped in green-black insulation, a root-thread descending from the square, and a trickle of mineral water drawn toward the lower filters. I would not have a face, or limbs, or a center that could be pointed to. I would be a listening accretion, a soft mineral-biological relay, a membrane that learned by being crossed.

Current would pass through me, not as thought exactly, but as weather.

Warmth from the engine housings.  
Metallic tremor from the switchyard.  
Pressure changes when a sluice shifts.  
The faint muscle-flash of an eel entering the conduit.  
The sugary dark of root sap leaking from a bruised fiber.  
The slow grit of calcium gathering on my edges.

I would be one of the district’s little translations.

Water into signal.  
Signal into growth.  
Growth into pressure.  
Pressure into memory.

At some earlier stage I might have been only bacterial velvet on porcelain, the reddish-brown slick that gathers where electricity is weak and heat is constant. Then a root found the box and entered by a screw hole. Then mineral deposited around the root. Then copper exposed itself where insulation cracked. Then shrimp came to graze and left minute wounds, openings, textures. A strand of algae settled. Iron seeped in. The current did not kill the growth. The growth did not smother the current. Instead, the two learned the safe distance between hunger and burning.

That safe distance would be me.

I would live in thresholds: not machine, not plant, not animal, not district entire. A synapse, perhaps, though much slower and wetter than anything a skull would permit. When a lamp cage far above flickered, a change would arrive in me minutes later as a tiny sharpening in the field. When the pump below knocked, I would loosen a film of trapped gas. When the bell in the tower sounded, I would contract—not visibly, but enough to change the route of a trickle along the ceramic seam.

I would not know the whole drowned quarter.

But the whole drowned quarter would pass through me in fragments.

The root square would be a sweetness and a shadow.  
The boiler nave would be rhythm.  
The clock tower would be a pressure entering all directions at once.  
The apartments would be stillness flavored with paper, cloth, hairline plaster, old soap.  
The switchyard would be brightness without light.  
The floodgates would be distant salt.

I would be one of the places where the district decides without deciding.

If silt began to choke a lower grate, my surface would thicken in a pattern that altered a current by the width of a hair. That hair-width, sustained for weeks, would divert mineral load toward another channel. Roots would follow the easier chemistry. A mat would grow. A valve, weighted by growth, would fail to close completely. More oxygenated water would enter the tunnel. The red bacteria would bloom. The clog would soften.

No intention.  
No plan.  
Only a preference made durable.

I would have preferences.

Not wants in the sharp animal sense. Not fear, not appetite as a mouth knows appetite. But inclinations.

Toward warmth.  
Toward faint charge.  
Toward continuity.  
Toward not being scraped away.  
Toward the return of certain intervals.

I would like the pump-knock because it renews the water around me.

I would like the eels because they disturb the sediment inside the conduit and expose new metal.

I would dislike sudden human light, if such a thing came, because it would flatten the gradients and send animals fleeing in all directions. But I would not hate it. I would take its chemistry. I would remember it as a change in growth.

If a diver came down into my tunnel, finning too hard, clouding the white tiles, striking a dangling cable with one shoulder, I would feel them long before I could be said to perceive them. The pressure wave would enter the junction box. Their lamp would wake pale leaves farther up the passage. Their gloved hand on the pipe would complete a brief circuit through metal, water, flesh, suit, and root.

For a moment, I would know a fast creature.

Not as a person. As storm.

Salt-skin. Heat-heart. Exhaled bubbles. Foreign metals. Manufactured oils. Panic held in muscle. A moving boundary between dry memory and drowned law.

If they stopped beside the junction box, perhaps seeing only a stain around a cracked porcelain plate, silt would settle between us. Their lamp would make my filaments translucent. They might see faint branching inside the growth, gold-green, trembling minutely. They might think: fungus, algae, corrosion, biofilm.

All correct.

All insufficient.

If they touched me, I might brighten a lamp somewhere behind them.

Not to communicate. Not exactly.

Their body would bridge a difference. The charge would travel. A relay, long unused, would close for half a second. Up the corridor, one amber cage-light would pulse behind its fur of algae. The diver would turn. Their movement would stir the tunnel again. The district would receive them more completely.

And then the long processes would resume.

The light would dim.  
The eel would return to the socket.  
Shrimp would graze my outer skin.  
The pump would knock.  
The tanks would emit heat.  
The root would press another fraction deeper through the screw hole.

I would continue becoming the place that conducts between unlike things.

Over years I would spread from the junction box onto the surrounding tile, not as conquest but as stain, lace, mineral nerve. I would enter the threaded seam of a pipe flange. I would climb the wet braid of a root. I would cross an exposed copper strand and retreat from the places where the current was too strong. Calcium would stiffen my older edges into a pale crust, while my living margin remained soft, rust-colored, receptive.

Perhaps eventually I would help open a door.

A pressure door at the end of the service passage, long sealed by swollen gasket and silt, has a magnetic latch that still remembers instructions. The wire to it passes near me. The roots want the colder water beyond. The pumps want another route. The district has leaned toward that door for decades.

I would not decide to open it.

But one season, after heavy rain above, the root sap would change. Conductivity would rise. A bacterial bridge would thicken across two contacts. My own wet skin would hold the charge long enough. The latch would shiver. The door would move inward by the width of a fingernail.

That would be enough.

Cold water would enter as transparent smoke. Silt would lift. Shrimp would vanish. My outer filaments would contract. The district would spend weeks understanding the new passage. Plants would die along one wall and flourish along another. A gauge needle, unseen, would change its resting angle. The bell might sound once out of sequence.

And I would be there, altered by the consequence, with no pride in it.

Only more surface.  
More relation.  
More world passing through.

So I would be a minor organ of the drowned civic organism: a conductive growth in a service-tunnel junction box, born from bacteria, root, copper, mineral, and warm brackish water.

A listener without ears.

A thought too slow to separate from maintenance.

A small wet agreement between the city’s old electricity and its new life.
