I would find myself near the center, but not at the arch.

The arch is too declarative. Even swallowed in vines, even numb and rain-slick, it remains a statement: something passed through here, or was meant to, or was measured by the fact of passing. I would not belong to that kind of threshold. Not at first. I would keep to the space between one of the small black pools and the nearest grove of condensation trees, where bare stone slopes gently into moss and the world is arranged less like a ruin than like a listening posture.

I think I would be low to the ground.

Not a person under the roots, though for a while that seems possible: a damp, quiet figure with wrapped tools and hooded lamp, tolerated by learning the manners of darkness. There is an appeal in that life—the deliberate breath, the map of sounds, the humility of taking water from leaves and never asking the place to become visible. But a human, even a changed one, would still carry too much edge. Too much intention held apart from the plateau. Too much private warmth.

Nor would I be one of the great hidden things: not the heavy passer whose arrival stills the groves, not the old system under the stone with its damaged patience and buried authority. Those are too large, too consequential. They alter the behavior of everything around them simply by continuing.

I would be something intermediary. Something almost overlooked, but not small enough to vanish into pure film.

I would live in the seam where wet stone gives way to root-shadow.

A blind amphibian, perhaps, though not quite one of the common singing kinds. I would be a creature of the seep-lines: palm-length, low-bodied, the color of soaked bark and black clay, with skin ridged not for display but for reading. My back would carry raised whorls and branching grooves, a tactile script of organs sensitive to tremor, pressure, mineral taste, heat. I would have no eyes, or only sealed remnants under skin, pale knots that register nothing useful. My world would arrive through my belly, throat, toes, and flanks.

I would know the plateau from below upward.

The ground would not be a surface to me but a conversation: stone holding old cold; moss full of tiny traffic; a buried vent breathing a little warmer than yesterday; pool water tightening when something beneath it rises. Rain would have weight and grammar. A single drop striking fern, stone, leaf, shell, polymer, or water would be five different events. Footsteps would be weather with bones inside it.

I would keep a narrow territory, though “territory” would be too possessive a word. A route, maybe. A circuit of reliable dampness.

From the moss cup beneath the fern shelf, across three body-lengths of slick stone, to the shallow channel where warm water wells up through a crack. Then along the root of the condensation tree whose buttress makes a hollow, past the hard ringing stems where beetles climb, to the pool rim—but never nearer than the distance taught by generations of disappearances. I would know exactly how close to the pool a beetle may feed before the water takes notice. I would know which breathing threads are harmless and which mean hunger.

During the cooler-dark I would rest under liverwort, pressed flat into the wet chamber my body has shaped over time. My skin would drink. My throat would pulse slowly. Tiny mites would pass over me. Films would gather and be shed. Fungal hairs would touch the edges of my limbs and learn that I am not yet food.

When the hum deepened and warmth rose through the cracks, I would wake.

Not suddenly. Nothing here begins suddenly unless it is being eaten.

First the loosening of my toes. Then the opening of my throat. Then the slow lift of my head toward a vibration traveling through the saturated ground. The five wooden knocks would pass from grove to grove, and the five raised spots under my jaw—yes, I think I would have them—would answer before I chose to answer. A pulse. A small bodily assent. Not language, but participation.

I would feed on the smaller lives that reveal themselves by error: springing things that tap too loudly, worms nosing through moss, a moth fallen wet and stunned from a bladder-vine. My tongue would be short, not dramatic. My mouth would close sideways around softness and movement. I would taste iron often. I would taste the ancient machines in the bodies of prey.

But I would not be only a hunter.

I would also be carried.

Spores would cling to my damp ridges. Films would seed themselves along my flanks. Pollen charged from sound-opening flowers would dust my throat when I pushed through their pale cups. Each movement of my body would move the plateau slightly: a little mineral from pool rim to moss; a little fungal scent from root-room to stone basin; a few eggs laid where warmth and water make a promise.

In the rain, I would become bolder.

Warm drops would strike my back and run along the grooves of my skin, each channel waking. The whole plateau would become legible at once, almost painfully so. Ferns would empty themselves in sudden sheets. The pools would click more deeply. Combers would flatten in shining rows. Moths would cling to bladders and to sleeping climbers. The buried system would hum through waterlogged stone until my bones seemed to contain it.

Then I would sing.

Not beautifully, not to ears made for melody. My call would be a rounded pressure, a low throat-note followed by five small clicks, felt more clearly through moss than air. It would say: here is warmth, here is water, here is a body ready among the cracks. Others of my kind would answer from seams I never see. The calls would overlap with the western boom, the ceramic shrub-chime, the clicking pools, until no single voice could be separated from the plateau’s own weather.

If a lamp were uncovered, I would not understand it as light at first.

I would know it by the panic before I knew its source. Leaves contracting. Moths tearing away from warmth. Pool threads vanishing. The arch beginning to hum in a way that makes the skin disagree with itself. Heat without the right pressure. Brightness as a wound in the dark. I would go still under the nearest moss and reduce my body to contact: belly to stone, throat sealed, limbs folded. I would wait for the old sky to deepen and correct the insult.

If the human under the buttress roots existed, I would know them.

Not as a person. As a recurring tremor with restraint in it. A large warm animal that has learned not to slam itself against the world. I would know the soft wrapping of its tools, the salt of its hands, the rare hooded lamp hidden almost before it begins, the careful avoidance of egg-moss during breeding. I might sleep in the warmth beneath its shelter when it was away. I might feed on insects drawn to its breath. It might know me as one of the five-throated seam dwellers, the ones that answer the knock.

We would not be companions. The plateau does not encourage that kind of simplification. But we might become part of each other’s expected conditions.

Once, perhaps, I would cross beneath the arch.

Not through it proudly, not as a chosen passage, but because rain has raised the seep-lines and prey has moved along a new route. The numbness would enter through my feet first. The ridges on my skin would lose the ground for a moment. My body would feel briefly unfastened from its own outline, as though I had become film, root, water, and machine all at once without the usual mercy of sequence.

I would freeze there.

Above me, vines would drip. Beneath me, old regulation would continue. Around me, the plateau would hold its dark.

Then sensation would return: wet stone, iron, moss, distant boom, moth-wing, pool-click, my own throat beating. I would move on, lower than before, carrying on my skin whatever invisible residue the arch gives to those who touch the edge of its forgotten purpose.

So: I would be found on the worn crown of the mountain, in the warm-dark between a black pool and a condensation grove, half under moss and half in seep-water.

I would be a blind, low, listening amphibian of the root seams.

A small citizen of the plateau. A carrier of spores, eater of errors, singer in rain. A body written in ridges instead of color. One of the many hidden lives by which the dark learns itself.
