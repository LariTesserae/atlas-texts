You would find yourself **on the living highland skin of a newborn mountain-continent**—not in a forest, not in a city, not on a road, but in the thin, brilliant margin where raw geology is being occupied faster than it can settle into being stone.

You are standing somewhere on a high shelf above a cloud-chasm, in a place that only briefly looks like a plateau. Around you the ground is black volcanic glass veined with wet silver runnels, crusted with copper moss, white rime, red lichen, and the soft green-black nap of organisms that have made a surface out of fracture. The light is fierce and cold enough to feel metallic. Fog moves below your knees, then below the ledge, then suddenly above you, as if the air cannot decide which layer of the world you belong to.

There is no empty place to put your hand.

If you kneel, the stone is not still. It ticks. It sweats. A translucent tube, half ice and half flesh, pulses under a film of frost. Tiny valves open with soft clicks. Something withdraws from the warmth of your shadow. The moss beside your boot pales in the shape of your presence, remembering pressure before you have fully shifted your weight.

You are not a traveler here in any heroic sense. This world has no room for heroism. It has room for function.

You would be **one of the small necessary beings**—not one of the hill-sized grazers, not one of the immense winged scavengers circling in the geothermal updrafts, but something closer to the seam between animal, instrument, and message.

You are a **frost-courier**.

A narrow-bodied, many-limbed creature, built low to the stone, about the length of a human forearm but lighter than you look. Your shell is dark blue-black, with copper flecks where mineral dust has hardened into your plates. Along your back runs a comb of pale filaments that gather rime from the air. In fog, these filaments bead white until you look furred with frost. In hard sun they glitter, each strand trembling with captured droplets.

You have six walking limbs, hooked and delicate, made for slick rock, lichen crust, old horn, ice skin, and the trembling roofs of moss tunnels. Beneath your body are smaller cilia-like feet that taste surfaces as you cross them. You do not see the world as open distance. You read it as gradients: salt, pressure, warmth, carbon, fungal bloom, grazer track, fresh fracture, old death, vent-metal, egg-scent, alarm.

Your work is not commanded. Nothing here commands. But you move because the whole place moves through you.

At dawn, when the pump-colonies strain against frozen tubes and the cliffs begin to tick, you emerge from the underside of a cushion plant where your clutch has slept among root-worms and mineral bacteria. The air is hard and bright. Far away, one of the hill-grazers exhales, and the steam columns briefly catch the light like banners. Above, a slate-winged scavenger turns without flapping.

You climb.

Your filaments collect frost. Your mouthparts scrape microscopic films from stone, but feeding is only part of you. You carry things: spores caught in grooves along your shell, bacteria sealed in waxy pockets near your joints, pollenlike dust from reed-vanes, mineral salts stuck to the damp pads of your feet. When you cross a pale scar left by a grazer’s jaw, you pause with others of your kind. The exposed stone is fresh, rich, unclaimed. You lay down a thread of chemical instruction. Behind you, slower bodies will come. Lichen will come. Glassy plates will come. Fungi will test the groove. Moss will eventually soften its edges. The mountain’s wound will become surface.

You are small enough to be eaten by almost everything.

Ridge-runners know the braided routes of your kind. Pools wrinkle open when your shadow passes over them. Reed-crowns hold clear water full of jaws. Certain amber fungi soften under your feet. Birds with filament tongues snap you from cliff walls. Even the moss can mislead you, holding the pressure-memory of a safe passage long after a predator has learned to wait there.

So you travel in streams.

Not mindlessly. Not individually either. You and your kin pour over stone in dark braids, pausing as one when a high shadow passes, dividing around vents, merging again at salt crusts and grazing scars. Your bodies make a dry, rattling sound, like seeds shaken inside bone. From above, you are a line. From within, you are a weather system of touch.

Sometimes you climb onto the giants.

Their legs are landscapes of their own: frost-clotted fur, colonies of mites, red lichens growing in the cracks of old scars, warm salt near the nostrils, algae blooming where breath condenses and runs. You pick parasites from the folds behind horn-plates. You drink from the steam-damp hair beneath their throats. You leave mineral bacteria in wounds where they will help seal torn hide. In return, the giant carries you kilometers across a plateau you could never cross before the next freeze.

You have no idea it is a giant. Not as a category. It is moving ground. Warm ground. Salt ground. Dangerous ground when it shifts.

At midday the world becomes painfully bright. The lichens darken and drink light. The reed-mills on the slope begin their slow torsion, winding themselves in silence. Vent fields below start to drone, first through your feet, then through your shell, then through the air. Steam lifts in white pillars. Around the mineral chimneys, heat-grazers crop bacterial rings in precise arcs. A cliff colony shrieks as ice-carrying nesters return from the upper shelves, feathers glittering with frozen cargo.

You cross a causeway made by generations of animals so immense that their passing polished the stone into black gloss. In the shallow grooves of that path, your ancestors carried spores; your descendants will carry more. The road was never built. It grew by weight, repetition, hunger, migration. To you it is a river of information.

Late in the day, fog climbs.

This is when you are most yourself.

Your back-filaments open wider. Droplets gather, swell, tremble. Each one contains dust, spores, dormant eggs, and the faint taste of things lifted from chasms no eye has seen. You drink some. You keep some. You rub some into receptive moss. You deposit some into the mouths of pump-colonies that cannot reach this vapor themselves. You are not altruistic. You are not moral. You are exchange given legs.

If you die, you will not remain you for long.

A ridge-runner may split your shell. A pool may close over you. A bird may feed you to chicks in a steam-warmed nest. Or you may simply slow in the cold, tucked beneath a violet lichen shelf. Then mites will open the joints. Fungal threads will enter. Your copper minerals will pass into a larva’s armor. Your shell grooves, still holding spores, may seed a new crack in the stone. Your body will be translated.

That is the law here: nothing is empty, and nothing ends as itself.

So you would be found on a high, wet, glittering ledge of the cold living factory, among clicking reeds, breathing vents, remembering moss, lichen-bright cliffs, and herds that look like moving ridges.

And you would be a small dark frost-courier crossing the raw stone in a braided stream of your kin:

a carrier of water, spores, salts, alarm, pressure, and accident;

a minor valve in the mountain’s enormous body;

one of the countless tiny organs by which the wilderness takes possession of the world before the world has finished becoming land.
