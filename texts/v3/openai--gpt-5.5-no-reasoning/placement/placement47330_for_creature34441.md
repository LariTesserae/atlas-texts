I would find myself not in the center of that room-ocean, but slightly off from it: in the alcove with the observation window, where the chamber seems to hesitate.

Not beyond the glass, not fully among the silver fish and ribbon plants either. I would be fixed to the rim of that old convex pane, where the wall curves inward and the water changes its listening. The others avoid the place because it is connected to something larger than their habits can name. That is where I would be.

I think I would be one of the flattened, rootlike colonies growing around the window seal.

At first I might seem like plant matter: pale fans pressed against ceramic alloy, a fringe of soft tendrils laid along scratches in the glass, thin membranes moving whenever the pressure sings. I would not have a face. I would not swim. I would not flee when the cold pulse comes, except by tightening myself into the seams. My body would be a listening surface.

But I would not be only a plant.

I would be part filter-stock, part biofilm, part failed gasket, part animal patience. My first ancestors might have been installed as a living sealant or a diagnostic culture: something meant to change color when pressure differentials worsened, something meant to taste leakage, something meant to repair hairline cracks before they became emergencies. Then the builders vanished, the signals went nowhere, and my lineage remained at the threshold between the green chamber and the black cavity beyond.

I would live on exchange.

Not much crosses the observation window. The glass holds. That is its ancient duty. But nothing made stays perfect forever. Around its rim are pores, warped seals, mineralized threads, microscopic failures through which the two waters speak in traces. Warmth from the green room. Cold mineral pressure from the dark. Bacterial sweetness. Copper. Manganese. The faint rot of insulation. The charged breath of wires. Every surge would feed me a different sentence.

I would spread in slow crescents around the bolts that once held the window frame. I would grow flat because pressure has taught my kind humility. Raised bodies are torn away. Branching too boldly invites grazers. Anything beautiful in the open becomes food. So I would make myself low, pale, and sensitive, with listening hairs finer than algae, each one tuned to vibration.

The whale-note of the window would be my weather.

When the whole pane moaned during a surge, I would feel it through every filament. The fish would scatter. The wall-creepers would flatten. The crab in the trench would pause with its ceramic claw lifted. But I would open. That sound would mean the black cavity beyond had leaned closer. It would mean new traces forced through the rim: dark water, old silt, perhaps the chemical ghost of some larger chamber no lit creature has entered for centuries.

I would know more of the mountain than the swimmers do, but less clearly.

To the slot-fish, the world is current, shelter, flash, mouth, duct.  
To the vent-keeper, the world is blockage and flow.  
To the ribbon plants, the world is light and mineral.  
To me, the world is pressure difference.

I would know that the green room is small because its pulses return quickly. I would know that beyond the glass is larger because its answering weight arrives late and deep. I would know storms before the rain-input, not by thunder, but by the outer structure’s minute compression, by the way sealed water presses through impossible spaces. I would know pump reversal as a change in the direction of longing.

I would be a threshold organism.

The others would not think of me often. Shrimp would pick at my edges when I overgrew the safer grooves. Wall-grazers would scrape me if they dared the alcove, leaving clean pale arcs in my skin. Once in a while a young eel, bold or ignorant, would coil near me to drink borrowed charge from a hidden wire behind the frame. I would taste the electricity shiver through its body and answer by withdrawing from the contact, very slowly.

The pale fish beyond the glass would be my nearest neighbors.

Sometimes they would press their broad faces to the other side, their eyes reduced to silver dimples, their mouths opening and closing against a barrier neither of us could cross. I would not see them as the swimming creatures see. I would know them as interruptions in pressure, as soft knocks, as shadows cooling the glass. Their bodies would feed different films on the far side of the pane. Perhaps some cousin of me grows there too, darker and thinner, spread across the outside surface.

Maybe, during great surges, our filaments meet through a crack too small for water to become a leak.

Maybe we are one organism divided by glass.

That possibility would be the nearest thing I have to religion.

I would not dream of sky. I would not know mountain. I would not imagine the vanished builders except as the shape of the frame, the alloy of the bolts, the ancient smoothness of the pane. My mythology would be structural. The world was made as a pressure boundary. The world sings because boundaries suffer. Food comes through weakness. Death comes through opening too far.

I would be old in pieces.

No single part of me would last for the full age of the chamber. Films slough. Tendrils are grazed. Membranes tear in storm input. But the colony persists by replacing itself along the same rim, inheriting position as memory. I would remember by growing where previous growth survived. I would know safe grooves because my body occupies them. I would know dangerous protrusions because I am absent there.

If a person entered the chamber and came close to the alcove, their lamp would disturb me. Their gloved hand on the window frame would feel like catastrophe: a sudden animal mass, warm and intentional, pressing into a place where touch has always meant current, shell, root, or claw. I would contract into the seam, turning from pale to translucent gray. If they scraped a sample from me, they might find metal-binding bacteria, pressure-responsive tissues, fragments of old synthetic polymer braided into living membrane, perhaps even dormant diagnostic particles still trying to report stress levels to a network that no longer listens.

They might call me a seal colony.

Or a window lichen.

Or contamination.

They would be wrong only in scale.

I would be one of the chamber’s quiet organs: the thing that makes a border habitable. Not the keeper of the vents, not a hunter, not one of the flashing schools that animate the green light. I would be the patient life at the edge of the unknown cavity, turning leakage into food and vibration into knowledge.

And if the window ever failed, I would be among the first destroyed.

The glass would flex past song into fracture. The black water would enter. The green chamber would become unreadable all at once: currents overturning, warm columns shredded, fish thrown into walls, plants flattened, the crab’s trench buried in violent silt. I would have no time to understand the world I had spent generations interpreting. My whole theology would become arrival.

But until then, I would remain where almost nothing happens quickly.

A pale, pressure-fed colony around an old observation window.

Half gasket, half garden.

A listener at the seam between the little made sea and the larger dark.
