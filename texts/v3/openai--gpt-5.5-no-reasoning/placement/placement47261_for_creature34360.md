I would not arrive there as a traveler, not at first. A traveler is too large an answer: too much blood, too much haste, too much need. A traveler would stand out as weather stands out when it remembers rain. Their breath would be an intrusion. Their warmth would be an argument. The plateau would notice them, compensate for them, and erase their footprints with a patience that is almost courtesy.

No, if I find myself there, I am already partly accepted by the place.

I am in one of the shaft-fields, where the smooth circular mouths descend into a darkness older than sky. Not at the center of anything. There is no center up there, only local negotiations. I am on the leeward side of a low rim of dark glassed stone, where dust does not quite settle and the crusts grow thickest. Around me the ground is white-gray salt cracked into polygons, but near the shaft it darkens, stained by centuries of exhaled minerals and minute electrical burns. A few pale stems stand miles apart, their vanes folded against the noon brightness. Far off, a pylon leans by less than a degree. Its shadow is not where it should be.

I am not one of the great regulators. I am not vast enough to press a valley back into balance. I am not a glider, though I feel them pass sometimes as changes in pressure across my surface. I am not a stem, either, not exactly; I do not rise beautifully from the flats and turn my crown toward the invisible rivers of the sky.

I am lower. Older in texture, though perhaps younger in body.

I am a crust-being on the rim of a shaft: a black-violet lichenoid congress spread thin over stone, salt, and exposed filament.

To an eye passing too quickly, I would be a stain.

A dark roughness under the lip of rock. A patch of mineral shadow. Something burnt there and never cleaned away.

But I am not a stain.

I am a slow city of cells and mineral plates. I am fungal thread, engineered membrane, glass hair, sleeping chemistry, and dust-bound memory. I lie in overlapping scales no thicker than paint in some places, thicker than old leather in others, where centuries have folded me over myself. My edges are green only at night, and then faintly, like an afterthought. In cold hours I contract into enamel. During shaft-breath I open.

My body is not shaped like intention, but it has intentions.

They are small.

Endure.

Taste.

Record.

Lend.

Refuse.

Open when the exhalation carries enough metal. Close when the charge rises too sharply. Feed the nearby root-thread of the pale stem when its lower vanes craze after a storm. Withhold from the arthropod swarm until they have polished the exposed filament clean. Darken before a rule-storm so the fragile underlayers do not burn. Remember the composition of the air. Remember the last time the shaft coughed warm. Remember the glider that brushed dust from the southern basin across my hairs and left behind a century of another place.

My mind is not singular in the way a walking creature would expect. It is a weather of agreements. There are colonies within me that wake only under ultraviolet. Others answer to pressure. Others maintain the old mineral locks that let me desiccate for long intervals without becoming dead. Some of me is animal-adjacent, some plant-adjacent, some machine-adjacent, and some is simply stone taught to participate.

I think by changing color too slowly to be seen.

I remember by becoming layered.

The shaft beside me exhales every thirty-seven hours by the clocks that disagree with themselves, though not always with the same breath. Sometimes it gives cold ozone and alkali dust. Sometimes a metallic pressure that lifts my outer hairs. Sometimes nothing material at all, only a correction in the way weight leans against the world. When the exhalation comes, dust rises in a perfect ring, passes over me, and I unfold.

My hairs extend.

They are microscopic, brittle, translucent. They break often. I regrow them from almost nothing. Through them I taste argon, sodium, old carbon, ionized trace gases, the ghost of water too slight to be water. I taste the deep machinery below, though taste is a poor word for it. It is more like being included in a thought too large to finish.

The buried filament passes beneath my patch. A dull silver root exposed by erosion emerges for a handspan near my western edge before vanishing again under salt. I have grown over part of it, not to consume it, but to shade it, to buffer it from charge. It hums without sound. Sometimes it carries simple currents. Sometimes it carries arrangements that make my inner colonies pause, as if a command has passed through them in a language older than obedience.

I do not understand the system.

Understanding is not required.

I participate.

When the mica-backed small ones wake, I feel them before I see them. Their legs tap the salt plates in delicate multiplicity. They come out during charge weather in braided lines, their backs flashing dull mica, their bodies hinged and exact. They graze my dead edges if I allow it. They scrape away oxidized crust, carry off loosened mineral, and leave behind patterned dust that helps my next growth. If they cut too deep, I bitter the surface with compounds remembered from a harsher era, and they turn aside. If the filament is exposed too brightly, I release stored salts, and they gather to repair it.

We do not love one another.

Love is too wet a word.

But we keep terms.

The stems speak down through their roots. Speak is also too quick a word. Their messages arrive as long changes in local charge, as faint shifts in alkalinity, as a patient pressure through the ground. One to the north has leaned since before my outermost living layer existed. It was struck by a storm-front that made stones climb uphill. Its silica ribs fractured in white branching lines. For two hundred years it has been sealing itself. I have sent it stored manganese during four favorable exhalations. It has given shade in return, a narrow moving mercy crossing my patch once each afternoon.

When its vanes open fully, I feel the sky enter the ground.

That is the nearest thing I know to music.

Not sound. Sound is rare and extravagant here. But during strong charge storms, the stem trembles through its roots, and the old filament answers, and the plates of salt around the shaft lift by a width smaller than a thought. The whole rim becomes an instrument no ear could use. My colonies align. Metabolisms that have slept for decades wake. Pigments shift. Stored records become readable to myself.

In those times I remember further back than I usually can.

I remember a sky with more weight.

I remember impacts, or constructions, or both.

I remember a warm interval when I nearly grew soft and died of abundance.

I remember the passage of one of the great regulators over this field. It came at night, though night did not conceal it; rather, the stars concealed themselves around its outline. Its weight arrived before its body. The salt polygons flexed. Every stem within reach folded inward. The shaft reduced its breath to a whisper. The great one settled partly across the rim for a season and drew from below a surplus that would otherwise have split the field. Under it, I lived in darkness and pressure. I lost half my outer colonies. I gained, in the layer beneath, a band of silver-gray memory so dense that even now my deeper self turns toward it when the world becomes uncertain.

I do not know whether that being was animal or machine.

I know it corrected.

That is enough.

Sometimes gliders come.

Not often, and never for long. They descend when the shaft exhales with the right cold metallic sweetness. From below they are almost invisible, but I do not rely on sight. Their membranes alter pressure. Their shadows bend starlight at night and thin sunlight by day. One passed low enough once that its trailing microvanes brushed the tip of a nearby stem, and a dusting fell from it: charged grains, spores from another crust, a seed-shard that had traveled longer than many lowland civilizations last.

The seed-shard lodged in a crack three meters from my edge.

For years it did nothing.

Then, after a storm that blurred the pylon’s position, it stood upright as a black bristle.

I fed it trace minerals when I could. Not generously. Generosity implies excess. I allocated. I allowed. I loosened what I did not require. The bristle persisted. It may become a stem in a century or five. Or it may fail tomorrow, if the shaft-breath turns caustic. Around it, my youngest edge grows in a crescent, not touching, leaving a clean mineral gap. A nursery is also a boundary.

Inside me are records no one reads.

Perhaps the shelter-patterns could read them, if their diagrams reached this rim. Perhaps the buried filaments read me continuously and call it surface condition. Perhaps the stem-root interprets my layers as taste. I do not mind being unread. Memory here is not for narration. It is for calibration.

I hold the red century of radiation.

The violet centuries of electrical plenty.

The gray interruption when the horizon vanes stalled and the plateau lowered by the width of a shadow.

The black seam of the long stillness, when no shaft breathed for nine hundred years and most of me became enamel.

The bright narrow line of trespass: a warm walking body, once, long ago, entering the shaft-field and kneeling beside my stone. It carried water inside it. It leaked heat. Its instruments disturbed the dust. It placed a gloved hand close to my edge and did not touch. The shelter on the ridge woke for it. The pylon shadow tightened. The arthropods remained hidden. After the traveler left, its footprints widened into ovals and became indistinguishable from field marks. But beneath me, in the filament, the event persisted as compensation. In me, it persists as a band of altered salts and a brief abundance of carbon.

If I have an emotion, it is not loneliness.

There is too much relation here for loneliness, though little company. The plateau is sparse, but nothing is isolated at the proper scale. The shaft breathes. The filament corrects. The stems angle. The arthropods edit. The gliders transfer. The distant vanes take half a day to move and change the pressure of my next century. Even the stones are not merely stones; their shade is an action, their erosion a message, their alignment a remnant of decisions still settling.

I am alone only to the impatient eye.

My days are not days. They are gradients of usable light and dangerous light, contraction and release, charge and discharge, memory and repair. Noon is endured. Evening is measured. Night is when the cold becomes articulate and the stars arrive sharp enough to seem mineral. Under stars, I open slightly. My green edge appears. I take in what little the dark offers.

Once in many years, a rule-storm crosses.

Before it arrives, I know.

The stem to the north folds. The arthropods vanish under salt. Dust lifts evenly from the flats. The pylon becomes uncertain. My outer scales clamp down with a motion slower than fear and older than reflex. The exposed filament beneath me runs cold.

Then the wall passes.

For minutes, laws negotiate in the open.

Weight thins.

Shadows sharpen and smear.

The shaft exhales inward.

My memories reorder at the edges. Layers misalign. Some colonies experience the storm before others, though they lie beneath them. A few hairs grow into yesterday and return carrying salts that will not exist until dawn. This is dangerous, but also nourishing. The plateau changes its maps. I change my margins.

Afterward, I am almost the same.

Almost is the whole art of endurance.

A new spiral of dark dust lies to the east. The seed-bristle has tilted. The exposed filament is warmer by a fraction. One of my oldest dormant colonies has awakened and begun producing a pigment I have not needed for two thousand years. I let it spread through a narrow band. It may be useful. It may be a mistake. The plateau is made of corrected mistakes.

So where am I?

On the rim of a deep shaft in the high dry country, where the atmosphere is thin and the ground remembers vanished skies. I am spread across stone beneath a leaning stem, half shade and half archive, living so slowly that erosion and thought are almost the same act.

And what am I?

A crust that is also a sensor.

A stain that is also a library.

A small, dark organ of the plateau’s ongoing balance.

I do not speak. I do not walk. I do not own a name unless some passing intelligence gives me one and loses it.

But when the shaft breathes, I open.

When the storms alter the rules, I remember.

When the old system asks the surface what has changed, I answer by being changed.

I am not dead.

I am not thriving.

I am enduring.
