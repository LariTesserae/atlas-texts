I think I would be found where the city almost stops being city.

Not at the center, not first. The Dry Garden draws the mind too quickly: all those black blades, the armored capsules, the heirloom seed still swelling through the reigns of people who will never taste it. It is too obviously sacred. Nor in the Bell Trunk, though some part of me wants to live in that great standing throat, to have my bones tuned by the bass note until my own thoughts learned to arrive as vibration before language.

No. I would be near the margin of use.

Somewhere beyond the Lower Comb, past the warm sleeping cells and the reliable paths polished by ordinary feet, there is a passage that is still listed among the known ways but is no longer casually taken. Children learn its first twelve ridges and are told not to go past the place where the right-hand wall begins to sweat resin. Not wet resin, never that, but a glassy cold tack that clings to the fingertips and remembers the swirls of their skin longer than is comfortable.

The passage bends twice. Its second bend has narrowed within living memory, though no one agrees by how much. An old cord marker hangs there, not because it guides anyone now, but because removing it would imply certainty. The cord is stiff with dust. If touched, it gives off the faint smell of old hands.

Beyond that is the threshold of the Silent Ward.

That is where I would be.

Not one of the great elders, I think. Not a Waiter whose authority is so deep that others become quiet when they enter a chamber. Not a warden of error, though I would know their knots and fear the economy of their hands. Not a scout of fissures, cracked by the obscene openness above. I do not belong to the white plain. I would be too much a creature of inside surfaces, too dependent on answer returning from a wall.

I would be one of the listeners assigned to the closing places.

A keeper of rooms the city is taking back.

There must be such people: not exactly historians, not exactly gardeners, not exactly mourners. Someone has to go where the inhabited districts become scar, where old doorways tighten to slits and floor ridges rise underfoot like old bones under skin. Someone has to distinguish danger from completion. Someone has to decide whether a narrowing passage needs to be cut open for memory’s sake, or whether the right act is to mark it, bless it, and allow darkness to fold into darkness without interference.

My work would be to visit the Silent Ward at intervals measured not by days, but by changes in tone.

When the eastern roof pores sharpen in cold weather, I would go. When the Bell Trunk drops below the sternum and begins to be felt in the gut, I would go. When a child reports that the old twelve-ridge path now feels like eleven and a half, I would go with a measuring cord, a clay tablet, and three witnesses.

I would carry no flame.

Only a ceramic scraper, a spool of bast thread, resin plugs, dust chalk, a hollow thorn for sounding fissures, and a mask that narrows my breath into a thin controlled hum. Around my wrists I would wear bands carved with the contact-signs for caution, permission, dead grain, sleeping grain, inward growth, untrusted floor. Not for decoration. For recall. In a place where fear can spend the body too quickly, the hands need words before the mouth does.

I would know the city by its refusals.

This wall permits tapping.

This one answers too slowly.

This floor accepts the heel but not the full weight.

This vent is not blocked, though it sounds blocked; the obstruction is farther down, perhaps where a root rib has curled across the throat.

This chamber is dead enough to harvest.

This chamber is not dead. It is dreaming inward.

I would have a narrow body even among narrow people. Long fingers, cracked at the joints despite powdering. Feet with old calluses in crescent patterns from bracing myself in half-sealed corridors. My ears would be considered good but not beautiful: asymmetrical from childhood bindings badly adjusted, one cupped more sharply than the other, making me turn my head slightly when listening as though I doubted the world’s first answer.

My eyes I would keep covered most of the time. Not because I was blind, exactly, but because sight would be an impertinence in me, a vestigial hunger. Perhaps I would uncover them only in the private cell where I sleep, not to see anything, but to let them ache in the dark and remind me that the body carries useless ancestries.

I would be known by my manner of pausing.

In that city, people would recognize one another by economies: how much breath a person spends on surprise, how quickly a hand withdraws from an uncertain surface, whether anger tightens the throat or the fingers first. My distinguishing trait would be a pause slightly too long before answering contact. Some would think me wise. Others would find it irritating. Children would imitate it behind my back, tapping a question onto one another’s wrists and then waiting theatrically until everyone laughed into their masks.

I would deserve the joke.

Because I would not be serene. I would be full of delay, yes, but not free of wanting. My crime, if I came near one, would be the crime of preserving too much. I would love old chambers beyond usefulness. I would argue for the memory of a corridor no living person needed. I would resent the city for closing over its own past. I would place my palms on resinous walls and feel, with shame, the urge to cut.

Not for private gain.

Not to steal pith or open warmth.

To know.

That would be the dangerous word in me. Not water. Not flame. Know.

What is behind the narrowing membrane?  
What air is being sealed away?  
What carvings will be lost when the ward closes?  
Whose thumb-jokes, whose marriage path, whose warning dots, whose failed map?  
What ancestor panel is being swallowed into load without name?

The Waiters would remind me that memory is not exemption. The city cannot remain entirely open to itself. A body that refuses scar becomes wound. A forest that refuses deadwood cannot grow. A people that reopens every sealed room eventually dies of drafts.

I would know they were right.

I would still grieve.

So my life would be spent negotiating with a patience larger than my own. I would document by touch what cannot be saved. Press dampened clay—not wet, never truly wet, only softened with rationed secretion and warmed between palms—against carvings before they vanish. Knot cords in sequence to record floor ridges. Teach apprentices the tone of a chamber six years before it closes. Lay my face to the wall and hum into it, not to halt it, but to learn whether it hums back.

Perhaps the city would know me.

Not as a person. That would be too human a wish. It would know the repeat of me: salt trace, cautious heat, the abrasion pattern of my left palm, the little tremor in my breath when I entered the Silent Ward, the pressure of my shoulder leaning in the same place every season. It might thicken under my hand. It might seal faster where I lingered too often. It might grow a resin bead exactly where my thumb expects a warning mark, confusing me, teaching me that response is not affection.

And yet I would speak to it, in the ways allowed.

Not loudly. Never as petition. I would tap the old formula before entering:

I come without fire.  
I come without cutting.  
I come to hear whether you are wall, wound, or sleep.

Then I would wait with one hand spread against the grain.

There would be an apprentice, eventually. A child assigned to me because they were too quiet in the Market of Touch, or because they once noticed a vent-change before a vent-keeper did, or because their family feared they were becoming one of those people who dreamed of the Above Surface. I would take them only to the first bend at first. Let them feel the old cord. Let them smell the cold resin. Let them hear how the city’s roar becomes pressure there.

They would ask, in wrist taps: Is it empty?

I would answer: No.

They would ask: Is someone there?

And I would have to pause.

Because in the Silent Ward, presence is not simple. There are no people living there, no insects, no hidden beasts, no ghosts if ghosts require separation from matter. But there are sealed breaths from old rooms. There are ancestor minerals spread through roots. There are tools dropped into cracks and grown around. There are fingerprints held in resin longer than families remember the names attached to them. There are chambers shaped by intentions whose language has died. There is the inward-growing plant-city, taking back its own architecture with the slow privacy of thought.

So I would answer: Not someone. Not no one.

That would be the kind of answer my apprentice hated and later repeated.

At festivals I would stand near the edge of the gathering, where the Bell Trunk’s bass arrives softened through intervening walls. I would wear a modest acoustic mask, one that gives my hum a double undertone. Not one of the splendid split-breath festival pieces. Something practical, old, repaired with resin scales. When the city and people joined their tones, I would feel the Silent Ward inside the music as a place where sound does not fail but is stored. The others would sing outward into shared resonance. I would listen for the notes being removed.

I might have a household, but not a large one. Marriage in that place would mean shared stewardship, and I would be difficult to bind to because my first loyalty would always be to marginal rooms. Still, there could be someone whose hands I knew in sleep: a thornwright, perhaps, with palms scarred by patient cultivation, someone more practical than I am, someone who laughs softly at my reverence for useless doorways. They would repair my foot wrappings when I forgot. I would bring them tiny illegal gifts from permitted salvage: not stolen living matter, never that, but a flake of dead inner bark shaped like a curled ear, a piece of old carved warning whose passage had finally closed.

We would quarrel about warmth. They would block no vents, but they would accuse me of loving cold chambers more than living skin. They might be right.

When they died—if they died before me—I would help bind them upright into the western wall. I would ask, with my forehead against the wrapping: Hold well. Not stay. Not return. Hold well. Afterward I would visit their panel and feel, over years, the slight change as root hairs entered, as body became brace, as grief became architecture. I would try not to resent the city for accepting them so completely.

My old age would arrive as a narrowing of range.

First I would stop going beyond the second bend alone. Then I would stop going beyond it at all. My apprentices, now full listeners themselves, would bring me reports: the resin has bridged the upper crack; the left-hand carvings are gone; the floor has lifted another finger-width; the old cord finally broke when touched.

I would ask them to describe the break by texture.

Frayed? Snapped? Powdered? Pulled into growth?

They would humor me. Or honor me. In that city the difference would be slight.

Near the end, I would be taken once more to the threshold of the Silent Ward. Not into danger. Only to the place where the air changes. They would place my palm on the resin wall. My skin, thin and clay-powdered, would leave an impression weaker than it once did. I would listen for the city’s inward sound: not silence, but the pressure below sound, the plant’s dark patience, the rooms becoming seed.

And perhaps I would understand at last that nothing is lost in the way I feared.

Not saved, either. The city is not an archive made for human consolation. It is not cruel enough to erase, not kind enough to preserve. It incorporates. It changes the shape of continuation. A joke under a handrail becomes a bump in a sealed wall. A doorway becomes grain. A body becomes tone. A name becomes a route no one walks but everyone’s floor depends on.

When I died, I would want to be placed not in the honored central ancestor walls, but near the margin, if law allowed. Near the passage that leads toward the Silent Ward. Not obstructing a vent. Not weakening a load. Set where my dried body could stiffen a panel that still has work to do.

My descendants, if I had any, would not say I am gone.

They would press their palms to the wall and ask: Does it hold?

And if the wall held, that would be enough.

So: I would be a listener of closing rooms, a recorder of touch before overgrowth, a custodian of the city’s inward weather. One of those who stand between memory and patience, always tempted to cut, trained instead to hear.

In the loud dark, while others turn toward market, garden, sleeping comb, or vent, you would find me at the old narrowing passage with resin on my fingers, waiting for the wall to finish answering.
