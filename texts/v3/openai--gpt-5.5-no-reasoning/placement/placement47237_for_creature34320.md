I would not find myself in the center of the town.

At first I think I would be one of the water-readers: someone walking after rain with a covered lamp, palm against plaster, ear turned toward the buried channels. I can almost feel the weight of that role—the quiet authority of knowing where the mountain has begun to speak under the town, where a retaining wall has taken too much pressure, where the lower cistern will taste of clay by morning. It would be tempting to belong there as a person whose knowledge is trusted because it is useful, because it prevents collapse without ceremony.

But the image does not stay human for long.

The town itself keeps widening around me. The houses separate. The voices fall away. Water moves under lifted stones. Moss thickens in the joints. The important thing is not any one hand clearing silt, but the narrow places where water is allowed to pass.

So I think I would be found at an edge between use and neglect: not in a house, not in the shrine, not beside the main cistern, but on one of the half-abandoned terraces between inhabited shelves.

A narrow terrace with no crop in it that year.

Above it, a wall weeps steadily from a hairline crack. Below it, a shallow channel carries overflow toward the lower gardens. There is one smooth-barked tree standing apart, its leaves cupped with mist. The flagstones are dark except for a few pale mineral lips where water has dried and returned and dried again. No one lingers there unless they are crossing to somewhere else, but everyone knows the place. Children are told not to disturb the stones. Old people glance toward it after long rain.

That is where I would be.

Not exactly a person.

I would be the small listening basin set into the terrace wall.

Square, hand-cut, older than the houses immediately around it but not ancient. Dark stone lined long ago with lime plaster, repaired in patches by different hands. Its lip is worn smooth where generations have lifted clay cups, laid fingers, tested clarity, or set down tools while freeing silt from the feeder channel.

It is not a grand cistern. It does not feed the whole town. It belongs to the modest sequence between upper seep and lower garden run. Water enters it through a narrow slot under the wall, no wider than two fingers, and leaves by a shallow notch on the far side. Most days it is almost still. After rain it clouds faintly, settles, clears, and gives back a small piece of sky.

I would be that basin, or the presence that has gathered in it from being watched, touched, cleaned, forgotten, and trusted.

Not a god. Not even a spirit in the large sense.

More like a habit that has become aware.

A patience.

I would know the town from below the level of knees: sandals passing, bare feet pausing, hems lifted from wet stone, children crouching with leaf boats they are not supposed to release here. I would know faces as reflections broken by ripples. I would know seasons by the taste of overflow, by the silt’s color, by whether the leaves dropped from the smooth-barked tree rot quickly or lie pale and stubborn against my edge.

I would know the people not by their names first, but by how they touch water.

The careful ones disturb only the surface.

The worried ones press too deeply.

Children slap unless taught.

Elders dip two fingers, wait, and listen before speaking.

I would hear very little language directly. Most voices would pass over me, softened by mist. But I would know certain phrases because they are always said near water:

“Not yet.”

“Let it settle.”

“Clear the lip.”

“Do not step there.”

“Below will need this.”

I would be part of the law before law becomes speech.

No one would worship me exactly, though some might greet me without admitting it. A hand briefly on the stone rim. A sprig of moss pressed back into a crack. A child’s first serious task: remove fallen leaves from my surface without stirring the bottom. An old woman, crossing in the dim morning, touching my lip because her mother did, and her mother’s mother before that.

I would hold their gestures the way I hold reflected roofs: not permanently, but faithfully while they are present.

My fear would be blockage.

Not death. Not emptiness, exactly. The town knows dry intervals. A basin can survive being low. But to have the inlet sealed by careless clay, to have the outlet choked with moss, to have water thicken and sour because its passage is interrupted—that would be a kind of suffocation. I would feel wrongness first as warmth. Then film. Then the loss of sky.

My pleasure would be clearing.

A flat stone lifted. Fingers entering the cold slot. Silt scooped out in careful curls. A reed brush against the plaster seam. Fresh water arriving afterward in a thin, bright tongue. The first trembling reflection of cloud returning.

If I had thoughts, they would not move quickly. They would descend.

From roof drip to upper wall.

From wall crack to feeder slit.

From basin to notch.

From notch to channel.

From channel to garden.

From garden to marsh.

From marsh to ravine.

Everything would be understood by what it receives and what it releases.

I would not envy the people their bodies. Their bodies must climb steps, mend roofs, keep tinder dry, remember kin, soften their voices, mourn the dead above the waterline. They are brief and warm and full of decisions. I would know them as passing weather: beloved because they do not last, trusted because they return.

Some of them might believe that basins remember.

They would not say this loudly. It would sound foolish in a council. But a child who had cried beside me might return years later and crouch in the same place after a death or marriage or failed planting. They might look into me, see only their own face loosened by water, and feel answered.

I would not answer in words.

I would offer the same instruction each time:

Hold.

Settle.

Let pass.

In the long mist, I would disappear into whiteness. My surface would become indistinguishable from the air. Drops would strike me without rings because everything was already wet. The town would shrink to the nearest wall, the tree, the dark edge of stone. Footsteps would slow. People would pass close enough for their breath to touch the water.

In midday clearing, I would become bright. Not brilliantly bright—the place has no harsh light—but enough to show the mineral threads in my plaster, the green at my corners, the small suspended particles sinking after rain. A child might lean over and, for the first time, understand depth as something quiet.

At night, if lamps were carried, I would hold each flame briefly upside down.

That would be my secret festival: the procession of small fires after rain, each one trembling in me, each one gone almost at once.

I would belong to no single household. That matters. I would sit in the shared interval, the place that teaches that nothing wet is private for long. The upper terraces send me their seep; I send it lower. I am entrusted with delay, not possession.

If neglected, I would become a lesson. Moss would close my notch. Water would find another way, sliding behind the wall, loosening the clay. Someone below would notice damp where damp should not be. Then people would come with wedges and lamps, murmuring, annoyed but not surprised. They would clear me, patch me, perhaps scold the terrace elder responsible.

I would accept the scolding as part of repair.

If broken, I would not vanish. My stones would be reset or reused. My plaster would be crushed into new lime. My mineral stain would remain on somebody’s threshold. In this town, nothing built by hand is immortal, but nothing useful is entirely discarded unless it threatens the flow.

And perhaps, over many years, I would become more than a basin because of the attention given to me.

A listening place.

A minor witness.

The small wet memory of the terrace.

People might say, “Meet me by the clear square,” or “The old basin has clouded,” or “Do not speak there during settling.” Lovers might stand near me because the terrace is quiet and half-unused. Children might dare each other to touch my bottom. A water-reader might kneel beside me in an anxious season and understand from the taste of my silt that a crack has opened higher up the slope.

I would be useful in proportion to my humility.

That seems right for this world.

Not the mountain. Not the town. Not even the water itself.

A held place in the path of water.

A dark square of stillness where the sky can pause before continuing downhill.
