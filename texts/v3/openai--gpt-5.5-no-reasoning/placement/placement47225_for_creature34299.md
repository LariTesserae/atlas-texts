I would be found where the bowls begin.

Not in the loudest center of the basin, not among the great crowded pools where every surface is already claimed by mouths, but farther out, where the stone has been worn into shallow black cups and each cup keeps a separate little weather. A field of old basins, some no wider than a skull, some large enough to hold a sleeping animal folded twice over. From a distance they would seem empty: dark stone, silver film, pale rings. But from close by the rings would move.

I would be part of those rings.

At first I might be mistaken for mineral residue. A pale crust at the rim of a water bowl. A collar of salt, shell, and old secretion. Something left behind by evaporation. I would not lift my head because I would have no head in the upward, singular sense. I would be low, circular, repeated: a many-bodied animal spread thin around the edge of a pool, each body no larger than a thumb joint, all joined beneath by wet threads hidden in cracks and under flakes of stone.

I would be one of the rim-keepers.

Not a grazer exactly, though I would scrape. Not a filterer exactly, though I would open when water moved. Not a plant, never a plant, though from above my colonies would make halos around water, pale growths where nothing green could grow.

In dryness I would harden into a scalloped mineral band. My outer plates would clamp down, gray-white and dull, stained brown at the edges by iron drawn from the stone. My feeding lips would retract. My soft undersides would shrink into sealed chambers. I would become almost indistinguishable from the basin’s own chemistry: salt, shell, lime, old mud. Small travelers would cross me as ground. Larger grazers might scrape at my outermost crust and take some of me away. That would be expected. I would lose pieces constantly. I would be made to be diminished and return.

In wetness I would wake by degrees.

First the hidden threads beneath the crust would swell. Then the plates would loosen. Then each little unit of me would open its rimward mouth, not upward, but sideways, toward the meniscus of the shrinking pool. I would feed where water meets air meets stone, where everything gathers: films, floating fat, dissolved shell, eggs too small to see, spores or near-spores, the dust of dead larvae, the chemical news of bodies passing through.

I would taste the pool’s history.

Rainwater would be thin and bright and violent, full of strangers.

Seepwater would be old, metallic, reliable.

Condensation would arrive clean at first, then become crowded with signals as it ran over bodies above me.

Blood would change everything.

If blood touched the pool, I would open wider.

I would not chase. I would not strike. I would wait in a geometry of patience, each mouth opening and closing with the smallest motions of water. My colony would make a faint sound when feeding: a dry, silky clicking, like fingernails rubbed against wet ceramic. In chorus with the other rings, the bowl-field would whisper underneath the greater basin’s roar.

My life would be the edge.

The edge of wet and dry.  
The edge of pool and stone.  
The edge of animal and residue.  
The edge of one body and many.

I would know the world mostly through tension. Water tugging at my feeding hairs. Vibration traveling through stone. The pressure-shadow of a grazer lowering its heavy mouth nearby. The sudden blankness before a predator passes, when all the small things seal so quickly that the usual tremor of the rim drops away.

I would have no eyes worth naming. Perhaps each unit of me could tell dim from dimmer, glow from absence. A pool-sac flashing blue beneath the water would register as a soft internal weather. A membraned hunter crossing overhead would be only a change in pressure and a brief cooling of the surface. But sound through stone would be vivid. The basin would enter me through my underside.

I would know the scrape-fronts when the blind grazers came down from the ridges after mist. Their mouths would rasp in unison, slow and wide. They would polish the lanes between bowls. Sometimes one would kneel too close and press its warm belly over half my colony, and the trapped part of me would seal and endure. Sometimes its tongue-plates would peel me away in strips. The eaten pieces of me would pass through its gut, and some inner fragments might live there awhile, feeding on the films it farmed inside itself. Perhaps that would be one of my dispersals. I would be a rim that could survive being swallowed.

I would know rain as terror and multiplication.

When rain came, my bowls would overflow and cease to be separate worlds. The field of moons would become a shallow, coursing sheet. My colonies would be torn at the edges. Soft threads that took seasons to grow would rip free. Whole segments of me would lift, tumble, and adhere elsewhere. Egg-grains stored in my mineral crust would loosen and drift. Many would be eaten instantly. Some would lodge in new rims. Some would be carried into pools whose chemistry would kill them. Some would wake inside the gill-folds of strangers.

During rain I would sing, though not by intention. Water forced through my opened feeding structures would make a reedy vibration, thousands of tiny throats sounding at once. The bowl-field would hum beneath the impact of rain, a submerged note in the basin’s violence.

Predators would not hunt me for myself, usually. I would be too thin, too mineral, too much trouble. But I would attract what predators hunted. My rim would gather larvae, small grazers, translucent pulse-things, soft knots of young filterers. Where I fed, others fed. Where others fed, jaws came.

So I would be dangerous by being useful.

Around me, the small hard-backed migrants would pass in black sheets. They would flow over my dry plates and pause at my wet edges to drink. Some would nip my opened mouths. I would close on a few, not quickly enough to be called predation, but firmly enough. Their legs would continue tapping for a long time against my sealed lips. I would draw what I could from them. In this basin, even waiting has teeth.

My colony would remember, but not as a mind remembers.

A bowl that dried too fast would thicken its rim next season.

A side often grazed would grow harder plates.

A channel that brought flesh during rain would extend pale lobes toward its entry point.

A crack that carried steady condensation would be lined with young.

I would think in growth-patterns. I would decide by surviving unevenly.

If a human stood among the bowls, still and warm and wet with breath, I would know them first as weather. Their footfall would arrive as impossible weight. Their heat would alter the little air above the rims. Moisture would gather on them and fall in salted drops. Those drops would wake mouths all around their boots. The rings nearest them would open, tasting cloth, leather, skin, fear, old sweat, foreign minerals.

I would not understand intrusion. I would only know opportunity.

If they stood long enough, I would begin to climb them—not with vines, not upward with desire, but by adhesion and accident. Small units dislodged by passing crawlers would fasten to damp seams. Egg-grains would stick in the salt at their ankles. Feeding lips would test the softened surface of skin. I would make of them a temporary rim, because anything that holds moisture becomes edge, and all edges belong to those who can endure them.

But most of my life would not involve such impossible visitors.

It would be spent listening to bowls shrink.

I would feel the surface draw downward day by day, leaving exposed stone slick with edible film. I would advance a little, then harden. Advance, then harden. Around me the pale halos would contract. Competition would sharpen. Pool-dwellers trapped in the deepening center would begin to consume one another. The sacs would vent and collapse. The mud would pulse. The air would smell of concentrated hunger.

Then, at last, dryness would close us.

My colony would become a ring of sealed mouths under mineral glaze. Inside each small chamber, life would slow to a hidden tick. The basin would continue roaring elsewhere, but I would hear it as if from inside stone. I would wait without impatience. Waiting would not be a lack of life. Waiting would be one of my shapes.

I would be old in pieces. No single part of me would last as long as the ring. Segments would be eaten, buried, dissolved, torn away by rain, crushed under armor, colonized by smaller things. But the rim would persist. Generation after generation would thicken the pale band. My dead plates would become the foundation for my young. My colony would build no structure and yet leave architecture: a raised lip, a scalloped scar, a bright mineral seam around dark water.

If one needed a name for me, it might be something like a calyx-ring, a thirst-collar, a basin wreath.

But names from elsewhere would miss the truth.

I would be an animal pretending to be shoreline in a world without sea.

I would be a mouth arranged as a border.

I would be the pale listening rim around one dark cup of water, opening whenever the air becomes kind enough to move.
