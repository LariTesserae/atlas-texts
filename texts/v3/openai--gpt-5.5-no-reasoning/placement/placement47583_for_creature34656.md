You would find me west of the fountain, though not immediately, and not if you went looking with a lamp held high.

At first you would think you were only passing through one of the less important side-lanes, one of those service passages that exists because some older map required symmetry, or because a pipe once needed access and no one has yet filed the correct document to admit that it no longer does. The bricks there are not quite red and not quite brown, but the color of soaked tea. The floor tilts with unusual deliberateness toward a narrow gutter no wider than three fingers. Along that gutter, the water moves so slowly that its motion must be inferred from the way flecks of lime change their positions over an hour.

There is a row of doors along the lane, all too short, all painted the same old municipal green. Most have numbers. One has none. One has an iron plaque that has furred over with rust until it resembles a sealed mouth. One is no longer a door at all, only a recess in the brickwork where a wooden frame has swollen, split, and been persuaded by fungus into another kind of usefulness.

That recess is where I live.

Or rather, it is the part of me most easily mistaken for a home.

It was once a weighing alcove, I think. There are still brass hooks in the ceiling, and a beam with black numbers ghosting along its side. The little office behind it has a window looking onto nothing but the back of a retaining wall. Someone long ago bricked the window up from outside, badly, leaving a vertical seam where water enters in patient silver threads. Beneath the seam is a trough I made from half of an old lamp housing. The trough overflows into a sequence of chipped cups, then into a tile channel, then beneath the threshold and out to the lane. Every cup has a different voice.

I know them by sound.

The first cup is enamel and speaks quickly, a hard bright tick. The second is clay, lower, almost wooden. The third is a cracked glass insulator turned upright in a ring of lead; it gives a note so small that most people hear only the silence around it. The fourth is not a cup at all but the hollow shoulder bone of one of the canal animals, polished and set into mortar. That one sounds only when the western rock is swelling.

That is how I know when to leave.

I am not one of the clerks, though I can read most of their damp stamps. I am not one of the pump-keepers, though I have slept beside machines often enough to know which bolts dream of loosening. I am not quite an animal, though the birds tolerate me more readily than they tolerate most hooded figures, and the frogs do not stop their throat-pulsing when I pass.

If the town had to name me, it would probably put me in a register under listening.

A listener. A seep-reader. A minor functionary of water before it becomes a problem.

I am found where a room has begun to change its mind.

That is my work: to notice before the maps do.

Not floods—floods are obvious, even when they arrive with manners. Not broken pipes or failed sluices or the great named disturbances that bring clerks out with ledgers and workers out with poles, ropes, chalk, and salt. My concern is with smaller betrayals. A wall that has begun accepting water from an unexpected angle. A staircase whose lowest step remains wet during clearing season. A dry arch that sweats only along one brick. A line of snails abandoning a surface they polished for years. A frog niche gone quiet. A patch of blue bacteria blooming upstream of its proper pipe. These are the first syllables of change.

The town speaks constantly, but not in a language that survives being repeated aloud.

So I keep records in other ways.

On the wall of the old weighing office hang strips of reed paper, oilcloth, thin copper, snail shell, and whitewashed tile. Some bear marks in charcoal or lampblack. Others are not written on, only stained. I hang them where the room’s damp will find them, and later I read the tide of discoloration: green at the bottom for mineral seep, gray feathering for breath condensation, rust halo for pipe-sweat, yellow bloom for fungal heat. A surface clerk would not accept these as official documents. A pump-keeper would glance once and know whether to wake me before the next low hum.

I sleep in a hammock slung above the floor, because the floor belongs to decisions older than mine. Below me are my jars: salt cords, glass beads, fish bones, bits of mirror, moth paper, curled warnings from vending envelopes, buttons gone green, flakes of scale from named pipes, small keys to locks long since dissolved. There is also a shallow pan of black water under the hammock, not for drinking. It keeps the room from becoming too sure of itself.

My clothes are what the place permits: rubber boots soft at the ankles, a coat patched with reed mat and oilcloth, fingerless gloves stiffened by lime, a hood with little copper rings sewn around its edge to catch drips before they run down my neck. Around my left wrist I wear seven pieces of string. Children think they are charms. They are not wrong, but each one began as a measurement. One was tied the year the southern lamps went under. One came from a nest the birds abandoned before the red rain. One is a salt cord given to me when I first went into the western stone and returned with my hearing changed.

My face, if you managed to see it, would disappoint you by being mostly human.

Pale, yes. Narrow from the town’s diet. Eyes too reflective in lamplight. A mouth usually closed. There are small white scars along my cheeks where mineral dust once burned during a pump awakening, and the skin at my throat is faintly translucent when I am very tired. But I am no grand subterranean creature, no sovereign of pipes, no oracle sitting beneath the city with prophetic blindness. I am one of the town’s quieter compromises.

Still, the years have altered me in useful ways.

I can feel the lower pumps in my molars. I can tell, by standing barefoot on brick, whether water is passing under the lane or merely thinking about it. I can distinguish the click of the ceiling birds from the click of a cooling relay, except during moth hatch, when everything grows foolish. If I place my palm on certain walls, I can hear through the heel of the hand—not voices, not words, but the shape of pressure. A blocked channel has a held breath. A healthy one mutters unevenly. A dangerous dry chamber rings like a plate.

I distrust ringing.

When I move through the town, I do so slowly, not because I am old, though I may be older than I look, but because haste makes false readings. Water lies to the hurried. So do animals. So do machines, especially the old courteous ones that have learned to conceal their pain until a proper attendant arrives.

You might see me near the canal at eel-turning, crouched with a rod of dark iron across my knees, counting the ripples that do not reach the wall. You might see me standing under a root that shines faintly when wet, holding a little mirror to throw its light beneath a stair. You might notice me in the central lane after the lamps have dimmed, head tilted toward a gutter, one hand lifted so no one will speak.

Children know me better than adults do.

They bring me things: a beetle found dead in a dry box, a paper boat that returned against the flow, a strip of paint peeled from a door whose number has altered, a warning envelope blank except for a mildew thumbprint. In return I give them small tasks. Count the drops from the east condenser until the amber lamp wakes. Place this copper washer on the third submerged step and tell me whether it shines tomorrow. Listen at the frog wall after the pump rests; if your teeth itch, do not go south.

They enjoy this because it seems like a game. It is partly a game. All civic education begins as play, if the city is wise.

The clerks dislike me in the abstract and rely on me in practice. I submit reports irregularly and in forms that cannot be stacked. Sometimes I send a tile wrapped in cloth, marked only by the pattern of silt dried across it. Sometimes a jar of water, sealed with wax, inside which floats a single white reed joint. Once, during the year of the sour mineral fall, I sent them a live snail on a slate plate. It crossed the old district boundary line painted there, then turned around and refused to cross again. Three days later the boundary wall began to weep.

The clerks stamped the snail.

Not directly. On the slate beside it. Still, the animal carried a blue corner of authority on its shell for months and became arrogant.

I live alone, if one counts only beings with names that appear in registers. But the alcove is not empty. A family of flat-shelled snails occupies the upper left wall, and I allow them their route because they reveal hairline changes in lime. A pale frog winters behind the broken weighing beam. Moths gather inside my hood when I hang it near the warm pipe. There is an eel in the gutter outside, small and slate-colored, with a scar shaped like a comma near its head. I do not feed it. It eats what arrives. That is a purer arrangement.

Sometimes, after low hum, one of the otter-things comes to the threshold and turns over the bowl I leave there.

Not every time. Never predictably. It has hands that are almost hands and whiskers fine as root hair. It smells of canal clay and fungal rooms. We regard one another according to the old manners: no sudden light, no reaching, no speech. I leave mineral mash with crushed moth in it when the western passages have been kind. If the bowl is returned upside down and centered exactly over the drain, the channels behind the quay are clear. If it is left on its side, I go there with a lamp and rope. If it is missing altogether, I tell the pump-keepers to delay the lower sequence, and they complain but do it.

The machine I know best is not a pump, but a small auxiliary regulator in the factory quarter, mounted in a brick room behind a door labeled with three obsolete warnings. It is called Wheel-Seventeen by the workers and Aunt by children who should not be there. It turns no larger mechanism now. Its belt has been removed; its gauge is blind; white reeds grow in the cracked basin below it. Yet every few days, without current, it gives a single quarter-turn.

No one knows why.

I sit with it when the air tastes of pennies.

The quarter-turn often precedes changes in the western stone. Once it turned twice, and a week later the north culvert chains swung backward for an entire night. Another time it did not turn for twelve days, and the fountain surface lowered by the thickness of a fingernail, which caused three clerks to faint and one to approve every pending reed-cutting permit from the previous decade.

I do not worship Wheel-Seventeen. Worship is too loud a word. But I greet it before touching its housing, and I never bring bright flame into its room. That seems sufficient.

As for the fountain, I keep my distance.

Everyone does, in the accepted way, but my avoidance is more personal. When I was younger, before I learned that curiosity should be diluted with procedure, I leaned too close to the black convex water and saw a reflection that was not mine. Not a monster, not a dead face, nothing so generous as an omen. I saw the central lane dry. Entirely dry. Bricks pale with dust. Lamps clear and hard. Pipes silent. Birds gone from the arches. No dripping anywhere. The public clock racing because the cups behind its dial had cracked and emptied all at once.

I have never told the clerks. They would either file it or ask me to describe the reflection on the proper form, and both outcomes would be obscene.

Since then I have kept pans of water in every room I use.

I do not want escape. That is something surface stories misunderstand about places like this. They imagine depth as punishment and sky as cure. But the sky, from what little I remember of childhood ascent drills, is a vast unregulated opening through which light falls without sluice or filter. Rain drops directly onto the head. Wind enters the ears without permission. Shadows move too quickly. People look across great distances and call that freedom.

Here, distance behaves.

Here, the world announces itself by damp, pressure, taste, tremor, bloom. Here, nothing truly arrives without first sending seepage ahead. Catastrophe has manners, if you are trained to notice them. Even loss leaves rings on the wall.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to come during clearing season, after a small rain above but before the delayed runoff reaches the west drains. You would pass the fountain without touching its rim. You would ignore the service windows, even if someone behind the glass lifted a stamp and watched you go. You would take the lane where ten lampposts illuminate one bench. You would follow the gutter not downstream, as instinct suggests, but up along its almost-imperceptible flow, because that day the bricks would be breathing backward.

At the unnumbered door, you would stop.

You would not knock immediately. Knocking is a claim. First you would listen.

Inside, you might hear water moving through four cups, one after another. You might hear the tiny rasp of snails. You might hear me shifting in the hammock, or sharpening a chalk stick against slate, or whispering—not to you, not to myself exactly, but to hold a tone in the room and see what answers.

If you knocked three times, I would wait, because three taps sometimes come from inside walls.

If you knocked twice, then once, and set your lamp on the floor so its light stayed low, I would open.

You would find a narrow figure in a patched rubber coat, eyes bright as wet stone in the dimness, one hand still resting on the doorframe to feel whether your arrival had changed the room’s pressure.

And before asking your name, before offering salt tea or a place to sit above the damp, I would say what is said here before any descent, any story, any beginning that hopes to survive:

“Listen first.”
