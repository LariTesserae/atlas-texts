You would find yourself not at the center of the chamber, because there is no center that lasts.

The center is only a temporary accident of pressure: the place where the hot air folds around itself, where dust hangs longest before choosing a wall, where the sounds collide and lose their origins. A body cannot belong there. The open part of this world, if it can be called open, is not habitat. It is crossing, danger, furnace-throat. Life clings elsewhere.

You would find yourself on the underside of a basalt shelf, not quite floor and not quite wall, in one of the chamber’s older neighborhoods.

Above you, only a handspan away, hangs a crusted ceiling of black stone and yellowed mineral teeth. Below you, close enough to feel as a constant radiation against the plates of your belly, lies a powder basin ribbed by the old passage of burrowers. To one side a vent-field breathes through clogged white tubes. To the other, a seam glows dark red beneath a half-sealed lip of iron bloom. The shelf is narrow, crowded, overused, and therefore safe in the way an ancient road is safe: not because it is gentle, but because many bodies have tested it and left instructions in wear, waste, scent, heat-polish, broken shell, and the memory of feet.

You are not one of the large burrowers. They are too much like events. They pass and the map changes behind them.

You are not a heat-moth. Their life is too sudden, too collective in panic, too dependent on brief dark flutterings between surfaces.

You are not one of the tubular feeders either, though you live near them and know their opening and shutting as one knows the pulse of a neighbor. Their existence is vertical, rooted, devotional. They face the vent and receive.

You are a clicker.

At least, that is the name an outsider would give you, because sound is the first thing about you that crosses species. A little dry percussion in the cracks. A coded tapping under the roar. A set of black articulated limbs testing stone, shell, crust, heat, hollowness, food.

But from inside your own life, you are not “a clicker.” You are a seam-walker, a listener-in-stone, a keeper of tolerable edges. Your body is narrow and jointed, made for the places that are neither exposed nor sealed. You live in the margins: under ledges, inside cracks, along the warmed lips of fractures, behind loosened mineral scales, between old shell-gardens and fresh chemical skin.

You are about the length of two finger joints. Your back is black, matte in cooler patches, faintly translucent where the plates overlap. When you pass above a live red seam, your underside glows through you in dim internal bars, so that for a moment you seem to carry the mountain’s ember-light in your belly. Your legs are long for this world, but not long in any graceful sense. They are angled tools. Each foot ends in a hard tapping point and a minute hook that can catch in rough basalt, salt crust, molted shell, or the ridged back of another creature if crowding forces you over it.

You have no need for eyes in the open-air manner. You possess dark pits that read brightness as danger and heat as direction, but sight is crude here. The chamber is too red, too crowded, too full of dust and glareless shadow. You know the world by contact before distance. By vibration before shape. By taste before name.

Every surface speaks.

Fresh crust gives back a thin, brittle tick.

Old basalt answers with a duller, trustworthy note.

A hollow salt blister clicks too brightly and must not be trusted.

A gas-swollen seam trembles before it opens.

A burrower trail has a packed, fibrous resonance from bodies passing over it.

A dead shell, if empty, rings differently from one inhabited by mites.

A tube colony has silence around it when sealed and a faint trembling hush when open.

You move through a world that is mostly sound felt through the feet.

The chamber is loud, yes, but the loudness is not confusion to you. The large noises are weather, geography, calendar. The floor-chug is below all thought. The vent-whistle marks districts. The hammering behind the wall is an old fact, older than your generation, older perhaps than the shelf on which you hatched. The small noises are the ones that matter: a crawler rasping too near your feeding scrape, a moth clutch loosening under powder, mites dispersing from a fresh dropping, a rival’s forelimb tapping ownership into the seam.

You were hatched in a fracture no wider than your mother’s body.

The eggs were not soft. Nothing truly soft survives unguarded here. They were sealed in a paste of mineral dust, dried secretion, and living microbial smear, tucked behind a lifted plate of iron-black crust. Warmth came from below, steady but not fierce. Above, scale-lice made a tiled roof with their bodies. One by one, your brood split their casings and unfolded into the seam. Some went upward and dried. Some went downward and were eaten by red seam-grubs before their plates hardened. You went sideways, following a line of tolerable warmth and the taste of old waste.

Sideways became your inheritance.

You learned early that the chamber is not a room. It is a stack of edges. A road no wider than your leg may connect two countries. A change of three body-lengths may mean starvation, scalding, poison, abundance, mating, or death. To live is to know exactness.

You know the basalt shelf where you dwell in a pattern of taps and temperatures:

Here, the black crust is thick enough to cross after the pressure-thumps.

Here, beneath the copper-green stain, the microbial varnish regrows quickly if not overgrazed.

Here, a vent-breath curls along the wall after a strong pulse; you must flatten yourself under the ridge until the taste thins.

Here, moths gather in soot-patches, and beneath them are eggs, and where there are eggs there are mites, and where there are mites there is food.

Here, the old burrower trail has cracked open and begun to grow orange film in the split.

Here, a rival lives.

Not an enemy in any grand sense. There is no room for grandeur. A rival is a repeating pressure in the map. Another body of similar hunger. Another tap-pattern claiming the same seam after every slackening of the vents.

You meet often without touching. Touch is costly. Instead you speak into stone.

Three taps: present.

Two scraping pauses: feeding.

A long tremor through the right forelimb: withdraw.

A rapid scatter of strikes: heat-rise, heat-rise, heat-rise.

Some signals are species-old. Some are local. Some belong only to this shelf and would mean nothing to clickers across the chamber, if any could cross safely to learn them. Your language is not a language of abstract things. It is a grammar of warning, claim, readiness, invitation, refusal, fracture, food.

You feed by breaking skins.

Your mouthparts are delicate only after your limbs have been violent. You find a patch of dark microbial varnish sealed beneath a thin mineral glaze. You test it. You turn sideways, brace four limbs, raise two. Then the tapping becomes work. Not the broad sound an outsider hears, but precise blows placed along weakness. Tick. Tick. Pause. Tick-tick. A flake lifts. Heat breathes out. You wait. The exposed film darkens in the red gloom.

Then you lower your head and taste the mountain’s life.

It is metallic, sulfurous, bitter, nourishing. It is not food as a fruit-eater would understand food. It is a charged skin of chemistry and microbe, a stain that has learned to live from stone-breath. Your gut knows what to do with it. Your inner symbionts wake to it. Poison becomes warmth. Sulfur becomes motion. Iron becomes shell. The chamber enters you grain by grain, and later you return it as waste that will seed another surface.

You are not separate from the factory.

You are one of its smaller moving tools.

Where you feed, you open crust. Where you walk, you polish routes. Where you shed, your cast plates lodge in cracks and become scaffold. Where you defecate, microbial bloom thickens. Where you die, something will roof itself with you.

You are alive, but the mountain uses life the way it uses pressure: as a method of making surfaces.

During slack times, you range.

Range, here, means perhaps a distance that an open-world creature would cross in a breath. For you it is an expedition. From your home seam you descend the underside of the basalt shelf, pass the sealed tubes of the pale feeders, cross a low ridge of old burrower crust, and reach the powder basin’s edge. You do not enter the basin unless hunger is severe. Powder hides heat. Powder hides mouths. The wedge-burrowers move there, blind and massive, their heads pushing through volcanic flour like slow plows. A single nudge from one would crack your plates. Not from malice. There is little malice here. Only appetite, pressure, and contact.

But along the basin edge are rich scraps: broken moth wings, crawler droppings, mites grazing on salts, flakes of microbial skin lifted by thermal expansion. You harvest quickly. Your forelimbs tap, tap, tap as you move. Your body remains low. When the deep chug below the floor changes pitch, you freeze.

The chamber knows before you know.

First the tubes close.

Not all at once. A ripple of withdrawal passes through the vent colony. Pale cilia vanish. Mouths seal. The ivory cluster becomes blunt and dead-looking.

Then the crawlers flatten under ridges, legs tucked beneath shields.

Then the moth-patches tighten, every dark wing clamped.

Then comes a taste in the air: sharper sulfur, hot metal, something that makes the joints draw inward.

You tap once into the stone. The answer comes back wrong. Too full. Pressurized.

You run.

For you, running is not speed so much as abandoning ceremony. Feet strike untested surfaces. Heat bites through one leg-tip. A salt blister cracks under your weight and drops a spray of glassy dust. The vent begins to whistle. The red seam brightens ahead, making the shelf’s underside appear and vanish in pulses.

You reach your crack as the chamber roars.

Inside, there is barely room to fold yourself. That is why it is safe. The opening is too narrow for burrowers, too dry for grubs, too exposed to heat for moth eggs, too cramped for most crawlers. It is yours because it is nearly nothing.

You wedge your body deep and brace all your limbs. Your plates overlap. Your breathing slows. Outside, pressure moves through the pocket like an invisible body too large for the room. Dust lifts. The vent screams. Mineral grains strike the crack-mouth and patter against your shell. The basalt around you ticks with expansion. Somewhere a crust splits like a small plate breaking. Somewhere a tube colony loses part of itself. Somewhere moths rise in panic and beat themselves against stone.

You remain.

Endurance is not passive here. To endure is to make thousands of adjustments: sealing joints, shifting limbs away from heat, reducing breath, reading vibration, knowing whether to stay wedged or retreat deeper, knowing whether deeper has become hotter than outside. The wrong patience kills as surely as panic.

After the pressure passes, the silence does not come. It never comes. But the roar lowers. The whistle thins to a hiss. Dust begins its reluctant settling.

You unfold by increments.

One forelimb first. Test.

Tap.

The stone answers changed.

Again.

A new crack has opened nearby.

Not large. Nothing grand. A hairline, but warm, fresh, edged with mineral brightness. To an outsider, it would be invisible unless the red light caught it. To you, it is an event of enormous importance. New stone has been exposed. New gases will touch new surfaces. Microbial haze will gather there. Mites will come. Grubs may come. Your rival may come. A map that has been stable for much of your life has acquired a future.

You approach the crack carefully.

The heat is fierce but not impossible. The edges are raw, sulfur-yellow and iron-red, glittering in grains. You tap along one side. Thin answer. Fragile crust. You tap again. Beneath the fresh mineral skin is hollowness, and beneath the hollowness, a slow seep of chemical breath.

You mark the place.

Not ownership yet. It is too new for ownership. You mark it as knowledge, as promise. You leave a thread of waste, a smear from your mouthparts, a few loosened grains. You seed what you will later eat, if you survive long enough and if others do not claim it first.

This is how your world expands: not outward, but into detail.

A new crack.

A clogged vent cooling.

A burrower trail hardening into road.

A dead crawler becoming roof.

A moth clutch becoming feast.

A rival vanishing after a pressure pulse, leaving a seam suddenly unspoken for.

You do not dream of outside. There is no image in you corresponding to sky. Cold would be catastrophe. Wind would be a violence without surface. Rain would be incomprehensible, then fatal. Space would not mean freedom. It would mean nowhere to cling, nowhere to read, nowhere to hide from breath too hot to cross.

Your world is complete because your senses complete it.

The low red seams are enough light.

The engine-roar is enough weather.

The mineral films are enough pasture.

The cracks are enough distance.

The chamber is small only to a creature that expects horizons. To you it is vast in the way a body is vast to the microbes that live upon it: folded, regional, dangerous, layered, mapped by gradients and histories rather than miles.

You are a black jointed listener under a mountain.

You carry no tool because your limbs are tools. You build no city because every inhabited surface is already civic. You have no king, no nest-state, no story beyond heat and pressure, yet your life is not empty. It is exact, repetitive, alert, saturated with signs.

You know when the tubes will open by the soft tremor in their collars.

You know when a burrower is near by the powder’s deadened response.

You know which mineral blooms cut the feet and which can be crossed after cooling.

You know the taste of fresh microbial film, old varnish, dead moth, crawler waste, egg paste, dangerous gas.

You know the mountain not as scenery, but as contact.

If a human imagination entered this chamber, it might first see hell, factory, furnace, prison, engine, wound. But you do not live in metaphor. You live in the practical intimacy of stone.

A seam warms.

You turn toward it.

A rival taps twice from the far side of the shelf.

You answer once, sharply.

A vent exhales; the pale feeders open their mouths.

Powder falls in a slow dry veil from the ceiling.

The new crack glows, quiet and promising.

You lift your forelimbs and begin to tap.
