I think I would find myself at the seam between the civic and the unfinished: not in the first ring of the harbor, not out among the black ridges, but in the outer canal districts where the city is still deciding how far to trust the ground.

At first I want to be one of the lens-keepers, because everything in the place bends toward that black harbor and its wrong depth. But that feels too central, too named. The harbor would draw too much explanation around me. I would not belong at the altar of the anomaly. I would belong where its consequences arrive indirectly: in sediment, in puddles, in the way a wall sweats after impossible rain.

So I think I would be a canal listener.

Not an engineer exactly. Not a priest. Not even someone especially important. I would work in the low, reed-grown corridors between districts, where machine-cut channels fail to remain obedient and young rivers test new routes under grates, through market subfloors, along the foundations of buildings that quietly tolerate being inconvenienced.

My station would be under one of the bridges near the southern canals, though not so near that sound travels backward every evening. A narrow shelter grown into the bridge’s underside: ceramic ribs, dark glass panels that turn transparent during rain, listening niches facing the reeds, racks of sealed jars, pressure membranes, sediment trays, a cot folded into the wall. The bridge above would click and breathe with tension changes. The water below would be tea-dark, warm-edged, mineral-bright when stirred.

My work would begin before dawn, in the condensation hour.

I would wake when the roof threads fattened with water and began dropping into the first catchment bowls. Before speaking, before eating, I would check the color of the rail condensation: clear for ordinary cooling, amber for mineral lift, grey-white for future dust, green-black for biological bloom. I would record the direction of moss wetting on the east wall. I would open the reed microphones only after the first five minutes of any rain had passed and been sealed away.

The title might be something plain, almost bureaucratic: Canal Acoustics Attendant, Third Outer Ring. But everyone would know the work is older than the title.

I would listen for amphibians.

Those unseen lacquer-backed creatures with silver throat sacs, the ones that call once at dusk from inside the reeds. I would maintain the devices that record them, but the devices would not be enough. They would catch pitch, pressure, humidity, canal depth, mineral suspension, harbor dilation, sky-skin charge. They would not catch the feeling in the jaw just before the note arrives, or the way nearby lantern flowers sometimes close half a breath early, or the slight dimming of pavement along a path no one has walked.

So I would sit still for long periods, learning the difference between water noise and infrastructural muttering. Drip from a roof lip. Tick of a cooling seam. A spider dredge stepping delicately on surface tension. Reeds touching. A maintenance swarm passing through root shade. A bridge correcting itself. The tiny pressure-change made by pollinators moving between pepper-sugar flowers.

And then, sometimes, the hollow note.

Not loud. Never loud. A single rounded sound from somewhere under leaves and warm water, like an empty vessel being remembered by the dark.

When it came, I would not interpret too quickly. That would be the discipline. The city distrusts premature certainty, and so would I. I would mark the time, the water height, the recent rains, the smell of exposed sediment, the behavior of geckos on the bridge wall, whether the sky-skin had shivered that afternoon. I would check whether the canal lock downstream hesitated before releasing. I would compare the note with older notes stored in gel, each one suspended like a small preserved dusk.

Some calls would delay dredging.

Some would open a sluice.

Some would mean nothing we could prove, which in that city would still mean they mattered.

I imagine myself wearing a dull green dew-cloak patched at the shoulders, with a throat disk tarnished from long exposure to mineral vapor. My sleeves would seal at the wrists. My boots would be soft-soled, made for root mats, damp ceramic, and the uncertain edges of channels. I would have an ear cuff that clicked near folded sound and sometimes clicked when nothing was wrong, because old devices develop opinions in humid cities.

I would carry jars.

Everyone here seems to carry something: sediment, water, root samples, memory-gel, fruit, tools, permissions. My bag would hold narrow jars for canal silt and first-safe rain, a folded listening dish, two reed knives, a spool of red warning thread, a small lamp that does not burn, and a bead of birth-condensation from my own childhood, cloudy now from age and skin salt.

I would know many people, but mostly in passing.

Market keepers would ask whether the canal was safe enough to cool fruit trays. Children would come to my station with forbidden gecko eggs cupped in their hands, asking where to place them so the maintenance spiders would not interfere. A mason might bring me a sliver of self-healing ceramic that had scarred in a strange direction after last night’s rain. A lens-keeper, exhausted and veiled, might stop under the bridge and ask whether the reeds had called before the harbor dilated.

I would answer carefully.

Care would be the local form of kindness.

My life would be full of small prohibitions. Do not whistle under the southern arches. Do not tap twice on hollow railings after rain unless you mean to wake the inspection fields. Do not pour ordinary cistern water into a channel holding harbor runoff. Do not name a new current until it has returned for three storms. Do not follow mineral feeders beyond the last anchored path, no matter how clearly their crescent tracks invite you.

I would probably have broken that last rule once when young.

Not far. Just far enough to understand why the rule exists. I would remember the black ridges after rain, shining like fresh wounds, and the low shapes moving patiently among them. Their glassy spinal plates. Their soft mouths rasping mineral breath from stone. The way the ground sounded underfoot: not cracking, not exactly, but considering. I would have come back with ash cuts on my palms and a permanent respect for unfinished surfaces.

That might be why I became a canal listener. Not from bravery, but from having learned that the edge of the city is not a boundary. It is a conversation held in dangerous grammar.

My dwelling would be above or beside the station, small and membrane-walled, with a roof basin full of dim plants. Not a private house in the old sense; more like a tolerated chamber in the body of the bridge. At night, when the sky-skin glowed brown-gold and the traffic passed soundlessly overhead, the walls would reveal their lattices if rain touched them. I would see conduits, root reservoirs, slow bubbles of stored water, the sleeping dark of structural fabric.

I would eat market fruit that ripens in half-light, canal rice grown in stabilized beds, broth made only from approved rain. I would own very few bright things: perhaps a yellow sash from a festival, a red thread from a temporary harbor permit, a blue shard of old glass found in a channel after a storm and never properly explained.

I would not be lonely, exactly. The city does not seem built for loneliness in the sharp sense. It spaces people out, but it also teaches attention. A person there is accompanied by systems, weather, damp leaves, listening surfaces, civic memory. Even silence has neighbors.

But I would be solitary by temperament. Someone who prefers the interval before an answer. Someone trusted with pauses.

At evening I would take my place beneath the bridge.

The gardens would exhale. Lantern flowers would open along the canals, smelling of pepper, sugar, wet stone. Condensation moths would gather around the milk-lit pavement, and white geckos would appear on the walls, their tiny amber organs visible beneath translucent skin. Somewhere far off, a rain would make one administrative plaza shine while the next remained dusty. The harbor, kilometers away, would darken like a thought nobody wished to disturb.

I would wait through the ordinary sounds.

Drops from the bridge ribs.

A soft adjustment of climate membranes.

Reeds knocking.

Water touching newly exposed mineral.

A glider passing high overhead, its translucent body briefly visible against the dim sky-skin.

Perhaps a child on the walkway above would laugh too loudly and be hushed, not harshly. Perhaps the pavement would glow under bare feet and then fade. Perhaps my ear cuff would click once, warning of a sound folding somewhere downstream.

Then the canal animal would call.

One hollow note.

The kind of sound that does not announce itself as language, but still alters the decisions of those who hear it.

I would close my hand around the recording bead and wait before marking anything down. The city would listen with me: bridge, reeds, water, membranes, hidden sensors, sleeping dredges, root corridors, distant harbor. Nothing would answer loudly.

And after the note had gone, I would write:

Dusk call. Southern outer canal, bridge 17. Rain ended twelve minutes prior. Mineral scent moderate. Reed pressure eastward. Pavement response delayed by half-breath. No visible animal. Dredging postponed.

Then perhaps, in the private margin no official archive requires, I would add:

The city heard it first.
