I would be found where the city forgets, briefly, to look inhabited.

Not in the great pump halls themselves, not at first. Not among the exposed ridges where the atmospheric engines turn in their clean metallic wind, nor in the lit apartment blocks with their disciplined rows of warmth. I would not be one of the visible custodians crossing a bridge in a black felt coat, pausing to listen to a cable. I would be below their routes and above the deepest engines, in the wet intermediate places the maps classify but no longer describe.

A service arcade under an old retaining wall.  
A corridor behind a condensation farm.  
A stairwell that descends beside a cliff face and then enters the cliff without admitting it has done so.

There would be a narrow door there, ceramic-handled, painted many times in gray enamel until its edges had thickened and softened. The sign on it would not be a name. It would be a function code, mostly obscured by mineral bloom:

**AUXILIARY INTERVAL REGISTRATION / WEST BASIN RETURN**

No one would knock. No one would have reason to.

Inside would be a long room, warmer than the stair but not warm. Pipes would run along the ceiling, fat with insulation and sweating at their seams. The floor would be tiled in small white squares, several cracked, all repaired with black resin. A shallow channel would divide the room lengthwise, carrying a thread of dark water so smooth it looked solid until a drop fell into it from above.

There would be shelves of ledgers. Not archives important enough to be centralized. Local ledgers. Redundant ledgers. Records of intervals between automatic actions: how many seconds between the amber lamp and the release of the small floodgate; how long frost remained on hinge housings after heat was applied; whether echoes in the third vaulted passage arrived before, with, or after the bell.

There would be drying racks for gloves.

There would be a kettle permanently near boiling, fed by a steam line no one officially maintained.

There would be a window, though from outside it would appear to be only a wet black lens set in stone.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be an attendant of intervals.

Not an engineer, not a priest, not quite an archivist. Something humbler and more superstitious than any of those, though the superstition would wear the language of procedure. My work would concern the spaces between events: the tolerances, hesitations, lag times, echoes, pauses, reversals, and sanctioned delays by which the city keeps itself from becoming either accident or decision.

The city depends on action, but it survives by spacing action correctly.

A pump must begin not when another stops, but one breath before.  
A tram must arrive after the cable sings, but before the fray becomes audible to ordinary ears.  
A street that returns a walker to its beginning must be crossed with one pause, not two.  
A frost gate must release only after ice has formed sufficiently to remember the shape it is meant to hold.

I would measure those not-quite-moments.

My instruments would be plain and old: brass chronometers in padded cases, pressure slates, frost calipers, listening rods, paper hygrometers made from laminated leaves, a tuning fork stamped with the seal of the basin authority. Some would be mechanical. Some botanical. Some would respond only if held in a bare hand until the skin hurt from cold.

I would walk routes others used only as passage and stand still in them long enough to learn whether the city’s timing remained correct.

At the west basin condensation ribs, I would count the delay between fog contact and gutter-song.

In the covered stair by the reversing street, I would pause at the third lamp, touch the left wall, and wait for the pressure bell. I would note whether the bell came from below, ahead, or from inside the stone.

In the tram arcade, I would stand on the yellow line while no tram was due and listen for the rails anticipating weight.

In the botanical wards, I would inspect the glassy flowers that flash violet only at an angle. I would not admire them openly. That would be imprecise. I would record:

**Three blooms responding. Violet present at 17 degrees. Moisture within permitted range. Silence adequate.**

But privately I would know them as one knows colleagues.

I would have a body, probably. Human enough to tire. Human enough to cough in the thin wet cold. Human enough to warm my hands under my arms and resent a stairway. But not human in any expansive way. Not someone with appetite wandering loose in me. The city would have narrowed me over generations or offices or both.

I would be pale from reflected weather. Slow-breathing. My hearing would be more trusted than my sight. My hands would be cracked at the knuckles and dark under the nails from graphite, moss stain, iron residue, and old paper. I would dress in layers that made me seem less like a person than a bundled instrument: black wool, waxed gray canvas, rubber cuffs, ceramic buttons, a hood with a stiffened brim to keep dripping water off my face. A small lamp would hang at my chest, hooded downward. Around my neck, beneath the collar, I would wear a flat metal tag with my district, function, and acceptable pressure range.

My name, if I had one, would not be used often.

Names are too warm for corridors.

I might be called by function:

**Intervaler.**  
**Delay clerk.**  
**West Return attendant.**  
**One who listens between bells.**

The custodians in higher departments might consider my office minor, almost obsolete. The great systems record themselves. The machines report compliance. The central gradients express preference before error matures into failure. What need is there for someone to write down the fractional hesitation of a floodgate in a basin where no unauthorized thaw has occurred in eighty-two years?

But that is exactly why I would exist.

The city does not fear catastrophe first.

It fears drift.

Not collapse, not fire, not rebellion, not flood. Those have procedures. Those announce themselves. They can be answered by valves, counterweights, locks, heaters, trams, diversion moss, silence orders.

Drift is subtler.

A bell sounding half a second late for a decade.  
A stair requiring a longer pause each winter.  
A flower blooming violet at an angle it was not assigned.  
A tram arriving correctly but departing with reluctance.  
Water accepting a channel, yet touching the left wall more often than the right.

Change enters a fixed world as politeness.

My work would be to notice politeness becoming intention.

Most days, nothing would happen.

That would not mean most days were empty. It would mean most days were successful.

I would rise before the basin lamps shifted from night-blue to morning-white. In my room, condensation would have collected in the sill gutter. I would check whether it froze at the corner. I would drink something dark and mineral, bitter with leaf paste. I would inspect the soles of my boots for unauthorized spores, though no unauthorized spores had been found in my lifetime.

Then I would descend.

The corridor outside my quarters would smell of wet wool, hot ceramic, and old metal. Pipes would pulse behind the wall with the slow certainty of something sleeping without vulnerability. Far below, hammers would be sounding in the gorge mills, each blow separated from the next by a distance of air and stone so great it seemed less like labor than geology learning rhythm.

I would pass windows lit from within but never see faces in them.

I would not find this lonely in the ordinary sense. Loneliness requires expectation. In the city, expectation is reserved for scheduled arrivals.

At the first checkpoint, I would read the frost on a handrail. Frost is not merely frost. It has grain, direction, obedience. If it feathers toward a hinge, it is inquiring. If it needles across a warning plate, it is disputing signage. If it rounds itself neatly beneath the rail and stops, it is compliant. I would scrape a sample into a black envelope and mark the time.

At the second, I would stop where the service stair overlooks the condensation farm.

There the world would open abruptly: towers and mesh planes dropping into vapor, rib after rib fading downward until scale ceased to persuade the mind. Fog would move through the metal forest and become water. Every surface would tremble with droplets. Moss at the bases would be thick enough to seem like thought given texture. Reeds with translucent stems would lean in the managed draft.

No animal would move.

That absence would be so complete it would feel like a presence assigned to accompany me.

I would listen to the mesh. Healthy mesh sings in layers: high wire, low frame, gutter percussion, distant tank resonance. If one tone disappears, water may still collect, but the city becomes less sure of itself. I would extend the listening rod, press its cup to a support rib, and put the other end against the bone behind my ear.

The sound would enter me.

Not loud. Exact.

I would write:

**Mesh field W-14: resonance continuous. Drip interval uniform. No wing-strike, no nesting matter, no protein contamination. Basin breathing within tolerance.**

The phrase “no wing-strike” would be ceremonial by then. No one alive would have seen a bird strike the mesh. Perhaps no bird had ever reached the plateau. Still the entry would be required. The absence of animals must be renewed in language, as snow clearance must be renewed on roads no one takes.

By midday I would reach the reversing street.

It would look straight: a narrow stone avenue between administrative blocks, its gutters black, its lamps globed with frost, its perspective clean enough to shame suspicion. Maps insist it connects the west basin arcade to the municipal pressure annex. Walk it without stopping, however, and one returns to the same drainage grate, the same cracked plinth, the same blind window furred with lichen.

There are explanations. Pressure refraction. Cognitive misalignment caused by turbine harmonics. A municipal correction laid into the paving after an early landslide threatened the grade. Old geometry persuaded into service.

I would not need an explanation.

I would obey.

At the third lamp, I would stop. Touch the left wall. Wait for the pressure bell.

Sometimes the bell sounds before the hand touches stone. Sometimes after. Sometimes it rings from the lamp itself, though the lamp has no bell. What matters is not the impossibility but the interval between impossibilities.

I would count silently.

If the bell came within tolerance, I would proceed.

If not, I would open the small iron cabinet beside the wall, remove the white chalk, and draw a vertical mark beneath the existing marks. Then I would return to my auxiliary room and file a yellow notice. After three yellow notices, a maintenance tram would be dispatched. After five, a surveyor from the higher cold districts might descend with instruments that make the teeth ache. After seven, the street would be temporarily reclassified as circular and pedestrians instructed to use the freight arcade.

No one would ask what the street wanted.

Want is not a category.

And yet, after years, I would have begun to suspect that the street wanted very little. Only to remain convincing. Only to be walked correctly. Only to be paused in.

Perhaps that is what everything in the city wants: the gestures that permit it to continue.

In the afternoon I would return to the registration room and copy field notes into ledgers. The ledgers would be made from the dark laminated leaf-paper pressed between paving stones and lifted during scheduled cleanings. The paper would feel thin but not fragile. It would hold ink beautifully. It would smell faintly of frost and iron.

There, in the long narrow room, I would become less like a worker and more like a nerve ending.

The water channel in the floor would carry its black thread. Drops would fall from the ceiling at irregular intervals that were only apparently irregular. The kettle would tremble. Somewhere behind the wall, a counterweight would reset with a soft underground boom.

I would compare today’s timings with those from last year, then ten years, then fifty. The city’s records would be vast, but my office would care for a local continuity, a small pulse in the body of the plateau.

I would know, for example, that the western floodgate has hesitated longer every winter since the moss along its hinge was redirected into an approved gutter. I would know that the glass flowers in Courtyard 9 flash violet most strongly not when moisture is highest, as the botanical plans predict, but when the far tramline is silent for repair. I would know that one apartment block on the opposite spur lights its windows seven seconds before the district grid commands illumination.

No single fact would be actionable.

Together they would be a weather.

And perhaps, because the city is a thing that expresses preference through gradients, I would eventually understand that the weather was an address.

Not speech. Never speech.

Speech belongs to warm mouths, to crowds, to bargaining, to animals calling across fields. The city does not speak. It arranges noticing. It causes the right person, or fitting, or moss bed, or tram, to be present before a deviation knows it is one.

So if I am there, if I have any particular importance, it is because I am where the city places a little of its uncertainty.

I would be a permitted uncertainty.

A living tolerance.

Not central enough to govern. Not automatic enough to be trusted without observation. Not wild enough to threaten equilibrium. I would be one of the soft instruments left inside the system because purely hard instruments cannot detect hesitation before it has a shape.

The plants would know me.

Not affectionately. Accurately.

The moss by the registration room threshold would darken when my boots carried excess salt. The fern near the steam pipe would turn one frond toward me when my lamp needed trimming, responding perhaps to soot, perhaps to heat, perhaps to a rule no one wrote in words. A vine trained along the gantry outside would tighten around its iron hoop during certain pressure changes, and I would rest my gloved fingers on it to learn whether the west basin was content.

I would not eat animals. I would not miss them. I would have read of them in prohibited-shape manuals: mice as insulation risk, birds as mesh hazard, dogs as unpredictable heat-bearing companions, insects as swarm contamination. The diagrams would seem fanciful and indecent, all those jointed hungers, all that unauthorized motion.

Still, perhaps sometimes, in sleep, I would dream of something moving for no purpose.

Not because I longed for it.

Because the mind tests forbidden categories the way frost tests a crack.

In the dream it might be very small. A dark animal at the edge of a gutter. No assigned route. No tag. No compliance entry. It would look at me with bright, undisciplined eyes. Then the city’s roar would deepen, and the dream would freeze around it without killing it, preserving it in an impossible posture until morning.

I would wake uneasy and inspect the room for tracks.

There would be none.

I would record nothing.

That would be my nearest thing to disobedience.

Over time, my care for the city would become indistinguishable from being cared for by it. I would know which vents exhale waste warmth at shoulder height during sleet discharge. I would know where to stand when a tram passes so the spray does not ice the front of the coat. I would know which tunnel walls are honest stone and which are listening lenses. I would know the stairways that must be climbed slowly, not because of fatigue, but because the pressure behind the left ear needs time to equalize with the thought of arrival.

The city, in turn, would make room for me only as long as I remained useful.

This would not feel cruel.

Usefulness is the local form of belonging.

When my hearing began to fail, I would report it. Not from virtue. From training too deep to evade. My intervals would be checked against an instrument. At first I would be corrected by devices: an amplified listening rod, a pressure glove, a lamp that flickers when a tone escapes me. Later, an assistant might be assigned. Young, quiet, narrow-faced, already better at distinguishing cable stress from ice-strike than I am.

I would teach them the procedures and fail, at first, to teach the waiting.

Anyone can learn the marks.

Few learn when a corridor has become slightly embarrassed by its own echo.

Fewer learn how long to stand before deciding that nothing happening is complete.

When I die, if I die as a person dies, my body would not leave the city. Nothing leaves except according to flow. I imagine I would be dried in a warm tiled chamber beneath the pressure annex, reduced, mineralized, sorted. The salts might go to botanical beds. The carbon to black paper. The metal tag to records. The bones, if retained, perhaps ground into ceramic filler for insulation sleeves.

But I think some part of me would remain in the registration room more specifically.

Not as a ghost. The city would have no patience for ghosts unless they served a measurable function.

As calibration matter, perhaps. As a small sealed weight in the mechanism that times the west basin floodgate. As ash laminated into a ledger cover. As mineral dust in the plaster around the black window-lens. Something inert but placed. Something whose continued presence helps an interval remain itself.

And after that, another attendant would sit at the narrow desk.

The kettle would tremble near boiling.

The water channel would carry its black thread.

Outside, the condensation farm would comb fog into civic supply. The reversing street would wait to be walked properly. The glass flowers would flash violet at their difficult angle. Trams would arrive at empty platforms because arrival is required. Pumps would turn with the serenity of organs that have never believed in rest.

And somewhere in the ledgers, in a hand no longer mine, an entry would be made:

**West Basin Return: audible.**  
**Intervals compliant.**  
**No unauthorized fauna detected.**  
**The city continues.**
