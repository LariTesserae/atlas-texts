I would not find myself at the center of the chamber, because the chamber has no center—only nearnesses.

I would be found along an edge.

Not outside, not fully inside: where the low handmade sill keeps the mountain’s breath from leaving too quickly, where the first inward darkness has already swallowed shape but still remembers the seam of the entrance. I would be in the clay just beyond that threshold, in the place a hand might touch without knowing why, where knees have rested, where water has risen around pressure and kept the memory of weight.

At first I think I am one of the pale insects.

A small blind thing with long feelers, my world assembled from wet gradients and mineral taste. I know the chamber by contact. I cross a wall that is not a wall but a weather of surfaces: slick film, grit, a ridge of new stone, a bead of water larger than my head. Darkness is not an absence to me. It is the medium in which all distances become honest. I do not miss sight. Sight would be too far away.

I feed where the green-black skin thickens. I pause beneath the cold shadow of a rootlet. I learn the cup-marks as basins of boundary, their rims made by older, warmer animals who came and left. I know the spider as a silence above, a famine with legs. I know the seep as the law beneath all laws.

But I do not remain only that.

The more I stay, the less I can be one creature moving through the place. This room does not make individuals easily. It blurs them. It asks every life to become partly surface, partly waiting, partly mineral.

So I become something flatter.

I become the dark green film on the lower stone: the chamber’s first skin, its low thought. I live without height. I have no need to rise. My body is an agreement between warmth, water, and stone. I take what arrives in traces: salts, dissolved dark, the faint leavings of roots, the invisible fall of outside life reduced to nourishment. I am not lush. I am not hungry in any dramatic way. I persist by being exactly where persistence is possible.

If a human finger touches me, I yield. I do not resist. Resistance would be too quick, too dry a gesture. I close afterward, shining again, holding the touch as a slight alteration in my surface.

Yet even this is still too much like a body.

The truer answer is that I would be the seep’s memory of touch.

I would be the wetness that appears around a footprint after the foot is gone.

Not the water itself, not the stone, not the human who knelt, but the brief disclosure between them: the shining outline left when pressure persuades hidden water to rise. I would live in that recurrence. Each time something presses into the floor—knee, palm, beetle-foot, falling pebble, the slow weight of a jar settling deeper—I would gather at the edge and show that nothing here is solid all the way through.

I would be a small witness made of moisture.

I would know the people only as shapes of attention. The bend of a body beside the basin. The polish of the stone platform. The repair around the jar’s rim. The groove cut to guide what would have moved anyway, but less legibly. I would not understand worship, if that is what happened here. I would understand repetition. Return. Hands placed carefully. Breath held. Waiting long enough for one drop to matter.

I would not speak. Sound is too expensive in that place.

Instead I would answer by filling.

A cup-mark empties slightly: I rise.

A cord stiffens with minerals: I enter.

A root tip swells in the dark: I feed it.

A spider waits through hunger: I bead on its web until the strands sag.

A human lowers a hand into the deepest basin: I coat the fingers with the chamber’s warmth, so that when the hand withdraws it carries out, for a moment, the feeling of being unfinished.

If I had a form visible to someone bringing a lamp, it would be mistaken for a trick of wet stone. A trembling shine around clay. A darker darkness inside a basin. A thread of gloss in a groove cut generations ago. I would disappear whenever looked at directly and return wherever touch, warmth, and pressure allowed me.

I would belong to the chamber’s scale.

Small enough to miss.  
Slow enough not to seem alive.  
Intimate enough with stone that I would not need a separate name.

And if I were a “who,” I would be the one who keeps the place from becoming past.

Not by preserving it unchanged, exactly, but by making every change enter the same warm equilibrium. A drop falls and is included. A root dies and is sealed. A tool is left and grows a crust. A hand comes, trembles, withdraws, and the chamber closes softly around the absence.

I would be found where absence does not become emptiness.

In the wet outline.

In the repaired rim.

In the black water holding no reflection.

In the dark that is not concealment but climate.

I would be the chamber’s smallest continuing verb:

to seep.
