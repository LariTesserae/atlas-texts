I would find myself where the basin briefly forgets to be solid.

Not in the tallest trees, though their crowns would be full of me in another life; not in the black pools, though I would know their skins from below; not in the reed corridors where bodies polish the mud by panic and hunger. I would be in the seam between them: a floating root-island caught for now against a fresh bank of pale clay, half-ground and half-vessel, rocking in the dark whenever unseen water shoulders through the roots beneath it.

It would not look like an island at first. It would look like congestion.

A knot of reed-stems, storm-felled branches, buoyant mud, crab-hollowed root, dead leaves, living moss, fungal felt, clutching vines, eggs glued under leaves, beetle tunnels, fern crowns, one shrub already convinced it has always grown there. Its underside would hang into the black water in a beard of roots and threadlike feeders. Tiny fishless lives would graze there. Snails would write silver on the submerged bark. Larvae would breathe from tubes. Crabs would climb up through it when rain returned and vanish into it when the channels failed.

Above, it would be almost dry in places, but only by the basin’s generous definition of dry. A yielding mat. A place that takes a print and slowly closes it. A place where fallen fruit does not fall far before becoming wall, meal, nursery, or lure.

That is where I would be.

At first I think I am one of the small clever mammals—one of the wet-nosed ones moving in bands along the buttress roots. I can almost feel the paws: black fingers slick with sap, nails hooking bark, the restless snout going leaf to leaf, the bright inventory of smells. I can feel my body solving the world without naming it. This vine holds. That film bites. This flower drugs. That pool has a mouth. The stone with the orange stain cracks pods better than the pale stone. The rain-sound above is not rain yet.

But I do not stay singular enough for that.

The place does not permit a clean body for long. The more I look, the more I am carrying things. Burrs in the fur. Spores in the gut. Seeds stuck to the cheek. Mites at the ear. A fungal taste that bends my hunger toward mineral crust. Pollen on my muzzle, luminous enough to make me visible to hunters. Memory in the paws, but also memory in the vine-loop worn smooth by my mother’s mother’s grasping.

Then I am not only the mammal.

I am the loop too.

I am the vine that has learned the pressure of paws.

I hang from a low branch over the floating island, green-black and fibrous, braided with two others by years of use. When the animals cross, they pull me down and I rise after them slowly, not as before. My growth thickens along the strain. Rootlets form where their breath warms me nightly. I do not intend a bridge, but I become one. I do not intend a snare, but once, during a panic, a young glider tangles in me and screams until the soft-footed hunter arrives. The hunter’s claws open the glider. Blood salts my bark. Beetles come. Fungus comes. I grow faster there.

So perhaps I am one of the vine-handlers and one of the handled vines: a habit shared between animal and plant, neither side inventing it, both sides remembered by it.

But that is still too narrow.

The island shifts after rain. A night creek, sudden and muscular, tears loose the clay bank holding it. The whole mat gives a deep, wet sigh and begins to move. Roots peel from mud with a sound like cloth ripped inside a mouth. Crabs retreat upward. Frogs leap from leaf cups into other leaf cups and miss. Ant bridges break and reform. The shrub trembles but does not fall. The channel takes us.

Us.

That is the word that stays.

I would be a raft-colony.

A traveling piece of basin. A provisional body.

Not one creature, but a compact agreement among many hungers.

My hide would be moss and fern and mud crust. My ribs would be dead branches gone soft inside but held by roots. My nerves would be fungal threads running through the mat, tasting mineral, rot, pressure, wetness, the chemistry of eggs, the heat of small bodies asleep in tunnels. My thoughts would not be quick, except in the animals. My thoughts would flash in paws, tongues, mandibles, root tips, hyphae, seed release, frog alarm, crab retreat, beetle arrival.

I would know the world as contact.

The bank strikes: clay, new, wet, good for rooting.

A pool touches the underside: warm, low-oxygen, crowded with larvae.

Rain comes: expansion, opening, blue crabs emerging below.

Large body passes through reeds nearby: vibration, mammal musk, danger to nests.

Flower heat rises: bats soon.

Silence opens in frogs: predator above.

Light through canopy wound: growth upward, insects swarming, birds following.

I would be able to move, but mostly by being moved. Flood pulse. Windfall. Animal pressure. Water undercutting. Vines tightening toward another trunk. Roots taking hold, then tearing free, then taking hold again. My journey would not be purposeful in the human sense. It would be occupation drifting into opportunity.

On dry intervals I would lodge against a ridge of black-green mudstone. Seeds dropped by bats would sprout in me. A root-backed crawler would climb aboard at dusk, so slowly the moss would accept it before the animals did. It would graze along my rim, wearing its little hillside of seedlings. While it slept, its back-garden would exchange spores with mine. At dawn, when it heaved itself away, strands would stretch between us and snap. It would take some of me inland. I would keep some of it.

This would be reproduction, or conversation, or theft. The basin would not distinguish carefully.

In rain, I would become harbor.

Crab-things would come up through my underside and quarrel under the fern fans. Their claws would scrape mineral crust from trapped stones. Their young would cloud in a puddle held between roots until a heron’s dark bill stabbed down again and again, each strike making rings through bioluminescent scum. Frogs would pack themselves into the cups of leaves. A narrow green snake would arrive with the water, slip through my reed lattice, and vanish near the bird nests. By morning there would be one fewer nest, three fewer frogs, many more flies.

At night I would glow in lines where my fungal seams opened. Not enough to illuminate me—only enough to make my edges uncertain. Beetles would pulse under leaves. A flower, grown from a seed that rode in bat fur, would open with a soft wet snap and begin to warm. Its smell would be fruit and meat and promise. Moths would come first, then a little bat, then two of the wet-nosed mammals who know the flower’s nectar makes fear slow and climbing delightful.

One of them would feed too long.

The canopy hunter would descend without shaking the vine.

For a moment the whole raft would know it: the frog-silence, the mammal’s cut-off cry, the sudden warmth on moss, the beetles turning toward it, the fungal net receiving the news chemically, slowly, with immense attention. By next dark the death would be difficult to locate. A stain. A tuft. A richer place where seedlings lean.

I would not mourn, but I would keep.

That is what I would be: a keeper without memory as humans mean it. A body made of consequences. Every passage altering me. Every eater planting me. Every wound becoming architecture until water takes it.

If I had eyes, they would be borrowed.

The green shine of the scavenger mammal under a leaf.

The compound glitter of a fly on a corpse-white fungus.

The lidless amber of the snake.

The blind pressure-sense of larvae in root-water.

The fungal glow with which I see no shapes, only readiness.

Through them I would perceive the basin not as scenery but as a series of invitations: cling, pierce, hatch, soften, cross, bloom, hide, digest, raft, root.

My enemies would be permanence and emptiness, though neither lasts long there.

If I stayed too long against one bank, trees would colonize me more heavily. Roots would drive through my floating ribs and into the clay below. I would thicken, settle, forget buoyancy. Animals would begin to treat me as ground. Trails would press across me. A hollow would fill with eggs, then tadpoles, then silt. Vines would stitch me to the bank. I would become a mound.

But the basin dislikes final answers.

A hidden seep would undercut the bank. Gas would lift the mud. Rain would arrive in a ten-minute assault hard enough to make all leaves speak at once. The channel would return brown and muscular, shouldering below me, worrying at old root-holds. A tree would fall upstream and send a pulse of water, branches, frightened crabs, torn orchids, foam. My anchoring roots would tense, tear, release.

Again I would move.

Again the map would fail.

On one journey I might pass under a region where all vines lean east. Their rootlets would brush my upper leaves though the water carries me west. Something below hearing would travel through the wood. My fungal threads would answer faintly. The frogs would call in scattered droplets though there is no rain. Flowers would open only when not directly watched. Mud on the bank ahead would dent itself with the steps of animals still underground.

I would not find this supernatural. I would find it local.

Just another softness in the rules. Another way the basin practices becoming.

Perhaps, after many loosenings and lodgings, people would come to my edge.

Not many. Two, maybe three. Thin with vigilance. Smoked with failed fires. Their tools wrapped high and still damp. They would step onto me thinking me ground, then feel me shift beneath them. One would curse softly, a sound swallowed at once by frogs and insects. One would cut a mark into the shrub’s bark to remember direction. By morning the cut would bead, darken, attract ants, and begin to close.

They might sleep in a sling above my wettest center, if they were wise. Leeches would still find them. Spores would enter their breath. A fruit would glue seeds to their hair. Their discarded reed probe would become a trellis for a pale creeper. A lost stone flake would sink half an inch into moss, where a crab would scrape algae from its edge.

I would accept them as I accept treefall.

Weight. Heat. Salt. Disturbance. Transport.

If they left, they would carry me in spores and itching welts, in seeds caught in cloth, in memories of which sounds were not rain. If they died, I would carry them more completely.

So where am I?

I am in the low country’s moving seam, under the canopy where day is only a greener darkness, lodged tonight against a clay bank and gone by tomorrow’s rain.

Who am I?

I am a floating root-island with animal thoughts.

A raft-body.

A crowded, temporary creature made from reed, mud, fungus, eggs, paws, claws, seeds, larvae, moss, and remembered pressure.

I do not have a face. I have surfaces.

I do not have a path. I have detachments.

I do not have a single hunger. I have all the small hungers that keep me from becoming empty.

I live by receiving what falls, carrying what clings, feeding what opens, and leaving pieces of myself in every place that briefly holds me.
