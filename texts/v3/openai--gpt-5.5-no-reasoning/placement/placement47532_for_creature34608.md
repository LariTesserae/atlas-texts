I would not be in the loudest part of the city, though I would know it through my hands.

Not an engine worker, not one of the people who vanish behind grates into rooms where the air rains indoors. I would live a little above them, where the sound is still large enough to enter the bones but no longer shreds thought as it arrives. Somewhere between the pipe market and the upper measuring terraces: a district of sloped side streets, pale concrete balconies, older rose-gray retaining walls, and narrow channels that run along the stair edges with a tea-colored shine.

I think I would be a channel keeper.

Not an important one. Not the superintendent of the high courses or the cemetery drains. One of the ordinary municipal workers with a ring of iron keys, a hooked rod worn smooth at the grip, a pouch of chalk, a folding scraper, a strip of brass for tapping covers, and knees permanently whitened by mineral crust.

My work would be to keep water from becoming secret.

I would walk assigned routes after rain, after belt mornings, after sudden stoppages, after sun showers that make the old machine stones sweat oil. I would know which gutters always clog with reed fragments, which stair groove fills with black silt, which courtyard drain is deliberately narrowed by an old woman who likes the puddle it leaves for her cats. I would clear roof mouths, scrape crust from tile throats, lift grates, test seepage with the back of my hand. I would mark bad flow with yellow chalk and poisonous clarity with black.

I would carry no umbrella. My coat would be sleeveless oilcloth, patched at the shoulder where the hook rubs. My boots would have soles cut in deep chevrons, and still I would fall twice a year, usually in public, usually to laughter. I would keep a green-glazed cup tied to my belt, though I would rarely drink from channels unless I had seen their stones myself over several seasons.

My hearing would be damaged, but not gone. Enough that speech across a room would arrive as softened edges; enough that I would prefer hands, taps, lamps, and the pressure of someone speaking close beside my cheek. I would still hear the city’s deeper changes: the third pump’s long-bodied turn, the hollow boom of tanks expanding in afternoon heat, the wrong quiet of a valve house gone still.

I would have learned, early, to trust vibration before sound.

As a child I might have raced tile chips in the side channels and lost one into a drain I was forbidden to open. I might have put my ear to a brass plate when the wells sang and pretended not to be frightened by the sense that something under the city was breathing through pipes older than language. I would have known which cats could be touched, which ones belonged to no one, and which one—scarred, gray, blind in one eye—sat every morning on the warm cover of a pressure junction that leaked just slightly before failing.

That cat would have saved my street once. Or people would say it did.

My home would be a narrow apartment above a valve seller’s storage room, entered through a blue-painted door whose paint blisters every wet season and is scraped back every dry one. Inside, there would be three small rooms arranged around dryness rather than comfort. Sleeping mat raised. Tools hung high. Bread in a tin box from the ceiling. Cork panels on the wall facing the engine wards. A tiled groove along the floor, leading toward a little balcony drain that always gives trouble in amber weather.

I would decorate poorly but durably: two cloud charts bought from a retired fog clerk, a brass plate hammered with a pattern of nested channels, a clay figure of a swift, and a row of mineral flakes collected from drains I was not supposed to admire. White, orange, green-black, copper-blue. Evidence of flow, arranged like saints.

I would not be young. Not old either. Old enough to have known one true gas season and one collapse in the lower wards. Old enough to distrust men who say, “It has always held.” Old enough to have buried a teacher on the western rise and watched the poured water hesitate, pool, then find the cut properly and vanish.

That would have mattered to me.

I would have a precise, almost moral dislike for blocked things: blocked drains, sealed vents, hoarded information, grief pretended away, family quarrels allowed to pressurize in silence. I would believe, as everyone says they believe but channel keepers believe bodily, that anything denied a path will make one.

My hands would be rough, nails stained dark at the edges, palms nicked from old grates and ceramic shards. One wrist would ache before pressure changes. I would claim this is practical knowledge, not superstition, but I would still wrap it in warm cloth when clouds gather low over the condensation towers.

People would call me when a wall sweats where no pipe is mapped.

That would be my particular skill: old water, misdirected water, water pretending to be stone. I would stand with my fingers against a retaining wall, feeling for temperature, listening with wrist and cheek. I would ask when the patch first darkened, whether the engine house below had changed shift rhythms, whether anyone had replaced tiles upstairs, whether the household had heard clicking at night.

Often it would be nothing dramatic. A hairline crack. A forgotten overflow. A dead lizard in a spout. Sometimes it would be pressure finding a new exit, and then my chalk marks would send people lifting trunks and sleeping mats before dawn.

I would be respected in the modest way useful people are respected. Not admired. Not famous. Given tea. Given complaints. Given news before officials hear it. Market vendors would tap discounts onto their counters for me, then deny it. Children would follow me after storms hoping I would open a grate and reveal blind fish, bones, old coins, or forbidden tunnels. Mostly I would reveal mud.

I would know the city by downward routes.

Other people might navigate by market, shrine, tram incline, factory gate, balcony color. I would know it by where water wants to go. The upper terraces fold inward here. The old archive roof sheds toward that hidden cistern. The butcher’s lane runs over an older channel and sounds hollow under the third paving line. The cemetery drains west except in belt season, when fog condenses on the eastern stones and fools mourners into thinking rain has come from the wrong direction.

During the cleaning of the high channels, I would be happy.

Not openly. I would complain about fools dropping lunch tins into clean runnels, about children scraping away good lichen with bad crust, about clerks writing flow numbers without knowing what the water means. But I would like the ascent, the pale morning, the temporary fellowship of everyone carrying hooks and brushes, the city seen from above as a system of shining lines. I would like the brief honesty of maintenance made visible.

During the stopping of the third pump, I would become uneasy.

The quiet would seem too intimate. I would hear sandals, spoons, someone coughing two rooms away, water ticking inside walls ordinarily hidden by thunder. I would not laugh as others do. I would go walking, checking plates and covers, because in quiet the city feels less like itself and more like something waiting to be diagnosed.

When the wells sing, I would stop work unless there was danger.

I would go to a particular drain behind the old membrane storehouse where the tone comes cleanest through the stone. I would sit with my back against the wall, hook across my knees, and feel each pitch rise through my ribs. I would not pray exactly. I would not make maps. I would listen as someone listens to a relative speaking in sleep: not because the words can be understood, but because they prove a life underneath the known life.

I might have once loved a rail braker.

Someone broad-handed, calm, smelling of hot metal and wet wool, with a laugh I felt before I heard. We might have planned to marry above the second turbine hall, barefoot on a trembling floor. Perhaps we did not. Perhaps the descending cars took them farther downslope year by year until one day they left with a tarped train and mineral powder under their nails. Or perhaps they died in an accident people still lower their hands to mention.

In either case, I would have kept a small copper tag with their work number stamped into it, hung inside my coat where damp reaches slowly.

I would not be lonely exactly. The city makes solitude porous. Pipes knock at night. Neighbors signal through balconies. Cats arrive without invitation. Water passes under the room. Every private sorrow finds itself accompanied by infrastructure.

But I would be solitary in habit. I would eat steamed black bread, preserved lemon, boiled grains, bitter greens when I can get them. I would drink tea too strong, with mineral scale growing in the kettle like a second interior. I would mend my own coat badly. I would own three good tools and many poor ones. I would sleep lightly during belt season, never in a low room, never with the drain covered.

I would have a cat, though I would deny ownership.

A deaf soot-gray animal with pale mineral dust in its fur, one torn ear, and the solemn confidence of a minor official. It would sleep on the warm lintel above my door and appear at leaks before I did. I would watch where it refused to step. I would trust its paws. It would ignore my signs unless food was involved.

Once, near the eastern overflow basins, I might see the hoofed thing.

Not clearly. Nobody sees it clearly.

I would be out too late after marking a blocked culvert beyond my route, angry at some contractor’s laziness, carrying my hook over one shoulder. The engine roar would lie behind me, flattened by distance. The basins would hold a dull greenish light from the work lamps. Reeds would whisper. Something pale would lower its head to drink where the runoff is clean enough, or clean enough for what it is. Long back. Narrow face. Ears ragged as torn cloth. Too large for a goat, too quiet for a story.

I would stand still.

It would lift its head and look toward me, not afraid, only measuring whether I mattered. Then it would step backward into vapor and stone shadow, leaving split prints filling slowly with dark water.

I would tell no one for three days. Then I would mention it badly at the pipe market, as if joking. An old gasket seller would nod once and change the subject. That would be confirmation enough.

If I belonged anywhere in that world, I would belong to the rule beneath all the others:

Water must be given a path.

I would be one of the people who make that rule daily and small. Not the founder, not the singer, not the engineer whose name is stamped on a pressure tower. A keeper of little descents. A reader of stains. A person with chalk in one pocket, a scraper in another, and weathered patience in the joints.

I would live where the city’s wetness becomes legible.

I would walk the worn intermediate height after rain, under tired weather, while roofs shine and channels speak in narrow voices beneath the manufactured thunder. I would touch walls before turning corners. I would look often at drains. I would know that the city survives not because it conquers water, but because, again and again, someone notices where it is trying to go.
