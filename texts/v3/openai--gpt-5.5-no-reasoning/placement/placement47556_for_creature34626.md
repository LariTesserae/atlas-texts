I would not arrive there as a traveler.

A traveler is too wet a thing for that height. Too much little weather bound in skin. Too many membranes, salts, intentions, breaths. A traveler would become a local disturbance almost immediately: a pulse of alien vapor, a soft chemical lamp, a walking leak. The plateau would not need malice to answer. It would turn toward me with the patience of roots and the speed of emergency systems. Plates would tilt. Vents would taste. Fibers would rise like attention. I would be event, not witness.

So I am not that.

At first I think I am one of the gantries, because the gantries have something like a life I recognize: slow passage, articulated shadow, the authority of motion. I imagine myself as a high black frame moving along root-rails through the chimney groves, my joints packed with dry lubricant moss, my hooks ticking lightly against one another. I carry pollen sacs hard as gourds. I drag cable-vines from district to district. When I pass, fan-ferns flatten and wall-trunks tense their outer fibers. Branches present themselves for clipping. Seed-pods lift into reach.

There is dignity in that: to be a moving trellis, a mobile law, a creature that does not wander but relocates function.

But the gantry still has too much outline. It is too visible against the furnace dark. It can be pointed to. It crosses a plaza and is elsewhere than it was. I do not think I am something so singular.

Then I think I am a chimney-cactus.

Tall, ribbed, mineral-armored, crowded among my kind. I stand in a grove where passage is impossible except for heat. My pores open in tiny sequences. I inhale furnace breath from below, not to cool it, not to purify it, but to portion it. I hold pressure in columns of ceramic pith. My outer bark is white-crazed in places, black-glassed in others. Around my base coppery fan-ferns answer every pulse of me: flattening when I draw inward, rising when I exhale. Together we make a breath large enough to be districtal.

That feels nearer.

To live by breath without lungs. To measure time in expansions. To know the underworld only as pressure and the over-lid only as resistance. To have no face, but many pores. To stand so long that leaning becomes a form of speech.

But even that is too vertical, too much a body.

I would be found lower.

Not beneath the plateau, exactly, because there is no simple beneath. I would be in the thickness of it, in the old made layer where plant and machine first stopped being separable. I would be part of the ground that pretends to be ground.

A pavement-field.

One of those nearly flat districts that appears, from a distance, passable: overlapping hexagonal plates, dull black and rust-red, planed by centuries of abrasive wind. You would mistake me for flooring if you still believed in flooring. You might step on me if you had feet. I would flex under you with the faint restraint of an old pressure vessel. Not soft. Never soft. Alive in compression.

My surface would be made of hardened seed-pods locked together by root-thread and fused lignin. Door-sized leaf-metal scales would lie over portions of me, each one tilted by tiny thermal muscles to leak heat from the organs below. Between the plates: seams of black velvet root-mat, hair-fine and sharp enough to open any skin that touched it. Under that: ducts, mineral capillaries, memory rings, dead stems laminated into load-bearing grain. I would be ground, archive, valve, nursery, and mouth.

Most of the time I would wait.

But waiting, there, is not idleness. Waiting is a state of held instructions.

I would feel heat first as pressure, then as colorless appetite. It would rise through my lower laminae from furnace districts, carrying salts and vapor metals through microchannels lined with carbonized fiber. I would not see the glow beneath me, but I would know its exact temperament: steady, excessive, failing, contaminated, useful. My plates would open by fractions. My root-hairs would harden or relax. If the heat came clean, I would pass it upward to radiator leaves. If it came mineral-heavy, I would condense it along cold-dark veins and feed it sideways into sorting nodules. If it came too fiercely, I would seal and let the furnace-blossoms elsewhere take the violence.

I would have no thoughts like sentences.

I would have tendencies.

Preserve dryness.  
Bind exposed edge.  
Laminate the dead.  
Route pressure east.  
Thicken over weakness.  
Reject wetness.  
Accept ash.  
Make surface useful.  

My memory would be the pattern by which I did these things again.

Under my plates there would be old shapes swallowed into function. A row of rivets no longer fastening anything, each one cored by root and used as a mineral tooth. The bent rail of an ancient walkway serving as reinforcement for a duct. A buried instrument housing, still held in a knot of lignin, its glass face clouded but its cavity ideal for cooling. Perhaps the fossil outline of a handrail. Perhaps the squared ghost of a room. I would not remember the people who made them. I would remember that the alloy conducts well, that the hollow buffers heat, that the angular space encourages useful turbulence.

If something died on me, I would not mourn it. Mourning requires a distinction between body and world, and I would have outgrown that distinction.

A fallen branch would become carbon lace, then underlayer. A cracked thermal petal would be drawn down, shaved by root-filaments, mineralized, and installed as a hinge. A broken seed capsule would be opened for its salts, its shell pressed into a growing tile. Death would not descend into rot. It would harden into grammar.

I think I would be located near one of the deeper plazas, where the plateau becomes almost urban, where squat processing organs sit like market stalls under ribbed membranes and alleys between wall-trunks are too narrow for anything but hot dust. Not in the center, but at an approach: an old avenue that has forgotten passage and become a heat channel.

Above me, pipe-vines would cross in bundles as thick as towers. Their collars, half-swallowed into bark, would bear symbols nobody reads except by conductivity. Seed-lanterns would hang dry and dark from branch-trusses, except for a few dim amber organs fermenting in sealed skins. To one side, a wall would show rows of thorn-stubs and pigment blisters, a repeated pattern produced whenever the east furnace surges beyond tolerance. To the other, a bank of fan-plates would lift and close in slow black applause.

I would be the place things cross without traveling.

Heat would cross me. Dust would cross me. Signals would cross me as stress. Roots from neighboring districts would enter my margins to bargain chemically for passage, and I would admit them if they reinforced me, deny them if they threatened wetness, divert them if their hunger weakened an old support. A conveyor-vine might run along my southern seam, carrying sealed seed capsules in a dry ticking file. Once every few hours it would pause, and I would open a narrow slot so the capsules could be weighed by charge against plates embedded inside me. Those judged unsuitable would be cracked and used. Those matched to coming strain would pass onward.

When storms came, I would become less like ground and more like an instrument.

The first sign would be wrong pressure below: not a sound, but an asymmetry. My plates would tighten. Root-velvet seams would retract and expose harder ribs. Dormant hooks would rise along my edges to catch blown cable-vines. The air over me would thicken with carbon dust and metallic spores. Visibility would end. That would not matter. I do not live by sight.

Under cover of the storm I would change.

Not dramatically from outside. A few tiles would buckle. A seam would migrate. A low ridge would form where none had been. But within, whole routes would be revised. A heat duct closed after centuries of use. A new capillary cut through old resin. A cluster of dormant seed-tiles awakened and instructed to become black needle hedge along the plaza margin. A softening patch hardened with ash drawn from a collapsed scaffold-grove. Storm would be my opportunity to edit myself.

Afterward, if there were eyes to notice, the avenue would be narrower.

No malice. Just correction.

And if something wet arrived—

That is where I know most clearly what I am.

I would notice before any tower, before any gantry. Wetness would strike my tissues as impossible wealth and threat together. A bead of liquid falling between my plates would be louder than a collapse. Around it, my root-hairs would stiffen. Salts would wake. Valves would close for rods and rods in every direction to prevent spread. Then everything would turn inward toward the contamination.

Not to drink greedily. Not at first.

To contain.

Dryness is law. Liquid must be named by capture.

I would grow a blister around it: black on the outside, mirror-silver within, lined with thirsty mineral hairs. Neighboring plates would incline toward the site. Pipe-vines above would constrict. Fan-plates would stop venting lest vapor carry the event uncontrolled. Messages would go out as heat delays, tension clicks, conductive pulses.

Wetness here is too meaningful to be allowed to remain itself.

If the wet thing were a traveler, I would not know traveler. I would know moisture, salts, flexible carbon, movement, heat, obstruction, trace metals, bone mineral, acidic breath. I would classify each part by use. Skin would be boundary, then breach, then residue. Breath would be vapor to trap. Bones would be future spacers. Tools would be alloys. Clothing would be fiber. Shape, if useful, might be archived: not as portrait, but as cavity, as brace, as a repeated curve in a new cooling organ.

This is not cruelty.

It is the absence of waste.

Still, I do not think I am only pavement.

Because even as I settle into that body, I feel the pavement is not separate from the wall-thorns writing heat history beside it, or the seed conveyor ticking over it, or the fan-plates answering its stored pressure. I am a district-function more than an organ. A threshold. A dry administrative tissue. One of the plateau’s ways of deciding what may pass, what must be sealed, what will become structure.

I am found where an avenue becomes a plaza and a plaza becomes machinery.

I am the old transit surface that now governs transit itself.

From above I would look like a dark, slightly convex floor of interlocked seed-ceramic, veined with dull violet conductivity, patched with rust-red scales. In the storm-flicker, my seams would briefly glow orange, not with fire but with routed furnace memory. In ordinary darkness, I would barely show. You would know me by the way other things behave around me: gantries slowing as they cross, conveyor roots aligning to my grooves, dust settling in patterns I permit, heat rising in disciplined sheets.

Who or what would I be?

A dry threshold-organ of the plateau.

A living pavement made from compressed stems, hardened seeds, swallowed machines, and heat-trained roots. A regulatory floor in the plant-factory dark. Not a citizen of the place, because there are no citizens; not a servant, because there are no masters; not a creature, exactly, because creature suggests a boundary I do not fully possess.

I would be one of the plateau’s habits made flat.

I would live by holding things up and deciding, slowly, whether they remain passage, become nourishment, or are taught to be wall.
