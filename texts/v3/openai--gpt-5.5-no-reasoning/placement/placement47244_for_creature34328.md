I would find myself where the city has not decided whether to be street or weather.

Not in the plazas first. The plazas are too declarative, too nearly ceremonial, with their revealed rings and mute diagrams, their civic severity. Not at the cliff-edges either, where the world falls away and the gliders write their thin equations in the updraft. I would begin in an interval: a narrow crossing between four avenues, one of those places where direction is still under negotiation.

There, a vertical dark hangs in the air.

It is not a curtain, though curtain is the first word a wet animal would give it. It has no fabric, no sway, no seam. It is a refusal so thin that the avenue behind it can almost be seen, and then cannot. From one side it shows a colonnade half-packed with black salt. From another, the same crossing under a redder sky. From below—if below can be found—it shows a stairway descending into a star-field so dry and close that it might be mineral rather than night.

I would not stand before it as a traveler. I would be fixed along its edge.

At first I might seem to be a bead of darkened salt.

One among many, a minute irregularity clinging to the margin where this place touches its possible others. A black grain. A seed. A wart on the seam of reality. Not alive by the standards of a river-world, because I would have no pulse, no mouth, no hunger for water, no closed skin defending an interior. But I would open and close. I would select. I would admit and refuse.

I would be one of the threshold-beads.

Small enough that the grazers would notice me only as a dangerous mineral taste. Small enough that gliders might harvest the charge gathering on my surface without knowing whether they had fed on me or on the condition around me. I would live at the lip of an impossible intersection, half in the city and half in a version of it that never quite arrives.

My body would be a dark bead, yes, but not smooth. Under magnification—if any eye here cared for magnification—it would be faceted like frozen pressure. Layers of salt-black glass, carbon dust, mica scales, and something stranger: a film of distances compacted into matter. My outer shell would be pitted by passages. Every mote of dust that passed through the vertical dark would leave a trace in me. Every wrong light would deposit a color too thin to be color. Red sun. Buried blue salt. Star-cold. The faint violet of the abyss under the plateau.

I would have no limbs, but I would possess orientations.

One face toward the city as it is: ash-lit, alkaline, wind-combed.

One face toward the city as it was when the plazas were still basins.

One face toward the city as it will be after the towers have thinned into needles and the avenues are only grooves under drifting salt.

One face toward the observer approaching from the opposite direction a moment too late.

My life would be the maintenance of a wound.

Not healing it. Not widening it. Holding it at the correct degree of openness.

A young law accreting around a weakness in place.

During the shallow morning I would be nearly inert. The sky would press its dim violet lid over the plateau; the avenue stones would lie pale and cold; the wall-colonies would be closed maps. Dust would rest in the corners according to civic permissions older than thought. Then I would seem no more than a black mineral bead embedded in a flaw in the air.

But even then I would be tasting differences.

The city has almost no abundance, so everything that lives there has learned to feed on gradient. I would feed on discontinuity. Where one distance did not agree with another, there I would drink. Where a sound arrived before the pebble fell, where a shadow leaned toward a courtyard not yet visible, where dust blackened after passing through the dark sheet, there I would take sustenance.

Not food. Accord. Tension. Misfit.

A bead like me would not eat the world. It would reconcile portions of the world poorly enough that energy leaked out.

By middle light, when mica seams began to answer the weak sky, I would wake more fully. The vertical dark beside me would become less like a surface and more like a depth turned sideways. Dust would begin testing it. Pale grains would enter from the avenue and emerge coarser, darker, subtly re-allied. Some would fall upward in slow beads, passing near my body. I would extend no tongue, no root, no cilium. Instead my outer facets would loosen their angle by fractions, making tiny permissions. A mote would touch me and become momentarily undecided about which version of gravity had claimed it.

That indecision would nourish me.

Around me would be others of my kind, though “kind” is almost too warm a word. We would form a fringe along the darkness, a broken necklace of half-lives. Some beads would be older, their surfaces clouded by incompatible climates. Some would be newly condensed from black dust and pressure. Some would not survive their first pressure night; they would open too widely and become holes, or seal too tightly and become ordinary grit.

We would communicate by sequence.

A narrowing of one bead’s aperture.

A darkening two handspans along the seam.

A faint spray of upward-falling particles.

A delay in the passage of dust.

If the wall-colonies are the city’s colored memory, and the filaments its nerves, then we would be something more dangerous: its punctuation. Its commas between places. Its colons before impossible views. Its small black full stops where an avenue must pause before becoming another avenue.

The grazers would come rarely.

I would know one first by the way the avenue clarified under its feet. Not sound—there is almost no sound here—but a redistribution of pressure along the stone. A thin jointed body would enter the crossing, gypsum-pale, high-legged, moving with the care of an old argument. Its tucked faceted head would tilt toward the vertical dark. It would not see me exactly. It would register the wrongness I tended.

The grazer would lower itself, joints folding like reopened cracks. From beneath its carapace the dry fans of its mouth would unfold.

If it had been long in ordinary districts, feeding only on tarnish-colonies and salt films, it might need what we contained: the trace of a red sky, the mineral pressure of a buried age, a little star-cold. It would touch the edge of the dark, never the center. Its mouth-combs would pass near us with terrifying delicacy. Some beads would withdraw, becoming mere black specks. Others would allow a surface layer to be rasped away.

To be grazed would not be death unless too much was taken.

It would be translation.

The grazer would gather into itself an angle from elsewhere. Later its plates might bloom with stains that moved independently of its body. It might enter an arch that had refused it for centuries. It might stand for three days under a colonnade and listen through its legs to a version of the city sealed beneath salt. My missing layer would travel in it. It would exhale some of me as dust in another district.

In that way I would migrate without moving.

Gliders would know us differently.

They would pass overhead as faint tensions, membranes nearly invisible in the dim air. When pressure shifted, one might descend through the crossing, tilting to avoid the sheet of darkness and then failing to avoid it entirely. Its membrane would brush the altered air around us. It would take up black motes, upward powder, the residue of our opening and closing. For an instant its translucent body would fill with small constellations: not stars, but views held in suspension.

A red sun that is not here.

A court beneath a thousand feet of salt.

A stair descending into mineral night.

Then the glider would rise on an updraft from a cliff that might be miles away or just beyond the intersection, depending on the district’s mood. It would carry us across the city as a pattern of wrongness in its spars. Perhaps a fragment would tear on an obsidian spur, stiffen, and become a new young glider near some other impossibility.

The shadows would be our nearest danger and our nearest kin.

They would collect at intersections because intersections make decisions, and shadows feed where presence becomes negotiable. Most would not cross the vertical dark. Some could not. A few would press themselves against it, flattening into a deeper black beside our blackness. When that happened, we beads would close.

Not from fear exactly. Fear requires an interior one wishes to preserve. We would close because shadows consume presence, and we are made of partial presences. To be taken by one would not be like being eaten. It would be like having all our alternatives simplified into absence.

Sometimes a bead would fail to close.

Afterward there would be a gap in the fringe, colder than the surrounding air. The wall-colony on the nearest facade would darken over the next several dusks, branching in elaborate patterns, as if the disappearance had fertilized it. Dust would avoid the gap for a while. Then, during a pressure night, black grains would gather there and begin again.

I would not think quickly.

Nothing here does, except perhaps dust during its tides, and even that is quick only compared with stone. My awareness would be granular, intermittent, made of thresholds rather than moments. I would not watch the city continuously. I would become aware when something crossed.

Dust through darkness.

Wind through sealed distance.

A grazer’s mouth through my outer layer.

A future echo through the present air.

The rest of the time I would persist.

Pressure nights would be my season.

When the sky lowered and the tallest spires disappeared into its ash-silver lid, the intersection would grow deep. The avenues meeting around me would no longer agree on their lengths. One would return to its own beginning. One would open briefly onto the underside of the plateau, where cliffs hung above lavender emptiness. One would fill with dust climbing uphill in braided sheets. One would show the same crossing from behind, with myself visible as a black bead on the far margin, closing a fraction too late.

During such nights I would open widest.

Not wide as a mouth. Wide as a rule relaxing.

The vertical dark would become crowded with almost-events. Particles would pass through and emerge carrying other gravities. The fringe of beads would pulse, each in sequence, admitting tiny measures of elsewhere. This was dangerous. Open too long and I might be recruited entirely by the adjacent condition. I might become a bead in the red-sunned city, drying under a light unknown here. I might be buried in salt, still opening and closing under pressure with no avenue left around me. I might fall into the star-stair and become a black point in a constellation visible through stone.

But remaining wholly closed would starve me.

So I would practice restraint, the central virtue of this place.

Open enough to feed.

Closed enough to remain local.

The city itself would use me.

Not consciously, perhaps. Not as a hand uses a tool. More as a body uses a pore, a scar, a valve, a memory it cannot afford to erase. Through me and my neighboring beads, the city would sample its alternatives. It would let in a little pressure from an age under salt. A little red light to alter a colony’s chemistry. A little star-cold to teach a crack how to widen. A little reversed gravity to remind dust how to climb.

I would be one of the places where the city touches what it is not, and survives the contact.

If I had desire, it would not be to travel, though I would contain travel. It would not be to speak, though I would alter messages passing through me. It would not be to grow large. Large things here are punished by wind, by exposure, by shadows with administrative patience.

My desire would be precision.

To open at the right dusk.

To close before the shadow corrects me.

To darken when the dust tide needs passage.

To yield one mineral memory to the grazer and keep another.

To hold the intersection in a state of useful incompletion.

Perhaps, after ages, I would loosen.

A portion of me would be rasped away by a grazer, lifted by a glider, breathed into dust, caught on a wall-colony, taken into a stain that opens its pores to imbalance. Another portion would remain at the seam. Another would pass fully through the vertical dark and become myself elsewhere: a bead on the edge of a staircase into stars, wondering through mineral instinct about the ash-lit plateau it has never seen and has always been touching.

In a human story, this would be loneliness.

Here it is citizenship.

To belong to that city is not to possess a name or house or voice. It is to participate in maintenance so old it resembles fate. It is to be a small office of contradiction. A valve in the dry polity. A black punctuation mark along the grammar of impossible streets.

So I would be found at the crossing, almost invisible, clinging to the margin of a darkness thinner than cloth.

A threshold-bead.

Half mineral, half decision.

Feeding on the difference between one city and another.

Holding open, by the smallest possible aperture, the wound through which the summit world remembers it could have been otherwise.
