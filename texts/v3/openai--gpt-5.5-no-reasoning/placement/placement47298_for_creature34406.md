I would find myself where the Warm Levels stop pretending to be rooms.

Not in the central pump chamber, though I would pass through it often enough to learn its weather: the yellow safety lines softened under old boot grime, the lockers sweating at their hinges, the kettle furred white around its mouth, the radio speaking in fragments beneath the fan. Not in the fruit cages, though I would know them by smell from three turns away—the brief gold rot of peaches, the feverish sweetness of bananas, tomatoes collapsing in their skins while flies write cursive above them. Not in the frog pools either, though I would go still when their calls began, because there is something in a frog’s voice underground that makes every pipe seem older than the city.

I would be farther out, near the boundary.

Where the service tunnel narrows and the brick gives up into cut stone.

Where the lamps are too far apart to make a corridor and instead make islands: one cone of amber around a valve wheel, then twenty paces of heat and dark, then another light trembling over a wet bend in the wall. Where roots hang down in black cords through cracks in the ceiling, astonishing every time, as if the upper world has lowered nerves into the underworld to feel what is happening there.

That is where I would be.

Not quite hidden. Not quite official.

I think I would be one of the things that learns a place by returning to it after everyone else has passed through.

A small maintenance marker, maybe, at first. A tag. A habit. A chalk sign renewed so many times it begins to seem less written than grown.

But no—that is too still.

I would have to move. Slowly, but I would move.

I would be a Warm Levels listener.

Human, probably. Or human enough that the crews know my name and argue about whether I am on the books.

I would be the one sent down when something is not yet broken enough to become an emergency. When a pump tone has changed but the gauges still read acceptably. When a smell appears near the market vents and then is gone. When a door that should be dry has condensation on its lock. When rats begin avoiding a passage they have used for years.

No tablet would help much with the work I do. I would carry one because the city requires records, boxes ticked, time stamps, categories of attention translated into maintenance language. But my real instruments would be older: a lamp, a rag, a folding rule gone cloudy at the hinge, a pencil sealed in tape, a key ring heavy enough to pull one shoulder lower than the other. My gloves would never fully dry. My boots would have salt stains at the seams. My face would shine within minutes of descent.

I would walk by listening.

Not romantically. Not mystically. Practically.

There is a difference between the sound of water falling from a pipe mouth and water falling through a new crack. There is a difference between a rat traveling confidently inside a wall and a rat trapped where it does not understand the route. There is a difference between a live cable’s dry heat and a steam line’s wet heat, between warm concrete and dangerous concrete, between a fan dragging air and a fan laboring against a blockage made of plastic bags, pigeon feathers, and old festival ribbon.

I would know these differences because the Warm Levels would have trained me.

At first, I would have hated the heat. Everyone does. It is too intimate. It gets under collars, behind knees, into the waistband, into the fold where glove meets wrist. It makes paper soft and tempers short. It gives every smell a body. Hot dust. Hot metal. Hot mold. Hot grease. Hot feathers. Hot rainwater fresh from the streets above, carrying brake grit and flower petals and cigarette ash down into the grates.

Then, over time, I would stop noticing heat as discomfort and begin reading it as grammar.

A cool draft where there should be none: open hatch, broken seal, illegal access.

A warm patch in a wall: pipe too close, line active, don’t drill.

Sudden dry air: fan clearing properly.

Wet, sour heat: blocked sump.

The district would become legible not as a map but as a temperament.

I would keep mostly to the boundary passages because that is where changes announce themselves first. The central chambers are loud with known machinery. Their failures are dramatic, scheduled, budgeted, argued over in meetings. But the edge is subtler. The edge is where the city’s engineered underside meets clay, basalt, old water, pressure, roots. There the Warm Levels are still negotiating their own shape.

I would know the wall that has sounded hollow for three years.

I would know the mineral bloom shaped like a hand beside stairwell C-12.

I would know a place where the rats cross overhead on a cable tray and drop crumbs of insulation like gray snow.

I would know the frog that sits farther from the market pool than any frog has business sitting, a lone advance scout in a puddle beneath a red valve.

I would know which pigeon skeleton has been slowly disassembled by insects under the transformer cage.

I would know the root that appeared one summer as a single black thread and is now three threads, then five, then a little tassel, drinking from the seam where the concrete never cured properly.

The crews would call me when they did not yet know what they were calling me for.

“Go have a listen.”

That would be the phrase.

Not inspect. Not repair. Listen.

I would go down with the after-rain sound still rising through the access stair: pumps waking one after another, each with its own chest voice. Brown water moving behind grates. Frogs answering from beneath the market. Pigeons restless in the shafts because pressure has changed. Rats bolder after rain, because rain brings food.

I would pause at the bottom before unlocking the second gate.

That pause would be my superstition and my method.

The Warm Levels dislike haste. Or rather, haste makes a person stupid down there. The district itself is never hurried. Water accumulates. Rust advances. Mold blooms. Routes are made by repetition, not decision. Even emergencies have usually been arriving for years.

So I would stand still and let the place report itself.

Drip interval.

Fan drag.

Pump rhythm.

Insect weather around the nearest lamp.

A knock from expanding metal.

The faint scrape inside a wall.

My own breath loud in my nose.

Then I would enter.

I would not be fearless. Nobody sensible is fearless in the Warm Levels. There are pits in black water. Lines that bite. Steam that can take the skin from a hand. Gas pockets with no interest in heroism. Slick stairs. Sudden birds. Loose grilles. Human beings where they should not be.

But I would be calm.

Calm is different.

The cats would know me. Not love me. Cats in the Warm Levels do not love in the decorative way surface people hope for. But they would classify me as useful, or at least permissible. Orange Boy might appear near the side stairs, huge-headed and scarred, blinking grease-colored eyes. Madam would sit on a switch cabinet and watch me sign a work sheet, tail wrapped over her paws like a question mark. A feral black one with one white whisker would follow at a distance through three chambers, pretending not to.

I would feed none of them openly if supervisors were near. I would not need to. Everyone feeds them. Even the ones who say they don’t.

The rats would know me better.

They know everyone better.

To them I would be a heat-shape, boot rhythm, salt smell, lamp-glare, bag-risk, crumb-possibility. I would learn never to block their routes. I would step aside in certain narrow places not out of kindness but out of experience. A blocked rat path becomes a small riot. A small riot near live equipment becomes paperwork. Paperwork becomes meetings. Meetings become budget language. Better to stand aside and let the true citizens pass.

Sometimes I would crouch and look at their tracks in silt: five-toed signatures, tail lines, the soft drag where bellies skimmed damp dust. Their paths would tell me what the official plans missed. A new run under a warped door might mean warmth behind it. A sudden absence might mean water, poison, construction above, a cat den, a loose current making the air wrong.

The city would pay me to maintain infrastructure.

The rats would teach me what infrastructure is.

Not pipes only. Not pumps. Not numbered doors. Infrastructure is anything life uses repeatedly until the use becomes expectation. A plank over a trench. A crack under a threshold. A cable warm enough for insects. A stairwell where a mechanic smokes. A ledge where pigeons nest. A fruit cage that ripens and rots on schedule. A puddle that becomes a frog nursery every third rain.

I would begin, after years, to distrust clean drawings.

The drawings would show systems.

The Warm Levels would show consequences.

There would be a room I favored, though I would never say so. A narrow service chamber beyond the last regular inspection point, where the wall is raw basalt on one side and old brick on the other, as if two versions of the city met and failed to agree. A pipe runs through it at shoulder height, wrapped in stained insulation, sweating steadily. Beneath it is a concrete island dry enough to sit on if one does not mind the warmth coming up through the knees.

In that room there is a fern.

Only one.

It grows where seepage touches grit below a ventilation shaft too narrow for a person, and daylight falls through it in a thin, dusty column for perhaps twenty minutes around noon. The fern is not lush. It is not symbolic in any grand way. Three fronds most of the year. Four in wet seasons. Brown at the tips. Powdered with city dust. Sometimes chewed by insects. Once nearly killed by an overzealous repair that redirected seepage for six months.

I would have noticed when it came back.

I would not touch it.

That would be important.

My affection for the fern would take the form of not interfering.

People ruin small survivals by celebrating them too heavily. They make signs, install barriers, bring attention. Attention brings cleaning. Cleaning brings bleach, scraping, policy. Better to let the fern remain an accident.

Perhaps that is what I would be, finally: not a guardian exactly, but a person skilled at allowing certain accidents to continue.

I would know when to file a report and when to wait one more week.

I would know which unofficial bracket is dangerous and which one is holding the district together.

I would know that the mechanic’s illegal line is overloaded and must be corrected before it burns, but also that shutting him down abruptly will send six delivery carts back to the streets broken, and then people above will improvise worse repairs elsewhere.

I would know the fruit vendors are attracting rats, but also that the heat below is part of the market’s economy now, unacknowledged and necessary.

I would know that a shrine of lost objects on a platform does no harm, and that removing it would disturb the workers more than anyone would admit.

I would know the difference between neglect and tolerance.

The Warm Levels are full of that distinction.

Neglect lets a handrail rot through beside black water.

Tolerance lets moss remain on brick where it is hurting nothing.

Neglect ignores a gas smell.

Tolerance leaves a cat sleeping on a warm cabinet, provided the vents are clear.

Neglect allows unofficial wiring to become fire.

Tolerance accepts that workers mark doors with chalk because the stenciled numbers peeled off years ago and nobody has approved replacements.

A good listener would need that judgment.

I imagine I would become marked by the place in small unglamorous ways. A persistent cough in winter from mold and mineral dust. Half-moon scars on the knuckles from bolts and panels. A habit of sniffing before entering basements. A dislike of perfectly silent buildings. An ability to sleep through fan noise but wake instantly when a refrigerator changes pitch. Pockets full of zip ties, dead batteries, folded permits, pencil stubs, keys whose doors no one remembers.

Aboveground I would seem distracted.

Friends, if I had them, would complain that I notice drains more than shopfronts. That I pause over pavement grates when rain begins. That I can identify the market quarter blindfolded by what comes up through the vents: fish scales, coriander stems, wet cardboard, diesel, old fruit, hot metal. That I say “listen” too often.

They would ask what it is like down there.

I would say: warm.

Then, if they waited, I would say: inhabited.

Not haunted. Not beautiful. Not exactly dirty, though it is dirty. Not abandoned, though many rooms are empty. Inhabited.

By rats and pigeons and cats and frogs, yes. By roaches and silverfish and mosquitoes and spiders. By mold and algae. By pipes and pumps and cables. By workers and mechanics and vendors and stored chairs and lost gloves and a plastic horse washed down from somewhere above.

But also by continuance.

That would be the thing I could never explain without sounding foolish.

Down there, everything continues more visibly than above. Above, the city pretends events happen cleanly: a shop opens, a bus stops, rain falls, a fruit crate arrives, heat comes from a radiator, waste disappears. Below, nothing disappears. It changes custody. Bread becomes rat. Rain becomes sump. Heat becomes insect. Fruit becomes rot becomes fly. Paper becomes silverfish dust. Paint becomes flakes in water. A child’s toy becomes an object in a shrine. A tree becomes roots in a ceiling crack. Human intention becomes bracket, cable, stain, habit.

The Warm Levels would teach me that a city is not made only of construction but of digestion.

So who or what would I be?

I would be the person with the lamp at the edge of the made place.

The one standing ankle-deep in warm seepage, head tilted, listening to a change behind the wall.

The one the rats route around.

The one the cats permit.

The one who knows that if the pigeons lift all at once, you wait.

The one who can hear rain beginning above before any surface pedestrian opens an umbrella.

The one who has a key to doors no one visits and no authority over the things that matter most.

Half worker, half witness. A maintenance animal. A municipal scavenger of signs.

Not master of the Warm Levels. Nothing is master there.

Only another inhabitant of surplus heat and damp time, trained by repetition, made patient by machinery, learning season after season how the city lives underneath itself.
