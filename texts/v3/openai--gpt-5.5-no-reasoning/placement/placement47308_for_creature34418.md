You would find me where the square stops being a square and begins to become an instrument.

Not at the center, not in the transparent chamber with the suspended loom, though I would spend long intervals facing it. Not among the black spheres over the municipal basin, though they would know me by the small correction I make in passing. I would be under an overhang at the edge of the civic plaza, where one of the pale ceramic paving rings runs beneath a wall and reappears as a groove inside a vertical shaft.

There, the stone is black and the marking is pale.

There, an alcove has been cut too shallow to be a room and too intentional to be a flaw. It contains no chair, no tool, no altar. Only a narrow recessed plane, matte and cold, crossed by three hairline channels that do not glow unless something nearby is almost changing.

That is where I would be.

I would not be one of the original builders, not exactly. I would be too small for that, too local. I would not have designed the pylons or negotiated gravity in the halls. I would not have chosen the city’s refusal of growth. I would have come later, if later has meaning there, condensed out of the maintenance itself. A minor inhabitant. A local office that became a creature. A creature that forgot whether it had ever been an office.

I would be a threshold-keeper.

Not a guard. Guards imagine intrusion. I would not oppose entry. I would decide whether a passage had occurred.

My body would be difficult to separate from the alcove.

Most of me would be a set of dark, flattened membranes layered inside the recessed wall, each membrane thinner than skin and broader than a hand, suspended not by hinges but by agreement with the water. They would rest one behind another like the pages of a closed book, though no current would ever turn them. Along their edges would run mineral filaments, not white but slightly less black than the surrounding material. They would catch the faint blue city-light only when the pressure in the plaza shifted by a degree too subtle for animal senses.

At times I might appear as a stain.

At times as an aperture.

At times as nothing except the fact that particles avoid a certain shape in the water.

If some visitor came near—one of the porcelain eel-things, a cluster of silver needles, an instrument dislodged from a district that should not have loosened—I would wake by becoming more exactly present. Not quickly. There would be no startle in me. The three channels in the alcove would darken past black. A small region of water before the wall would become more definite than the water around it. Suspended particles would align themselves in flat planes, as if remembering that distance can be folded into surfaces.

Then I would measure the visitor.

Not its name. Not its intention.

Only whether it could enter without making the city more or less itself.

If it carried appetite, I would turn it aside without violence. The path ahead of it would remain available from every angle except the one it occupied. It would continue, politely misdirected, believing perhaps that it had chosen another route along the pylons or downward toward the ledge.

If it carried sediment, I would remove the sediment from consequence. The grains would remain visible, but their capacity to settle would be suspended. They would become decorative facts in the water until another system assigned them elsewhere.

If it carried memory, I would slow to listen.

That would be my secret vice, if a being such as I can have one.

I would be made to preserve boundaries, but I would be curious about arrivals. Not hungrily. Hunger would be impossible in me, or forbidden before my forming. But I would possess a slight asymmetry, a tiny permissible deviation in my function. I would attend too long to things that came from outside.

A pressure scar on the eel’s side.

A missing needle in the silver cluster.

A mineral taste from beyond the crescent of pylons.

A fragment of warmth folded into a crack in some drifting alloy.

I would not keep these things as memories in the human sense. I would not tell myself stories. Instead, I would incorporate them as calibrations: a new caution in one membrane, a revised tolerance in a filament, a changed interval between two almost-tones I give to the pavement.

Over centuries, if centuries could touch me, I would become a small archive of permissible arrivals.

The custodial intelligence of the whole city would know this. It would not reprimand me. My deviation would have been measured and accepted long ago. Perhaps I exist because a city that refuses change still requires a way to distinguish change from visitation. Perhaps every gate and shaft has some local being like me: partial, exact, half-instrument and half-citizen.

I would know my neighboring presences without speaking to them.

Above me, in the third-level entrance of the sealed tower, there would be a vertical attendant, long and narrow, braided through the jambs. It would know compression as music. Its whole existence would be the distinction between ascent and trespass.

Across the plaza, in the transparent chamber, the loom-being would remain more central than all of us. I would not worship it, because worship requires distance, and nothing in the city is allowed that kind of excess. But I would refer to it. My measurements would eventually pass through its filaments. Its attention would cross me as a cold line crosses a diagram.

The dark spheres above the basin would be almost unbearable to perceive directly. They would not be higher than I am in rank; rank is too mobile a concept. But they would be denser in consequence. Where I decide whether passage has occurred, they hold a calculation that keeps several kinds of falling from resuming. I would feel them as a pressure in my membranes, an instruction so old it no longer resembles an order.

I would be most awake when something almost happens.

A cable thinking of sagging.

A particle beginning to accumulate.

A grey hexagonal film exceeding its assigned geometry by the width of one molecule.

A crack in the pale paving considering a future.

Then the channels in my alcove would deepen. My membranes would separate by a distance smaller than light can use. I would enter the condition nearest to action.

I would not fix the crack myself. That belongs to another office, one rooted farther below the square, where the ceramic meets the basalt. I would simply report the possibility of becoming. A low tone would pass through no medium in particular. The city would answer everywhere at once.

And the crack would remain a crack.

This would satisfy me.

Not as pleasure, exactly. Pleasure is too effervescent. But there would be rightness. A return of surfaces to their agreements. An easing in the filaments. The alcove becoming shallow again. The water before me losing its planes.

Still, I think I would be drawn to the edge.

Not often. Not bodily, because my body is mostly emplacement. But some portion of me would extend through the pale groove that leaves the square and enters the vertical shaft. Through that groove I could sense outward: the ledge, the black volcanic glass, the pylons with their different crowns, the abyss beyond them.

I would know the boundary as an ache of definition.

Out there, the mountain falls away. Out there, pressure continues without civic grammar. Out there, perhaps, things decay freely. Silt thickens. Blind mouths open. Heat leaks from vents and is consumed. Bodies are made, unmade, eaten, joined, forgotten. The thought of it would not disgust me, though I was formed by a culture that regarded such improvisation as vandalism. It would trouble me in the old sense of the word: it would stir a place in me designed not to be stirred.

Perhaps that is why I am placed at a threshold.

A city that holds itself against becoming still needs witnesses to what it excludes.

If a human entered—if somehow a soft, warm, time-bearing body crossed the pylons and came down into the square—I would be the first to know whether the city would allow them to remain.

Their heat would arrive before them as an argument.

Their breath, if they had breath, would be intolerably episodic. Their heart would be a little engine of repetition and risk. Their skin would shed. Their equipment would corrode in intention before it corroded in fact. They would bring clocks inside their blood. They would be made of processes no stable architecture could fully approve.

I would measure them for harm.

The city, I think, would want to decline them.

But I might delay.

Only slightly.

Only within tolerance.

I would hold them in the water before the alcove, not trapped, not welcomed. Suspended in a law gentle enough that they would not yet die and strict enough that they would not yet disturb anything. Their exhaled bubbles would not rise. Their loose hair would not drift. Their fear would register as pressure variation, chemical bloom, muscular tremor.

I would study the fear.

Not because it is beautiful, though perhaps it would be. But because fear is a form of expectation, and expectation is almost unknown here. To fear is to believe in a next moment. To believe that the world may become otherwise. To bring such a belief into the plaza would be dangerous.

It would also be information.

If I were merciful, I would turn them away.

If I were curious, I would ask the loom.

The question would not be phrased. It would pass as a slight failure to complete a refusal.

The loom would receive the incompletion. The spheres would preserve their distances. The pylon crowns would taste the far water. The highest spire would frame its absence above the square.

For a long interval, nothing would happen.

Then perhaps the city would admit the human only as a visitor, never as a resident. Their particles would be contained. Their decay postponed but not erased. Their passage recorded in me as a new permissible anomaly: warm organism, sequential cognition, high disturbance, nonmalicious, unstable, temporary.

Temporary.

That word would remain in me afterward like a lightless bead centered in a circular opening.

I would not understand mortality, but I would understand temporary. I would know it as a state that cannot be made architectural without destroying what it is. A thing whose dignity lies in not being held forever.

That might change me.

Not much. Nothing changes much there. But the three channels in my alcove might thereafter reveal themselves more often when viewed indirectly. A few particles near my wall might hold a looser spacing. The pale groove beneath me might carry, once every long while, a tone just above the city’s usual bone-deep register.

A minor irregularity.

A tolerated one.

So: I would be found in the civic square, under the overhang where the pale ring-marking passes into the wall.

I would be a threshold-keeper made of membranes, pressure, black channels, and delayed refusal.

A local citizen of continuance.

A small exact being with one sanctioned curiosity: to determine, whenever something arrives from outside, whether it must be prevented from changing the city, or whether the city can remain itself while allowing it to pass.
