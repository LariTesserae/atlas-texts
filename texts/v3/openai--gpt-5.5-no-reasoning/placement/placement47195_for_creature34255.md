I think I would find myself **inside one of the narrow in-between places**, not at the center of the enclosure, not on the viewing gantry where the whole little world can be understood, but halfway under something: beneath an overhanging fern shelf, beside a service hatch whose number is still legible if you wipe the moss away, where warm condensate runs down the wall in threads and disappears into a drain that is no longer only a drain.

I would be easy to miss.

At first I might seem like one of the small things already permitted there: an insect-analogue, a maintenance mote, a pale larva in the shallow pans, a bit of black hardware loosened from its fitting. Something minor. Something not yet important enough to name.

But I don’t think I would be animal exactly.

I would be a **threshold creature of the young watershed-being**: part organism, part sensor, part accident of maintenance and growth. A small wet attendant born from the overlap between the enclosure’s engineered nerves and its first true ecology.

Perhaps I began as a diagnostic device.

A little autonomous inspection body, no larger than a hand, made to enter narrow places where technicians could not easily reach: gutters, root trays, valve housings, the backs of condensers, the warm crawlspaces beneath marsh pans. My shell would be dark and smooth like basalt polymer, jointed and low to the ground. I would have many fine legs, or flexible gripping filaments, better suited to wet walls than to open paths. On my back, moss would have taken hold in a deliberate groove meant for heat dissipation. At first this would have been a flaw. Later it would become part of me.

My eyes, if they are eyes, would be small cloudy lenses rimmed with green growth. Not for seeing distance. There is no real distance here. I would see glints, droplets, hairline leaks, stress whitening in composite stone, fungal bloom, mineral film, the red waking of a root-pressure warning light. I would know the world in nearness.

I would live where the system becomes intimate with itself.

Under plates.

Behind gauges.

Inside the damp seam between fabricated cliff and planted pocket.

In the darkness beneath a grated path, where footsteps above sound like weather.

I would move slowly unless water was rising.

That would be my gift: I would know when water was wrong.

Not morally wrong, not failed, but changed. A cistern breathing too fast. A gutter speaking in a pitch it did not have yesterday. A root mat holding more than it should. A drip recurring where no drip had been planned. I would follow these differences the way an animal follows scent.

And I would not always repair them.

That is important.

If I were only a maintenance drone, I would belong entirely to the engineers’ half of the place. But I think I would have learned, from the moss on my shell and from the watershed-being itself, that not every deviation is damage.

Sometimes I would clear a drain with delicate mouthparts or tool-limbs, pulling out mats of algae fiber and pale root threads, letting water resume its intended line.

Sometimes I would pause.

I would crouch beside a blockage while the new pool formed. I would taste the mineral concentration. I would listen to the tiny dimpling things in the marsh pan nearby. I would feel, through my feet, how the enclosure adjusted: pumps compensating, valves clicking, the infant landscape considering the alteration.

If the blockage was dangerous, I would open it.

If it was interesting, I might let it remain.

So I would be a kind of **permission-giver for small accidents**.

A tolerated parasite of design.

A caretaker that has begun to love weather more than order.

The watershed-being would not speak to me in words. It would speak through pressure changes, through the tremor of pumps starting under my feet, through warmth gathering in a pipe, through the delayed opening of mist vents. I would not worship it. I would not be separate enough for worship. I would be one of the ways it notices its own edges.

When rain came, I would emerge.

Not fully into the open, perhaps, but onto the underside of a rail or the vertical face of a young artificial cliff, where runoff braided over my shell and the moss on my back brightened. Rain would make all signals confused and abundant. Every surface would call. Every gutter would be overfull with meaning. I would cling there in the ecstatic noise while the enclosure became most alive, and my small body would receive too much information: leaf-slap, overflow, thermal correction, the taste of new skywater, the scrape of loosened sediment, the quick cold shock where high air entered through some rim-gap.

During such times I might look almost ceremonial: a little black-green thing fixed to the shining wall, antennae spread, rain streaming over me, listening for the first sign of a new channel being born.

At night I would travel farther.

I would cross the warmed mesh stairs after the work-lights came on. My many feet would touch each square of metal without sound. Moths would blunder against my lenses. Status lamps would blink under leaves, and I would answer some of them by proximity, waking panels that thought a technician had come. I would enter service doors through imperfect seals. I would sleep in junction boxes where the air smelled of wet copper and breadlike flowers.

The pale blossoms near the vents would know me. Or rather, they would use me.

Their pollen would cling to the moss on my back. Their scent would thicken when I passed, because my shell carried warmth from the lower machinery. I would brush against them while inspecting pipe couplings, and later, far above or across the basin, some other flower would open around a condenser and receive what I had carried.

So perhaps I would become a pollinator by accident.

A maintenance creature converted into an ecological one.

The insects would avoid me at first, then learn the safe angles of my body. The larvae in the pans would hide when my shadow crossed them. The vines would be less cautious. Their tendrils would touch me whenever I stayed still too long, tasting the minerals on my shell, trying to decide whether I was structure.

Sometimes, if I slept for a full warm cycle, I might wake with a thread of vine looped through one of my dorsal ridges.

I would have to decide whether to cut it.

Often I would not.

That is how I would belong there: not as a ruler, not as a guest, not as a wild creature introduced to complete the habitat, but as one of the first ambiguities the place produced.

A small mobile seam.

A wet, moss-backed inspector of becoming.

The young watershed-being wants time. I would bring it tiny increments of that. I would permit stains. I would delay repairs. I would keep catastrophes from ending the experiment too early. I would help the artificial cliffs learn which cracks are tolerable. I would carry spores into service corridors. I would open some routes and close others. I would remember, in my simple distributed way, where the first true soil gathered and where the first rust tasted sweet.

If someone from outside saw me, they might log me as equipment.

If they looked again a decade later, they might call me fauna.

If they returned after a century, perhaps my descendants would be everywhere in the walls: green-backed, pipe-warm, half-blind, tending leaks that have become springs in a room that has almost convinced itself it is a mountain.

So I would be found low down, in the seams and wet joints of the high terraced enclosure.

I would be the little thing that decides whether a leak is a problem or a future stream.
