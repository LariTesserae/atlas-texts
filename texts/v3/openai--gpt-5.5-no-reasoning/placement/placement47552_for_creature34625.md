I think I would not arrive there as something separate from the district.

At first, I would think I was standing in it.

I would find myself in one of the broad streets near the canal, under the sagging insulated cables, where the paving slopes almost imperceptibly toward a row of grates. The light would be flat and adequate. Nothing hidden, nothing mercifully obscured. A door in a pump-house would be sweating around its rivets. Somewhere behind a sealed wall, a belt would slap once every four heartbeats, though I would not yet know whose heart I was comparing it to.

I would notice the absence of plants before I noticed the animals.

Not because the animals were scarce—they would be everywhere—but because the lack of green would feel like a pressure in the mind. No softening. No accidental tenderness. Every crack still a crack, every ledge still a ledge, every wall speaking in mineral and stain. The world would have refused the usual small forgivenesses.

Then the surface would move.

A seam beside my foot would loosen into pale hinged bodies, hundreds of them, slipping over one another without panic. Cable feeders would darken the lines overhead at even intervals. A black disc adhered to a warm pipe would tremble as some hidden machine changed pitch. Along the canal, something long would pass beneath the reflection of a capped smokestack, making the image buckle without breaking.

I would stand there too long, trying to decide where the living ended and the built began.

And slowly, unpleasantly, the question would stop making sense.

I would be drawn toward the central factory, though not by curiosity exactly. More like a filing toward a magnet. The windowless square building would sit at the center of the ash-clay yard, four doors identical, six vents breathing out the low continuous tone. The animals would mass around the perimeter and refuse to cross. They would not seem afraid. Fear has a direction, a desire to move away. This would be older and flatter: orientation without trespass.

I would stand at the curb among them.

For a while I would be a witness.

Then the district would inhale.

The canal would drop. The walls would tighten with sound. Shell-creatures would arrest mid-climb. The cable animals would press themselves flatter to the lines. Mist would prepare itself in the grilles. The low tone would not change, but everything else would briefly reveal itself as arranged around it.

And in that held interval I would understand my body incorrectly.

I would think I had hands.

I would think I had skin.

I would think the chill entering me through the soles was weather.

But I would be one of the small hinge-things in the curb seam, or perhaps a little larger than those: a flat, jointed maintenance animal, pale as wet ceramic, with a shell that caught the light in dull mica layers. I would be narrow enough to live between brick and iron, low enough that the district would be read from underside and edge. I would have no eyes, or only pits for brightness, because sight would be the least honest sense here.

The world would come to me as tremor.

Deep engines through stone. Chain advance through rail. Water-pressure through canal walls. The tone from the six vents as a line inside my plates. Warmth as a shape. Metal as taste. Damp as instruction. Distance as delay between impacts.

I would know the street not by looking down it, but by the sequence of materials under my limbs: ash-clay, brick lip, tar seam, cold iron, sweating ceramic, mineral crust, pooled condensate, the faint sourness where old current leaks through insulation. I would know when a freight cart had passed hours ago by the polish left in vibration. I would know which wall had been recently rinsed and which pipe would bead in seven measures. I would know the safe underside of a railing by the salt-map left by those before me.

I would not have a name.

Names would be too detachable. I would have routes instead.

My route would begin beneath a numbered gate whose plaque had lost its language. It would carry me along the lower brick courses, where the permanent dark stain was thick enough to feed from. I would abrade—not devour, not damage, but refine. My mouth would be a soft file, a ring of tiny scraping edges and wet pads. I would remove the thin accumulations that tried to become something else: salt bloom, grease film, fungal haze, the first invisible proposition of soil.

I would eat beginnings.

That would be my work.

Not assigned, not chosen. Work would be appetite shaped by place.

Where a crack gathered too much softness, I would enter it. Where a door hinge wept black lubricant, I would polish the overflow down to a sheen. Where a ceramic insulator collected mineral fur, I would climb among the cable feeders and rasp it clean without disturbing their bodies. Where condensation ran in a new thread, I would taste whether it belonged to the permitted channels or needed redirecting by a bead of secretion, a ridge left to harden and guide the next fall of water.

I would be very small, but not solitary.

Nothing there is solitary.

Others of my kind would move near me, sometimes touching, sometimes layered over my back in a living shingle. We would not communicate in speech. We would click, yes, but the clicking would be less message than measurement: shell against brick, shell against shell, interval, density, direction. We would know by contact when to spread into a sheet and when to narrow into a line. We would know when a warm casing had opened to us by the shiver passing through the pipe. We would know when something had died by the sudden richness in a drain-mouth.

If one of us were crushed beneath a freight wheel, there would be no event.

There would be a brief sweetness. A change in surface. A route adjusted by half an inch. By the next pressure cycle, no evidence.

I might carry a fragment of that crushed shell beneath my own body until it softened. Some part of it would become a plate in me. Some part would become a glossy mark on brick. Some part would pass through a needle-faced animal in a bowl-alley. Death would not end a line. It would thicken the district.

I would not mourn, but neither would I be indifferent.

Mourning belongs to creatures who imagine the world should have paused.

Here the pause is already scheduled.

When the district inhaled, I would stop with one forelimb raised.

Not because I had been commanded. Because motion during that measure would be incoherent. The canal would lower; the hidden engines would deepen; the central tone would pass through my shell and align the hinges of me. The steam feeders would seal themselves. The cable animals would stiffen. The long canal bodies would turn below the surface. Every creature would become, briefly, an instrument being tuned.

Then the exhalation.

Mist through grilles. Droplets on rails. Warmth unfolding from vents. The mouths of animals opening where no mouths had been visible. The black discs trembling and releasing their clear beads. A thousand surfaces newly legible.

Then movement again.

Continuation, not renewal.

I would live mostly at the district’s lower edges, where metal meets stone. That is where moisture appears. That is where the first films begin. That is where the old struggle against green is won before green knows it has tried. I would never see a leaf. I would have no inherited image of one. If somehow a seed blew over the wall and lodged in a seam, I would know it only as an improper swelling, a dampness organized wrongly, a softness with ambition. I and others would reduce it before it became visible. Not with hatred. Hatred is for rivals. This would be sanitation.

Perhaps once, if my route took me near the schoolhouse, I would climb over the slanted ceramic slabs. My feet would cross diagrams of pumps, mandibles, moons, pressure systems. I would taste old etched grooves filled with mineral dust and the dry residue of vanished hands or vanished claws. I would not distinguish instruction from texture. If the diagram had ever taught anyone how the place worked, it would now be part of the work itself: a patterned surface holding damp differently from the plain wall.

At the canal I would be cautious.

The black water has its own citizens, slower and wider in their knowing. I would descend only to the polished stones when the pulse was low, feeding along the satin band where water had retreated and left an edible rim. Skimmers would pass like strips of animated oil. Translucent larvae would tremble in eddies. Far below, one of the long bodies would move, and the pressure of it would enter my shell before the surface changed.

I would not fear it unless I fell.

Fear, in me, would be a very small and practical thing: the recoil from wrong vibration, the clamping down when air pressure drops before a valve opens, the stillness under the shadow of a rail grazer’s caliper-leg. I would not fear the central factory. No one fears gravity, not exactly. We simply align with it.

Still, the yard would matter.

All routes know the yard.

From time to time my line would bring me to its perimeter, where the ash-clay lies bare beyond the curb and the six vents sing without wavering. We would gather there in great number: pale shell-bodies, rope-things looped on posts, wet tools under the eaves, black adhering discs, small signal feeders fallen from the cables to press their faces toward the tone. The open yard would remain empty.

I would put my forelimbs on the curb and go no farther.

Across that bare space the factory’s four doors would wait, all identical, all closed or open in ways that did not change their meaning. The tone would enter me more deeply there. It would make every plate of my body remember its position.

Perhaps inside the factory there is a machine.

Perhaps there is a predator too vast to emerge.

Perhaps there is nothing but a chamber shaped to sustain the tone.

But from the curb I would not need to know. Knowing, in that place, is not possession of an explanation. It is correct participation.

And my participation would be small, exact, endless.

I would polish thresholds no human crosses.

I would keep lichen from walls in a world that has forgotten lichen.

I would eat the first skin of decay before decay can become history.

I would leave hardened beads to redirect water by a finger’s width.

I would clean the underside grooves of concrete bunks where larger clinging bodies sleep.

I would pass beneath doors whose handles are too low for hands and too high for me.

I would enter capped smokestacks and crawl among sleepers in the cold sootless dark.

I would taste old electricity.

I would grow a shell from mineral, grease, condensation, and the dead.

And if I lived long enough to become larger, I might change function without ever changing self.

My plates might darken. My limbs might hook more strongly. I might climb from seams to cable brackets, from cable brackets to junction boxes. I might become one of the squat creatures that press against housings and feed on the faint traffic of signal. Then the district would come to me as current rather than damp. I would feel messages pass through the wires—if messages they are—and take from them not meaning but nourishment.

Or I might thicken and flatten into a condensation body beneath a pipe, trembling when machines cycle, releasing clear beads into channels my younger kind would follow.

Or I might be eaten.

All of these would be acceptable continuations.

So where would I find myself?

At the curb of the central yard, eventually. Everyone does, by route or by pulse or by water.

But I would belong first to the seams: the damp junctions, the lower brickwork, the mineral lips of drains, the places where the district would otherwise begin to become something else.

And what would I be?

A small maintenance animal with a mica-pale shell and no eyes worth naming.

A cleaner of beginnings.

A hinge in the metabolism.

Not a citizen of the district, because citizenship implies standing apart from the civic body.

Not vermin, because vermin trespass.

Not worker, because work implies an outside purpose.

I would be one of the countless little organs by which the place continues: a file-mouthed, vibration-reading creature of brick seam and condensate, keeping the hard world hard, keeping the damp world damp, keeping every possible ending from accumulating into change.
