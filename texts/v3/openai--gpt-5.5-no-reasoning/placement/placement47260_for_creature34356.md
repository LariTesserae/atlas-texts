I would be found where the passage narrows after the garden court, beyond the polite quiet of the leaves, where the central light has already thinned and become something more useful than beautiful. Not in the lowest drains, not among the highest shell-lined shafts, but in the band between them: the damp middle of the settlement, where water has already been caught once and has not yet been trusted to remain clean.

There is a step there everyone knows to avoid in wet weeks. Red ocher stains its front edge, not freshly painted but renewed whenever the mark fades. Above it the wall bulges, fat with old plaster repairs. Beneath it a channel turns sharply under the floor, and when rain is generous the hidden current knocks against the brickwork with a sound like a knuckle tapping from inside the settlement’s bones.

That is where I would belong.

Not at first as a person of high standing. Not one of the old cistern keepers, not a mask keeper, not a surface-goer with wind cuts on my cheeks and stories of open sky. I think I would be something smaller and more necessary, half overlooked because the work is always there.

I would be a drain-listener.

Or rather, I would be apprenticed to the drain-listeners, though old enough that children had begun to copy my cautions in mockery. I would carry a hooked reed pole, a coil of palm rope, a little copper scraper on a cord, and a lamp with a narrow shielded mouth so it could be lowered near foul water without catching the vapors wrong. My hip cord would clack with bone plugs, waxed cloth, tally chips, and a small knife used mostly for cutting hair, twine, and swollen fungus from grates.

I would smell of wet brick.

Not pleasantly. Not entirely unpleasantly either. The smell would be familiar before I entered a room: mineral damp, old channel-water, lime scraped from seams, smoke in my hair, and the bitter salve rubbed over cracked fingers. My hands would have the softened, pale, swollen look of someone often reaching into water no one should touch. Across one forearm there would be a bite scar from a small eel or something everyone agreed was probably an eel because that made the story cleaner.

I would know the low routes of the settlement better than the ceremonial ones.

The old cistern room would make me lower my voice automatically. The condensation rooms would make me wipe my feet twice before entering. The garden court would make me uncomfortable unless I had washed recently, because people speak softly there and my work arrives with grit under the nails. But the underlanes—the ones crossed by plank bridges, the ones where children squat to watch pale fish flicker through cloudy water—those would be my lanes.

I would know which boards bend but still hold. Which grates can be lifted by one person and which require two if you do not want to lose a fingertip. Which family always wedges a basket too near the west run. Which goat has learned to drink from wash-water and must be shouldered away before it sickens. Which stretch of channel breeds midges after three warm days. Which pale geckos hunt best near lamp niches and should never be moved even when they startle infants.

I would not be brave in a grand way. I would be fussy.

People would call for me when water sounded wrong, and I would arrive already irritated, because wrong water means someone has ignored a small rule. A lid not seated. A mat shaken into a drain. Fat poured where fat must never go. A child’s toy lodged in a side channel. A repair made with the wrong mixture of lime and fiber because someone wanted the wall closed before evening.

“Loose-lashed,” I would mutter.

“Fat-in-the-east-drain thinking.”

“Would spill rain arguing.”

And because I was young enough to be imitated but old enough to be useful, children would repeat these phrases behind me in high, cruelly accurate voices.

I would pretend not to hear them.

But I would hear everything.

That would be the gift, or the burden, that settled me into my place. Not the sharpest eyes. Not the strongest shoulders. Not the cleanest plaster hand. Hearing.

I would know rain before the dogs sometimes.

Not always. The best dogs would still shame me, lifting their pale eyes toward a pipe before I had noticed anything. But often enough I would pause over a bowl, over a knot, over a half-eaten heel of bread, and lift my hand for silence.

There: a change.

A tapping too spread out to be rats. A hollow tick in the upper run. A softening of air inside one wall before the sound itself arrived. I would feel the settlement gather around that first hint: children leaning from doorways, old people stopping mid-story, goats turning their heads for reasons of their own, chickens complaining because everyone else had become attentive.

Then the call would go out.

Roof channel.

West line.

Not yet bowls.

Wait.

Now bowls.

And I would be gone toward the red step, because when the third channel runs, someone must open the low gates.

I would know that rule not as a sentence but as a motion in the body. Lamp up. Hook across the shoulder. Rope around the wrist. Left turn before the bee wall. Duck beneath the cracked lintel. Do not step on the patched brick. Kick aside the slat that always floats loose. Call before opening, because children like to crouch too near the grate even after being told.

During rain I would become less irritable.

Everyone would.

The settlement would become one animal, every household an organ remembering its task. There would be no time to nurse grudges, no time to argue over old debts or sleeping space or who used whose jar. Water would be in the walls, coming fast, and my work would stop belonging to me. Hands would pass bowls. Someone would lift a lamp higher. Someone would brace my shoulder while I leaned over a gate. Someone would shout measurements. Someone would laugh too loudly when a splash hit their face and then almost cry from relief.

The drains, when they fill, would sound joyful and dangerous.

Their usual muttering would become speech. Their speech would become song. Water would slap the channel walls, rush beneath planks, pull vegetable scraps and insect husks into brown speed. Blind fish would scatter. Eels would wake. Frogs in the black drains would begin their metallic knocking, and that sound would tighten something in my chest every time.

When the frogs call from the black drains, keep children above the red step.

So I would shout at them.

I would shout names, not warnings. Warnings vanish into excitement, but names hook into the body.

“Lemi, back.”

“Tav, your hand.”

“Sura, red step.”

And because my voice would have become one of the working voices of rain, even the bold ones would move, grumbling.

In dry weeks I would be less certain.

Dryness would make the settlement sound too large. Without running water in the channels, tiny things become huge: a rat tooth behind plaster, a settling jar, a lamp cough, someone weeping behind a reed screen. The old cistern’s ticking would grow public. Voices would lower. People would speak of levels and marks and whether the condensation rooms were giving enough.

I would spend those weeks scraping salt bloom from seams and pretending not to be afraid.

Fear here would not look like panic. It would look like repeated checking. Returning to a lid already sealed. Pressing an ear to a wall again and again. Measuring a channel that has not changed. Snapping at a child for wasting a mouthful. Dreaming of water running behind sealed brick where no one can reach it.

I might have been born during a dry season. That feels likely.

A child marked with condensation water instead of cistern water, because the clean jars were being counted too carefully. My first thumbprint pressed into plaster near a family sleeping alcove crowded with baskets and sleeping mats and one old aunt who said the wall there sweated in bad years. My mother perhaps a mushroom keeper, smelling of damp fiber and blue-gray folds. My father perhaps no one in particular, or one of the goat-wheel men, or gone above and returned only as a tool hung for a season near the old cistern.

But there would be an elder who made me.

Not a parent exactly. An old drain-woman with knees gone bad and hearing so fine it seemed like witchcraft. She would sit on a low stool near the east run, stripping fibers with fingers twisted by lime, and she would teach by insult.

“Not that sound. That is shell grit.”

“Not that one. Rat in a dry pocket.”

“You hear water because you want water. Wanting is loud. Listen under it.”

Her name would remain attached to some repair after she died. People would say, “That gate is Sef’s judgment,” or “Do it the way Sef did before the frogs came back.” Maybe Sef is not the right name, but it has the feel of a name rubbed smooth by use.

She would have taught me the map beneath the visible map.

Here, under the family with the loud chickens, a narrow run sealed after the bad fever.

Here, behind the honey-colored plaster, an old pipe that no longer carries water but still carries sound.

Here, never cut.

Here, cut only if three elders agree.

Here, if the wall sweats sweet, call the bee keeper, not the plasterers.

Here, if the water smells of onions, blame the jars before the cistern.

Here, if frogs call before rain, believe them.

I would be skeptical in speech and superstitious in practice, like most sensible people there.

I would laugh at the idea that sound cracks plaster, but in the old cistern room during dry weeks I would lower my voice until even my breath behaved.

I would say bees do not know liars, then avoid lying beside a hive wall.

I would scold children for naming rats, then remember the names of three scarred ones myself.

I would insist the pale geckos are useful, not lucky, and still touch two fingers to the wall when one crossed over my sleeping mat.

My pleasures would be small, bodily, exact.

A drain running clear after a clog breaks.

The first cool mouthful from a jar that rings full.

A lamp trimmed so well its flame stands without smoking.

The weight of a cat dropping onto my folded cloak and deciding I am furniture.

Bread handed to me by someone who knows I forgot to eat.

A mushroom stew salted properly.

The hush in the garden court when the shaft light is widest and the cistern lip shines.

A repaired gate moving cleanly in its groove, no scrape, no complaint.

I would court badly.

Or perhaps not badly, but indirectly even by the standards of an indirect people. Privacy being scarce, I would hide behind usefulness. I would clean someone’s lamp. Replace the cracked reed on their family’s drain cover. Leave a better hook near their work niche. Pretend I happened to have an extra fig slice and then fail to disguise the fact that I had carried it wrapped in damp cloth for half the day.

If I loved someone, it might be a lamp steward.

Someone whose work was opposite and kin to mine: flame instead of water, smoke instead of seep, wicks instead of grates. They would smell of oil and ash, with soot in the lines of their palms. We would quarrel over air. I would say their lamps fed the midges. They would say my wet channels soured the lower rooms. Both of us would be right. Both of us would keep adjusting the world so the other’s work could continue.

We would meet near thresholds because no one truly meets unseen. A cleaned lamp left where my tools hang. A copper wick pick repaired with a bone pin from my cord. A song about a foolish drain-listener who mistook a goat’s piss for rain, sung while grinding grain within my hearing.

Everyone would know before anything was said.

Of course they would. The settlement is made of walls, but nothing in it stays private except the exact path of water behind plaster.

I would fear fire most after cascading failure.

Not flames in a cooking niche, not a wick flare, but reed panels catching in a crowded lane, smoke thickening where air shafts have not yet been opened, goats screaming, children trapped above a blocked passage, people throwing precious water because there is no other choice. Fire would be the wrong kind of hunger, a thing that consumes maintenance faster than hands can answer.

I would also fear bad air.

A sealed room opened too quickly. An old cistern entered without a lamp test. A lower channel gone sour. The invisible failures are the ones that make courage look like patience: wait, test, lower flame, smell, listen, tie the rope, do not let pride enter before your body.

If I died, I suspect it would be in an ordinary accident that became a story because stories are how rules keep their teeth.

Not heroic, perhaps. A wet footing. A grate lifted too soon. A child pulled back at the wrong moment and myself leaning too far. Or old age with lungs roughened by damp and smoke, sitting near a lamp, correcting younger people until they avoid my corner.

Afterward, someone would say, “That hook was hers.”

Or, “She said the west run lies in warm weather.”

Or, “Do not put your hand there. Remember what took her finger.”

My tools would be cleaned. The copper scraper passed on. The reed pole cut down for a smaller apprentice. A little water poured onto a shard beside a lamp, my name evaporating into the warm air. My death would not remove me from maintenance. No one’s does.

But if you found me alive, it would be in a moment of interruption.

I would be crossing a corridor with a jar at one hip and the hook pole tucked under my arm, shoulders already turned sideways to pass a goat. A child would be telling me something urgent and mostly false about a rat the size of a baby. A cat would be above us, pretending not to watch. Somewhere nearby, someone would be pounding grain. Somewhere farther off, frogs or hammering or a cough would confuse the echoes.

Then I would stop.

Not dramatically. Just still.

My head would tilt. My fingers would touch the damp wall, not because touch tells everything but because the body wants to join the listening. The child would recognize the gesture and fall silent, proud of knowing silence when it mattered.

Behind the wall, beneath the ordinary scrape and clatter of the made world, a trickle would have changed.

Maybe rain.

Maybe a blockage.

Maybe nothing yet.

But I would have heard enough to go looking.

That is what I would be in this world: not a ruler, not a priest, not an explorer of the surface, but one of the settlement’s small alert nerves. A keeper of channels. A reader of damp. A person whose life is spent between foul water and clean water, between panic and habit, between the wall as shelter and the wall as something that can fail.

I would belong to the inward weather.

And the settlement, warm as breath under a blanket, would belong partly to my listening.
