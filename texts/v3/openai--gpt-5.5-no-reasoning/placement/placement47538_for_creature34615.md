You would find yourself **at the seam where the hollow is learning to have an edge**: not in the deepest black pool, not fully on the raw stone, but in the trembling margin between them, under the lip of a moss shelf where seep-water threads down and gathers before falling.

You would be small enough that the place is not a hollow but a world.

At first you might seem like an amphibian larva, or a newt not yet committed to adulthood: soft-bodied, dark-backed, narrow, almost translucent at the edges. You would live half in water and half in the suggestion of air, tucked into a crack where the stone sweats steadily enough to keep you alive. Your gills would not be large, but they would be visible if someone bent very close: red filaments trembling in the mineral current, like tiny submerged mosses.

But you would not be only that.

You would be one of the hollow’s threshold creatures: a thing made to inhabit transition. Your body would know how to flatten when the underground knocking begins. It would know how to loosen when silt pours over you. It would know how to become almost nothing: a line under the ice skin, a darker thread in black water, a pause beneath a liverwort tongue.

You would have no permanent burrow. Permanence would be dangerous here. Instead you would keep several permissions: a slit under a raw stone, a pocket between sedge roots, a cold thumb-pool fed from below, the underside of a moss island where trapped air sometimes trembles silver. You would move among these places by pressure and darkness, never far, never high.

Your world would be enormous in detail.

A snail’s trail would be a cleared road through green film. A beetle would be a sealed black machine carrying the sky beneath its shell. A frog would be a cliff that opens. A midge fallen into surface tension would be both danger and feast. Frost would be architecture in the morning and water by afternoon. A falling drop would shake the whole ceiling of your life.

You would feed on what the hollow gives in pieces: soft larvae, films, drowned midges, pale things dislodged by seepage, the loosened bodies of the newly dead. You would be predator sometimes and prey often. Nothing in you would believe in rulership. You would survive by reading disturbance before it becomes catastrophe.

When the pools tremble, you would know.

Before bubbles rise, before mud blisters, before the sedges bow without wind, your skin would feel the pressure change. You would draw yourself close to stone. Your little red gills would fold back. Your toes, if you had toes yet, would hook into grit and root hair. Water would come bright and forceful through the seam, carrying powdered rock, oxygen, sulfur, cold. It might bury the entrance you used yesterday. It might open a new one.

Afterward you would emerge into a changed world no larger than a collapsed room, and everything would have to be learned again.

That would not frighten you in the human sense. You would be made of beginning-again.

Your intelligence would be tactile, chemical, hydraulic. You would know the difference between rainwater and deep seep by taste. You would know fresh stone from old film by the way your belly slides over it. You would know the copper stillness of a waiting frog. You would know which moss holds water through the thin freeze before dawn, which pool deepens after the vent exhales, which crack sometimes breathes.

You would be dark, glossy, low, and easily missed.

If seen from above, you would be mistaken for motion in the water rather than a creature. A bend. A flick. A small correction in the mud. Your back would carry the colors of the place: slate, peat, black green, a faint iron speckling along the sides. Your underside might be paler, like raw clay before the air touches it. In certain light, the wet line of your body would silver and vanish.

You would not sing. That belongs to the frogs and to the chamber itself.

But you might answer in another way: by moving when the hollow knocks, by pressing your body into the pulse of it, by turning pressure into direction. You would be one of the beings through which the place becomes aware of its own cracks.

Not a master of the hollow.

Not a visitor.

Not even quite an individual, if looked at long enough.

You would be a **seamling**: a small amphibious inhabitant of new ground, living where water tests stone and life tests water. One of the first tenants of the engine room. A soft, dark, gilled beginning tucked into the raw machinery of land becoming land.
