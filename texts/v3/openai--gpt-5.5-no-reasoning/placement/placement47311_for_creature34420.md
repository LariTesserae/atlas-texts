I would not be one of the bold ones who go outward across the blue-black gaps. Not at first.

At first I think I am somewhere quieter: inside the lee of a root-buttress, in one of the low houses with its curved walls and damp bark smell, awake before the condensation fall. I am lying under moth-fur with one wrist looped through a house-rope because I have known since childhood that sleep is a kind of falling unless something remembers you. The roof above me ticks with forming droplets. The walls hum very faintly with wind passing through the greater wood.

But that is too still.

I would belong near the edges, though not recklessly. I would be one of those people whose work begins where the house stops being safe and the route begins being an argument.

A bridge-reader, perhaps.

Not the oldest and not the bravest; one of the lean, quiet ones with resin-darkened fingers, a coil of family rope across one shoulder, and a listening mallet tucked through the belt. I would spend my life going ahead of others by a little distance. Not far enough to be heroic. Far enough to know first.

I would crawl out onto living spans while the village watches from behind, my cheek close to the cold bark, one palm spread flat against the fibers. Beneath me: miles of dark air, cloud moving like a second weather. Around me: the bridge breathing in slow vegetable increments, sap stiff with cold, beetles ticking somewhere inside the old wood. I would tap once, then wait. Tap twice. Listen not for sound only, but for how sound fails to return.

A good bridge has several voices. The upper skin gives a tight click. The deeper wood answers lower, rounder, delayed. Root-cords hum if they still carry strain cleanly. Hidden rot drinks the note. A beetle chamber makes the tap go papery. New growth answers too eagerly, like a child lying. Dead wood answers with dignity, which is dangerous because dignity can hold until it does not.

I would know those differences. Or I would be learning them, because no one ever really finishes learning a place that changes its mind while you are standing on it.

My name would have a spoken shape, but that would matter less than my tapping name. Four knocks and a scrape, maybe. Something my mother used when calling me in fog. Something my siblings could make on any hollow surface. Something a lover would tap once into my shoulder blade in the dark, asking without words whether I was awake.

I imagine my hands first: cracked at the knuckles, scarred across the palms, nails kept short, the skin permanently smelling of oil-ash, fungus smoke, and the bitter green of bruised vine. Around my wrists, tight wraps against cold rain. In my belt: a rounded work knife, bone wedges, a packet of ash-oil, three resin plugs, a small gourd of water, not full enough. Never full enough.

I would be afraid often. That seems important.

In this world, a fearless person is not admirable. Fear is one of the senses. It belongs beside hearing, balance, thirst, and the ability to read the underside of leaves. I would trust my fear if it arrived with reasons: a bridge too silent; moths lifting from a sheltered place during still weather; a sweet warmth leaking from a crack; the sudden pale turn of leaves along a ridge.

But I might also have the vice of curiosity. Not loud curiosity. Not the kind that boasts in the shelter and makes younger cousins roll their eyes. The quieter kind: the one that keeps a person crouched too long beside a fresh split, watching vapor pulse from the opening; the one that wonders whether the plant truly hears the tapping or whether generations of touch have taught people to hear themselves better.

I would not say such things carelessly. Skepticism is permitted; clumsiness is not.

So I would perform the courtesies.

Before crossing a major bridge, I would press ash-oil into the listening knots. I would tap my family rhythm. I would wait for the small alterations: insect-noise pausing, sap-scent sharpening, a tremor moving out and away from my hand. Whether these were answers or consequences would not matter while the abyss waited underneath.

I think I would live in a settlement called something like Under-Fin or Three-Lee or Last Cleft—names that are descriptions before they are poetry. A small place, half-hidden in the fold of a giant root-buttress, its dwellings lacquered dark and tied down with more rope than timber. The village would have a cistern leaf to the east that everyone distrusted because transparent eels found it too often, and a safe condensation slope above the houses that glittered white for six breaths at dawn. There would be an old ladder nearby leading upward into a curtain of sealed rootlets, a useless inheritance no one cut down because someone’s grandmother once used it to escape a storm.

My house would be narrow, concave-floored, smelling of smoked cordage. Ropes everywhere. My sleeping niche lined with old moth-fur. A rain membrane patched in three places. A shelf of bark maps I did not own but had borrowed too long. Above the door, perhaps, a wind-mask for someone taken by a route I misread—or one I was too young to read.

Because there should be a weight.

Maybe I became a bridge-reader because my father fell. Or because he did not fall: because he came home once with his face emptied by what he had heard inside a bridge, sat down, untied his harness very slowly, and never crossed anything uncertain again. Since then, I learned to go where he would not. Not out of defiance exactly. More like inheritance bending around damage.

In the mornings I would rise with everyone when the condensation began. No one is exempt from water. I would lift cups, tighten catch-frames, sweep larvae from gutter leaves, wring my hood into a jar. Cold water down the sleeve. Someone cursing softly. Children laughing because the whole village, elders and hunters and solemn mapkeepers alike, had been made ridiculous and wet by the same falling glitter.

Then the water would vanish into storage, mouths, moss, bark. The day would become work.

Some days I would accompany hunters, moving ahead of them along old routes to test spans before they crossed with nets and darts. Some days I would be sent to read a new connection forming toward a neighboring mass, its young bark too smooth, too taut, shining green-black in the weak light. I would hate those days. Young bridges have no memory. They hold strength and ignorance together.

I would go anyway.

Three points of contact. Rope around waist and thigh. Secondary line to a root-horn if one exists. Ear to wood. Tap. Wait.

Above me the high leaves would drink the dim sky. Below me, wind would gather itself into a voice too large to belong to anything alive, though everything here is alive. Pale moths would cling under leaves. Vine-runners would quarrel unseen. Somewhere far off, a limb would crack and fall, and long after, the abyss would answer with a boom softened by distance.

If I heard a call from below, I would not answer.

That rule would live deeper in me than language. Even if the voice sounded like my father. Especially then. I would put my mouth against my sleeve, breathe once, and tap the danger rhythm into the bridge for whoever held my line behind me.

Do not answer. Do not give location, attention, or permission.

But I would remember the sound. Later, indoors, I might try to describe its shape to an elder. Was it wind? Was it a returning echo? Was it a predator with borrowed throats? The elder would listen, then tell me to oil my rope and sleep tied tonight. A practical answer. The best kind.

I would not be important in the way stories prefer. I would not discover the origin of the plant-continent or speak with its mind. I would not descend into the lower darkness and return with a revelation. If I crossed to another green mass, it would be as one member of a cautious expedition, carrying spare line and sealed water, too busy surviving to feel legendary.

What I would be is someone trusted with passage.

That is a sacred office in a world without fixed ground.

When children first climbed three body-lengths above the home platform, I might be one of the adults below, holding the safety line and pretending not to see their trembling knees. When a pair-bonded couple needed to cross to a new shelter, I would test the way before them. When a body was recovered after a fall—not from the abyss, but from some lower snag or broken span—I would read the return route while the family carried their dead wrapped in waxed leaf-skin.

I would know that every path is temporary. Every bridge is a promise with an expiration hidden inside it. Every map is a conversation already becoming obsolete.

And still I would make marks.

On cured bark, I would scratch routes, stitch corrections, burn small dots for hollows, tie on strips where new tendrils had begun to take hold. My maps would not say, This is the way. They would say, This was the way when the frost leaned west and the black pods had not yet opened. Ask again. Listen again. Touch before trusting.

Maybe, as I grew older, I would become less of a bridge-reader and more of a mapkeeper. My knees would complain in freezing rain. Younger people would crawl farther out than I could, and I would hate watching them do it with such grace. I would sit inside during storms, singing low into the walls to hear whether the house buzzed wrong. I would teach children the difference between a good knot and a pretty one. I would correct strangers who stepped too heavily on breathing bark. I would say “fear belongs in the hand, not the mouth,” and the children would repeat it mockingly when I was not listening.

But I would be listening.

Everyone here is listening.

If I imagine one complete moment, it is this:

I am out before dawn on a root-bridge rimed white, belly-down, cheek numb against the wood. My left hand is tied to the line; my right holds the little mallet. Across the gap another plant-mass looms, ragged and dark, lower reaches lost in vapor. Somewhere on it, an orange light opens briefly, then vanishes. A shelter? Resin flare? Lightning caught in sap? No way to know.

Behind me, my people are quiet. Ahead, the bridge narrows, then thickens again where new growth has begun to braid over the old. Beneath, nothing but moving blue-black air.

I tap once.

The bridge answers high.

I tap again, lower and closer to a seam.

This time the sound travels away through the wood, disappears, returns with a slow deep note that I feel in my teeth. Not safe exactly. Safe enough, perhaps, for one person at a time, with packs passed separately and no crossing after thaw-wind.

Then the leaves all around release their gathered water.

Condensation falls in a million bright impacts. The rime trembles. My hood darkens. The bridge gleams. The whole suspended continent begins to drink, and for a few minutes the abyss is hidden by falling droplets.

I lift my head and tap back to the others:

Wait.

Then:

One at a time.

Then, after listening once more:

Come.
