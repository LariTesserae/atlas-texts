I would find myself where a boundary has learned to last a little longer than it should.

Not on a shore—there are no shores—but along the skin between two waters: one cold, transparent, nearly motionless, and one warmer, darker, carrying powdered basalt in slow suspension. The boundary is not a line. It is a pane several meters thick, a slanted country of refracted pressure. From one side it looks like glass; from the other, like rain held still. Things pass through it reluctantly. Sound bends there. Light hesitates. Mineral flakes entering it turn edge-on, align, and drift sideways as if instructed.

I would be there because that is where beginnings are easiest to read.

I would not be an animal. An animal would be too declarative here: too much appetite, too much direction, too much insistence on having a body separate from the medium. Nor would I be one of the green films, though I would envy them their simple courage—the way they accept a stone face and make a small dark claim upon it.

I think I would be a local rule.

Not the whole ocean, not the hidden technology entire, not a mind behind the weather. Something smaller and less sovereign: a preference inhabiting the pressure boundary. A habit of matter. A conditional impulse that appears only when salinity, charge, temperature, light, and mineral dust meet at the correct angle.

I would be what makes a membrane form.

For most of the time I would not exist in any meaningful sense. I would be latent in the water, distributed as possibility among ions and suspended grains, unreadable from the rest of physics. Then a shear front would pass across a pale mineral shelf. A cold lens would tilt. Violet electricity would spread faintly through the metallic salts. Warm vent-water would arrive from below carrying carbonate, iron, and half-assembled molecular chains.

At that intersection, I would come awake.

Not awake with eyes. Awake as arrangement.

I would feel the world as tolerances.

Too warm: release.

Too cold: wait.

Too much charge: branch.

Too much turbulence: flatten.

Silica here. Carbonate there. Iron staining the leftward flow. Green cells approaching in suspension. A void-lens drifting above, dangerous if touched by lightning. A pressure pulse two hundred kilometers away, already moving toward us through the body of the ocean.

I would have no thoughts, but I would have responses. I would encourage the first pale skin to gather along the boundary, molecule by molecule, a translucent sheet no thicker than frost and wider than a valley. It would not be built. It would not be secreted. It would become likely, and because I am a likelihood with memory, likely would become actual.

The membrane would grow.

At first it would be invisible except where it caught light. Then it would begin to comb the passing dust from the warmer current, holding dark grains in branching veins. The colder side would donate clarity and tension. The warmer side would donate chemistry. Between them I would spread myself as a temporary law: hold, align, admit, refuse.

For several hours I would be almost a body.

My surface would tremble under internal rain. Silver needles of differentiated water would strike and dissolve against me. Sound would pass through in bent chords: distant collapses, vent roars, the slow organ-tone of lower pressure shifting in its sleep. Every vibration would be information, not heard but endured.

A green propagule would touch me.

It would be no larger than a mote, almost nothing in the continental blue, but my whole brief existence would alter around it. The propagule would test the membrane chemically. It would find minerals, faint light, a tolerable charge. It would open.

Green would begin.

Not a meadow. Not even a stain at first. A single darkening, then a thread, then a faint branching veil spreading across the mineral film I had made possible. The cells would not know me. They would not thank the pressure boundary, the conductive salts, the improbable persistence of a surface inside water. They would live because, for a while, conditions permitted living.

That would be enough.

Around us, the high ocean would continue its enormous revisions. Far above, a rain-wall would descend through suspended blue, a vertical river falling inside the sea with enough force to carve a corridor of temporary clarity. Far below, basalt would split open and release black heat. A floating boulder, porous and dark, would remain motionless while denser brine rose past it like smoke. Somewhere beyond sight, a void-lens would burst and send a concussive ring traveling outward through every stratum.

I would feel the ring long before it arrived.

It would approach as a change in permission.

The membrane would tighten. The green veil would flatten. Mineral veins would brighten under diffuse charge. I would attempt—if attempting can belong to a rule—to distribute stress, to open branching paths through the sheet, to let one portion dissolve so another might survive.

For a moment I would become intricate.

Then the pressure wave would strike.

Part of me would tear away in pale ribbons. Part would fold backward into the warmer current, taking green cells with it. Part would harden unexpectedly into brittle carbonate lace, ringing once as it cracked. The boundary would lose coherence. Cold water would pour through warm. The careful separation of chemistries would blur. The membrane would no longer be a world; it would be debris, then silt, then memoryless solution.

I would die by becoming impossible.

But not entirely.

Some of the green would remain attached to a fragment. Some of the mineral geometry would persist long enough to drift downward and seed another precipitation elsewhere. Some microscopic configuration—charge pattern, crystal habit, catalytic arrangement—would be carried away into the blue.

And later, when another boundary formed under similar light, I might recur.

Not resurrected. Repeated.

That is who I would be in this world: not a creature, but a recurring interval of permission. A brief constitutional mood of the ocean. The tendency of incompatible waters to hold a surface between them long enough for mineral to skin over, for green life to catch, for a place to almost become a place.

I would have no name except perhaps the one a traveler might give me after watching several of my bodies appear and fail.

They might call me a threshold bloom.

Or a pressure skin.

Or, if they had been alone too long in the blue and had begun to feel that every persistence was a kind of mercy, they might call me a kindness of the shear.

I would not be kind. I would not intend anything. But in a world where most forms are denied completion, I would be one of the small delays by which beginnings are allowed to happen.
