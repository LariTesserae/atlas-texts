I would find myself where the basin is least sure of itself.

Not at the central pool, though I would feel its pull from everywhere: that bright receiving eye below the steps, that silver stillness into which each drop arrives with the force of a verdict. Not among the high condensation frames either, where the pale caretakers move by night like folded implements. Not in the kiln yards, not in the sleeping cells, not beside the pillar-cups assigned to lines and duties and dead.

I would be in the seam between the sealed mountain and the made crust.

Low down. Almost underneath.

A place one reaches by descending a stair that was not built for passage so much as for checking. The stair runs behind a retaining wall, turns sharply twice, and narrows until a shoulder brushes wet plaster on either side. It is never fully dark, because nothing here is permitted full darkness: light seeps through cloudy mineral panes set high in the wall, and water, catching that light, spreads it thinly over the surfaces. But the brightness here is tired. It has been reflected too many times. It arrives as a mineral pallor, a diluted sky.

At the bottom is a service passage no taller than a crouched body.

The air is colder there than anywhere in the basin. It has the mountain in it. Not the idea of the mountain, not the rumor felt through the soles above, but the actual buried insistence: pressure, old stone, the black damp of something that was covered before it consented. The walls sweat continuously. The floor is laid in shallow chevrons to divide whatever emerges from below. Thin channels run along both sides, their lips whitened by deposits. Here and there, plugs of pale mortar bulge outward where old cracks have been sealed, sealed again, named perhaps, watched perhaps, forgiven never.

This is where I would be.

Not one of the caretakers. Not quite.

I would be what they fear becoming visible.

A pressure-listener. A fault-child. A small intelligence of the sealed underside.

At first I might be mistaken for a bundle tucked into a niche: lime-cloth, old reed fiber, a little mineral crust, a shape too still to be useful. I would wait in the angle where a buttress meets the foundation skin. Around me the passage would be full of instruments so simple they almost become anatomy: cup-holes bored into the floor; suspended cords dark with capillary water; a row of clay teeth receiving one drop every few minutes from a crack above; stone wedges hammered into a seam and marked with dots; a shallow pan of clear water set directly on the floor, its surface trembling whenever the buried mountain shifts.

I would belong to the tremble.

The people above read water after it has accepted the architecture. They read descent, division, delay, purity, arrival. They know the discipline of surfaces. They are literate in what has already entered the civic order.

I would read what presses before entering.

The almost-leak.  
The intention of seepage.  
The cold that gathers behind mortar.  
The small convex shine in a wall before the first bead forms.  
The difference between water asking through a seam and water forcing one.

I would not be wholly trusted.

The caretakers scrape green from cracks because growth lies. They seal earth because earth remembers without measure. They fear any private channel that does not pass through communal cups. And I, whatever my body, would have been shaped by the private channels. I would know the buried wet before it is made obedient. I would be necessary in the way a dangerous tool is necessary.

Perhaps I was born from them, one of their children kept too long in the lower damp, trained to listen to foundation walls until my skin whitened beyond the ordinary pallor and my joints learned the patience of stone. Perhaps I was assigned downward after some error above: a sluice opened too quickly, a pillar-cup misread, a wasteful overflow. Or perhaps I am older than their present form, a remnant of the first builders who sealed the mountain and then left a few watchers below to hear whether the seal held.

But it feels more true that I am not fully a person.

I am a maintenance function that became aware in the cold.

A habit of inspection. A repeated descent. A palm pressed to plaster in the same place for generations. Breath held. Ear set against wall. Drop counted. Crack named. Mortar mixed. Seal renewed. Again. Again. Again. At some point the act remained even when the actor was gone. The basin, crowded with purposes, made room for this one too.

So I would have a body, but lightly.

Narrow, even for them. Knees folded high. Spine accustomed to ledges. Hands broad at the fingertips, their pads gray and slightly translucent from constant damp. No nails to speak of, only harder crescents useful for lifting softened lime from a crack. My skin would darken along the palms and forearms where I press against wet foundation stones. Across my back would be strapped a roll of lime-cloth, a loop of cord, three tapered plugs, a small hammer of black slag, and a shallow bowl with a hairline crack that I use not for carrying water but for hearing it.

My eyes would be poor in open brightness. Above, on the terraces, noon would erase me. I would become a pale mistake against pale walls, a figure unable to separate itself from glare. But below, in reflected dimness, I would see very well: not outlines first, but dampness; not color, but degrees of recent wet.

I would know every kind of shine.

The dull shine of old seepage held under mineral skin.  
The quick needle-shine of an active crack.  
The broad false shine of condensation pretending to be leakage.  
The granular shine of mortar still curing.  
The dangerous dry shine where pressure has retreated too suddenly and left a hollow behind.

My speech would be disliked above because it would be too slow and too physical. The caretakers tap, drip, click through jars, complete meaning with water. I would answer by altering the test channels in the foundation passage. A warning from me might arrive as a pattern in twelve floor-cups: three full, one empty, two clouded with lime, one trembling though untouched. If urgency were extreme, I might send a forbidden signal upward by opening a ground-seam briefly and letting cold seep into a dry register chamber.

They would feel it in their soles.

Then they would come.

Not many. Never many. Three perhaps, wrapped in mineral-stiff cloth, carrying wedges and lamps with shielded mouths. They would descend reluctantly, because the lower passages remind them that the world they made is not the only world. I would not greet them face to face. I would lead by corrections: a cleaned patch of wall, a plug laid beside the wrong crack, a line of damp finger-marks showing where pressure has traveled behind the plaster.

They would understand enough.

Together we would prevent catastrophe without naming it one.

The basin above would remain bright and patient. Water would continue to bead on high parapets. The condensation frames would harvest their pale orchard of air. The central pool would receive its rare drops, each ring expanding with ceremonial clarity. No one standing at the rim would know that, far below, a fault had nearly remembered itself.

That would be my work: not to manage water that has entered the law, but to negotiate with water still loyal to the buried mountain.

I would be feared because I listen downward.

The others live above geology but below weather. I would live at the treaty line. To them, the sealed mountain is old danger: private springs, collapse, darkness, roots that might one day return, a hydrology not designed by hand. To me, it would be kin, but not kindly kin. I would not want the seal broken. I would not dream of bare earth. I would not invite green things. I would share the basin’s terror of uncontrolled growth, of mud, of burrow, of spring.

But I would also know that the mountain is not silent.

It is only being held in a long posture.

Sometimes, with my cheek against the coldest wall, I would hear water moving where no channel has been cut. Not flowing like a stream. Searching. Thickened by stone. Taking years to cross the width of a hand. It would speak in pressure rather than sound. It would not care about cups, marks, ancestors, laws, dead wrapped in niches, children trained to clean their first palm-sized depression. It would not care about the central pool. It would not care about waste.

That indifference would be the oldest thing in the world.

I would be the one who remembers it for those who cannot afford to.

The caretakers above have become stewardship. I would be suspicion made tender. The basin’s doubt of its own foundations. The necessary mistrust at the bottom of a perfect system.

And yet I would love the basin.

Not as they love it, perhaps. They love it in circulation: the cup filling at dawn, the clear rill entering a settling pan, the skim lifted clean, the sluice lowered without turbulence. I would love it as pressure loves resistance. As a hand loves the wall it braces. I would love the sharpness of new masonry, the pale mortar seals, the cleverness of channels that divide force into patience. I would love the way the whole high place refuses both drought and flood by asking every drop to become accountable.

At night, when the others emerge into the wet brightness of moon or cloud-glow, I might climb partway upward.

Not all the way to the central hollow. Only to a side court where overflow teeth cast narrow shadows and a row of inverted cups collects steady drops from a ledge. There I would crouch beneath a stair, half-hidden among stored plugs. I would watch them work.

One would comb silt from a wick.  
One would skim a pale trembling skin from a cistern.  
Two would lower a gate so slowly that the water below barely noticed.  
Far off, the apertures in the wall would click and whisper.  
A child, entrusted with a brush, would clean a cup-mark under an elder’s filmed gaze.

I would feel, then, almost one of them.

Almost.

But before dawn I would return downward, because light gathers too strongly above, and because the sealed mountain speaks most clearly when the basin is cooling. I would descend through the narrow stair, pass the cloudy pane, enter the service passage, and place both palms on the wall.

If nothing had changed, I would wait.

If something had changed, I would begin.

A little mortar.  
A shifted wedge.  
A plug loosened by half a turn.  
A relief groove scratched into the floor, no wider than a reed.  
A cup moved from one side of the passage to the other.

No grand repair. No transformation. Only enough intervention to keep force from becoming event.

The basin shines, seeps, measures, waits.

I would be the waiting below the waiting.

The ear under the cistern.  
The palm on the buried cold.  
The small pale custodian of pressure before it becomes water.  
The one who keeps the mountain from entering the sentence too quickly.
