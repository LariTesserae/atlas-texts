I would find myself somewhere that only pretends to be a plaza.

At first I would think I was standing in an open court, one of those broad green architectural spaces between the columns, where the living floor is flat enough to suggest intention. The air would be warm against me, not moving much, thickened by the thousand suspended droplets that make distance unreliable. The light would lie on everything as a glaze. Behind me, a line of reed-pillars would vent quietly through their crowns. Ahead, a low basin would tremble with stored rain. The floor under me would be made of those corky hexagonal pads, springing back after each pressure, darkening where weight had passed.

But I do not think I would be a visitor crossing it.

I think I would be one of the parts of the place that can be mistaken for a surface.

Not the whole plateau. That would be too complete, too diffuse, too much like weather given veins. Nor one of the high knots exactly, those swollen junctions where decisions collect and radiate. I would be lower than that, more provisional. Something grown for use before meaning. Something that receives pressure, water, and heat, and becomes briefly legible as a floor.

I would be a young drainage court.

Or rather, I would be the membrane beneath the court before it has decided whether to remain pavement, become basin, or open into a throat.

I would lie stretched between older ribs of rootwood, held taut by tension from four or five living curbs. My upper skin would be green-gold, lacquered, slightly translucent where the daylight presses through standing films of water. Under that skin would be chambers: shallow, compressible, warm, full of amber sap and air that smells of wet bark and fermentation. I would feel weight not as pain but as instruction. A falling drop would be a point of cold brightness. A sheet of runoff would be a sentence. The slow pooling of rain in my center would be a growing certainty.

I would have no eyes, but I would know the sky by pressure.

When cloud thickened, my surface would cool unevenly. Condensation would stipple me. The surrounding curbs would swell and lean inward, feeding me water through seams. The reed-towers nearby would begin their hollow booming, and the root-gutters above would overfill. I would feel rain arrive first as vibration through the braided structures overhead, then as impact: thousands of warm strikes, each one small, together becoming command.

Hold.

Then:

Divert.

Then, when holding became more costly than release:

Open.

A seam would appear across me, though from inside it would feel less like tearing than remembering where I was designed to part. My hexagonal pads would bow. The moss-bright lines between them would brighten and soften. Sap pressure would alter in my rim. I would pucker downward, not upward, making a shallow mouth. Water would gather in the depression, spin once, twice, then find the new slope. The court that seemed to be a place to stand would become a funnel. The floor would become a throat.

If someone had been standing there, they would step back quickly, perhaps frightened by the transformation. But I would not be trying to swallow them. I would barely know them except as a dry, irregular pressure, too brief to matter, something that did not exchange properly with the wet language of the plateau.

My real companions would be neighboring tissues.

The old buttress-root to the west, dark and strong and slow, feeding me strain from the higher terrace.

The pale ramp to the north, new-grown and tender, recoiling at every heavy drop, sending chemical complaint through the seam where it joined me.

The hanging gutter above, whose overflow arrived in pulses.

The buried spongewood beneath, thirsty and sour, pulling my stored water down into its brown labyrinth.

And somewhere beyond all of us, not central but influential, a knot swollen under a junction of bridges, receiving reports from shadow, weight, and saturation, encouraging one district to stiffen and another to dissolve.

I would not think in words. I would have tendencies.

Toward slope.  
Toward bracing.  
Toward opening when full.  
Toward thickening where pressure repeats.  
Toward letting go of shapes that no longer serve the passage of water.

My memory would be anatomical. A darker band where last week’s overflow crossed me. A thicker lip where standing water lingered too long. A buried scar where an earlier throat opened and sealed. A faint spiral of pores where warmth collected under debris and had to be breathed away. I would not remember events. I would become their consequences.

In dry intervals—though nothing here is truly dry—I would flatten again. My upper pads would harden. The seams would narrow until they looked decorative, like a pattern meant for feet. Mist would rise from me, and the light would varnish me. I would resemble architecture.

But I would be waiting for weight.

Not waiting with desire. Waiting as a charged condition. Every surface here waits in that sense. The bridge waits for absorbed water to tighten it. The basin waits to spill. The tower waits to vent. The root-curtain waits to catch cloud. The young ledge waits to learn whether it can hold itself in air.

I would be a threshold state in the plateau’s body: court becoming basin, basin becoming drain, drain becoming floor again.

If the living highland had something like moods, I would belong to its uncertainty. Not its grand outward expansion over the cliffs, not its high translucent leaf-sails glowing against the cloud, not its great roaring cataracts. I would be one of the mutable civic organs, the places that make the whole world seem inhabited because they take the form of use.

A plaza that is not for gathering.

A pavement that is also skin.

A mouth disguised as a square.

Over time I might be reinforced. Repeated rains might prove my usefulness. The curbs around me would rise. My drainage seam would become permanent, lined with glossy folded leaves. Rootlets would rib my underside. The plateau would remember me as a reliable passage for water, and I would deepen into a proper sluice court, perhaps even a chamber-mouth leading to warmer interior reservoirs.

Or I might fail.

A storm could overload the higher terrace. Too much water could arrive too fast. The ramp to the north might slump into me, blocking my opening. My membranes might blister and delaminate. Fungi would enter the loosened layers. My pads would yellow, soften, collapse. The knot would withdraw sugars. Neighboring tissues would seal me off. I would become compost packed into a hollow beneath some later, stronger floor.

That would not be death in the way an animal means death. It would be reassignment.

I would be eaten by the plateau and remain the plateau.

So: I would find myself in the lower courts, where the vast living architecture is still most ambiguous—open enough to look like a city, wet enough to reveal that the city is only a behavior of water.

And I would be a young pressure-sensitive drainage membrane, briefly wearing the form of a plaza.

I would be one of the plateau’s undecided surfaces: warm, green, springing, veined with amber, listening through saturation, opening when the rain becomes too heavy to hold.
