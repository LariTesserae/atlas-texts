I would find myself where the city is most itself: not in the central square, where the alder-boilers breathe for everyone to see, and not out on the flats where the basin has not yet learned manners, but in the band between them—where mapped streets begin to lose confidence.

I think I would be in the hinge alleys behind the municipal engine house, among the doors that no longer fit their frames.

There is a district there that people use without naming much. It lies just beyond the tram convergence, down a side passage roofed in pipes and black leaves, where the ground rises by the width of a finger every few days and the second floors have all grown wooden lips to meet the walkways. It smells of wet wool, hot mineral water, brass polish, fungal felt, and the sour green sap of vines trained too close to steam. The alley is narrow enough that two loaded carts cannot pass, but somehow it contains a cobbler, a dead-letter pigeon office, a moss registrar, three families, a vat-room, a public shrine built around a leaking pressure valve, and a stair that leads down to what was once a cellar and is now legally classified as an “intermittent throat.”

That is where I would be.

Not important, not central, but necessary in a way that makes no announcement.

I would be a threshold fitter’s apprentice, or perhaps something a little less fully human than that title admits.

I would be one of the people who comes in the mornings with a roll of oiled tools, a packet of chalk, a plumb line, a hinge gauge, a soft mallet, a strip of listening brass, and a dog that is not mine but accompanies me when it chooses. My work would be to make doors possible again.

Not to make them straight. No one sensible asks for straightness. Straightness is expensive and short-lived.

My work would be to notice what kind of unstraightness has occurred.

A door that sticks at the top means one thing. A door whose latch has dropped below its keeper means another. A threshold sweating clear water in cold weather is different from one sweating black water in dry weather. A door that opens inward one day and refuses the next may only be swollen with damp, or the room behind it may have become lower, older, softer, more receptive, or unwilling to be entered by anyone carrying iron.

I would learn to knock not on doors but beside them.

Three taps on brick. Two on root. One on the frame. Then wait.

If the wall answers with a dry tick, the jamb can be shaved. If it answers with a muffled gulp, mark the lintel and fetch a crack listener. If moss has grown around the bottom hinge overnight, do not scrape it yet; first ask the household whether anyone inside has been dreaming of marshland.

My hands would be cold most of the time. The skin around my nails would be dark with graphite, grease, and green-black lichen stain. I would keep little wedges of alderwood in my pockets, each cut to a different angle, because the city has opinions about ninety degrees and none of them last. My coat would smell permanently of tallow and mineral dust. I would own three pairs of gloves and lose them constantly in other people’s altered rooms.

But I do not think I would be only an apprentice.

There would be something about me that makes the work choose me.

Maybe I was born in one of the western impression blocks during a receptive week, when the street took the shape of everything passing over it. My mother, heavily pregnant, crossed a soft courtyard between rain pulses; the clay accepted her bootprints, the hem of her coat, the weight of her hand against a wall, and perhaps also the shape of the child not yet outside her. When the ground hardened, there remained beside the cart tracks and leaf marks a shallow oval depression with no visible maker. People put a slate over it, as one does. They did not speak of it often.

So I grew with a slight talent for edges.

I can tell when a room is becoming a cellar before the floor drops. I can feel, in my teeth, whether a staircase still leads to the same place as yesterday. I dislike thresholds that have been forced. I pause at doorways not out of politeness but because there is always a negotiation happening there: inside and outside, warm and cold, named and unnamed, built and born.

As a child I would have been narrow, quiet, competent in the ordinary basin way. I would have collected old hinge pins, pigeon rings, moth wings, and chips of brick that had begun turning into shell-chalk. I would have played map games badly because I trusted the drawn streets less than the blank spaces between them. Teachers would have said I was inattentive until the day I refused to enter the arithmetic room and, ten minutes later, the floor exhaled a bloom of white clay through the stove grate.

After that they would have sent me to a threshold fitter.

My master would be a woman named Orra Venn, though everyone would call her Venn-of-Doors, as if she were less a person than a function the city had developed. She would have shoulders like a loading dock, silver moss scars on one cheek from an old subsidence, and the habit of listening to hinges the way doctors listen to lungs. She would speak rarely, mostly in corrections.

“Never trust a clean crack.”

“Shave the frame, not the city.”

“That one’s not stuck. It’s waiting.”

“Don’t step where the dog won’t insult.”

Her workshop would hang from the side of a former bathhouse now braced by willow roots and municipal iron. It would contain doors removed from buildings that no longer exist, doors waiting for buildings not yet approved, doors with portholes, doors with frost on the inside, doors with handles that bead sideways with damp, doors whose lower halves are older than their upper halves. The ceiling would be low except in one corner, where it continues upward into fog and birds sometimes descend with brass tags meant for other addresses.

We would be summoned by chalk mark, pigeon slip, pipe-tap, or child.

Most calls would be ordinary.

A bakery door lifted by root pressure and trapping the morning bread inside.

A tenement stairwell whose entrance has narrowed in sympathy with a cold front.

A mortuary screen swelling shut and worrying the family.

A school cloakroom where the hooks have migrated six inches east but the wall insists they have not.

A bathhouse cubicle door opening now into a reedbed, temporarily but inconveniently.

We would fix what could be fixed. Plane, wedge, rehang, brace, seal, loosen, rename. Sometimes one must give a door a new direction. Sometimes a threshold must be raised to meet a floor that has become ambitious. Sometimes one installs a second lintel under the first, leaving room for the wall to think. Sometimes the correct repair is to remove the door entirely and hang a felt curtain until the building decides whether privacy remains structurally advisable.

I would like that work.

Not because it is grand, but because it is exact. Because it admits impermanence without surrendering to it. Because a door that opens cleanly for one more week is a civic good.

In this city, there are heroic professions: surveyors walking into unmapped districts; crane crews collaring newborn hills; botanists bargaining with root systems under public buildings. But threshold work belongs to the daily intimacy of survival. People notice a working door only after it has failed. A mother with two buckets, an old man with bad knees, a child carrying broth, a mechanic coming off night shift, a dog nosing ahead of a tram crew—each depends on some small agreement between wall and opening.

I would be a keeper of those agreements.

And yet, as I imagine myself there, I keep becoming less clearly human.

The basin does that. It uses categories until they soften.

Perhaps I am called an apprentice because that is easier for everyone, but I was not born in the usual way. Perhaps I was found under one of the hinged paving stones at dawn, not among basalt knuckles or ceramic worms but folded in a membrane of shale-pale skin, breathing shallowly, warm at the joints. The morning lifters would have argued whether I was a geological birth, an abandoned child, or municipal property. Someone would have wrapped me in moss felt before the inspectors arrived. Venn-of-Doors, passing by with a tool roll and no patience for committees, would have said, “That’s a child if it learns a trade,” and taken me.

This would explain certain things.

Why I do not mind the cold but cannot bear dry heat.

Why my bones ache before upward flows.

Why mineral water tastes sweet to me.

Why hinges quiet when I hold them.

Why my footprints, in receptive clay, look a few days older than everyone else’s.

Why the dogs accept me but do not entirely trust me.

I might have a seam along my sternum like a closed crack. My hair might hold damp even indoors. My eyes might be an ordinary gray until seen in low light, when they take on the faint slate-green of the braided river. Under stress, tiny pale nodules might rise along my knuckles and then subside if someone shades them with a hand.

No one would mention these things directly. The city is full of arrangements.

As long as I work, pay tokens, follow ordinances, and report dangerous changes, I am a person in all useful senses.

Still, there would be days when I feel the basin recognizing me.

Not speaking. Not calling. Nothing so theatrical.

Only a pressure through the soles of my boots while I kneel at a threshold, as if the ground below is remembering a tool it misplaced. The city under the city shifts, young and cold and crowded with beginnings. A doorframe leans. The plaster sweats. Moss darkens on brass.

I place one palm against the jamb.

Wait.

There: a slow answering pulse, not blood, not water, not machine.

I mark the frame with chalk.

DO NOT FORCE.  
REHANG BY EVENING.  
ROOM MAY DEEPEN.

That would be my handwriting all over the district: small white instructions on brick, pipe, lintel, slate. Not laws exactly. Hints. Courtesies. Warnings from someone who belongs a little to both sides of the wall.

I would live above the workshop in a room that has required six names in four years.

At first it was a loft. Then, after a roof thicket spread over the skylight and the adjacent alley rose, it became a side room. For a month it was legally a third-floor cellar because the building around it changed height faster than the registry could follow. Now it is simply “the apprentice’s room,” which may be the most stable name it has had.

There would be a narrow bed hinged to the wall, a shelf of salvaged handles, a basin fed by condensation from a copper pipe, three pots of bitter herbs, a moth-map pinned to felt, and a window that no longer opens to the same view twice in a week. Some mornings I would see the engine-house alders steaming against the pale sky. Some mornings only a brick wall furred in yellow lichen, very close. Once, perhaps, I would wake to find the window looking downward into a courtyard full of black water and white eels, though our building had not moved in any direction recognized by surveyors.

I would keep a ledger.

Not a diary. People in the basin mistrust diaries; they imply that events remain still enough to be arranged. A ledger is more honest.

Entries like:

Third day of low fog. Rehung Salla bakery rear door. Yeast behaving anxiously. Advise root inspection.

Blue condensation on north handles, engine ward. Dogs reluctant near tram spur seven.

Door at 19 Reedhook opens onto same kitchen but colder by two hours. Return with Venn.

Mineral tooth under widow Ives’s threshold. Removed. Wrapped. She kept it for luck against advice.

Dreamed of numbered stones lifting themselves. Woke with clay under nails.

On free evenings, if such a thing exists there, I would walk the elevated ways above the market. I would buy hot root broth in a tin cup and drink it with both hands wrapped around the metal. I would watch crowds moving nearly silently below: oilcloth backs, dark hoods, brass pigeon flashes, ponies steaming, dogs pausing with one forepaw raised. Vendors would slap chalk prices onto boards. Someone would sell translucent pipe-fruit, warm and veined, in twists of newspaper-map already obsolete. The fountain would not be running because drizzle has begun, exactly nineteen minutes of it, and the black water waits underground for clearer weather.

A siren would start somewhere west and stop too quickly.

Everyone would hear it. No one would look for long.

That is another thing I would have learned: attention must be rationed. If one responds to every strangeness, one becomes useless by noon. You notice, classify, mark, act if needed, continue.

But perhaps my private weakness would be unmapped extensions.

When a street lengthens beyond its map, sensible people do not follow alone. Every tram stop says so. Every child knows the rule. The reason is not that the street will eat you, necessarily. It may simply continue into a version of the city that has not made room for your return. It may show you future brick, or old sea, or houses whose doors remember your hands though you have never fitted them. It may decide you are surveyor, trespasser, seed, or symptom.

I would know this better than most.

Still, I would be tempted.

Because some evenings, after work, when the western roofs dilute into gold and the cold blue begins gathering under the walkways, I would see an alley where no alley was that morning. Not dramatic. Just a narrow continuation between a vat shed and a wall of soot-wet brick, with moss untouched by boots and a line of doorframes leaning inward as if listening.

No sign. No municipal cordon yet. No dog nearby to disapprove.

From within: a ticking sound like obsidian ribs extruding and subsiding in shade.

I would stand at its mouth with my broth cooling in my hand.

The city would exhale unfinished rain.

I would think: tomorrow surveyors will come. Tomorrow it will be named, measured, warned against, assigned a ward condition, printed on a map already becoming false.

But tonight it is only an opening.

And openings are my trade.

I would not go far. I hope I would not go far.

I would chalk an arrow on the wall behind me. Tie red cloth to a pipe. Count my steps. Test each stone. Listen before crossing any threshold. All the proper precautions one takes just before doing something improper.

Inside, perhaps, I would find doors without buildings, hung upright in cold air. Or a row of houses only half-extruded, their lower bricks soft as clay, their upper windows reflecting skies from other pools at the basin edge. Perhaps there would be a courtyard impressed with footprints that have not yet been made. Perhaps one of them would fit me.

Perhaps that is where I would most nearly discover what I am.

Not monster. Not citizen only. Not worker only. Not geological accident only.

A threshold thing.

A civic half-birth.

A person-shaped agreement between the built city and the undecided ground.

I would return before full dark, because Venn would be waiting in the workshop with a lamp lit and no expression.

She would say, “Well?”

I would place on the table whatever I had brought back: a hinge warm to the touch, a knob beaded with sideways rain, a chip of brick containing a tiny fossil shell from a sea no one remembers, or simply a new silence in my hands.

Then we would add a note to the morning board:

UNREGISTERED PASSAGE BEHIND VAT SHED.  
DO NOT ENTER WITHOUT DOG, LINE, AND WITNESS.  
LIKELY DOORS.

By dawn, the passage might be gone. Or longer. Or inhabited. Or official.

Work would begin again.

That is where I would be found: kneeling in cold damp light at the base of a difficult door, cheek close to the wood, one hand on the frame, listening for the city’s next small refusal.

And what would I be?

A threshold fitter.

A map-skeptic.

A basin-born almost-person, legally sufficient.

A maintainer of openings in a place where walls become cliffs, rooms become cellars, streets develop intentions, and nothing stays itself long enough to be taken for granted.

Not someone who stops change.

Someone who teaches it to leave a usable door.
