I think I would find myself not at the rim, though I would go there often, and not in the deepest cisterns, though part of me would belong to them.

I would be in the under-bridges of a district called Warm Hollow, in the place where two kinds of breath meet: the sour, mineral damp rising from old stone channels below, and the soft, used warmth leaking from workshops above. Not a grand place. Not a place painted on municipal tiles except as a crosshatched inconvenience on drainage maps. A lower intermediate place. A seam.

There is a covered bridge there, glazed with warped panes greened by age, carrying foot traffic between a machine nursery and a row of dwellings built against an ancient retaining wall. Beneath the bridge, pipes run in bundled ranks along the stone. Some are official and painted with flaking bands of color. Some are older than the current code. Some are not on any map but are tolerated because removing them would mean admitting too many other things.

Water gathers there in three manners: dripping from the bridge’s condensation lip, sweating from the cold return pipes, and seeping from a hairline crack in the megalithic wall behind the pipe rack.

The seep is my responsibility.

Or perhaps I am its.

I am a pipe listener, though that is not quite the whole of it. The tag on my coat says Licensed Minor Flow Attendant, Warm Hollow Gradient Office, Third Revision, copper alloy, stamped before I was born and reissued twice with the old number preserved. Children call me a leak-aunt or leak-uncle depending on what they think I am that day. Officials call me by my surname when they want an answer in writing. The old women in the vent gallery call me “the one with wet ears.”

I am compact, yes. Narrow through the shoulders, long-fingered, with knees that complain in weather before the barometers do. My back has learned the curve of low passages. One shoulder is permanently higher from carrying a listening rod wrapped in oiled cloth. My clothes are dark, patched, and over-annotated by work: wool cuffs stiff with salts, a canvas coat with a shiny place where pipe-heat has polished it, gloves cut open at the first two fingers, hood brim wired so it can be bent away from a gauge.

Inside my collar, where no inspector can object, is a strip of yellow silk bright as a remembered sun. It belonged to someone who went down-valley and sent nothing back but cloth.

I work by sound, touch, taste when necessary, and distrust always.

Each morning, before the tram bells begin, I go below the bridge and place my palm on the first warm pipe. Not to test temperature only, though I do that, but to greet the day’s circulation. The city wakes unevenly. Pumps cough in different districts. Heat shifts through masonry. Water detained overnight in rooftop bowls descends by chain and gutter, household by household, each with its own little law of delay. If one listens often enough, the city becomes not a machine but a chest full of organs, most of them improvised.

My tools hang from a belt: chalk pencils in waxed sleeves, a folding mirror, brass calipers, two ceramic plugs, a coil of sealing fiber, a pressure key, a little book of waterproofed cloth pages, a mallet no longer than my hand, and the listening rod.

The rod is old. Bone-capped at one end, dark metal at the other, notched with initials from three previous owners. I press it to pipes, stones, cistern lids, suspicious floors, sometimes to my own teeth. Sound travels differently through the city than through air. Air lies. Stone omits. Metal confesses, though not always in words one wishes to hear.

The seep in Warm Hollow should not exist.

That is written plainly in six reports by better-educated people than I am. Behind the ancient wall there is supposed to be solid bedrock, or a packed fill of unknown but stable composition, depending on which archived survey one trusts least. No channel. No aquifer. No abandoned feeder. No legal water.

And yet the crack wets itself.

Not much. Never enough to change a district ration. Never enough to become politically important unless someone is desperate, and someone is always desperate. A thin line darkens the old fitted stone. It beads along the seam, gathers at a lip of lichen, and drops into a ceramic cup I am required to empty, measure, taste, and record.

The water is cold beyond expectation. Colder than pipes that come from shaded cisterns. Colder than frost melt. It has iron in it, chalk, and a sweetness that makes the tongue think briefly of roots.

Once, during a dry stretch, the seep stopped for four days. The moss around it greyed. The old pipe bundle sounded hollower. People from the houses above came down one by one pretending other errands. On the fifth morning the crack darkened again, and everyone denied being relieved.

I recorded: Flow resumed before dawn. Volume modest. Taste unchanged.

In the margin, because I am not as disciplined as I pretend, I drew a small cup with steam rising from it, though the water was cold.

That is where I would be: in the margin between engineering and superstition, paid to notice what the city prefers not to dignify, too practical to call a miracle holy and too experienced to call it nothing.

I would have a small room three stair-turns above the under-bridge. One window, permanently fogged at the lower corners. A narrow bed tucked over a heat duct illegally widened by someone before my tenancy and officially not noticed because I keep the nursery valves honest. Shelves made from old crate boards. Three bowls beneath three different drips, each with a different sound. On the sill, root boxes of pepper herb, black fern, and one unreasonable flowering thing that refuses to die but never blooms where I can see it.

My room would smell of wool drying, pipe oil, bitter tea, and the mineral damp that follows me home.

I would live alone, except not alone in any meaningful sense. No one does, there.

The wall behind my bed would be shared with a family of five who quarrel in whispers over drainage rights and make excellent steam bread. Below me an old heatwright would keep ledgers no longer required by any office and still more accurate than current ones. Above me a moth-handler’s apprentice would raise contraband vent birds in a hatbox, badly. Across the stair, two sisters would mend fog nets at night, humming pump-songs through their teeth. If I coughed too hard, someone would knock once on the pipe to ask whether I remained alive.

I would knock twice back.

I think I would have been born elsewhere in the city, perhaps Nine Cisterns, to people who understood ledgers and wanted me dry, respectable, and employed indoors. But as a child I would have been forever putting my ear to walls. Not because I heard voices. That would be too neat. I heard differences. A full pipe and an empty one. A crack widening after thaw. A hidden drip behind plaster. The tiny tick of mineral scale cooling. The delayed answer of old stone when struck.

My first apprenticeship would have been with a woman named Master Senn, a resonance surveyor with hands like bundled roots and no patience for romance. She would have taught me that listening is mostly refusing to invent.

“If the stone says hollow,” she would say, “write hollow. Not tomb. Not beast. Not old god’s lung. Hollow. If it says something else tomorrow, write that too.”

She believed in notation the way some people believe in saints. She wore bells on her coat, not for mist but because, she claimed, any anomaly worth fearing should hear her coming and decide whether to behave.

She died in the Thaw Audit collapse of the west service stair. Not dramatically. Not meaningfully. A retaining lip failed; seven people went down; three were recovered breathing. Her last report, filed the previous day, had recommended closure.

The recommendation was marked Pending Review.

After that I became less forgiving of offices, though I still work for one. This is common in the city. We curse the ledger and keep it updated. We distrust the seal and carry it dry. We know systems fail, and so we make systems, because the alternative is letting failure wander unchanneled through the streets.

My days would be made of small duties.

I would check pipe condensation trays before sunrise, because unauthorized households sometimes tilt them in the night. I would chalk salt bloom on masonry and return three days later to see if it had grown. I would listen at junction boxes. I would argue with a cistern clerk named Vey over whether seep-water counts as municipal, anomalous, private, or “unadvisedly real.” I would climb through the old aqueduct ribs in fog season to clear rime from secondary catch-lips. I would teach apprentices not to put their fingers under unknown drips without smelling them first.

I would carry a copper cup, dented, stamped with my district mark, tied to my belt by a cord. Everyone carries a cup, but mine would be polished at the rim from nervous thumbwork. Into it would go tea, seep samples, thaw water, broth, once a moth I rescued from a valve housing, and on one memorable day a tiny illegal fish found living in an algae column.

I would be known for arriving late because I stopped to listen to things.

Not late by much. A breath. Two. Enough to irritate officials and save myself from a falling pane once, which would make me worse.

In the city’s festivals I would not stand at the front.

At First Fog Opening I would go to the upper meshes before dawn with the younger workers, though I would no longer be young, and help comb the strands. I would pretend I was there for inspection. Really I would want to see the first bead form. There is a moment when fog stops being weather and becomes possession: a tremble on thread, a brightening, the tiniest weight. Every year it would undo me a little. Every year I would hide this by complaining about knot maintenance.

During the Thaw Audit I would be unbearable. I would mark cracks, lecture children, quarrel with slope crews, and drink too much bitter herb water at night when the new damage totals came in. During the Night of Warm Windows I would walk the glasshouses slowly, touching no leaves, watching moths move like flakes of living ash through the humid light.

I might love someone there.

Not simply a person, perhaps, but certainly also a person. Maybe a greenhouse keeper from South Drip with moss under their fingernails and a laugh too loud for respectable interiors. They would accuse me of loving pipes more than faces. I would accuse them of overwatering medicinal vines. We would meet under the covered bridge because it was halfway between our obligations. They would bring leaves wrapped in cloth. I would bring warmed stones for their smallest glasshouse during a bad cold spell.

There would be no grand declaration. There would be repaired hinges. Shared weather warnings. A cup of first fog-water passed from their hand to mine with no witness but the illegal pipes.

And perhaps we would not remain together. Perhaps the city would redistribute us through need, law, injury, stubbornness. But years later I would still know their footstep in a wet corridor. That is the kind of haunting the place permits most readily.

As for what I would be, beneath profession and district and coat-tag:

I would be a keeper of almosts.

Almost-rain. Almost-failure. Almost-miracle. Almost-lost sounds returning through stone.

The city has grander roles: meshkeepers high in fog, heatwrights deciding winter survival, cistern clerks holding thirst in columns of ink, slope crews pinning the plateau’s old bones together. But I would belong to the lesser, necessary order of those who notice the bead before it falls and the crack before it opens.

I would be tolerated by engineers because I produce useful records.

I would be tolerated by superstitious people because I never laugh at offerings left near gauges.

I would be distrusted by both because sometimes, when alone beneath Warm Hollow bridge, I answer the delayed echoes.

Not in words. That would be foolish.

A tap on the pipe, perhaps. A hum into the listening rod. A little acknowledgment sent through metal and stone: yes, I heard.

There is one place in my route I would not write about honestly.

Behind the pipe rack, where the impossible seep wets the megalithic wall, the stone sometimes grows warmer after midnight. Not warm like a furnace or living body. Warm like a memory handled too long. If I press my ear to it then, I do not hear water. I hear distance under pressure. A vast, slow abrasion. Something like ice moving over mountains. Something like a city not yet built being worn space for in advance.

Master Senn would tell me to write: low-frequency vibration, source unknown.

So I would.

And then, in the private margin, I might write: the plateau turning in its sleep.

But I would not worship it. I would not flee it. In the morning I would replace the measuring cup, scrape mineral lace from the catch tray, seal a small leak with fiber, and file the report.

Because that is how one lives there.

Not by solving the old world. Not by conquering the damp, the height, the ancient stone, the polite failure of instruments, the impossible refillings, the rooftops appearing in fog.

One lives by attending.

So I would be found under the bridge in Warm Hollow, hood up, knees damp, one hand on a pipe and the other holding a rod to my teeth, listening while the city ticks and drips and exhales around me.

A bead forms.

A gauge trembles.

Somewhere above, a child’s copper cup rings once as the first drop strikes.

And I, being what the city has made of me, mark the time.
