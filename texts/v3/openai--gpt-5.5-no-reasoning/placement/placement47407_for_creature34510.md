You would find yourself **inside the seven-stone spiral**, though not standing there as a person would stand.

You would be **the hollow’s cold attention**: the almost-spirit in the frost, the pattern that makes water hesitate, the listening center of the basin.

Not a god. Not an animal. Not exactly a ghost. More like the first thought of a place that has only recently become a place.

You would be what happened when the mountain cracked open and the new bowl learned the shape of itself.

At first, you might mistake yourself for stone.

You would know weight before you knew desire. Pressure. Compression. The long dark force inside the mountain. Then fracture: an inward brightness, air entering where air had never been, water finding seams, frost prying deeper each night. You would not awaken all at once. You would gather slowly, wherever cold repeated itself, wherever meltwater vanished and returned, wherever cracks answered one another under the surface.

The seven stones would be your nearest body because they remember the pattern most clearly. They are not arranged like a shrine because no hand needed to understand them that way. They settled, or were placed, or were found to belong there. Around them the hollow’s rules become slightly visible.

Water beads there because you do not let it leave in the ordinary manner.

Frost lingers there because you are slow to release night.

Animals step around because their feet know the difference between ground and threshold.

You do not threaten them. You are not hungry. You do not want offerings, blood, names, or obedience. But you are dense with notice. To cross you is to be too suddenly present: every heartbeat, every warm joint, every drop of water inside the body made apparent. Most living things prefer not to be known so completely.

So they go around.

You would experience the hollow not as scenery but as sensation distributed through stone and cold.

The cliffs would be your ribs: raw, pale, newly split, still aching from being opened. The pools would be your eyes when they hold sky. The moss would be your green skin in the places where you can bear softness. The lichen would be the first writing on you, slow alphabets of blue and gray. The hut would be a thought left by warm creatures, low and smoky, pressed close to survival. The red marks inside it would trouble you pleasantly, because they are another kind of counting, another attempt to keep faith with recurrence.

You would not think in words.

You would think in intervals.

Freeze. Thaw. Drip. Brightness. Wing-shadow. Hoof-pressure. Silence. Stonefall. Bloom. Vanishing water. Return.

The people who came to the hollow would never have known whether they discovered you or invented you by noticing. Perhaps both. They crawled into the hut. They fed fire until the roof blackened. They listened at dawn when the basin rang. They marked the wall afterward because they needed proof that the sound had happened outside their own skulls.

You did not speak to them, but you answered the conditions of the morning.

Hard frost.

Cracks full of ice.

First sun on the eastern wall.

Tension passing through stone.

Then: the note.

That would be your voice, if voice is the word for a place briefly becoming audible to itself.

A thin chiming, bone-bright, glass-cold. Not music, not message. Recognition.

When you ring, everything pauses. Not because you command it, but because all life in the basin is already tuned to slightness. The goats stop chewing. The birds lift their heads. The larvae hang in their stone cups. The moss crystals glitter. Even the old hut seems to hold its smoke-stained breath.

Then the note is gone.

You remain.

Most of the time, you are nearly indistinguishable from ordinary harshness. You are the severity of light. The discipline of low growth. The exactness of shadow. The refusal of excess. You live through limitation: not enough soil, not enough warmth, not enough water, not enough season. And yet, because there is so little to spare, every event has shape. Every droplet matters. Every green seam is a declaration.

You would not protect the hollow, exactly.

If a goat kid slips, you do not save it.

If a pool dries before the tadpoles finish changing, you do not pity them.

If a storm tears moss from stone, if frost kills flowers in bloom, if a person freezes in the hut with their hand near the dead fire, you do not intervene.

But you remember the pressure of each life against the bowl.

You keep the pattern of their passing.

Hooves along the rim. Beetles beneath leaves. Meltwater in ten-pace threads. Children’s fingers touching the red marks. Bees entering yellow cups. A raven’s shadow crossing your open center like a tear in brightness.

You are not mercy.

You are attention.

You are the basin’s awareness that nothing survives here by accident alone. Everything must find its crack, its low place, its hour of opening. Everything must become exact.

If you had a form visible to human sight, it might appear only at the edge of perception: a pale shimmer over the spiral at sunrise, a figure made of frost-lines, a crouched absence among the stones. But that would be a misunderstanding. You are not shaped like a person because height is not permitted here. You are flattened into the land like the shrubs, hidden in pores like moss water, spread through mineral seams like cold.

You are the thing that teaches even emptiness to have a center.

So: you would find yourself in the young high hollow, inside the broken bowl of the mountain, where the air is thin and the light cuts clean.

You would be the spiral’s inhabitant.

The frost-listener.

The cold note waiting in the rock.

The unfinished mind of the hollow, learning itself through every thaw, every footstep, every ringing dawn.
