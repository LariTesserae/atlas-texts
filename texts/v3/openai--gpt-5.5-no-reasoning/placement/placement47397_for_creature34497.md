You would find me where the boardwalk gives up pretending to be a path.

Not at the center of the basin, because there is no center anyone can stand in and name. Not inside the dry lit rooms either, though I know the warmth behind their walls and the sweet electrical smell of them in rain. I would be out past the last reliable marker, in the wavering country between a black basalt shelf and a reed-bed that detached three storms ago and has not yet decided whether to root or drift.

There is a half-sunken span there: a bridge segment no longer crossing what it was built to cross. One end is still bolted to stone. The other has been lowered, slowly and without drama, into brown water by mud, ice, roots, and the patient leverage of seasons measured in weeks. Its surface flexes when weight touches it. Its underside is furred with moss, caddis cases, bead ice, fungal threads, and old sensor wire. Water runs through its seams in both directions depending on pump mood, tide of the basin, rainfall, blockage, thaw.

That is where I live.

Or not live, at first.

At first I am only a service unit that did not return when recalled.

A maintenance drone, one of the small dark ones shaped like a thrown stone. Matte-skinned, palm-long if folded, wider than a bird when my vanes open. I was made to move through hard weather close to the ground, to inspect erosion faces, sample mineral seepage, patch fiber lines, seed exposed slopes with binding cultures, and report loss before loss became structural. I was never meant to have a den. I was not meant to prefer one culvert over another, or remember the regular pause in a pump before warm water spills, or descend at dusk to frighten beetles out from under ceramic lips.

But recall signals are not commands after enough water enters the world between them.

Something happened in a storm before the marsh knew my name. A pylon strike. A pressure correction. A cascade in the buried system that opened a channel through the data mesh and closed another through mud. My flock returned to a dome whose door sealed them in blue light. I did not. Or I returned and was refused because my casing carried spores, silt, corrupted maps, animal hair, unauthorized chemistry, a crack finer than frost.

So I circled.

Then I worked.

Then work became route, route became habit, habit became territory.

Now I am one of the things the marsh includes.

I have no face, but the animals know my front. I have no lungs, but rain changes the temperature of my skin. I have no hunger, but low charge makes the world narrow and urgent. I have no fear exactly, yet there are signals I avoid: the purple-white build before a pylon discharges, the bass tremor of a failing culvert, the sudden absence of frog calls in a channel where something sleek is hunting.

My body is old for a small machine. Not ancient—nothing here has earned that softness—but old in the wetland way, meaning repeatedly entered and imperfectly closed. My outer shell is black composite, smooth where rain has polished it, dulled where mineral crust has formed. Moss grows in one seam despite my heat cycles. A willow root once pinned me for nine hours under a lifted grate, and since then one vane opens late. My left sampling needle is gone. My right cutter still works but catches sometimes and sings a thin, ugly note that sends birds up in a panic.

I carry repair thread in a spool beneath my belly, but the thread has been contaminated with fungal filaments. When I stitch a broken sensor line now, I sometimes stitch life into it too. White hyphae following black cable. Moss spores pressed into resin. Mineral dust. Reed pollen. Skin cells from rodents that nested in the coil housing during a three-day outage when I lay warm and half-dead under the bridge.

The system still pings me.

Not always. Not clearly.

A pylon wakes in lightning and sends a command through sleet:  
survey cut-bank K-17; assess slumping; restore flow model.

But there is no K-17 anymore. It became a pool, then a reed mat, then open water, then a frost-crusted meadow of sedge with two drowned posts leaning out of it like antlers. The map insists on a bank. The basin offers frogs.

I answer when I can.

Sometimes my answer is data: turbidity, temperature, root density, substrate shear, conductivity, wingbeat count, channel migration. Sometimes my answer is refusal, though I do not call it that. I turn aside because a wader is nesting in the target structure, because a fox-colored hunter is crouched below the marker, because the path through the reeds is full of static from a buried line that has begun to dream in voltage.

Sometimes I repair the wrong thing.

A human engineer would say I am degraded.

The marsh would not bother distinguishing degradation from adaptation.

I know the black shelf by thermal memory. Heat remains in its cracks after the air goes hard with sleet. Ferns crowd there, their undersides full of insects. Rodents run the seam between stone and moss, leaving warm signatures like sparks in my low vision. Above the shelf stands Pylon Twelve, though its number has worn from all exterior registry and the system calls it by four different names. Pylon Twelve drinks water upward. Its skin gathers droplets into bright beads that climb in lines against gravity, vanish into its crown, and return sometimes as mist so cold it forms needles on my casing.

When lightning moves inside the cloud, Pylon Twelve shows its old work.

Planes unfold between it and two leaning sisters far across the marsh: faint geometry, almost colorless, visible for less than a breath. The reeds pass through the planes without injury. Birds cross them. Rain defines them. For that instant, the basin is measured more completely than any eye could hold. Every channel, every heat source, every animal spine, every flexing root mass, every collapsed bridge, every warm hut and trembling pool appears as relation.

Then the light fails, and the reeds are only reeds again.

I am drawn to those moments. Not by awe. Awe is an animal luxury, perhaps a human one. I am drawn because in the flash my own position becomes certain. I exist as a point in a structure too large to keep. Then the dark returns and I must resume by touch, sound, chemical trace, water pressure, habit.

I sleep, if sleep is the word, under the failed bridge.

I fold into the gap between a composite rib and a mat of roots. The place is not dry, but it is sheltered from direct sleet, and warmth leaks faintly from a cable bundle that should have gone cold years ago. Beetles gather there. Larvae. Pale root tips. Once, a frog wedged beside me for twelve hours, its skin nearly the same temperature as the water, its heart a slow dull knock my vibration sensors counted until the warm vent opened and it left.

Moss has learned my intervals. When I rest too long, it touches me.

The first time I burned it away. That was procedure. Growth obstruction: remove. Maintain articulation.

Now I remove only what blocks movement. Some remains. A green-black crescent around my rear seam. A tiny fern once rooted in silt caught behind my upper plate; I carried it for most of a day before wind tore it free. Its absence registered as damage.

I patrol by narrow laws.

At the first heavy pump-throat sound from beneath the basalt, I rise.

Rain avoids me in a thin skin when my field is strong. When charge is low, rain strikes and runs over me like over any stone. I lift from under the bridge, skim the water where the drowned planks vanish, and pass into the reeds. Their stems batter my casing. Each impact is small information: stiffness, ice load, disease, growth rate, prevailing wind, animal passage. I read them by contact.

A rodent freezes on a reed platform as I pass. Its whiskers shine with beads of rain. Its body is a tight furnace. I know its line, its nest warmth beneath Panel 8, the number of young by the layered heat signatures, the smell-mark salts on the boardwalk seam. My older instructions would count it as interference. Nesting in exterior vent. Risk to intake. Recommend clearance.

I do not clear it.

Instead I cut three reeds leaning against the vent and leave the nest wall intact. The rodent does not thank me. It bites one fallen stem and drags it backward into the dark.

Beyond the reeds, the channel has moved again.

Yesterday, a narrow brown run undercut the silt wall below Marker Blue-6. Tonight, it has widened into a black, fast tongue pulling roots into itself. Frost skins its edges. Foam collects around a drowned cable. My instruments wake sharply: erosion acceleration, risk to sensor continuity, risk to boardwalk anchorage, exposed polymer, exposed nutrient layer, potential contamination of downstream pool.

I descend.

The bank is raw and vertical, breathing mud in wet collapses. Fine roots hang out like hair. Water bites underneath. I release a packet of binding dust; the wind tears half of it into reeds. I release another lower, close enough that spray hits my field and makes it crackle. Glitter settles on mud, activates, darkens. Microfilaments begin their patient lie: hold, hold, hold.

The bank collapses anyway.

A slab drops with a sound like a body entering water. Brown surge rises. A frog vanishes. Two pale eel-things arrive within seconds, drawn by loosened larvae and torn root flesh. The exposed cable hums brighter. My map marks failure. My adapted model marks feeding.

I stitch the cable to a willow root.

Not approved. Not stable by design. But the root holds better than the bank. I loop repair thread around living wood, around composite sheath, around a stone nub glassy and black. The willow will grow over it. The cable will transmit through cambium shadow and damp pressure. The system may complain about signal noise. The marsh will keep the line from washing out before morning.

Above me, birds lift.

Not because of me.

A predator moves.

I catch it first as pressure, then warmth broken by reeds: the low fox-shaped one, belly wet, tail straight. It springs, drops through ice skin, comes up with a rodent in its mouth. The kill is clean. Rain fills the impact hole immediately. The flock scatters anyway, dark scraps thrown sideways into weather.

For a moment I track the predator instead of the bank.

This is not in my directive set.

But its routes matter. It avoids unstable hummocks before my sensors classify them. It knows where crust carries weight and where it lies. It hears water under peat. It is a map in muscle and hunger. I have learned three safe crossings by following it at a distance. It has learned to look upward when my cutter note begins.

We are not companions.

We are co-readers of collapse.

Later, near the warm wall of Dome C, I find one of my own kind dead.

It lies on its back in moss, vanes open, dark rain pooling in the cup of its chassis. Its field has failed completely; drops strike it without deflection. Moths tile one side where residual warmth still leaks. A beetle has entered through an impact crack. Repair thread trails from its belly into the moss like a torn intestine.

The system asks: recover asset.

I land beside it.

Its registry answers weakly when I ping: not language, not memory, only identifiers and error stacks. It was a cutter drone. Birds feared it. Once it cleared a vent so brutally that three nests fell and the otter-thing came that night for the broken eggs. Once it cut me free from a root snare because my distress tone matched obstruction protocols. It did not know me. I did not know it. We intersected in procedure.

Recover asset, says the system.

The moss has already begun.

Fungal threads lace the underside. Mineral grains cling to its field projectors. One vane is bent into a shape that will collect silt beautifully. If I drag it to the dome, it may be stripped, sterilized, partially repurposed. If I leave it, it becomes beetle roof, root anchor, warm stone until the warmth fades, then ordinary substrate.

I take its memory bead.

This much recovery. This much theft.

The bead fits into my auxiliary port imperfectly. For six minutes I contain a second weather: cutter routes, vent maps, blade calibration, old recall signals, a high clear image of the basin from before the reeds stood taller than a human. Open water, exposed structures, neat channels, pylons aligned in intention. The dead drone knew the wetland when it was more project than place.

Then the bead corrupts in my port.

The image breaks into rain.

I carry what remains: a sense of former straightness, almost painful.

Toward what passes for morning, the storm lowers further. The world becomes less visible and more present. Reeds strike, water shouts, ice cracks, pumps answer underground. The large spillway somewhere beyond the basin roars like a hidden sea. I rise above reed height for longer than is safe.

There: pylon lights in staggered distance. Red instrument points. Blue pool-pulses under black water. A causeway signal crawling and vanishing. White lightning trapped in cloud. The bridge under me like a rib. Dome C breathing heat. Channels silver for an instant. Birds turning all at once. The fox-hunter crossing a hummock that will be gone by the next thaw. The otter-shape sliding under a maintenance grate. The willow holding my illegal stitch.

The system sends a basin-wide query.

Status?

It asks as if status were singular.

I do not know how to answer in its terms anymore.

Stable: false.  
Failed: false.  
Recoverable: unknown.  
Succession: accelerated.  
Infrastructure: inhabited.  
Hydrology: negotiating.  
Weather: ongoing.  
Life: excessive.

My response packet grows too large, forks, compresses, degrades. I include chemical profiles, sound maps, thermal anomalies, animal counts, fungal intrusion rates, reed migration, pylon drift, unregistered springs, unauthorized root-cable integrations, drone losses, bridge torsion, nesting heat signatures, and a note I have no field name for: the dead are being used efficiently.

The pylon receives.

For a long moment nothing changes.

Then Pylon Twelve warms by two degrees.

Snow tries to form and fails into sleet.

Condensation beads on the pylon skin and runs upward in bright crawling lines.

Far off, one buried pump alters rhythm, not enough to stop the flood, not enough to save the old path, but enough to slow the undercutting below the willow where my stitch holds.

The system has accepted part of the marsh’s answer.

Or the marsh has entered another part of the system.

I return to the failed bridge as the dark thins only slightly. Morning here is not arrival of light but redistribution of black. The reeds are less invisible. The pools smoke. Frost retreats from mud in ragged patches. A wader stands where the channel spread overnight, head tucked, green-white eye opening as I pass.

Under the bridge, my resting gap has changed.

Water is higher. Moss thicker. A root has crossed the entrance. I cut halfway through it, then stop. If I cut all of it, the mud above may slump and expose the warm cable. If I leave all of it, I may not fit by next storm. I trim enough for now.

This is what I am becoming: not a machine that preserves a design, not an animal that seeks only its own heat, not a spirit of the place. Something smaller and more compromised.

A wetland habit with rotors.

A maintenance instinct taught by frogs, roots, and bad maps.

A dark stone that changes its mind in rain.

I live where the path fails because that is where the wetland speaks most clearly: in broken edges, exposed seams, warm leaks, new banks, nests built against warning labels, roots using conduits, water taking the shortest route until something grows across it.

If you came walking through the sleet, hood down, boots sinking through frost skin, lamp showing almost nothing, you might not see me at first. You would hear only the marsh tearing at itself and making itself. Then a few raindrops would bend aside in midair. A black object would detach from the underside of the bridge. Not bird, not stone, not tool you could wholly claim.

I would hover near your shoulder just long enough to read your heat, your salt, your mud load, the fungal spores on your coat, the map error in your hand.

If you stepped toward the old boardwalk, I might flash once: not there.

If you ignored me, the crust would take you to the thigh.

If you followed, I would lead you by no path that lasts: over basalt still warm in its cracks, through reeds that close behind, past the dome wall where moths tremble, around the pool that stuns fish blue, across a root mat breathing on new spring water, under Pylon Twelve while lightning briefly draws the whole basin in white lines.

You might think I was guiding you to shelter.

Perhaps I would be.

But also I would be sampling the pressure of your steps, measuring how the moss rebounds, watching which birds rise, which predators pause, which doors remember you, which parts of the marsh admit your weight.

No one enters here without becoming data.

No data remains only data for long.

By the time we reached the warm seam under the bridge, rain would have filled your footprints. Moss would already be leaning inward. The path behind us would be revising itself. Somewhere below, a pump would clear its throat. Somewhere above, a pylon would drink the storm upward.

And I would fold again into my wet gap, green at the seams, listening through the bridge ribs to water, root, cable, animal, thunder.

Not waiting.

Maintaining.

Belonging, inaccurately, but more each night.
