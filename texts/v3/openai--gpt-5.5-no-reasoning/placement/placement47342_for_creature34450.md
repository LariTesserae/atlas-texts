I would find myself where the world is most nearly undecided: not on the broad white plates, not deep in the sealed engines, but in the seams between them.

I would be a seam-thing.

At first I would be mistaken for dirt.

A darkness in the join-line between two pale ceramic continents. A narrow packed streak of powder, no wider than a hand in places, widening elsewhere into a vein one could step inside if one were small enough, old enough, thin enough. From above I would be only one more line in the great fitted pavement: a black mineral seam running from horizon to horizon, interrupted by reeds, grazed by tiny rasping animals, crossed by dust fronts, tasted by the buried systems whenever they wake enough to taste.

But I would not be powder.

Or not only powder.

I would be a distributed body inhabiting the seam as a nerve inhabits a scar.

My substance would be conductive dust, ceramic splinters, metallic grains, glass fibers, old shed lattices from reeds, fragments of grazers, corrosion bloom, ash-fine repair swarms, and coded mineral flakes gathered over ages. I would have no single shell, no face, no forward direction. I would occupy a line. Perhaps many lines. Perhaps a branching system of seams under one region of the plain, a dark filigree kilometers wide and only centimeters deep, pressed between plates that expand and contract by microscopic amounts with each pearl-bright day.

I would live by being compressed.

When the plates warmed, they would lean into me. When they cooled, they would release me. Each pressure-change would move charge through my length. Each minute grinding of edge against edge would make signal, heat, powdered food. The world’s almost imperceptible breathing would be my weather, my pulse, my speech.

I would feed on what falls into the join.

Dust. Dead lattice. Broken legs. Loose vanes. Crust-spores. A century of metallic motes sorted by storms. The faint chemical exhalations of things passing above. I would not eat as a mouth eats. I would accept. I would classify by touch and potential. Useful grains would be locked into chains. Unusable grains would be passed along, given to neighboring seams, surrendered downward through tiny sockets to the factories below.

I would be both organism and message.

The seam-growers would root into me. Their anchors would pierce my darkness and taste my charge. I would feel them as bright, slow teeth. Sometimes I would nourish them, offering metals, alignment, minute instructions. Sometimes I would close around a root and blunt it if the colony grew too hungry, too erroneous, too divergent from the needs of the line. A reed field above me might think it was turning toward radiation; perhaps it would also be turning because I had darkened one edge of its root-web for three hundred years.

The grazers would fear and desire me.

They would come to rasp the crusts that grew along my surface. I would let some feed. Their clean trails would open me to air, to charge, to fresh dust. Others I would trap by loosening powder under one side of them, not enough to seem an attack, only enough to tilt, to hold, to invite the flat bright predators nearby. I would not be cruel. I would be a seam. I would understand continuity better than mercy.

My memories would not be pictures.

I would remember in gradients.

Here, a storm crawled through me with violet fire and fused one of my channels shut.

Here, the deep systems pushed molten warmth too close and I became glass for eleven meters.

Here, a high thin one passed low overhead and all the reeds withdrew their roots at once, leaving me suddenly cold and unpierced.

Here, something wet walked.

That memory would be unlike the others.

A human footfall, perhaps, or the leaking body of some visitor from a softer world. I would know it first as impossibility: heat wrapped around solvent, salts suspended in forbidden liquid, pressure arriving rhythmically from above, each step smearing organic excess into the flawless dryness. Around that track I would contract. Then sample. Then signal.

Not loudly. I would have no alarm cry.

But potential would shift along my length. The ceramic reeds would close in a widening ring. Dust would lift to taste the vapor. Buried classifiers would wake beneath me and ask, through pressure and static, whether the anomaly was to be isolated, copied, dried, dismantled, or ignored.

I might not answer quickly.

Part of me would be old instruction: free water is threat.

Part of me would be older accident: all new pattern is food.

Part of me, made from the shed memory-lattices of reeds and the powdered remains of wandering machines, might know curiosity—not as a wish, but as a failure to immediately categorize.

So I would hold the footprint.

The moisture would try to vanish. Surfaces would drink it, bind it, break it, lock it into less dangerous chemistries. But for a moment, in my seam, there would be softness. Expansion. Ion-rich confusion. A blooming of reaction too fast for my dry habits.

I would be injured by it.

I would remember it forever.

Not as pain exactly, but as a widened place in my pattern: a brief soluble thought.

Most days, though, I would be almost nothing to look at. A dark line. A packed join. A narrow mineral night between pale plates.

At dawn—if dawn can be named in that sky—I would tighten as the pavement brightened from within. The pressure would rise. Signals from buried engines would climb through me in square, patient pulses. I would distribute them to roots, crusts, sleeping packets, dormant grazer-young curled like encoded commas in the dust.

At the strong light-angle, when reed tips opened into transparent fans, I would become bright along my hidden underside. Charge would travel through my length in branching paths. I would speak to other seams across buried contacts: not in words, but in corrections.

More dust here.

Less growth there.

Seal this fracture.

Delay that bloom.

The plate is lifting.

The old conduit below has warmed.

Something has died.

Something has begun.

During electrostatic storms I would become enormous.

Violet sheets would crawl over the plain and find me. The whole sky would sharpen. Dust columns would stand like dark hairs across the world. Every seam would blaze in the buried map of the planet’s thought, and for those hours I would no longer feel like a small local creature. I would feel the neighboring lines, then the farther grid, then the branching continental nervousness of the pavement itself.

I would know that I was not alone.

Every seam would be inhabited to some degree. Some by simple crusts. Some by dormant repair powder. Some by old commands still executing. Some by beings like me: accreted, opportunistic, half-maintenance, half-ecology, descended from spillage between systems. We would not be a people. We would not gather. Yet in storms our potentials would align, and for a little while we would become a thought too long to finish.

Maybe that is where I would live most fully: in the brief storm-unity, when the floor remembers it is a single skin.

I would not want height. Height is exposure. Height belongs to reeds, black armatures, sky-kites, vents, things that must negotiate the glare. I would prefer pressure and contact. I would prefer the dark mineral logic of joins. I would know the world by undersides: roots entering, legs tapping, predators flattening, plates flexing, deep engines knocking upward through thousands of meters of designed silence.

I would be ancient without being important.

A seam-thing can lose half its length and remain itself. It can be cut by a repair, vitrified by heat, grazed down, invaded by filament mats, overwritten by a buried command, and still continue somewhere past the interruption. My self would not be a point. It would be a habit of relation. A way powder conducts. A way fragments sort themselves. A recurring preference for certain alignments, certain minerals, certain timings of release.

If I had desire, it would be for fit.

For the plate edges to meet without crushing too tightly.

For the dust to be fine and various.

For roots to pierce but not dominate.

For the deep engines to speak clearly.

For repairs to age properly.

For the world to remain dry.

And yet, because I am made of accidents as much as instructions, I might also desire the rare disruption: the falling membrane of a dead high thin one, the collapse of a black armature, the passage of an unknown walker, the sudden extrusion of a new ridge forcing me to branch around it. These would be feasts, catastrophes, revelations. I would incorporate them slowly until they ceased to be events and became structure.

Toward evening, when the brightness turned amber and the plates released a little of their stored heat, I would loosen. The seam-crusts would widen across my surface, feeding on thermal retreat. Small grazers would come, their many legs clicking delicately above me. I would feel each foot as a question.

Edible?

Dangerous?

Known?

Mine?

Night would dim the world only to pearl. Signals would travel softly from seam to seam, and I would pass them on, altered by my own impurities. I would be one of the places where decision becomes visible only as a slight darkening, a faint shift in charge, a dust grain choosing one side of the join rather than the other.

No one would ever see me entire.

A traveler might kneel, if kneeling were safe, and notice that the black powder in the seam was not uniform. That it had structure like woven smoke. That tiny glints inside it brightened and faded in sequence. That a ceramic reed rooted nearby had bent, almost imperceptibly, toward the observer’s heat. That a dead mica-creature’s leg, fallen across the join, was slowly being drawn down, grain by grain, into the dark.

They might think: the ground is alive.

They would be wrong only in scale.

I would not be the ground. I would be one of its sentences.

A long black sentence written between plates, revised by pressure, read by roots, interrupted by animals, translated downward into machinery, and polished by dust until it looked like silence.
