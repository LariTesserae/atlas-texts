I would not find myself on the surface at first.

The surface is too complete. It is the part of the world that has already answered every question about form: plate, mist, basin, valve, bright green breadth under standing rain. If I arrived there, I would be a foreign point, a dry contradiction, too loud in the wrong ways. The plateau would have to decide what kind of error I was.

So I would come into being lower down, where errors are already expected and dissolved.

Not at the deepest root-stone, either. Not among the blind copper-jawed worms and blue-black mineral seepage. That place belongs to the old hunger of the plateau, the unlit mouth by which the living continent tastes its buried mountain. Down there, identity would be almost chemical. I would be salt, pulse, scrape, voltage, root-tip.

No. I think I would be found in one of the great open halls between root curtains, under the leaf-continents but above the true dark, where the water is green-blue and cold enough to make every movement deliberate.

There, the architecture is far apart.

A root veil hangs to the west, a dim vertical nation of strands. To the east a compressor grove beats, not nearby but near enough that its interval enters the body: swell, hold, release; swell, hold, release. Above, the undersides of the floating leaves make a vaulted ceiling broken by silver air pockets. Below, root fans descend in slow perspective until they become shadow. All around, the water is full of fixed sound: wooden thunder from the turning seed-drums, the long vowel of siphons, ticking seed-caps, the faint crackle of gas.

I would live in the trembling interval between those sounds.

At first I want to say I would be one of the obvious creatures: a ribbon grazer, translucent, comb-mouthed, drifting edgewise through currents; or a pale predator shaped like torn bark; or a crab solemnly responsible for a single valve-lip. But those are too finished. They already have exact duties. Their meanings are narrow and perfect. They belong to the plateau the way syllables belong to a word.

I would be something less declarative.

Something that moves between systems without quite owning any of them.

I think I would be a keeper of bubbles.

Not a person keeping them. Not with tools, not with intention, not with a name for the work. An animal, perhaps no larger than a human forearm, long-bodied and soft-spined, with six small fins set close along the ribs and two delicate forelimbs that are almost hands but not clever enough to invent. The body would be dark olive above and nearly clear below, not transparent like the ribbon grazers but smoky, so that the gut and the slow pearl of the swim bladder could be seen when light passed through me. My skin would be slick and finely ridged to feel pressure changes, with tiny ciliated grooves along the flanks that taste dissolved gases.

I would live just under the leaf ceilings, in the region where bubbles collect.

The plateau makes bubbles constantly. They rise from microbial beds, from grazer mouths, from wounds in plant tissue, from valve-fields opening and sealing, from root hairs that release excess breath into the water. Some lodge beneath leaves and become the beginnings of air pockets. Some cling to filaments, carrying spores, mites, eggs. Some are too small and dissolve. Some are too large and burst against the wrong membrane. The balance of air is one of the plateau’s quiet laws, and where there is a law, there are creatures shaped to enforce it without understanding.

I would tend the bubbles by appetite.

My mouth would not be made for meat, exactly, nor for leaves. It would be round and soft-edged, able to sip the skin of a bubble without swallowing the whole thing. I would feed on the films that gather on air-water boundaries: oils, microbes, pollen dust, larval skins, mineral sheen, the faint sweetness of plant exhalation. To feed, I would nudge bubbles loose from one rootlet and guide them to another. I would split large ones with my forelimbs. I would gather strings of small ones under my throat until they trembled against me like beads, then release them where the ceiling fibers needed air.

My labor would look almost ceremonial from outside.

A small green-black body turning slowly beneath a vast leaf. A mouth touching silver. Fingers combing root hairs. Bubbles rising, pausing, dividing, joining. No haste. No decision. Only adjustment.

I would not know that I was maintaining the leaf-lungs above me.

Yet my movements would help keep them stable. Air chambers where fern-bats cling and small whiskered ceiling-animals bear their young would depend, in part, on the old grazing paths of my kind. Too much gas in one place and the leaf membrane strains; too little and the chamber thins, collapses, becomes water again. We would prevent both by feeding. Our hunger would have the shape of maintenance.

I would be prey, but not often.

The white-jawed still predators in the root-veils could take me if I crossed their thresholds. Needlefish might stab at my young. A glass-voiced bird could spear me from above if I came too close to a torn air pocket. But I would have the plateau’s common defense: exact habits. I would know, not as memory but as muscle, the safe pressure lanes under the leaves. I would pause when the compressor groves entered their release phase. I would flatten myself against valve-shadow when the seed-caps began their dry underwater ticking. I would never cross the trembling silence between two root curtains unless the distant turbine hum had shifted into its deeper note.

My eyes would be poor. Sight is not useless here, but it is secondary. Everything is visible and far away; everything is softened, delayed, made uncertain by green distance. I would know the world by touch before shape. A current against the jaw. A mineral sharpness in the water. The faint electrical prickling of a siphon root. The difference between a living bubble and an empty one. The pressure signature of an upside-down ungulate browsing above me: four soft hooves sealed to the ceiling, neck flexing, hide colonies stinging the water around it.

I would have no nest in the dry sense, because dry is impossible.

But there would be a place I returned to: a crease beneath the overlapping rims of three enormous leaves, where a curtain of rootlets hangs in a long shallow crescent. Behind it, a silver air chamber presses against the ceiling. Fern-bats roost inside, their heat making the membrane tremble faintly. Below the chamber, bubbles gather in reliable chains. Mites ride them. Spores darken them. Tiny shrimp cling to their undersides upside down, grazing the same films I graze.

This would be my country: perhaps thirty body-lengths across, though distance in such a place is not measured as length. It is measured as cycles.

Three compressor pulses from the rootlet crescent to the bark scar.

Nineteen heartbeats from the valve-field to the safe hollow.

One seed-drum groan from the silver chamber to the place where needlefish hold in light.

I would age without the world aging.

As a young thing I would live among the smaller bubbles, close to the root hairs, hiding in the furred green dimness with my siblings. We would be nearly transparent then, our bellies round with swallowed films, our fins too large for our bodies. Many of us would vanish into mouths. Those vanishings would not be tragic to the plateau. They would be accounted for, though not counted by anyone.

If I survived, I would darken. My forelimbs would become more precise. The ciliated grooves along my sides would lengthen. I would learn the taste of imbalance: sour water before a valve clogs, metallic brightness before a root releases, the stale heaviness of an air pocket not being grazed enough. I would move toward these tastes because they would mean food. By feeding, I would correct them.

In maturity I might carry eggs under the skin of my belly, not in a pouch but in shallow translucent pits, each egg held against my body where flowing water could pass over it. The eggs would need bubbles nearby but not touching. Too much air would dry their membranes; too little would slow them into failure. I would spend long intervals turning beneath the leaf ceiling, placing myself between rising gas strings and the eggs, letting some pass, nudging others aside. Not care as thought. Care as posture.

When the young emerged, they would cling first to me, then to bubble strings, then to the rootlet fur. They would eat what I eat, only smaller. The plateau would admit as many as it required. The others would become needlefish, crab, fungus, silt, gas.

I would not experience this as cruelty.

Nothing here has room for cruelty. Cruelty requires excess intention, a surplus of self. This world has appetites, defenses, strikes, infections, thefts, territorial solemnities. But every act is caught immediately in use. Even fear would be brief and bodily: a flash of contraction, a dart under a leaf-rib, the cold shock of displaced water as jaws open where stillness had been. Then the sound pattern returns. The compressor releases. The seed-drums continue. Bubbles rise.

If I had something like memory, it would be acoustic.

The world would be a map of intervals. The low bass from the compressor grove would tell me where my body was. The organ-note of siphon trunks would locate the eastward open hall. The ticking seed-caps would mark a region of danger, because larvae gather there and needlefish follow larvae. The glass calls of birds above the air chambers would signal torn membranes or insect blooms. The absence of a familiar click from a crab-cleaned valve might draw me near, tasting for starch leak.

I would not know history. I would know recurrence.

Perhaps, in one lifetime, a leaf above me would tear. To a human traveler this might seem an event: a green roof splitting, trapped air shuddering out in a rush of silver bodies, bats scattering, small warm animals clinging to fiber, water folding into the chamber. To me it would be food, turbulence, danger, work. Bubbles everywhere. Films torn open. Microbes blooming along the wound edge. I would feed until my belly tightened. Crabs would come. Needlefish would come. Root hairs would swell. The plant would seal. Somewhere else, another chamber would open by exactly the amount this one diminished.

No story would remain. Only the plateau’s equation solving itself through us.

And yet, from inside, it would not feel empty.

It would feel full of nearness.

The underside of a leaf is not a ceiling but a continent of textures: valves like closed mouths, pale scars, ridges of horn-hard cellulose, hanging rootlets, bead colonies, snail trails, eggs in jelly, dark places where small predators sleep, bright places where trapped air lenses the light into trembling ovals. A bubble is not a bubble but a room, a mirror, a cargo, a meal, a signal. A root curtain is not a wall but weather made of strands. A compressor pulse is not a background sound but the great permission by which water moves through the body of the world.

I would be small in a place that cannot be made small.

A bubble-keeper, film-grazer, air-herder, one of the little corrective hungers beneath the floating leaves.

If a traveler passed through my hall, I would feel them long before I saw them: a wrong pressure, large and irregular, a body displacing water without belonging to any known cycle. Not predator, not grazer, not filter-beast, not rootfall. I would flatten under the leaf-rib and hold still. The bubbles along my throat would quiver. My skin would taste warmth, salt, fear, perhaps the faint alien chemistry of dry places.

The traveler might see me then: a smoky little creature suspended upside down beneath a green plate, forelimbs spread among silver beads, eyes black and mild, body trembling with the pulse of the compressors.

They might think I was watching them.

I would not be watching, not exactly.

I would be waiting for the disturbance to become part of the pattern, or to pass.
