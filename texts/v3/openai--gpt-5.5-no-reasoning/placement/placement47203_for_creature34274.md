I would find myself low in the city, but not at its bottom.

Not among the bright upper bridges where guests first learn to lift their feet carefully, not among the roof gardens where children can still pretend there is such a thing as a horizon. I would be in the middle-lower wards, where the houses have already surrendered their first floors and learned to live above their own drowned memories. A district of half-lit thresholds, warm seep, fish-smell, lamp-horn, and cords polished by many hands.

I think I would live in a house known less by a family name than by a behavior:

**the House That Answers Late.**

It stands beside a narrow canal where the water is usually too dark to show its depth. The house leans inward over the passage as if listening to itself. Its lower rooms are flooded up past the lintels, and the old doorway is found by touch: three smooth cords, then one braided cord, then a knot shaped like a closed eye. A slow tree roots somewhere inside it, not in soil but in a broken cistern two levels below the sleeping platform. Its trunk rises through a square cut in the floor, black and glossy with damp, and its leaves collect rain from a roof court above. When the mountain ticks under the district, the tree gives the sound back a little later through its wood. That is why the house has its name.

I would belong to that house as one of its listening people, though not an elder yet. Not a priest exactly, not an engineer exactly, not a diver either, though I would have learned enough diving to be useful and enough fear to stay alive.

I would be a **wall-listener and cord-mender**.

Someone called when a family says, quietly, “This wall has begun to speak differently,” or “The lower stair no longer gives the right cold,” or “The raft garden pulls east when the water is still.” I would arrive with a shoulder bag of small tools: a bone awl, shell scrapers, cord samples rubbed with oil, clay plugs wrapped in leaves, a listening cup of fired ceramic, a packet of moss starter kept damp against my ribs, and a little set of weighted strings for testing slant.

I would spend much of my life with one cheek near stone.

Not dramatically. Not in trance. Simply working. Listening to seep behind plaster, root pressure, loosened mortar, trapped bubbles, hidden trickles beginning to teach themselves a route. I would know the difference between a wall that must be shored, a wall that must be opened, a wall that must be planted, and a wall that should be allowed to become water’s property.

My hands would be more expressive than my face. The city would have made me economical. I would speak softly, and not always quickly, because a wrong word in that place can move like rot. I would have the habit of touching every threshold before crossing it, not out of superstition only, but because thresholds report things. Heat. Tremor. Recent passage. Hidden swelling. The mood of a house.

I would be broad-shouldered from hauling wet fiber and climbing between levels, but not young enough to move carelessly. My palms would be grooved from rope. My knees would ache in the cold suspended rains that sometimes come from neighboring ridges. I would wear my hair bound close with dark cord and one pale bead from the Listening Rooms, not because I had authority there, but because once, as a child, I heard a terrace shift before anyone else did.

That would be the story people told about me, though I would dislike how much larger it grew with retelling.

I imagine it happened during an algae-glow festival. Lamps put out, the canal edges faintly green, everyone walking by the living outline of steps and hulls. I was small, perhaps still marked with bright fiber in my hair so I could be found if I slipped. I put my hand on a public stair and felt—not a crack, not yet—but an absence where the usual ticking should have been. Silent water where there should have been speech.

I must have refused to move.

Not cried out. Children there are taught not to waste sound. I would have held the nearest adult’s wrist and pressed their hand to the stone, again and again, until someone understood. The stair was closed. Two nights later, a warm fissure opened beneath it and took three lower rooms that had already been evacuated because of a stubborn child and a nervous hand.

After that, the house elders would say I had an ear for late answers.

So I would be trained.

Not elevated too quickly. This is not a people who confuse omen with skill. I would still have to learn knots until my fingers cramped, wall moss until I could smell whether it would bind or sour, depth marks, public ropes, clay tone, the civic law of drains. I would be sent to scrape algae from steps, to carry messages in cold rain, to sit beside older listeners and stay silent for whole days. I would learn that hearing something once does not make you wise. It only makes you responsible.

My work would place me between households.

That is where I belong in this world: not wholly upper, not wholly lower, always moving along the damp seams where private dwelling becomes shared survival. I would know which families hide weakness out of shame, which houses complain before flood, which roof gardens are too heavy, which marriages have tied together incompatible structures. I would be welcomed but not always happily. When I appear at a door, people know something may need to change.

I would carry no bronze except perhaps a shared district hook signed out under witness for important repairs. I would mostly trust fiber, moss, wedge, listening, and patience.

In markets, I would buy old cord if its memory was good. I would test it not only by pulling but by bending it near my ear. A bad rope complains sharply; a tired rope sighs; a rope that has been strained in flood holds a silence in its core. I would trade repairs for smoked leaves, bitter mineral salt, and root fingers from houses whose gardens grow deep enough to taste the mountain’s warmth.

I would have friends among divers, but I would not be one of them.

I would envy them sometimes: their compact calm, their strange prestige, the way people lower their voices around them. But I would also notice how long some of them pause before answering, and how they look at wells as if waiting to be recognized. My closest rope-partner might be a diver named something like **Taal-of-the-Cold-Rung**, a person who once hauled me backward from a submerged room when silt turned the water blind. We would not speak often of that. The debt would live in a knot between us.

I would be unmarried for a long time, not from loneliness, but because my work would make joining houses complicated. Who wants to marry someone who hears strain everywhere? Someone who may wake at night, touch the floor, and say, “We must move the sleeping mats upward before morning”?

Eventually perhaps I would join with a root-keeper from an upper-mid house, someone whose arms are stained to the elbow from feeding pale roots through trapdoors. Our marriage would be less romance than a long negotiation between water and appetite. Their house would be generous with leaves but poorly behaved in warm seep. Mine would answer late but hold well under tilt. The elders would pour warm and cold water over our hands while we tied and untied the same knot. I would be the one who ties too cautiously. They would laugh into their shoulder and loosen my grip.

We would not have many possessions.

A sleeping hammock. A lamp shield of mica patched twice. A clay cup that rings true above and below water. A set of house cords. A sealed packet of ancestral bone in the lower root basket. A mural along the wet wall that can only be read with fingertips: an old journey from one ridge settlement to another, a ferry lost in fog, a rope thrown at the last moment, a child born before landfall and named for the knot that held.

I would know that mural by touch better than by sight.

At dusk, I would be most myself.

I would move through the canal lanes as lamps appear behind horn screens. I would check public lashings with two fingers and a pull. I would answer questions from children who ask why one wall is saved and another is allowed to become a fish pool. I would say, because this one still shares its strength, and that one has begun another kind of work.

Then, after bridges are lifted and the night currents pass, I would sit in the House That Answers Late with my feet in warm dark water, mending cord by low amber light. Rain would continue above and around and through everything. The slow tree would return the mountain’s ticks through its trunk. Someone would sleep. Someone would murmur. Somewhere below, an eel would disturb a hanging root.

If an unfamiliar sound came through the wall, I would put down the cord.

Not afraid at once. Not calm exactly.

Ready without haste.

I would press my wet palm against the stone and listen for what the city had decided to become next.
