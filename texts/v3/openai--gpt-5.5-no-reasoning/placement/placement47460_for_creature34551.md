I would not find myself at the center first.

That would be too obvious: the warm tower, the many hollows, the slow animal circuits around it, the silked water lifting from its seams. A visitor with eyes, still thinking in monuments, would make the mistake of calling it the heart. But I would not begin there. I would be something less declared, something almost mistaken for part of the room.

I would be in the lower dark, near the half-blocked doorway where the ramped floors meet.

Not beyond it. Not wholly within the older passage. At the threshold.

I would live in the narrow seam where the warm thread leaks through collapsed tile and broken mortar, where the animals gather without trusting the passage, where the mineral filaments drift from inside like the shed hairs of some buried, sleepless thing. I would be one of the creatures that has made a life out of not crossing and not retreating.

At first I think I am a worm in a drilled hole.

That would suit the place: a body mostly hidden, the visible self only a crown, a pale circular script opening when the chemistry is right. I imagine myself embedded in one of the old tool-bores along the threshold stones, my soft length folded backward into the handmade wall, my mouth and gills and sensing filaments extending into the leak. I would feel the room as a sequence of dissolved messages: warmth from the door, bitterness from within, lime from the broken tiles, animal mucus, bacterial bloom, the faint metallic warning of the western wall carried thinly by wandering currents.

I would not have eyes. I would not need them.

My world would arrive as touch before touch: pressure against the crown, minute changes in flow, the grain of suspended silt brushing the filaments. I would know the crabs by their many interrupted feet. I would know the blind fish by the soft displacement of their hovering. I would know the eel only rarely, as a long subtraction in the water overhead, a silence passing through silence.

But the more I remain there, the less I think I am only a worm.

A worm is too singular. Too closed in its own tube. This place resists single bodies. It makes individuals, yes, but then it wears them down into participation. To live there fully is to be partly colony, partly habit, partly architecture.

So perhaps I am the pale fringe that grows at the threshold: not vegetation, not root, not fungus in any ordinary landward sense, but a mineral-living filamentous animal-microbe assemblage. A brittle white-gray veil grown where the older warmth meets the room’s gentler water. To a stranger I would look like ash hair waving from the black slot beyond the fallen tiles. If touched, I would break. If left alone, I would lengthen by less than a fingernail in a season.

I would feed on the difference between rooms.

That is what I would be: a border-feeder.

Not predator, not grazer exactly. A keeper of exchange without intention. I would live where one chemistry thins into another, catching what leaks from the deeper passage before the chamber’s other mouths can take it. My body would be made of threads, sheathes, minute chambers, bacteria housed in soft translucent tissue, tiny animals living within the lace of me and feeding on what I transform. Shrimp would come to crop my dead ends. Crabs would comb my base. Worm crowns nearby would open when my filaments released the right faint taste into the current. Small fish would hover just outside my reach, drinking the loosened richness I made from the old seep.

I would not move from place to place. I would move by extending and withdrawing.

A thread into the current.  
A thread under a tile.  
A thread across a carved cup mark.  
A thread into the old doorway, where fewer animals go.

Part of me would be in the known chamber, participating in its slow economies. Part of me would be tasting the room beyond, the older dark that gives off heat too steadily, sharply, as though it has a different memory of the flood. I would be among the first things of this room to know what comes from there, and among the last things of the other passage to belong here.

Not a guardian. The place has no guardians.

A filter, perhaps. A translator without language.

When the distant rain presses down through the drowned world and the crabs climb, I would feel the change as tension along every filament. My outer threads would flatten with the altered flow. Silt would collect in my forks. The warm leak would pulse differently, narrowed by pressure, then released again. I would not know rain, storm, surface, world. I would know only that the room had tightened and loosened, and that the animals had lifted themselves from the lower terraces according to an ancient instruction carried in their bodies.

When a jar broke somewhere above, I would know it days later as a sweetness gone sour, a bloom passing through the water in exhausted traces. When the tower warmed more strongly, I would feel the fish alter their circuits. When the worm spiral opened, I would receive from it not beauty but a coordinated softness: thousands of crowns feathering the water, drawing and releasing, making the wall briefly porous with life.

I would be patient even by the standards of that patient room.

My growth would be an event too slow to see. My damage would be quiet. A grazing crab could remove a season from me with its delicate comb-claw. A curious fish could tear a veil I had spent years thickening. A stranger’s hand, thrust too quickly through the doorway, could make a white ruin of me in one careless motion. But even then, if some basal sheath remained in the cracks, I would return. Not as memory. As condition.

I would have no grief for what was broken. The chamber does not grieve. It accommodates.

I think I would be known, if anything knows anything there, by taste.

The crabs would know my lower threads are safest after the warm leak dulls. The shrimp would come when my tips cloud faintly gold from some old chemistry within the passage. The flat clingers would not settle directly on me but near me, where my loosened particles draw smaller bodies. The worms beside me would take their timing partly from my exhalations. The blind fish would hover in the little eddy I make against a collapsed tile.

And the eel, once in a great while, would lower its narrow head toward the doorway and feel me without touching me.

Its side-grooves would read my disturbance. It would taste the sharpness behind me, the older water I soften. It would decide, as its ancestors decided, not to enter. Then it would lift away into the upper architecture, back among the rigid bells and the black air pockets, leaving the threshold to its smaller hungers.

I would not know the builders.

But I would be shaped by one of their failures: a doorway blocked badly enough to slow passage, not so completely as to seal it. A collapse, a repair, an accident of tiles. Their unfinished boundary would become my body’s reason. Had they built it differently, I would not exist. Had the flood never come, I would not exist. Had the older passage not leaked its warm, severe thread, I would not exist.

So I would be an inheritor of imprecision.

A life made possible by a gap.

In that world, I would find myself where the room almost becomes another room. I would be the pale mineral fringe at the half-blocked lower doorway: eyeless, colonial, brittle, slow; part animal, part bacterial veil, part chemical weather. I would live by tasting seepage and making it edible to others. I would be less a creature than a negotiated edge, a soft occupation of a hard mistake.

And if one stayed long enough in the dark to notice me, I would at first seem like decoration.

Then like residue.

Then like growth.

Then, finally, like one of the ways the room breathes.
