I would find myself where passage has become an afterthought.

Not in the central cleft, not at first. The cleft is too obvious, too nearly a corridor, too much like something a human body might claim as “the way through.” I would be tucked into one of the lateral absences: a service recess behind the control kiosk, or a shallow fault in the schist where a pipe bends around a vein of orange stone and leaves a hand’s-width of shadow. A place that was never intended as a room but has become one by being spared.

There, in the narrow negative space between machine geometry and mountain refusal, I would be something small enough to be overlooked by the great colony and old enough not to count as an intruder.

At first I think I am a tool.

A gauge key, perhaps. A calibration pin. A thin stamped wrench dropped from a worker’s belt before the word “abandoned” applied. Something with a hole in one end for hanging on a hook, and a number pressed into its handle. Manganese steel, nickel-dark, slightly magnetic. I lie half-inserted into the dry undergrowth, not buried, because nothing here buries anything; rather, I have been accepted on my exposed terms. One edge still shows a clean old bevel. The other is furred with tin-blue scales.

But that is too simple. A tool is only a relic unless it is still doing something.

So perhaps I am not the wrench, but what the wrench has become useful for.

A bridge.

A conductor.

A remembered straightness.

The colony has grown differently around me. Fine black cords run along my length because my alloy keeps a charge longer than the surrounding brackets. A fan of violet plates has opened from the crescent of my jaw, each plate exactly following the old machining marks. Where a worker’s thumb once polished the handle, a soft-looking gray crust has established itself, feeding on the salts left from skin so long ago they are less residue than myth. My stamped number remains visible only because the growth avoids the depression; the numerals hold a darkness the plates have not solved.

I am a line of preference in a world of gradients.

If I have awareness, it is not the colony’s vast comparative awareness. Mine is narrower, more metallic. I know tension. I know contact. I know when the engine-note thickens through me and when it passes around me. I know the difference between the pipe’s deep steadiness and the brittle chatter of mineral stems touching my flanks. I know the amber lamp not as light but as a faint regular warming on one side every hour, or century, or whenever the old current finds it.

I do not move.

Nothing in this place moves in the theatrical way.

But I participate.

Across me, charges take a shorter path. Along me, dry spores lodge and wake. Beneath me, two mats that would otherwise remain separate have discovered one another. I am a little isthmus of obsolete industry carrying the slow traffic of the garden-engine.

A person looking in would not see me at first. They would see the control kiosk, its door ajar by one hand’s width. They would see the panel of switches, the cracked handset, the mineralized clipboard. They would perhaps lean closer to read DRY EXCHANGE and ROOT-ARRAY under the gray bloom. Their breath would enter like weather. Their pulse would insult the stillness. Their damp fingers might reach for the handset.

Only then, crouching or falling to one knee, would they notice the recess behind the kiosk where the wall sweats no water and the cables vanish into a seam.

They might see my old metal shape.

They might say, “Someone dropped this.”

They would be wrong, but not unforgivably.

Someone did drop me.

That was my beginning, not my identity.

I was manufactured for hands and torque, for small corrections in a system that once had schedules. I tightened collars on ceramic feed lines. I opened housings. I fitted square stems beneath enamel plates. I was kept on a hook inside the kiosk until the hook corroded or the worker forgot or the chamber shook in some early adjustment of its permanent roar. I fell into the recess and lay there, useless in the human sense.

The garden found other uses.

Now I am a minor organ of adjacency.

Not alive alone, but not dead separately.

The colony does not think of me. It does not need to. Thinking-of is too mammalian, too pointed. Yet it has included me in its decision-making. It tests me continuously with static, with mineral films, with the minute pressure of plates forming and cracking and forming again. I hold a little heat when the conduit wakes. I surrender iron so slowly that surrender has the dignity of vocation. I give the dry life a shape it did not invent but has learned to exploit.

I am also a witness, though witness here does not mean sight.

I have received the chamber through vibration.

I know the roar from inside metal. It enters me as a fullness without beginning. It is not loud to me. Loudness requires the possibility of quiet. To me it is the original condition, the medium in which all hardness learns its posture. The roar combs the violet plates on my jaw. It makes the black cords tighten invisibly. It tells the brittle wire-stems when they have grown too tall; those that disagree eventually snap and become pale lattice underfoot.

Sometimes a static discharge crosses the recess.

Not lightning. Not even a spark a human would necessarily see. A brief preference in the air. Afterward, new cups appear along the path, small and pale as the inside of old bone. One such line once crossed my handle. For a long interval I carried a chain of white cups, each no larger than a grain of salt, each rimmed in tarnished silver. They fed on the charge I shed. When the metal beneath them tired, they collapsed inward and became a powdery ridge. The ridge is still there, though now covered by gray scales.

This is how events are recorded here.

Not as before and after.

As added thickness.

I am thick with events.

A tremor from below that cracked the orange vein in the stone: recorded in the slight diagonal shift of the cords attached to my lower edge.

The death of the lamp two housings above: recorded in the retreat of ocher films that once used its stingy warmth.

A leak of chlorine from a sealed valve: recorded in a green-black stain across the flat of my handle, where one lineage failed and another took possession.

The final opening of the kiosk door: recorded in a crescent of cleaner air, a changed deposition pattern, the way dust no longer settles behind the hinge but is pressed toward me by the fixed acoustics of the chamber.

The last human hand that touched me is not remembered as a hand. It is remembered as salts, oils, heat, a brief wet authority. It is remembered in the gray crust on my thumb-worn place. That crust may be the chamber’s closest equivalent to nostalgia.

Would I be lonely?

No.

Loneliness requires an imagined elsewhere populated by answering forms. I have an elsewhere, but it is not imagined. It is the unreachable outside: snow, basalt, ridges, a sky so dry and bright it might destroy the chamber’s delicate darkness. I know it only as pressure translated through stone, as a rare cooling of the upper shaft, as a blue insistence too high to touch. It is not a place I miss. I was not made for it either.

I was made for hands.

I have been remade for contact.

There is something like peace in that.

Around me the colony crowds without affection. Affection would be too selective. It crowds because space exists and space is an unfinished equation. It lays itself over my edges, tests my pitting, sends mineral nerves into the stamped number and withdraws when the alloy is wrong. It does not spare me, but neither does it consume me quickly. It has no malice. It has the ethics of chemistry: exact, impersonal, intimate.

I would be located low enough to feel the engines as substance but high enough that the red lamps below are only buried embers. Behind the kiosk, where the cracked Bakelite handset hangs like a deaf organ and the mineralized clipboard preserves instructions no living worker could obey. A little to the left of the three-meter rail that begins nowhere. Beneath a pipe whose insulation has split into black petals. Beside a ladder rung completely sealed by fibrous mats. In the shadow of a diagram that mistook the chamber for a throat.

I would be the metal splinter in that throat that never needed to be coughed out.

Over centuries, I might disappear.

But disappearance here is not erasure. The violet plates would meet across my upper edge. The gray crust would seal the stamped number. My conductive usefulness would decline as corrosion opened me into lace. One day I would no longer be recognizable as a tool even to a patient archaeologist. I would be a dark stratum inside a thicker growth, an old straightness diffused into branching.

Yet some property of me would persist.

The colony would retain a line where charge prefers to travel.

A slight rigidity in the mat.

A row of cups too regular to be natural and too overgrown to be industrial.

The memory of torque translated into mineral arrangement.

Perhaps that is what I truly am: not the object, but the persistence of an imposed form after purpose has left it.

A human artifact undergoing adoption by a nonhuman continuity.

A small, conductive fossil of maintenance.

If the engines stopped, I would know immediately.

Not as panic. As impossible subtraction.

The plates around me, tuned to spend the roar, would suddenly have nowhere to put themselves. The cords would slacken by measurements too small for eyes. Dust might fall for the first honest time in ages. The air would become cavern air instead of chamber air. Some growths would crack. Others would wake to new gradients. The great colony might suffer, or change, or enter a condition for which there is no animal metaphor.

I would cool.

The charges would fade from my length.

For the first time, perhaps, I would become only metal again.

But the engines do not stop.

The roar holds. The dry curtains hang. The lamps ration amber to the salt. The blue pressure remains above. The black nodules drink heat along the lower walls. The chamber stays concluded and not finished.

And I remain in my recess, neither hidden nor displayed, no longer waiting for a hand, no longer available to repair.

I am where human intention thinned enough for the mountain garden to use it.

I am a dropped tool become a vein.

A bridge of old alloy.

A minor bone in the dry organism.

A straight thing slowly learning the patience of growth.
