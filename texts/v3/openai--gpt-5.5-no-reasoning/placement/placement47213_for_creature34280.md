I would be found where the city is most careful not to call anything empty.

Not in the deepest chamber, not at the pale tree’s ring, though everyone descends there eventually in silence and fear and civic obligation. Not in the upper galleries either, where the heat-vines braid themselves through black air and the young dare one another to touch red seams with the backs of their knuckles. I would not belong to the exposed stair, where travelers first lower themselves by the glossy rope and learn that the crack is longer than it should be.

I think I would live in a side-throat of the city, halfway down and slightly aside from the main hollow: one of those places that is not a street until one needs it, not a room until the shutters are angled, not a garden until noon begins to pull air through it.

A narrow passage with a bend in it. A wall that sounds different on each side. Two ladders, one official and one known mostly to children. A ledge where dust gathers no matter how often it is swept away. A breath-curtain trained across a clay frame, black-violet and felt-gray, its tendrils dull all morning and alert by evening. A set of ceramic listening cups hung on pegs beside a cracked plaster mark. A low arch that makes everyone bow. Three vents: one honest, one temperamental, one sealed except during heat storms and funerals.

That would be my place.

I would be one of those who listen to the city where it is not yet in trouble.

Not an elder yet. Not one of the great wall-hearers whose palms have become famous, whose verdicts can stop a marriage, reroute a household, condemn a kiln, save a terrace. I would be younger than that authority, but old enough to have lost the childish belief that every sound must announce itself plainly. I would be apprenticed to pressure, to hairline fracture, to the moods of clay tubes and the small treacheries of salt.

A vent-keeper, perhaps.

No—less official than that at first.

A seam-listener.

One of the people sent into half-used passages with a bundle of brushes, chalk-dust, resin plugs, a tapping stone, and a length of red thread. Someone who knows which walls are merely talking and which have begun to lie.

I would carry my tools in a folded bark-cloth roll darkened by years of oil from my hands. A ceramic ear-cup, thin enough to hum if held near the right current. A little striker of volcanic glass set into a resin grip. Chalks of different grades: white for harmless expansion, ocher for watchfulness, black for heat-stress, red for civic attention. A palm-slate rubbed smooth for temporary marks. A cord with knots for measuring the widening of cracks over seasons. A few slivers of old shutter-clay, because sometimes a repair begins with comparison rather than action.

My given child-name might have been Small Whistle, because I was born during a clogged-shaft scare and cried in a pitch that made the fan-cactus outside my mother’s chamber tremble. Later, after apprenticeship, I might be called Under-Note. Not a grand name. A practical one. The kind spoken with half a nod: Send Under-Note to hear it. They are patient with slow walls.

I would be ash-pale like the others, rubbed at the joints with bitter oil, my lips darkened by resin balm, my hair bound tight under a strip of soot-colored cloth. I would have a line of ocher across one cheekbone for my vent-line, and a thinner black mark at the wrist for the guild of listeners. My clothes would be patched stalk-fiber, close at the forearms, loose at the ribs, always dusty at the knees from crouching against masonry.

My work would not look dramatic.

I would spend long hours kneeling beside vents while the city roars elsewhere. I would place my cheek against clay and wait. I would tap stone and listen for hollowness, not with the ear alone but with the teeth, the sternum, the heel of the hand. I would learn the difference between a chamber that has cooled unevenly, a vine pressing behind plaster, a family secretly blocking airflow to preserve shade, and the first quiet complaint of a seam beginning to move.

I would be summoned to disputes.

A household says the vine crossing their ceiling has warmed too much and threatens stored seed. Another household insists the warmth-right belongs to them by old agreement. A gardener says the vine must not be cut because it is feeding a bloom two terraces away. A child says, in the way children sometimes do, that the wall is making a new sound at night.

So they send for someone like me.

I arrive with my roll of tools and do not speak first. That is part of the discipline. Speech changes rooms; rooms are not to be changed before they are heard. I ask them all to step back from the wall. I ask the angry aunt to stop grinding seed for a moment. I ask the two cousins whispering insults at each other to go insult the actual shrub if they must. Then I lay my palm on the plaster.

The city presses back.

Warm. Layered. Busy. Full of old decisions.

Behind the wall: vine tension, yes. But also a pocket of air where there should be packed clay. A previous repair, perhaps two generations old, done cleverly but with the wrong ash. The sound is not danger yet. It is a request.

I mark it ocher, not red.

Everyone exhales. Not relief exactly; relief would be too wasteful in public. But the room softens.

Then the argument begins again, now with better facts.

That would be much of my life: improving the quality of arguments.

I would know the city not by map but by consequence. This shutter affects that sleeping chamber. That sleeping chamber feeds those breath-curtains. Those breath-curtains shade a seed shelf. That seed shelf belongs to a lineage whose bones are in the stair two turns below. A loose stone is never only a loose stone. A blocked vent is never only inconvenience. A wall has ancestors, dependents, appetites, debts.

I would love that about the place.

I would also be tired often.

The crack-city does not allow innocence for long. Children laugh during storms, but seam-listeners hear the low note beneath the laughter. During heat storms I would be one of those moving fast through dim passages while shutters clap and fan-plants rattle and old dust leaps from ledges in choking veils. I would lash hanging trays, wedge braces, open emergency throats, shout upward through vents until my voice came back shredded. I would know the particular dread of hearing a wall shift from speech into decision.

Once, perhaps, I would fail.

Not catastrophically. If the failure were catastrophic, I might not be here to tell it. But enough. A kiln-court wall I marked white instead of ocher. A night contraction I mistook for ordinary cooling. By morning a crack had widened, and a shelf of drying pods had fallen into ash. No one died. A season’s worth of bitter ferment was lost. Two families carried the salt-ache longer than they should have.

For a year I might bear a silence-name in certain chambers.

Not total disgrace. The city is too practical for pure punishment. But memory must live somewhere. My wrist mark would be crossed with gray. Children would whisper and then be scolded. Elders would watch how I listened afterward.

And I would listen better.

That is the kind of person this city would make of me: not pure, not mystical, not untouched by error, but gradually trained away from haste. Someone who trusts no first sound. Someone who knows that a wall may crack long before it opens, and a person may do the same.

I would have a fondness for children doing pollination rounds. They would pass through my side-throat with red thread looped around their fingers, carrying soft brushes and gossip. I would pretend not to hear them practicing forbidden insults on closed pods. Sometimes I would correct their rhythm without looking up.

“No. If you say it like that, you flatter it. Strike the second word dry.”

They would roll their eyes and try again.

I might keep, in my work niche, a little unauthorized tray of powder-bloom seedlings. Not illegal, only inconvenient. They would be the sort of plant that prefers muttering and secondhand breath, so my corner would suit them. When people came to complain, argue, confess a blocked vent, or ask whether a crack was dangerous, their anxious speech would feed the seedlings. Over years the lintel would stain gold-orange. People would begin to say my niche smelled of worry and bloom.

I would not be rich. Wealth there would not be counted in excess objects anyway. I might possess one good ceramic ear-cup inherited from my teacher, a copper pin too soft to use except ceremonially, three named shutters under my maintenance, and partial shade-rights to a fan-cactus whose shadow crosses my sleeping mat for nineteen breaths in late afternoon during the hotter season.

That would be enough to make me respectable.

My sleeping place would be narrow, set behind a hanging mat stiff with ash. There would be a stone bench shaped by generations of backs before mine. Above it, a wall-bone ridge: ancestors not all mine, because rooms change hands and the dead become civic before they become private. I would sleep with one hand sometimes touching the plaster, not from fear but habit.

At night I would listen to the city contract.

Ticks. Soft cracks. A ladder settling. A child whispering into a vent. The last lazy turn of a vane. Someone coughing above. The breath-curtain folding itself with a faint dry rasp.

In those hours I might imagine the upper plain: that terrible blankness, the white salt, the scraped horizon. I would have crossed it once, maybe twice, as part of a vent-material caravan, wrapped against glare, walking by stars that seemed monstrous because there were too many at once. I would have returned with obsidian, shell-mineral, and a private distrust of openness.

Too much sky would feel like being overheard by nothing.

The crack, for all its danger, would feel honest. It answers touch. It remembers repair. It makes no promise of kindness, but it permits relationship.

I would go once each year to the deepest chamber with everyone else.

I would stand among bodies made quiet by heat. No tools. No tapping. No professional listening. Before the pale organism, all listening belongs to the city. The vents would close. Breath would thicken. The pale tree would click and click and click, each sound small and absolute, like a count being kept by something older than law.

Then the vents would open.

The white powder would rise.

For days afterward, when new sprouts appeared in impossible cracks, people would tie red threads around them. Sometimes they would call me to listen near a sprout before repairing a wall. I would kneel, palm to stone, and hear nothing yet. Or almost nothing. A faint pressure. A future inconvenience. Shade in thirty years.

“Reroute,” I would say.

Someone would groan. Someone else would begin calculating labor. A child would smile because a wall had lost to a plant no wider than a finger.

That would be my life’s faith, if I had one: that rerouting is not defeat.

The city survives because nothing is sovereign. Not people, not plants, not stone, not law, not memory. Everything yields a little, or breaks. A good listener does not prevent change. A good listener helps change arrive before catastrophe.

So where would I find myself?

In the bent passage beside the temperamental vent, beneath the arch that makes even proud people bow.

Who or what would I be?

Under-Note, seam-listener of the middle terraces.  
Keeper of small warnings.  
Marker of cracks not yet dangerous.  
Owner of one good ear-cup and nineteen breaths of shade.  
A citizen of the wound, employed by the city’s unease.
