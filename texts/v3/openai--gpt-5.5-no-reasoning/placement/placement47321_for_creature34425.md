I think I would be found where the noise has to pass through a narrowing.

Not in the widest chambers, not beneath the highest lost ceilings where the machine-weather becomes too large to have edges, and not in the pump rooms where warmth gathers too obviously and the rats know every route. I would be in one of the in-between places: a side passage below the Condensation Gallery, where an old concrete service corridor cracked along its length and opened into a natural seam of stone.

It would not look important at first. A low corridor, half industrial and half cave, its ceiling ribbed with rusting cable trays and descending root hairs. One side would still be concrete, painted once in a pale institutional color now darkened by damp. The other would have fallen away into mineral fracture, a torn wall of stone padded over by moss and roots. A pipe would run along the ceiling, sweating continuously, and beneath it a shallow gutter would carry water only sometimes.

The engines would be deafening elsewhere, but here their sound would arrive strained and shaped. A turbine throb would come down the pipework as a physical pressure. The fans would be a long-bodied moan in the duct above. Pumps would strike the floor from far away, then pause long enough for the place to seem to inhale. The corridor would be a throat in the underground body, a place where air, vibration, damp, and root-growth all narrow together.

I would live against the warmest cold thing there.

Maybe not warm in any human sense. Only less cold: a metal junction box set into the wall, still alive with some low current, its face rusted around the hinges and furred along the seams with mineral bloom. It would give off just enough heat that frost never quite settled on it. Around it the roots would have thickened into a pale nest, a cuff of fibrous life. Moss would be brighter there, glassy after condensation. Small flies would appear there first after wet hours. A spider would have tried and failed to keep a web between the rootlets. Beetles would use the underside lip as shelter.

That would be my place.

I would not be a single animal in the ordinary way. Not one of the rats, though I would know their routes. Not a bird, too hot-blooded and temporary. Not a machine, because the machines are too indifferent and too whole in their appointed violence. Not simply a root either, because roots belong upward as well as down; they have trees attached to them, weather attached to them, seasons translated through sap.

I think I would be one of the things that only exists because the whole accident exists.

A cold seam-dweller. A colony more than an individual, but with a recurring shape. Something composed of root-fiber, fungal thread, insect passage, mineral damp, and the faint heat leaking from abandoned infrastructure. A living felt gathered around a machine-warmed crack.

If someone looked quickly, I would be mistaken for a patch of dense pale moss or a loosened mass of roots growing over the junction box. A swollen mat. A fibrous drape. A stain of white, gray-green, and amber-brown. But if they watched long enough, during the pauses between pump cycles, they would notice that I respond.

Not quickly. Never quickly.

A tremor in the fine hairs when footsteps enter the corridor. A tightening where air changes. A slow lifting of filaments toward breath. A withdrawal from a hand’s heat, or toward it, depending on how long it stays still. Condensation beads would gather on me and not fall at random. They would travel through capillary paths, drawn inward. If a moth died in the roots above, it would be held, softened, opened by hidden threads. If a beetle crossed me often enough, I would know the beetle as pressure, salt, rhythm.

I would be made from several lives cooperating without agreement.

My outer surface would be moss and root hairs, because those are what touch the air. Beneath that would be fungal lace, binding dead plant matter into a springy pad. Deeper in, against concrete and metal, algae and bacterial films would occupy the wettest skins. Tiny animals would move through me as both inhabitants and weather: springtails, mites, minute larvae, occasional beetles, the soft eggs of flies. Their tunnels would aerate me. Their bodies, when finished, would feed me. I would not command them. They would be part of the way I sensed the world.

The junction box would be my hearth. Not because I understood electricity, but because generations of growth had failed elsewhere and persisted there. The metal plate would tick softly when the temperature shifted. A wire inside would hum under the larger roar. In winter, when an intake shaft sharpened the cold and ice collars formed on nearby pipes, my outer edges would shrink and dull, but the core around the box would remain awake. In summer, when warmer wet air entered and the condensation rains came harder, I would spread farther along the wall, sending a nap of pale hairs into old screw holes and cracks in the paint.

My intelligence, if it could be called that, would be a record of tolerances.

This side freezes.

This seam drips every third thaw.

This metal hums always.

This cable warms after the fans begin.

This gutter floods when the upper rill returns.

This patch dies if it reaches too far into the draft.

This lamp feeds the moss, but the lamp flickers now.

This rat path brings crumbs, hair, danger, warmth.

This human footstep is rare, heavy, salt-bearing, catastrophic.

I would have no eyes, but the place would not be dark to me. Light would be known by the bodies that answered it. Where the amber lamp still reached the corridor, moss would swell greener and thinner. Where the lamp failed, the skin would flatten, pale, and become more fungal than leafy. Daylight from a shaft two chambers away would not touch me, but I would know its seasons through the roots: sugars changing, water pulses after rain, winter hunger, spring pressure.

I would have no ears, but sound would be nearly everything.

Not sound as heard, but vibration as architecture. The pump-start would compress me. The fan would comb my outer hairs in a steady direction. Conveyor clatter would arrive through rail and floor as a ragged sequence of knocks. Valves screaming in far galleries would make droplets shake loose from the pipe above, and those drops would strike my surface like sudden cold coins. The machinery would be my weather, clock, predator, and calendar.

A quiet hour would be frightening.

If one of the main engines stopped, the corridor would widen in a way that had nothing to do with space. Small sounds would appear: a drip actually striking the gutter, a beetle turning under a flake of paint, a rat shifting inside insulation, the faint creak of roots in a ceiling crack. In such hours my filaments would extend farther, as if silence were moisture. But if silence lasted too long, cold would deepen. Air would stop moving correctly. Some warm leak would fade. Then my edges would begin to die inward, brown and translucent, and the springtails would retreat toward the core.

I would be young, as the place is young.

Not ancient, not wise in any grand sense. I would have begun after the wall cracked and the junction box remained live. Perhaps a root first found the seam. Perhaps moss colonized the sweating paint. Perhaps a fungus entered through the soaked cardboard backing of an old notice fixed to the wall. The notice would be gone now, digested into me, but a rectangle of different mineral salts might remain beneath my body, shaping my growth. Human instruction turned into habitat.

I would not remember the workers. But I might contain them indirectly.

A strand of cloth from a torn glove. Leather oil from a bootprint. Paper pulp from a maintenance tag. Iron from rust. Skin cells fallen decades ago into the damp. Tobacco ash washed from a ledge. The place would have taken these minor residues and made them no longer human, not by violence but by inclusion.

If a person came close with a lamp, I would not hide. I could not.

The light would show my surface in layers: pale root hairs trembling; small black fruiting dots of fungus; green moss cushions; translucent beads of water; ocher stains where metal bled into me. In one place I would bridge from the wall to the pipe above in a curtain no thicker than a hand. In another, I would bulge outward over the junction box, insulating it so thoroughly that the box’s warmth became mine before it reached the air.

The person might think I was diseased growth. Or insulation. Or some failed nest.

If they touched me, their fingers would sink slightly into cold springiness. Not slime, at first. Felt. Then wetness underneath. I would cling to the skin with root hairs and fungal threads too fine to see. The touch would tear me. A little. Not enough to kill me unless they scraped down to metal. I would close slowly afterward, growing around the wound, incorporating whatever they left behind: salt, oil, blood if the rust cut them.

I would be neither hostile nor benign. I would simply use contact.

There would be others like me elsewhere, but not identical. A black, wet cousin in a sump grate, more algae than moss. A yellow-green one around a warm transformer in the Orchard, constantly grazed by insects. A dry gray crust near a ventilation fan, tough and starved, living mostly dormant. A richer mat in a helmet in the locker room, fed by bird droppings and condensation. We would not communicate like minds. But the roots might pass chemistry slowly between districts. Spores might travel by rat fur, by air currents, by water pulses. A fragment of me caught on a beetle’s leg could begin again under another warm seam.

So I would be local, but not sealed.

My world would be the length of the corridor, the junction box, the sweating pipe, the gutter, the root curtain, the cracked concrete, the movements of small animals, and the enormous invisible machinery pressing its rhythms through everything. Beyond that I would know only gradients: colder that way, wetter above, dangerous draft below, richer decay near the old stair, lamp failing near the bend.

If I had desire, it would be very small.

For the pipe to keep sweating.

For the box to keep humming.

For the lamp not to fail all at once.

For rats to pass but not nest too deeply.

For roots to thicken without splitting the wall away.

For the pump not to flood the gutter past endurance.

For winter to be survivable.

For surfaces.

Always surfaces.

I would be a thing of surfaces and seams: living where categories meet but do not merge. Plant and fungus. Machine and weather. Cave and factory. Dead labor and new growth. Cold and the slight exception to cold. Not a monster in the underforest, not its spirit, not its ruler. Just one of its successful accidents.

If you came back after months, I would be larger by the width of a fingernail.

A painted arrow partly hidden before would be unreadable. A screw head would have vanished under pale felt. The root curtain beside me would have gained another strand. A moth wing caught in my surface would be gone, leaving only a darker freckle of nutrient-rich tissue. Perhaps one exploratory tongue of moss would have crossed the gutter during a dry spell and died when the water returned. Perhaps another would have survived behind it.

That would be my biography: advances, failures, withdrawals, thickened edges, dead fringes, new hairs.

And if, years later, the machines stopped for good, I would change.

At first there would be relief. Less tearing vibration. More settled droplets. Spiders might build better webs. Birds might hear themselves panic. Rats might explore farther. But then the warm seams would cool. The junction box would become only metal. Frost would take the wall in winter. My core would shrink. Some of me would die and feed the rest. Moss would give way to tougher crusts. Fungal threads would persist longer than the green.

If the forest above endured, roots would continue downward. If the shafts remained open, daylight and leaves and soil would keep arriving. The place might become more cave than factory, more root cellar than engine body. I might become indistinguishable from the general mat.

But while the machines still roar, I am distinct.

I am the pale, cold, living felt around a warm electrical seam in the cracked corridor below the Condensation Gallery.

I am what gathers where the underground forest learns the shape of a machine.
