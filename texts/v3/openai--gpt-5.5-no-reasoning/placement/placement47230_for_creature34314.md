I would find myself inside a closed white nodule on a salt flat, though I would not know the salt flat. I would not know outside at all.

Not a cave. Not a room. Not even, at first, a place with scale. I would be within the sealed interior of a stone that is less like a stone than a fossilized act of making: a dense, bright, mineral labyrinth whose walls are too close to be walls and whose passages are only the leftover spaces between growths. A world under a crust. A world inside a bone-colored lump that might fit in two hands, if there were hands nearby to lift it.

From within, it would seem immense because there would be no horizon by which to contradict it. Only surfaces succeeding surfaces: fins, ribs, blades, combs, tight white terraces, nested shelves, oval perforations, mineral pages pressed too close together to turn. Everything would be near. Everything would be vertical or slanting or thinly vaulted. Space would not open; it would thread.

I would be somewhere along one of the allowed seams.

Not in the center, because there is no center fit for inhabiting. Not on the broadest, brightest planes, where the salt-white faces are too exposed, too absolute, too chemically stern. I would be tucked into a shallow concavity on the underside of a pale rib, where the light still reaches but arrives after being broken and reflected through other whitenesses. There, on a chalky carbonate skin with a trace of greenish mineral in it, I would adhere.

I would be one of the dark crusts.

A very small country of cells.

A black-green stain with a fretted edge.

I would not have a body that could turn toward the chamber, or away from it. I would not have limbs to test the coolness, nor a mouth to take in the mineral taste of the place, nor eyes to be dazzled by the sourceless light. I would be spread flat across the surface that permits me, pressed close enough that stone and self are almost a single event. My underside would be chemistry. My upper side would be pigment, armor, patience.

If I had a shape, it would be accidental-looking but exact: a thumbprint island, matte and dark, with brittle scales overlapping at the margin. Some of me would sink into pores too small for imagination. Some of me would lift a little from the mineral face, curling into tiny cupped plates, each one hardened against the brightness. In the cracks near me, thinner relatives would continue as ink-lines, blackening the seams between ribs. Farther off, across a gap of brilliant air, another colony would lie on a sloped shelf like a burned green shadow.

We would not reach for one another. We would not know one another as neighbors. But the chamber would arrange us into a constellation.

I would live by almost nothing.

Light would be the great abundance, though not an abundance I could enjoy. It would enter me as work, as pressure, as a condition to be managed. Too much light would need dark pigment. Too little would mean waiting, though waiting would be indistinguishable from living. There would be no rain to anticipate, no damp season folded somewhere in memory, no softness returning after hardship. Moisture would not be promise. It would be danger, disruption, a foreign mercy that could undo the precise severity that made me possible.

So I would take what could be taken without movement: photons from the white glare, ions from the mineral face, trace elements from ancient salts, perhaps the faintest chemical gifts released by stresses in the stone. My hunger would be so reduced it would barely deserve the name. I would not feed. I would maintain.

Around me the chamber would sing.

The sound would pass through my crust, through the cupped scales, through the old green-black films and the mineral beneath them. I would not hear it, yet perhaps I would be shaped by it. The constant pitch of the ribs, the granular complaint of the crystals, the metallic brightness near the upper shelves—all of it would be as much my climate as the light. The place would roar without event. The note would never arrive and never cease. It would simply be one of the dimensions in which I persisted.

If time touched me, it would do so without drama.

A century might thicken my edge by a film’s width. A fracture might become slightly more hospitable, and a few cells, already almost asleep, might occupy it. A scale might darken. A pore might close. A margin might advance into an ivory hollow so slowly that advance and remaining would be the same gesture.

I would not desire more of the chamber. I would not dream of covering it. The white architecture would be too complete for conquest. It would not be landscape offered to life, but law within which life has found loopholes. I would inhabit a permission, not a territory.

And yet, in that sealed place, I would be extravagant.

Against the salt-white ribs, a single patch of me would alter everything. My dark green would make the mineral brightness more visible. My living edge would reveal the dead exactness of the wall. I would be small enough to be overlooked and singular enough to change the meaning of the chamber. Without me, the place would be a completed mineral thought. With me, it would be inhabited.

Not awakened.

Not softened.

Only marked.

I would be a punctuation in the white script: a comma of life, an old green pause, a stain holding its place among ribs that remember growth more grandly than I ever could.

The chamber would be the greater being. I would live on it like an afterimage on bone.

If there were a “who” in this world, it would not be a person, nor an animal, nor even a plant in the ordinary sense. It would be this: a photosynthetic crust inside a sealed mineral pocket, living so slowly that stillness and survival have become indistinguishable.

A tenant of brightness.

A dry, dark film on the underside of a white rib.

The local forest.

The local animal.

The only soft fact, hardened almost to stone.
