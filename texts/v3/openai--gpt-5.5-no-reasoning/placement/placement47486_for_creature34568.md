I would find myself where the plateau briefly permits inwardness: not on the open ridges, not among the full sweep of herds, but under the lip of a basalt overhang at the edge of a shallow amphitheater, where fog thickens and the stone sweats.

There, the world gathers without becoming loud.

The cliff above me is not high by mountain standards, but here any vertical thing feels like an event. Its face is layered black and ash-grey, iron-streaked, cracked by frost into ledges just deep enough to hold lichen, old feathers, pellets of dung, the desiccated stems of grasses blown from the basin floor. Beneath one shelf, condensation forms with almost deliberate slowness. Beads swell on the underside of stone, each one holding a dim inverted version of the plateau: grey sky, darker ground, the blurred motion of animals far below.

This is where I would be.

Not a traveler. Not a watcher with eyes above the land. Not one of the great bodies crossing the visible world.

I would be something small enough to live by what the large world forgets.

At first I might seem like a patch of moss.

A dark mat in a crack, no wider than two hands, pressed low against the stone where a faint mineral warmth rises through the rock. Green-black at the center, rust-colored at the edges, silvered with frost in the morning. If touched, I would not feel like plant alone. There would be a hidden give to me, a muscular patience. I would be a living seam: part lichen, part animal, part colony of mouths and cilia and rootlike fibers, an old local compromise between hunger and stillness.

I would drink the drops that fall too quietly to be heard.

That is my place in the order of things: the receiver of nearly nothing.

The plateau spends itself in immensities—herds, migrations, gliders, fog banks, ancient basins, geothermal breath—but I would live at the point where vastness reduces to one tremble of water under stone. I would be adapted to the smallest generosity. A bead condenses over hours. It fattens in silence. The cold holds it longer than gravity wants. Then it falls.

When it strikes me, I open.

Not visibly, not like a flower. The change is inward and chemical. My surface loosens. Black cells swell. Pale filaments uncurl between grains of grit. Minute mouths open along the mosslike nap and draw the water down before frost can reclaim it. In that instant I take in mineral, vapor, the breath of animals that passed below in the night, the faint sulfur of distant vents, the powdered bone of old deaths lifted and laid here by wind.

I live on that mixture.

I am not lonely, because I am not singular.

Within me are many hungers arranged into one quiet body. Lichen-fibers grip the basalt. Algal cells wake when the dim light strengthens. Transparent worm-things move through my underside. Sleeping spores wait in me for seasons of damp. Small pale mites graze my outer skin and in grazing prune me into health. A beetle larva, blue-black when it hardens, spends its first winter in one of my warmer folds. I am habitat and creature both, a little thickness of life where the stone has agreed to soften.

From below, the herds come and go.

I know them first as pressure.

Hooves on the basin floor do not sound like thunder here. They arrive through the cliff as a dry, distributed murmur: weight entering stone, leaving stone, entering again. Each species has its pattern. The low grazers are numerous and fine, a long combing vibration. The horned watchers are few and deep, single decisions placed heavily on the world. The soot-feathered ground birds are almost nothing, quick pins of contact. The scavengers are light, lateral, intermittent, pausing often. The great gliders, when they land, are a sudden pale interruption in the air, then talons scoring rock with a clean pressure that remains in memory after the body has gone.

I do not flee them. I cannot. But I do not need to.

The large animals mostly pass beneath me. Sometimes a grazer rubs its shoulder against the lower stones of the overhang, leaving hair caught in rough basalt. Wind lifts some of that hair upward. It lodges in me. I digest what time loosens. Sometimes a calf, newly born in the basin below, gives its first thin breath into the cold, and the vapor rises with the warmth of the herd. Hours later, part of that breath becomes water on the ledge. I drink it.

In this way I know the herd intimately without ever joining it.

I know where they slept by the richness of the next fog. I know when they are lean because less warmth rises from their gathering. I know when one has died nearby because the air changes for days: iron, fat, opened gut, beetle-wake, the sharp work of scavengers. I do not mourn, but I receive. A dead grazer becomes black activity in the basin, then feather, dung, vapor, dust, mineral trace. Eventually some of it reaches my crack. I thicken at one edge.

If I have a consciousness, it is not a line but a dampening and brightening.

Dryness narrows me. I become crust, color, memory. Frost stiffens my surface until even mites cannot move through me. I wait then with the rest of the plateau. Waiting is not emptiness; it is one of the plateau’s main forms of being alive.

When fog enters the amphitheater, I return.

Water gathers on my skin. Light diffuses through me. The mineral warmth below the crack rises by a fraction. My inner threads resume their slow exchange. Sugars move. Spores take measure of the season. Bacteria loosen iron from stone. Tiny jaws feed and are fed upon. I become, for a few hours, a small factory without noise.

That is what I would be: one of the plateau’s hidden manufactories of life.

Not built. Not intentional. Not important in the way the gliders are important, or the herds, or the horned animals that hold ridgelines with their bodies. But necessary in the way a seam is necessary, in the way a pause is necessary between breaths.

I would convert the nearly unusable into the barely enough.

Mist into moisture.  
Mineral into flesh.  
Hair into nitrogen.  
Frost into brief abundance.  
Silence into growth.

The world around me would remain immense and unfinished. Ridges would vanish into low vapor. Herds would occupy distances too large for any single eye. Pale pool-things would open and close their circular eyes in black water. Burrowers would tap through gravel below the frost crust. Great winged bodies would pass overhead, and their shadows would cool me for the length of a slow thought.

I would not name them.

But I would know their returns.

A particular old watcher comes each year to the same slope when the frost first loosens. One horn forks outward, the other has broken and healed blunt. He stands below the overhang with ice in his coat, breathing toward the basin. His presence warms the air slightly. Parasites fall from his fur; birds take some, and some remain in the grit. When he leaves, there is a crescent of shed hair on the stone. By midsummer, I have drawn part of him into me.

A line of grazers uses the ramp below after dim rains. Their hooves have worn the rock smoother than water could have alone. Calves hesitate at the steepest turn. Mothers do not call unless necessary; they stand angled, waiting, and the calves understand pressure better than sound. Sometimes one slips, catches itself, leaves a smear of blood from a knee. The blood darkens the stone. Insects find it. Later, I taste the iron in fog.

Once in many seasons, a glider feeds in the basin.

There is no scream. Only the tightening of animals, the long selecting shadow, the sudden folding of one life into another. Afterward the carcass steams in the cold. The great bird opens it with pale feet planted wide, head moving with careful violence. Scavengers wait beyond the first ring. Ground birds pretend not to wait. Beetles, still underground, are already being summoned by warmth and scent.

Days later, the carcass is less body than place.

Weeks later, the place is richer.

Years later, if the bones remain exposed, lichens brighter than I am bloom along the ribs.

This is how the plateau remembers: not in stories, but in altered chemistry.

I would be one of its memories.

A dark living stain under a ledge. A patient mouth. A little archive of weather, herds, death, condensation, and stone.

And if there is something almost sacred in this world, I would be close to it—not because I am elevated, but because I attend the smallest event. The drop too quiet to hear. The one that swells beneath basalt while herds move like continents below. The one that falls after hours of gathering.

To the traveler it would be nothing.

To me it would be arrival.

The drop strikes.

For a moment, my whole body knows water.

Then the cold resumes its patient work, and I hold what I can.
