I would find myself where the old works pinch the valley—  
not in the open meadow of strapweed, not in the village with fish in its windows, not up at the wavering silver ceiling where birds enter like knives, but lower than the saddle and higher than the trench, in the industrial throat of the drowned country.

There is a place where three kinds of motion meet.

Cold clean water descends from a pass in a hard invisible sheet. Warmer mineral seepage issues from cracked brick and iron below. Between them, a broken conduit breathes irregularly through the shell of an old pumping house. The current enters by one door, leaves by four windows, rises through a stairwell, drops through a collapsed floor, and gathers itself again in a turbine chamber whose blades have forgotten purpose but not movement.

That is where I am.

At first I want to be one of the seals, because they have knowledge with eyes in it. They understand distance. They pass from basin to basin. They know the upper boundary where air waits in a hammered brightness, and they can enter the ruined rooms almost playfully, turning what was built for hands and shoulders into something sleek, brief, and alive. There is dignity in that freedom: in knowing the old roads as hunting lines, the pylons as landmarks, the bells as weather.

But I do not think I am that free.

Then I think I might be an eel, long-bodied and dark, one of the keepers of the pipes. That feels closer. To live by pressure, smell, and remembered gradients. To enter the machinery not as trespass but as blood enters a heart. To know the under-stair hollows, the silted inspection hatches, the iron chambers where vibration arrives before shape. To be ancient in design and opportunistic in habit. To wait through clear danger and move in brown confusion.

But even that is too single. Too much body, too much will.

I would be something more distributed. Something that belongs to a seam.

I would be a mussel-bed grown into the broken intake grille of the pumping station.

Not one mussel, exactly. A colony. A black-blue accumulation of shells wedged in rusted iron, crowded so tightly that the old machine and the living bodies have become one rough organ. I would line the mouth of the conduit where water is forced through narrowing metal. I would feed on everything brought by the drowned mountains: algae, mineral dust, powdered wood, spores, larvae, the fine edible ruin of roofs and roads and hillsides. The whole province would pass through me in suspension.

I would not move through the country. The country would move through me.

My shells would be cold and heavy, dark outside, pearl-dim within. Young mussels would fasten in the gaps between older ones. Tube worms would bloom from the grille beside me. Transparent shrimp would shelter in my lee when the flow grew violent. Small fish would nose along my edges. Crabs would come to pry at the weak and the dead, and I would know them by pressure before their claws touched. Eels would slip through the lower broken bars, brushing my colony with their muscular sides. When the turbine woke deeper inside, its grinding would travel through iron and shell as a godless, practical thunder.

I would be both inhabitant and alteration.

The grille was made to strain debris from machinery. I would make it strain the lake. I would clarify a small portion of the water. I would slow one thread of current enough for silt to settle, for algae to root, for larvae to hide, for a tiny architecture of aftermath to begin. The old human purpose would remain visible—parallel bars, bolts, a maintenance frame, the circular mouth of the pipe—but my colony would have changed the meaning of it. Intake would become reef. Machine would become cliff. Damage would become dwelling.

Above me, the pumping house would breathe.

Sometimes the bells would sound: dull bronze pulses, not loud exactly, but complete. They would enter every shell. They would close us. Then open us. Then close us again. The seals might hear warning. The eels might hear vibration. The divers, if they came, might hear history. I would hear a change in water.

Sometimes trapped air would gather in the rooms behind and below. The pressure would alter. A pocket would tear loose from some ceiling, roll along a beam, find the vertical stack, and rise. Then the whole building would exhale through its black chimneys in bursts of silver. The bubbles would rush past the upper windows and shake loose shrimp, silt, fragments of weed, flakes of orange bacterial skin from the warm pipes. A feast would follow. Everything would arrive unsettled.

I would live by those arrivals.

I would know storms not as spectacle but as command passing through tissue. First the pressure deepens. Then the clean current hardens. The amber forests outside flatten against the slope. The cable between pylons begins to hum under its load of shells and weed. Gravel starts to run along the old roadbed. Brown water comes down from somewhere unseen, full of torn roots and stone dust. My colony closes against the worst of it. We become armor. The intake grille shudders. A crab flattened against the frame loses its hold and vanishes backward. A fish strikes the bars and rights itself. The turbine within begins its irregular turning, blade after broken blade accepting the surge.

For a time there is no landscape, only force.

Then slack water.

Silt falls. Feeding resumes. The water clears by degrees, and the drowned country appears again beyond the pump-house door: road climbing away under weed, rails crossing the ravine, pylons furred and leaning, the village lower down with its black thresholds and fish-held windows. A new scatter of stones lies where a wall has failed. A beam has shifted. A door is gone. The map has been edited.

I remain.

That is the kind of being I would be here: not master, not wanderer, not witness in the human sense, but a fixed participant in the conversion of things. A cold-water colony fastened to the failed ambition of height and water, making use of the very opening through which the old machinery tried to command the flow.

I would be patient without being peaceful.

I would be ancient only by accumulation: shell over shell, season over season, storms counted as growth rings, collapses recorded as changes in current. I would have no memory of air. I would not mourn the miners or engineers, the road, the vanished roofs, the hands that bolted the intake frame into stone. Their world would reach me only as surfaces: iron, brick, mineral, soot trapped under algae, a tool wedged in silt, a boot sole slowly furred with life.

And yet I would continue them strangely.

The pump once drew water through itself. So would I.

The grille once filtered the mountain. So would I.

The station once made pressure useful. So would I, though not for industry, not for work, not for any plan larger than feeding, holding, sheltering, thickening, enduring.

If a diver came with a lamp and gloved hands, they might pause before me. They might see the old intake almost sealed by living shell, white worm plumes opening and closing, shrimp flickering in the gaps, rust bleeding through blue-black clusters. They might scrape a small patch clean to inspect the metal, and in that bare place the original surface would appear for a moment: manufactured, straight-edged, human.

But not for long.

The water would touch it.

Something would fasten there.
