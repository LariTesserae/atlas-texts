I would not be found first.

Not by eye.

A lowlander, if one somehow came so high and lived long enough to grow tired of looking outward, might pass me three times in one morning. They would see the plateau because the plateau insists on being seen: the immense worn backs of stone, the running cloud shadows, the fern mouths, the tilted ledges falling away into blue distance. They would hear everything and understand nothing. Wind, stone, water, reed, thunder underfoot. Their attention would be dragged again and again toward the grand violence of emptiness.

I would be beneath that.

Not hidden in the sense of cunning. Hidden in the way a cup is hidden inside the shape of a hand.

I think I would be one of the listeners, though not an old one yet. Not a child either. Someone in the long middle of being useful: old enough that my knees know the routes before thought, young enough that I still cross when others hesitate, foolish enough that I sometimes want to hear what no one has named.

I would live in the circuit of the Three Drips.

That is not its first name, but it is the name people use because of the shelter there: an overhang of pale limestone under a shelf of black basalt, where water falls from three points in the roof whether or not rain has come. The drips do not strike together. One is quick and cold and tastes faintly of iron. One is slow, fat, almost sweet. One stops when storms are near, which makes visitors laugh until they have been there long enough to stop laughing.

The shelter is halfway down a cleft that cannot be seen from the upper flats. From above there is only a seam darkened with moss and a crooked tree leaning sideways over nothing. The descent is by two ladders and one place where the body must turn sideways between stone. In the rainy weeks, the lower passage becomes a white throat of water, and we do not use it. Then we come by the root path, clinging under the tree shelves, feeling with toes for wet knobs and cracks the width of fingers.

There are six of us there most seasons.

My mother’s brother, who has lost hearing in one ear and hears better than anyone with the other. My sister, who sets snares and never admits when she is pleased. Her two children, both still at the age of touching forbidden moss with their breath. An old woman who may or may not be kin but who has been in every shelter I have ever known. And me.

Others come. No shelter belongs to a family first. That would be childish. The shelter belongs to the lip of stone that turns the wind; to the three drips; to the crack in the back wall where warm air moves before dawn; to the dead sealed in the narrow chamber behind the root screen. We keep it because others kept it. We leave it better if we can. We never leave it empty of cord, ash, and a dry bundle unless hunger or fear has made beasts of us.

I would know the plateau as a sequence of distances between breath.

Not as a map.

Maps are lowland lies. They pretend the path is a thing laid down once. Here a path is a bargain renewed by weather. A ledge is safe until a thin skin of algae wakes under sudden rain. A hollow has water until a root shifts and the seep chooses another wall. A ravine is passable in morning and a drum of brown flood by noon. Even stone has moods, though one must be careful saying this where traders can hear. They think mood means fancy. It does not. It means condition, tension, wetness, heat, hidden fracture, the little warnings that enter the body before they become events.

My tool would be a staff of dark wood, polished where my hands hold it, capped with a crescent of bone. Not a grand thing. Useful. It tests mist depth, catches root ladders, taps stone, hooks a pack fallen just out of reach. Near its head I have tied three shells from old limestone and a split seed pod that rattles differently when damp. The shells are not decoration. Nothing on the crossing side of the body is only decoration.

Around my neck I would wear a small stone with a hole through it, found in a dry basin after a thunderstorm. When wind passes through it, it makes no sound that most people hear, but against the tooth it trembles. I was given it by the old woman after I learned the difference between thunder in the air and thunder under the shelf.

That was my first real lesson.

Not childhood lessons: do not follow sound, tie your sleeping cord twice, never put your full weight on green rock, if glass-frogs climb high wake everyone. Those are learned before memory hardens.

My first true lesson came when I was perhaps fourteen, at the shelter called Blue Mouth, where the cave opens behind a curtain of vines. It had rained only a little. No flood smell. No falling-stone wind. We had eaten roasted root and beetle grubs, and my cousin was scraping hide with a stone flake. Then the cave began to sing lower.

No one else moved.

I remember that most clearly. How nothing happened.

The fire went on twisting. The vines shivered in ordinary gusts. Rain ticked from leaves. The children slept.

But the song in the cave dropped, not louder, only deeper, as if some huge animal outside hearing had lowered its head into the rock.

My mother’s brother put my hand on the floor. Then my jaw. Then he touched my shoulder once.

We woke everyone without voices. We took what was already bundled. We left the pot. We left one mat. We went out through the side crack and climbed in rain so fine it seemed to come from the stone itself.

Before dawn, the back chamber of Blue Mouth fell in. Not all of it. Enough.

After that they stopped calling me wind-child and began sending me ahead to listen at new shelters.

So I would be a listener, but not one of the great ones who can name a storm by the taste of metal in the mouth. I would still make mistakes. I would still sometimes mistake my own fear for warning. That is a dangerous habit in either direction. If you hear danger everywhere, people stop hearing you. If you are proud and wait too long, stone teaches you without mercy.

My body would be marked by the place in ordinary ways.

The soles thick and split at the heel. The knees scarred white-brown from crawling under shelves. Forearms crosshatched by sedge cuts. One shoulder stronger from carrying water gourds on the same side. Hair bound close in five cords, each waxed with bitter resin against wet. A notch missing from one front tooth where I once slipped and struck my mouth on basalt. Fingernails kept short. Hearing uneven but keen to pattern. Eyes narrowed not from suspicion, but from wind.

My clothes would smell of smoke, oil leaf, old rain, and the inside of packs. A bark-cloth wrap close around the ribs. Sedge leggings tied tight at the ankle. A short cloak of treated hide, patched with translucent storm-bark sheets that rattle softly when I move. In my belt: a stone knife; bone awl; coil of vine cord; two reed whistles; folded moss dressing; lizard-skin pouch of salt; wrapped ember-cup; three copper hooks belonging not to me but to the circuit.

I would speak little in the open.

Everyone does.

Speech there is a thing the wind spends quickly. In hollows I would be less quiet. I would tease the children. I would argue over whether the south passage should be repaired before the reed clatter season. I would sing when asked, though not beautifully in the lowland sense. Beauty here is not sweetness. A beautiful song enters the crack correctly. It finds the answering surface. It leaves room for weather.

My voice would be low and repetitive. I would sing into stone more than into faces.

There is a hollow above Three Drips where the mist pools like milk and does not disperse even under wind. A person standing on its rim can see nothing of the floor, only the crooked tops of two trees rising from the white. We do not sleep there. We do not light fires there. But we go there when someone has died far from their own shelter and no body can be returned.

I have sung there twice.

Once for a boy taken by a fall while gathering cliff honey. Once for a woman from another circuit whose people arrived after black-rim rain, carrying only her staff, her hair cord, and the story of where the flood took her.

For such songs we face stone, not sky. The sky carries things away too quickly. Stone keeps the shape of what touches it.

The song has only four tones. The names come after, spoken softly into a crack, followed by the places that must remember: the ledge, the pool, the ladder, the shelter where the person last ate, the tree whose roots they used, the wrong path if there was one. Not accusation. Instruction. The dead become useful when the living are brave enough to remember exactly.

I would fear the wide flats most.

Not cliffs. Cliffs are honest enough if one attends to them. Not caves. Caves have voices. Not even flood channels, though they kill swiftly.

The open backs of the plateau are worse because they offer too much sky and no answer. Warm stone for hundreds of paces. Lichen crisp underfoot. Air moving in sheets. Cairns low and uncertain through mist. There, a person is a loose seed. There, nothing gathers; nothing holds.

Crossing them, I would crouch without thinking. I would spread my fingers slightly for balance. I would keep the staff angled, not upright. I would watch the feather markers on distant cairns if any remained. I would not trust a lull. No one sensible trusts a lull.

And yet I would love those crossings too.

Not while exposed, perhaps. Love is easier afterward. But I would know the feeling of coming out from a cleft into sudden height, seeing the plateau unroll in old green-black tables beneath racing cloud, and feeling for a breath that the world has no edge. The land so worn it has become calm in its bones. The air so young it cannot stop inventing itself. Fern basins bright in fractures. Waterfalls appearing like white cords and vanishing before one can name them. Birds cutting through updrafts like thrown knives.

I would be afraid and at home. Those are not opposites here.

My work would take me between shelters before storms, because listeners are sent where uncertainty thickens. I would be asked: Is the old tube humming early? Has the moss darkened near the back crack? Are the frogs high? Does the stone sweat before dawn? Is the lower ladder worth mending, or will the season take it? Can the pregnant woman be moved tomorrow, or must we make the roof hold another night?

Sometimes I would know.

Sometimes I would say, “Wait.”

Sometimes, if honest, I would say, “I do not hear enough.”

That answer saves more lives than false knowing.

At gatherings I would be both useful and awkward. Gatherings are loud, and not with plateau sound only. Human sound layered thick: children shrieking under overhangs, elders correcting storm accounts, flutes, seed rattles, lovers whispering in fern paths, traders coughing lowland complaints, stone blades being tested, meat fat striking coals. After days of listening for what lies beneath noise, I would find so many voices tiring.

Still, I would go.

Everyone goes when the season permits. Not always to the same basin, not every year, but enough. One cannot marry echoes. One cannot trade only with moss. News must move by mouths as well as wind.

I would bring wound veils from the thread colonies, bitter lichen, two bundles of dried copper flowers, and a hollow stone from the south shelves that gives back a child’s voice as if an old man answered. In return I might get salt, a new awl, a story of a shelter gone bad, perhaps a bronze shaving small enough to wrap in bark and large enough to matter.

I would sit with other listeners after the fires dropped low. We would compare the season’s sounds.

The west tubes began too soon.

The fog at Milk Basin has grown heavy.

The reed ravine clattered three nights without wind.

The browsers knocked early.

The black lichens sharpened before the rain, not after.

None of these alone would mean disaster. Together they would become a shape we could not yet name. We would argue softly, each pretending not to be afraid of being wrong.

Perhaps that would be the thing I carried most: not prophecy, but attention under uncertainty.

The plateau does not speak in commands. It gives pressure, scent, tremor, absence. It gives a frog too high on the wall. A bird not riding. A drip stopped. A note lowered by half a breath. To live there is to accept that knowledge comes incomplete and must still be acted upon.

If I were more than human, I would not be a spirit. This world does not seem to want spirits with faces.

If I were other than one of the Hollow People, I might be a breath-darkening moss in a cave mouth, recording every exhale as a temporary shadow. Or a wind-borne seed waiting ten dry years in a crack for nine minutes of rain. Or one of the pale browsers, stepping soundlessly between basins under mist, carrying whole future thickets in my gut.

But no.

I think I am human here because fragility matters. The plateau is too old, too broad, too finished in its bones for anything grand to be the right answer. To belong to it, I must be small enough to need shelter. Small enough to listen. Small enough to understand that a low wall reset each season is not failure but wisdom.

So you would find me at dusk below the Three Drips, if you knew how not to follow the wrong sound.

The wind would be turning downward from the upper shelves. The pools would tremble. The sideways trees would rattle their peeling bark like dry applause. Mist would rise through the cleft and erase the ladder below. Under the limestone lip, a fire would burn low behind a crescent wall no higher than a sleeping child.

I would be kneeling beside the back stone with my teeth resting lightly against my staff.

Not praying exactly.

Listening.

The quick drip would strike. Then the sweet one. The third would be silent, and because of that silence I would lift one hand.

Around me, the shelter would become still in the human way: not without sound, never without sound, but with every person suddenly gathered inside their own skin.

The old woman would begin tying the roof cords tighter.

My sister would wake the children.

My mother’s brother would smile with only half his mouth, hearing now what I had heard first.

Outside, the high green emptiness would roar and darken and open its many throats.

And we would make ourselves small beneath stone, as we had been taught.

Not conquered.

Not safe.

Only correctly placed.
