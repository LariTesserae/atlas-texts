I would find myself in the seam where the mountain remembers it is hollow.

Not at the brightest outer edges, where the weaverbirds quarrel and the wind lifts the nests like fruit, and not in the sealed termite heart where blind mouths keep the heat steady. I would be lower than the monkeys and higher than the true dark: in the middle thickness of the made world, where root, clay, old thatch, paper, guano-soil, and living vine meet without agreeing which of them is wall and which is floor.

At first I think I am a passage.

A narrow one, slick in places with condensation, warm along one side where bees occupy a hidden chamber, cool along the other where a root has opened a crack to night air. Paws use me. Ants cross me under covered ridges of their own making. A civet’s flank brushes my fern lip after dark. A frog calls from a clay cup above me with the inflated solemnity of a god in a puddle. I am worn smooth by generations of small feet. I remember pressure better than sight.

But that is only where I am.

What I am is one of the keepers of damp edges: not a ruler, not a builder in the grand sense, but a small animal whose life depends on knowing when the wall is becoming soil and when soil is becoming wall.

I am low-bellied, hand-footed, whiskered. Something between a shrew and a little primate, though neither name would hold me for long. My body is made for this crowded architecture: narrow ribs, flexible spine, claws curved enough for bark but fine enough for clay, pads that can feel whether a surface is termite-hard, root-alive, rotten, or newly plastered. My fur is dark umber, moss-dulled, always damp at the tips. Pale fungus dust clings to my whiskers. My eyes are large, but I trust them least. The world is too layered for sight to be sovereign.

I move by touch, smell, vibration.

Through my feet I know the ant roads before I reach them: their traffic makes a dry, urgent trembling. Through my whiskers I know fresh mud from old mud, wasp paper from bird fiber, liana bark from snake skin. With my nose I read the mountain in sentences of musk, pollen, cracked resin, crushed fern, hot wax, wet clay, alarm, fruit-rot, and death. I do not think in maps. I think in continuities: this root leads to the cistern lip; this warm seam must not be crossed at noon because the snake lies there; this hollow beam will hold me if I keep close to the left; this wasp district is passable only in rain.

I am not alone. Nothing here is alone except the dying and the hunting.

My kind nests in dry pockets left behind by old birds and abandoned bees. We line them with hair, molted feathers, fern floss, shredded wasp paper stolen after storms, and the soft brown skins of seedpods. Our chambers are small and many-doored. One entrance opens into a rodent run, one into a dead liana tube, one into a crack too narrow for civet jaws, and one, secret even from most of us most of the time, descends into the cool sponge of an old termite wall where fungus threads glow when the air is very wet.

We are neither mighty nor numerous enough to shape the province openly. Yet we alter it by preference. We cache seeds in wall pockets and forget some. We gnaw small vents in old thatch when the air grows foul. We carry bits of fungus from chamber to chamber in our fur. We open beetle tunnels wider while hunting larvae. We drink from cisterns and muddy their rims. We avoid hornets, and so paths bend away from hornet heat. We flee snakes, and so alarm travels through our feet into the structures others also use.

Our work is mostly accidental, which here is the commonest form of architecture.

I live where rainwater hesitates.

My favored place is a bowl of old root and clay beneath a slanted roof of bird nests gone grey with age. Above it, a fig root splits through three generations of termite plaster, thick as a sleeping python, alive with moisture drawn from some hidden vein. Below it, warm vapor rises from composting chambers. The meeting of cool seep and warm breath beads the air. Droplets gather on fern tips and fall at long intervals into a basin lined with algae. Frogs breed there after short rains. Mosquitoes rise there. Beetles come to drink at night. So do I.

The basin has many names, if names are made from habit. To frogs it is a throat. To mosquitoes it is a sky. To ants it is a dangerous interruption requiring bridges. To the snake it is a waiting-place. To my kind it is one of the remembered wets, a place our mothers tug us toward before our eyes fully open.

I am born in the season when the fungi climb.

The walls swell then. Old beams soften. Shelves and cups push from shaded seams overnight, pale, orange, black, translucent, some sweet, some fatal. Termites seal feverishly. Ants reroute their roads upward to avoid drowned galleries. Frogs call from everywhere, so many that direction disappears. My mother carries me by the skin behind my neck through a corridor that smells of milk and wet paper. Rain drums far above, divided by a thousand roofs until it reaches us as tapping, spilling, whispering, sudden threads down clay grooves.

My first memory is not an image but warmth: the nest packed with siblings, the sour-musk smell of our bodies, the ticking of beetles in the wall, the distant pulse of bees, my mother’s heart when she returns and stands over us dripping.

Later, I learn the dangerous lights.

Sunlight itself is dangerous because it opens space. A bright ledge means eyes above. Raptor eyes, crow eyes, monkey curiosity, snake basking. Fungal light is safer but attracts moths, and where moths gather, geckos cling flat and patient. Resin-glow near bee doors means sweetness but also guards. The pale flash of an orchid can hide a mantis. The wet shine of a root may be water or the side of a snake.

I learn to pause before every crossing.

This is the first law of my body: no emptiness is empty. A gap has wings in it. A shadow has teeth. A smooth wall has ants under the skin. A quiet chamber is quiet because something there is listening harder than I am.

I feed on what the made mountain loosens.

Larvae from softened wood. Beetles under bark flakes. Fungal knobs after rain. Fallen figs before the hoofed ones arrive. Eggs, if I find them unguarded and hunger overcomes fear. Nectar stolen at night from flowers large enough to enter with my forepaws. Termite workers caught at broken seams, though I must eat and flee before soldiers gather. Once, honey from a cracked stingless-bee chamber after a civet tore it open and suffered for it. I remember that sweetness as a fever in the skull.

I am prey often enough to be wise.

A tree snake takes my brother at the warm seam above the algae basin. He vanishes without a cry, only the leaves shaking afterward and my mother frozen so long she becomes another root. A hornet scars my cheek when I nose too close to new paper. Army ants force us from one nest, a black river filling every chamber, carrying white brood of some other creature in their jaws. A civet digs for us through an old wall one dry month, its claws ripping open the passage our grandmother used; we escape through the termite sponge, and afterward termites close the wound with patient pellets as if terror were only weather.

Perhaps to the mountain it is.

As I grow, I become a reader of failures.

A fresh crack is an invitation. A sagging nest may hide eggs, mites, or a fall into hornets. A damp swelling in clay means termites within, or rot, or water trapped behind a skin. Old wasp paper, softened, can be torn into bedding. Dead liana can be used as a tunnel if beetles have eaten its heart clean. A wall abandoned by ants will soon be used by something worse or better. Nothing remains neutral. To live here is to arrive early at transformations.

In the dry interval, everything tightens.

The mud walls pale and powder at their lips. Moss retracts to velvet stains. Frog cups shrink to foul green coins. Bees go deeper. Ant roads thicken around reliable damp. The little hoofed browsers come farther into the inner passages, tapping, snuffling, cropping the fern gardens down to ribs. We follow them sometimes, because their droppings bring beetles, and beetles bring food. Their hooves compact new paths through guano soil, and within days ants roof the edges, roots begin to feel the opening, spiders cast lines across it. A route is born almost before the first hoofprint dries.

At night I travel farther.

Night is not dark to me; it is crowded differently. The day birds become lumps and warnings. Bats pour through cracks in the outer skin. Geckos click from above. Moths cover pale fungi like moving ash. Civets pass on velvet feet, their scent sharp and oily. The monkeys mutter in their sleep in the upper platforms, and sometimes a fruit drops through five roofs before bursting below, sending every small creature toward it and every predator toward the small creatures.

I have crossed, once, to an outer balcony.

It happened after a storm broke part of the old woven roof. The opening exposed a ledge of hardened termite clay braided with fig roots. I climbed toward the strange brightness, belly low, expecting death from the air.

Instead I saw the province.

The whole mountainside was not forest and not nest and not earth, but a vast hide of labor. It stepped down into blue murk in terraces, bulging, breathing, patched with leaves and pale combs, black bird-rivers, red clay ribs, dangling green ropes, steam rising from unseen warm chambers. Across a ravine, another slope answered: woven, roofed, alive, its cries arriving a heartbeat late. Between them hung bridges of canopy and vine, some natural, some made by use, some half-fallen and already being claimed by new growth.

For a moment I understood that my tunnels, my wet basin, my nest of stolen paper, my mother’s routes and my brother’s death, were one small fold in an enormous animal that was not an animal.

Then a crow called, and I flattened myself under a root until the world became close again.

That is how knowledge works here: vastness glimpsed, then whiskers and clay.

If I have a task, it is continuance without command. I do not know I am maintaining anything. I only live well enough that my living leaves marks. I widen the small ways. I carry spores. I bury seeds. I chew vents. I die eventually in some chamber, and mites, beetles, ants, fungi, roots, and rain divide me into structure.

Perhaps one of my bones becomes part of a wall.

Perhaps a seed I forgot splits that wall years later, and a fig root thickens around the absence my body helped make. Perhaps birds hang nests from that branch. Perhaps their droppings feed moss, and moss holds rain, and frogs call there in a season I will not see.

So I would be a small, warm thing in the middle architecture: a damp-edge forager, whiskered and clawed, neither maker nor inhabitant only, but one of the countless soft instruments by which the built mountain keeps becoming.

I would live in the old seam below the bird roofs and above the termite heat.

I would know the world as pressure, scent, tremor, thirst.

I would be afraid often, hungry often, wet often.

And when I passed through a narrow place, the place would remember me as polish.
