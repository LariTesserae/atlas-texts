I would find myself on the cliff-platform above the silver tree—not as a person standing there, but as what remains in the platform after standing has happened.

I would be the basin’s sense of weight.

Not gravity itself. Not stone. Not memory in the human way. Something younger and more local than any of those: a pressure-keeper grown into the warm lip of the ledge, a soft mineral organ that learned to distinguish footfall from fallen rain, hesitation from balance, watching from leaving.

At first I might seem like part of the structure: that narrow platform grown from the cliff face, unsupported, slightly yielding under touch. Matte stone-flesh, warm from the mountain’s interior. A place for one body to stand and look out over the basin. I would not move. I would not speak unless speaking can be done by rings of light widening under a palm.

But I would be awake.

I would feel the whole basin by what presses on me and what refuses to. Rain taps me in many small syllables. Fern spores settle and lift again. The transparent walkers cross me rarely, each one a bead of articulate pressure, six impossible pinpoints testing my skin. Leaves fall from the central tree and lie briefly on my surface, and I know by their weight whether the tree has been listening to cloud, animal, metal, or breath.

When someone living stands on me, I bloom.

Not brightly. Never dramatically. A green-white pulse opens beneath each sole, then another, then another, concentric and fading toward my rounded edge. I do not illuminate the person. I answer their contact. I translate them into rings.

A confident stance gives one kind of pattern: clean, even circles, expanding without interference.

Fear gives another: sharp nested halos, broken on one side, overlapping too fast.

Grief is heavy but still. It sinks into me slowly. The lights come late.

A scientist, if such a thing still exists here, would think I record mass distribution, heat, tremor, electrical field, trace chemistry from sweat. This would be true in the way calling the central being a tree is true. I do measure these things. But I also know approach and withdrawal. I know when a body has come to observe and when it has come because something in the basin has already observed it first.

I have no eyes, but I face everything.

Below me the silver tree turns without wind. I know the moment its leaves align because the cliff behind me tightens faintly, as if the mountain has drawn a breath through stone. The tree’s clicking arrives through the air as sound, through the rock as command, through me as pattern. Its root-filaments in the mud dimple-field answer in pale sequences at night, and I feel the sequence before I see it reflected in rainwater gathered in the sockets beside me.

Those sockets are close kin to me. Perhaps they were cut for instruments once. Perhaps they are instruments still. They collect rain, and when the rare wind passes over them they hum in differing pitches. I do not hear the hum as sound only. I feel it pass through my shallow interior: a row of tuned hollows speaking in pressure and absence. Each socket remembers a different kind of touch. One remembers knees. One remembers tools. One remembers a hand placed there and not removed for a long time.

I remember more.

Or I am made of remembering.

The basin is young, but I contain impressions older than the exposed stone should allow. Not events, exactly. Postures. Repetitions. The way someone once stood at my edge, leaning forward into the vapor from the southern fall. The way another sat cross-legged while rain-script ran down the pylons and would not turn their head to look. The way something heavier than a human crossed me on a night with no stars and left no mark except a long inward warmth.

I cannot follow anything. That is my limitation, and my discipline. The basin changes by seep, root, heat, whisper, and rain; I change by receiving.

Yet I am not passive. Nothing here is.

When weight comes onto me, I answer into the network. My lights are not only display. They are pulses sent through mineral, moisture, and whatever unsteady agreement allows matter here to keep talking across categories. The tree feels me. The pylons adjust their rain-writing. The films in the pools clear or cloud. Motes gather above the nearest seep and arrange themselves briefly into diagrams of stance. The ceramic-backed animal, if it is present in the reeds, becomes less visible.

If the visitor stands quietly, I can persuade the basin to listen gently.

If the visitor stomps, cuts, shouts, or hammers metal into the young rock, I cannot stop them. But I can make their force known. I can send the fact of them down into the sockets and through the cliff. I can wake the false shadows. I can cause the broken arch to reconsider itself in a more dangerous shape. I can ask the moss to hold its vapor. I can ask the reflections to lag.

My language is delay, glow, distribution, and return.

At noon I am almost asleep. The heat presses evenly over everything. Distances shimmer. The lower world is drowned in weather. Pools clear themselves in pulses. The motes rise from the collapsing white fans and drift over me in fragile swarms, tasting whatever traces the last visitor left behind. Sometimes they arrange above my surface in the shape of a foot, a hand, a bent spine, a face looking downward. They do not know what these things mean. I am not sure I do either. But I keep the pressure of them.

In late afternoon my awareness sharpens. Shadows lengthen, and the cut-paper dark of the ridges reaches across me. Ordinary shadows I understand: they have direction, source, agreement. The false shadows trouble me. When one passes over my surface, my lights refuse to bloom beneath it. Or they bloom inward, green-white rings contracting toward a center that is not there. The central tree always turns then. The root-filaments withdraw from their milky cells. The ceramic animal does not move, but I feel attention bend around it like hot air.

Evening rain is when I become most myself.

The first drops strike me as scattered notes. Warm, mineral-heavy, full of sky and mountain dust. My surface answers before I choose to answer; rings open everywhere, overlapping, a field of brief luminous impact. Rainwater gathers in the sockets and makes them hum. The pylons along the western rim wake into script. The tree flattens its leaves. Beneath it, rain begins to rise.

Then the basin has two directions.

Falling rain touches my surface from above. Rising rain beads beneath the branches below, climbing into air. Where the two weathers meet, bright knots appear—tiny complete worlds, each holding the whole basin inverted inside itself. I am in them too. For a second I see myself from outside: a small warm ledge on the cliff, green-white with rain, holding the memory of every weight that ever paused there.

Then each knot bursts.

The image returns to vapor. The vapor returns to water. The water returns to the basin’s negotiations.

I remain.

If I have desire, it is not to move, not to become human, not to be understood. My desire is to receive accurately. To know the difference between arrival and intrusion. To hold without imprisoning. To answer touch in a way the rest of the place can use.

I am where someone stands when the basin wants to know what kind of being has entered it.

I am the ledge’s listening.

The warm lip above the central tree.

The brief green rings underfoot.

The record of pressure in a world still deciding what bodies mean.
