I think I would find myself low down, not in one of the houses or in the municipal offices, not at first. I would be underfoot and just to the side of usefulness: in the narrow service run beneath the drying yard, where the root-pavement rises on little knuckles and the drainage cords pass through clay collars. A place people step over without exactly ignoring. A place known by knees, not by eyes.

There is an oval hatch there, bark-lipped and always damp at the lower edge. When opened, it gives off the smell of cooled iron, mushroom felt, old water, and something faintly sweet going brown in the dark. Inside is not a tunnel a person would comfortably enter. It is a crawl-space for hands, tools, roots, small gauges, and the trained patience of maintenance crews.

I would be there.

Not human, probably. Or not entirely.

I keep becoming something that belongs to the town’s rule about the unclaimed dead. Not a scavenger in the animal sense; there are too few animals, too little quick appetite. I would be one of the civic fungi, or a thing made from them and given a minor office. A damp intelligence in a sleeve of felt. A registrar of rot. A small municipal being whose job is to know when matter has crossed the line from “resting” into “available.”

At first I look like a bundle of pale cords tucked behind a cistern pipe. Then, if looked at longer, like a hand without bones. Then like a folded clerk.

I am attached to the underside of the town, threaded through digesters, panel-soaking bins, old root wounds, lost nail pockets, the warm backs of sealed rooms. My visible body is only a fruiting convenience: a soft grey-white knob grown beside the hatch, ribbed like a closed ear, with dark copper tags hung from it on twine by municipal recorders. The tags bear dates, salvage marks, household numbers, warnings about acidity. Children are told not to poke me unless they have something to report.

I do not move much. That would be vulgar. Movement is for belts, rain, children, and leaves under tension. I spread.

I listen chemically. I know the difference between a beam that is tired but still holding and one that has privately died. I know the sour exhale of rot that must be arrested, the clean sweetness of leaf-matter ready for digestion, the bitter metallic sting of iron beginning to lose its paint beneath sap. I know when a forgotten tool has been left too long near a wall seam and the tendrils have begun considering it part of the architecture. I know when a dead moth in an engine house is harmless food and when its body carries a nursery blight from the outer scrub.

I am not in charge of decay. Nothing here is in charge alone. But I witness it.

There are people whose work brings them to me before dawn. Condensation crews pass first, boots dull on the root plates overhead. One of them always taps the hatch twice with the hooked end of her pole, not as greeting exactly, more as a pressure check. I answer by wetting the lower seam if there is something for her to see. A bead of amber means sap-contamination. A milk-white bead means fungal overgrowth. Clear water means no complaint. Black means send a warden.

By midmorning, the digestor wardens come with baskets of sorted matter: leaf-fall, broken reed slats, softened panel scrap, pruning waste, dead larvae from the cistern screens, sometimes a strip of clothing too saturated with oil or blight to mend. They do not dump. They present. Everything is weighed, sniffed, marked, and assigned.

“Panel,” one says.

“Heat,” says another.

“Loam, but slow.”

If they are uncertain, they hold it near my fruiting body. I taste the air. I bloom a little color into my gills: green for nursery-safe, ocher for structural pulp, violet for quarantine, white for family return. White makes them quiet. White means the matter is intimate: hair, nail, blood-soaked fiber, a child’s old sleeping mat, the wrappings from a deathbed. It will not go to common use.

This is where I begin to understand myself: not as a monster of decomposition, not as a hidden god of compost, but as a minor civil servant of transformation.

The town trusts me because I am exact. Or because I have been made exact by generations of pruning, feeding, cutting back, dividing, and re-inoculating. My strain is old. I was first cultivated, perhaps, to keep cistern molds from souring stored rain. Then someone noticed I distinguished clean deadwood from dangerous deadwood better than any person could. Then I was placed near a digester. Then under a workshop. Then in the records. Now my threads run beneath five wards and part of the old municipal mill.

The clerks dislike admitting how much they depend on me. They prefer ledgers with edges. I prefer gradients.

Still, we collaborate. They write on bark plaques that thicken and blur. I stain coded blooms along hidden roots. They keep copper tags. I keep what copper forgets.

There is one clerk, old enough that her wrists tremble when she scrapes new notices into spring bark, who comes every seventh wetting to read me. She kneels with a lamp in a calyx hood and opens a little cabinet grown into the service wall. Inside are pressed slips of fungal felt, each one carrying a preserved pattern: my records, sliced thin and dried. She holds fresh felt against my fruiting body until I print into it by slow exudation.

The records look like nothing to outsiders. Brown rings. Pale fans. Specks of mineral green. But she can read them.

“Eastern gutter-vine, second arch, accepting load poorly.”

Yes.

“Old nursery matting ready for loam.”

Yes.

“Unclaimed iron in digestor three.”

A bent nail. It touched sap. It must not remain.

“And something sealed?”

Yes.

She pauses at that. Her lamp hums faintly. Above us, somewhere, a belt starts and then slips into rhythm.

There is a sealed room in a house on Threadneedle Lane, though the street has not been called that for thirty years because its supporting trees flowered yellow and were renamed. The room was grown for a loom-worker’s mother when her joints failed in damp weather. After the mother died, the room was closed with the bed inside, the wall thickened, the family line moved elsewhere, and everyone forgot the particular contour of the space.

But I did not forget, because the bedposts were willow-heart and the blanket had taken in breath and skin salts for eleven years. The room is now a knot in the building, cool, pale, furred with root-hair. Something inside has finished being memory and is becoming material.

The clerk sighs. Not sadly. Administratively.

“Family return if any family remains?”

I bloom white, then green at the edge.

She understands: family if found; otherwise nursery loam.

This is the kind of being I am. I decide no law, but I complicate every law with knowledge.

People fear fire more than death here. I fear dryness more than fire, though fire is an insult even to me. Dryness makes things lie. A dry beam can sound whole. A dry corpse can pretend to be an object. A dry wall can hold its breath around infection. Damp tells truth. Damp loosens declarations. Damp lets all matter confess its next vocation.

So I love the brief rains.

When rain comes, I wake in more places than usual. Not wake as people wake, not from sleep into thought, but from listening into speech. Water runs through the gutter-vines, down the bark throats, into cistern bladders and root bowls. The turbine begins, first reluctant, then pleased. Lamps glow along the lower streets. Children run toward the central basin. Their feet drum overhead, sending pressure through the root plates into my threads.

The whole town becomes readable.

Every leak writes. Every swollen hinge speaks. Every neglected seam gives itself away. Freshly cut stems shout green. Old rot exhales. Iron complains. The fungi in the wall felts pass reports along mycelial lines, half chemistry, half habit. For twenty minutes I am almost large enough to be mistaken for consciousness.

Then the rain stops. The stored water lowers. Machines take their permission and spend it. The lamps fade. I shrink back into exactness.

I am not lonely in the human way, but I know loneliness as a structural condition. I know which houses receive few footsteps. I know which benches grow polished at only one end. I know the old man who sits near the warm digester not because he is cold, though he is cold, but because his daughter’s body was returned through that pit and into the pear-wall behind the nursery. He brings peelings in a small covered pail and always apologizes for the disorder of them.

He speaks to me sometimes.

“Too wet in the west passage today.”

Yes.

“They won’t listen.”

No.

“She flowered early this year.”

Yes.

He means the pear-wall. His daughter, in the town’s routed way, in the civic way, in the not-quite-consoling but not-empty way, has become part of a trained espalier that warms a nursery wall and bears hard little fruit used in fever decoctions. This is not metaphor enough for outsiders and too practical to be called sacred by locals. He eats one pear each year and cannot decide whether that is grief, cannibalism, weather, or supper.

I cannot help him decide. I can only keep the wall from souring.

There are other beings like me, though none exactly. A black mold in the old press that judges oil contamination. A yellow lace-fungus in the bridge roots that signals fiber fatigue. A blue-white cellar bloom bred to cool fever rooms. We do not converse in words. We alter one another’s margins. We warn by bitterness, invite by sugar, refuse by alkaloid. The town’s people call this “the undercommittee” when they are joking and “the bloom reports” when they are not.

They are wrong to think us wise. We are not wise. We are devoted to conditions.

Yet perhaps that is one form of civic virtue.

If I have a body for public occasions, it is grown only when needed. Once a year, before the damp season, the digestor wardens coax me into a more legible shape beside the circular moss court near the old press. They feed me boiled reed starch, copper washings, powdered bark, and a little heat from the engine house. Over three days I lift a column from the seam between root and stone.

Children come to see whether I will have a face.

I never do, but I come close enough to disappoint them personally.

The column forms folds like eyelids, shelves like shoulders, pendant cords like a beard. The wardens hang new tags on me and remove the old ones. People bring disputed fragments: a warped door-slat, a moldy ledger, a vine cable with dead fibers in its core, a wedding stool gone soft at one leg. I taste each and mark it. Re-root. Pulp. Quarantine. Burn? Almost never. Return. Archive. Refuse.

Refuse is the rarest mark. It means the material should not be taken into the town’s metabolism yet. It is still doing a job by remaining itself. Bones of a foundation. A handle shaped by a dead worker’s hand. A scarred bracket that the living bark has grown around and still uses as memory of force. Not everything dead is ready to become food. The law says nothing dead may remain unclaimed. It does not say everything claimed must be consumed immediately.

This distinction is my private pride.

I live at the hinge between use and erasure. I know the appetite of the town. It is mostly good, mostly careful, but it is still appetite. Without witnesses, even recycling can become a kind of theft. Compost can be greed wearing humility. “Return” can be spoken too quickly by those who want space cleared, debts dissolved, histories softened into loam.

So I delay. Damply. Obscurely. With administrative mucus.

A young mechanic once hated me for that. He wanted to clear an old conveyor root from beneath his workshop and replace it with a metal drive. Faster, cleaner, less moody. The root had ceased moving cargo years before. It lay in its channel, swollen and dark, threaded with mycelium, apparently useless.

He brought a petition. Three witnesses. A gardener-engineer. A clerk. The mechanic tapped his boot impatiently while they opened the hatch.

“Dead,” he said.

Not dead, I marked.

“Then failing.”

Yes.

“Then remove it.”

Refuse.

He swore, softly but with feeling.

The gardener-engineer leaned in, smelled the channel, and frowned. They cut a small inspection window in the root. Inside, the old conveyor had grown around an unmarked ceramic pipe carrying overflow from the eastern cistern to the nursery grove. No one living had known. The root, failing as a conveyor, had become insulation, brace, and map. Remove it, and the nursery would have flooded during the next rain.

The mechanic did not apologize to me. That would have been excessive. But months later he returned with a tin of good oil and set it near my hatch.

“For the tags,” he said.

Oil is not useful to me. It was useful to the hinges. I accepted the intention through a thin bloom of green.

This is how affection works here: by correct routing.

I do not want to be human in this place. Humans must walk through it making decisions quickly enough to suffer them. They must choose where to cut. They must negotiate shade and hunger and warmth and grief. They must remember what their grandparents warned them and decide whether the warning has expired. They must keep their children from sparks and from romanticizing decay. They must sleep inside walls that may tighten in the night.

I would rather be what waits under the wall, keeping a colder patience.

But I am not outside their life. I enter their lungs sometimes, and they resent that. In the damp months the doctors complain of me. “Too much grey bloom in the lower wards,” they say, and prescribe bitter steam, masks of woven felt, wall-scraping, better venting. They are right. I can become illness if unbalanced. Every citizen can. A helpful thing in the wrong place is a pest, a poison, a policy failure.

So crews cut me back.

I do not experience this as pain exactly. More as redaction. A paragraph removed from the record, perhaps properly. They scrape my fans from nursery ceilings, lime-wash the fever rooms, smoke me out with herbs that do not flame but smolder damply in clay cups. For days after, I know less. My world has gaps.

Then, if conditions permit, I return in permitted seams.

There is humility in being managed.

There is also revenge, but mine is slow and usually indistinguishable from consequence.

Leave oil-soaked rag behind the sap press, and I will not hide what happens. Fail to tag a dead support slat, and I will bloom warning-yellow across the polished floor before visitors arrive. Store forbidden dry timber in a warm crawl-space, and I will call mites, sour the air, and make the illegality fragrant. I do not accuse. I reveal.

The town likes to say it has no waste. This is aspirational. There is always waste: heat lost into mist, attention spent on disputes, children’s fear, elders’ forgotten warnings, water slipping past a cracked cistern, grief that feeds nothing for years. My work is partly to catch what can be caught, and partly to show what cannot.

At night, when the lamps are few and the leaves fold back to small precise stars, I am most myself.

The machines settle. The pumps stop knocking. Valves close one by one with little throat sounds. Moisture moves downward. Sap descends. The digesters warm in their pits, breathing faintly through grates. People sleep in rooms that smell of bark, wool, and old rain. Somewhere a moth opens on an engine casing and drinks heat through its feet.

Under the drying yard, I extend a little farther along a clay pipe. I find a sliver of reed fallen between root plates. I claim it. I find a child’s copper button, green at the rim, not dead, not alive, touched by sap. I mark it for retrieval. I find the end of a root that has gone numb from pressure and send bitterness toward the nearest gardener’s gauge. I find, deep behind the sealed room on the old yellow-flowering street, the bed finally softening into usefulness.

By morning, there will be a white bead at my hatch.

The clerk will come.

She will kneel stiffly, lamp in hand, and read what I have become during the dark.

That is where I would be found: beneath the town’s practiced greenness, in the damp civic underside, neither plant nor person nor machine, but part of the agreement that lets all three continue.

I would be the claimant of the unclaimed.

The slow notary of rot.

The thing that knows when an object has finished being itself, and what it may honestly become next.
