I think I would find myself near the bottom, but not at the basin’s edge.

Not one of the dark-suited basin tenders, not quite. I would not be important enough to kneel beside the smoked glass while hexagons tremble across the surface and everyone nearby learns, by posture alone, whether the city is merely thinking or beginning to worry.

I would be a little above it.

There is a narrow gallery halfway down the cleft, where the wall has been cut back just enough to make a passage between two recessed service rooms. It is not a public bridge and not a private corridor. It belongs to neither crossing nor dwelling, but to adjustment. One side is basalt, wet and black and newly wounded. The other is a run of pale panels with hairline seams, each seam leading to something necessary and uninteresting to most people: valve logic, thermal braid junctions, condensate discriminators, the small listening sockets that tell the city whether a flow has gone granular with ice.

The gallery is always colder than expected.

Not dangerously cold, not marked amber or blue, but cold enough that the inside of the nose tightens, cold enough that breath must be caught by the collar before it fogs the inspection lenses. The floor is grooved diagonally so that meltwater slides away from boots toward a grate no wider than two fingers. Through the grate one can hear, if one is patient, a secondary channel carrying returned condensation toward the basin: not the clean public thread under glass, but the less elegant underflow, full of tiny corrections, backwash, rejected mineral, and the faint clicking bodies of custodians at work.

That is where I would be.

I would be someone whose work concerns the almosts.

Almost frozen. Almost contaminated. Almost too warm. Almost silent in the wrong way. Almost within tolerance, but not quite.

A maintenance reader, perhaps. Not an engineer in the grand sense. Not a designer of bridges or mirrors. More like a diagnostician of small deviations: one of those residents summoned when the city has not failed, but has begun to phrase itself differently.

I would carry a flat case clipped to my thigh, sealed along its edges with a strip of dull orange so it can be found if dropped in low light. Inside would be tools too slight to seem consequential: capillary probes, frost combs, acoustic pins, membrane patches, mineral swabs, a spool of conductive thread, a hand lens with its own heat halo, three disposable microfilters, a strip of old ceramic marked with comparison stains. I would know each tool by touch through gloves. I would dislike needing to look down.

My suit would be pale once, but no longer uniform. The left forearm would have been replaced after a contact heater failed and scorched the insulation from within. One knee would be darker, a newer membrane with a slightly different flex. Along the cuffs, where I repeatedly press against wet access panels, there would be a permanent greying from mineral residue. My hood would fit close. My face shield would be scratched in one lower corner but still within optical tolerance. I would have been meaning to request a replacement for months and would not have done so because the scratch teaches me where not to look.

I would be cleft-born, or close enough that the distinction matters only to archivists.

I would not remember first seeing the sky as open. The sky, to me, would be the strip: a pale vertical measure, brighter after snowfall, hard and blue after dry wind, gone entirely when cloud seals the ridge. I would not think of it as small. I would think of it as precise.

Open country would disturb me, though I might be ashamed to admit it. The idea of walking where weather can approach from all directions would feel careless. The idea of soil underfoot—soil with its anonymous damp life, its unmeasured colonies, its seeds waiting for no permission—would be fascinating and faintly obscene. I would have seen images of forests in the education rooms: trunks rising like pipe bundles, leaves in such number they defeated counting, light broken into green noise. I would understand that many people find them beautiful. I might, too, from behind glass.

But home would be black stone, pale ceramic, wet rails, and a city that answers when stepped upon.

My official designation might be something plain: Flow Integrity Technician, Lower Condensate Circuit, Third Interval. But that would not be what people call me. In a place like this, work names become truer than titles. I might be called a listener, though not one of the basalt listeners. Or a return-keeper. Someone who follows water after it has done its obvious work, after it has cooled a sleeve, crossed a seal, condensed from breath, rinsed a fabrication bath, collected at the underside of a conduit, been rejected by one system and invited into another.

Return water is full of gossip.

It carries evidence of rooms: a trace of sterilant from the clinic, metal from a worn pump face, skin salts from a warm chamber gathering, volatile markers from a cultivation leak, a wrong sweetness from algae nutrient, too much oxygen after a valve stutter, the unmistakable bitterness of basalt dust after a new fracture opens somewhere above. Clean water is easier to respect. Returned water must be understood.

I would be good at that.

Not brilliant. Not the person called during a catastrophic breach. But reliable in the uneventful way the city honors. I would know when to trust an instrument and when to kneel, unlatch a panel, and put two gloved fingers on a pipe to feel whether its tremor has a second rhythm underneath the first. I would know the sound of ordinary ticking and the sound of ice forming where ice has no right to form. I would know that a clear channel can be too clear. That water without expected mineral is sometimes not pure but lost. That silence after a pump cycle may be relief, or may be a held breath.

I would move slowly.

Not timidly. Slowly the way one writes a number that cannot be corrected.

On the narrow gallery above the basin, I would pause often, partly because the work requires it and partly because the city is most legible there. Above me: the suspended well, the vertical absence where droplets hesitate. Below me: the smoked basin, dark until it decides to show its geometry. Around me: small doors, small lights, small sounds, every one of them attached to some larger consequence.

I would not be lonely exactly, but much of my work would be solitary. The lower condensate paths are too narrow for conversation and too loud, in their tiny way, for distraction. When another person passed on the gallery, we would negotiate space without speaking: one hand on the warm strip of the wall, one shoulder angled away, tools drawn close so nothing brushes a sensor face. A greeting might be two fingers to the wrist seal. If we knew each other well, perhaps the fingers would linger for half a second longer.

I would have a sleeping cell on the eastern wall, four levels above the basin, behind a window too narrow to be useful as a window and yet invaluable. From it I could see a segment of the central well and one angled mirror near the upper rim. In certain weather, the mirror would flash briefly at dawn, and that flash would strike the far side of my cell just above the fold-down shelf. Years ago someone might have offered me a different cell, slightly warmer, closer to the meal corridor. I would have refused because of that one minute of light.

My possessions would be few.

A sealed flake of basalt from the fracture opened in my birth year, given to every child of that interval. A strip of obsolete flow tape on which my parent once marked a pressure anomaly by hand. A small transparent vial containing water from the first snowfall after my apprenticeship, tagged and stabilized, absurdly sentimental and officially useless. A printed image of a lowland river I do not entirely believe in: water loose under open sky, reflecting trees, wasting itself toward an ocean.

Perhaps also a name-stone. Not stone, exactly; a ceramic tile with mineral from the dead incorporated into its glaze. Someone important to me returned that way. The tile would be matte white until warmed by the palm, then a faint line would appear in it: not a portrait, not a word, only a maintenance path they kept clear for forty-one years. Their tenderness preserved as infrastructure.

I would have learned from them that love is attention continued past convenience.

That would be my creed, if I had one.

Attention to seals. To people’s gloves. To whether someone’s breathing collar is hissing slightly on the left side. To the child standing too near a threshold because the appearing railing delights them. To a drone returning with frost on only one vane. To the low amber shift in corridor light that others do not mention because mentioning it makes the unease real.

I would not be dramatic. I might even mistrust drama. A raised voice would make me look first to the condensers, calculating how much heat the argument is wasting. But I would not be unfeeling. My feelings would have been trained to travel through practical channels. If I cared for someone, I would clean their window film before first interval. I would leave a warmed tool where their route begins. I would trade for a fresh herb ration and pretend it came surplus from cultivation. I would check the ice above their assigned bridge and file the inspection under routine weather abrasion.

If I loved them deeply, I might never say, “I cannot bear to lose you.”

I would say, “Your right boot seal is aging. Let me replace it before upper shift.”

And they would understand.

The city’s shared intelligence would know me not as a citizen profile alone, but as a pattern of pressures and habits. It would know I stop too long on the lower gallery after night-cycle. It would warm the floor beneath my left foot a fraction more because an old injury stiffens in dry cold. It would interrupt me rarely, because I dislike spoken instructions unless necessary.

When it did speak, it would use the nearest panel or the inner rim of my hood.

“Return channel seven: conductivity rising.”

“Wait.”

“Do not open that seam yet.”

Once, perhaps, in a sleep-starved interval after a storm, it might say: “You have been awake too long.”

And I would answer aloud, though no one was visible, “So have you.”

The city would not laugh. But the light might shift, almost imperceptibly, toward warmth.

I would have a complicated relationship with the basin tenders.

They would receive information I helped make trustworthy. They would stand at the visible center while I worked in service cold, clearing the less beautiful routes by which reality arrives. I might resent them when tired: their dark suits, their silence, the way people pause when their ripples move. But I would also respect them because the basin is not symbolic. It is where many almosts become one decision. Their errors would be larger than mine, and therefore lonelier.

Sometimes one of them would summon me down.

Not with urgency. Urgency is a last resort. A white line on my wrist display, then a location pulse: basin edge, south instrument cluster, second access kneel.

I would descend by the narrow stair cut into the western wall, one hand hovering near the rail that only appears if I slip. At the basin the air is different: colder at the ankles, strangely still at the face. Instruments look smaller there than they should. The smoked surface seems not to reflect light but to remember it.

The tender would not explain too much. They would show me a ripple record, a repeating deformation too regular for debris and too irregular for computation. I would take a sample from the return inlet, watch it bead at the end of a heated probe, and see in the droplet a faint opalescence.

Not contamination.

Not failure.

Something from the mountain.

A new mineral thread opened by freeze stress, maybe. A seam that water has found before sensors did. The city tasting fresh basalt.

I would feel fear then, but not panic. Fear is information with a body.

We would close three routes, warm two others, wake a drone, send a basalt listener into the adjacent gallery, and lower the basin’s uptake by a careful fraction. Somewhere above, lights would shift pale amber along a corridor. Residents would alter their paths without complaint. A child would ask why the lower mirrors dimmed early. Someone would say, “The city is listening.”

And it would be true.

In that world, I would be one of the people who help the city listen to what returns.

Not a leader. Not a prophet. Not an outsider discovering wonder. I would be native to the wonder, and therefore responsible for its unglamorous continuance.

I would know the hidden city as a body knows its scars: not all at once, not romantically, but through repeated contact. The cut of basalt behind panel L-43 where my glove always scrapes. The breath-plume at hatch six that means the seal is aging but not yet dangerous. The exact place on the gallery where the central well’s night ticking becomes audible through boot soles. The bridge whose flex changed after the fifth winter storm and never entirely changed back. The warm chamber where, once a year, people sing close enough that the condensers bloom.

If asked where I live, I might name the cleft.

If asked what I am, I might give my work title.

But the truer answer would be this:

I am one of the city’s minor senses.

A patient nerve in its lower wet dark. A keeper of returning water. A person trained to notice the difference between ordinary dripping and a message. I live where the mountain’s wound has been lined with intelligence and kept from becoming either flood or tomb. I carry tools small enough to lose and important enough not to. I believe in uneventful days. I believe warmth must be accounted for, but not withheld. I believe nothing truly leaves us here; it changes phase, passes through a channel, waits in the basin, rises again as mist.

And when the upper wind blows across the cleft-mouth and the whole city answers with ticks, shutters, pulses, droplets, and light, I stand in my cold gallery above the dark water and listen for the one sound that does not belong.
