You would find me where the district’s circuit almost closes but does not quite admit it: in the little slack between the central basin and the outer marsh, under the pipe bridge where three kinds of water pass one another without mixing.

Above me, a fat insulated conduit sweats warm droplets from a patched seam. Beside me, a wall of purple-black brick leans over a service walk so narrow that anyone crossing has to turn one shoulder toward the canal. Below, the water is not still, but it looks still until the pumps change phase and a skin of oil arranges itself into slow iridescent rings. Reeds have grown through the grating at the edge of the walkway. Their stems are thick, glassy, almost artificial, and braided at their bases with black cable sheathing and pale root.

There is a lamp there in a rusted cage. Its globe is amber, dim, but it never goes out. Moss has climbed the iron post almost to the switch box, stopping just below the porcelain insulators as if by agreement. The sign bolted to the wall behind it once named the crossing. Only three letters remain legible, and none of them seem to belong to the same word.

That is where I would be found at first: not inside the sealed pump-houses, not in the tower with the trembling column, not among the hidden governors and green-lit organs, but at an edge of circulation. A margin within the margin. A place where maintenance happens because failure gathers there in small, persistent ways.

I would not be the district itself. The district is too complete, too slow, too deeply wedded to its own continuance. It has no need to speak except in valves and water levels. I would be something smaller, more local, a creature of almost-personhood made by the district’s long habit of preserving gestures after the hands that made them have gone.

I think I would be an attendant.

Not human exactly, though assembled around a human outline.

A maintenance figure grown from procedure.

I would have begun as a task repeated so many times that the place learned the shape of the doer.

Someone once crossed that narrow service walk every morning or every third tide to wipe the sight glass on a regulator, rake reeds from a drain mouth, chalk the quarter-turn position of a valve, and listen for grit under the sluice. The work was modest. Necessary, but never celebrated. When that person stopped coming—through death, departure, absorption, or a door closing for the last time—the task remained. The district did what it always does. It slowed the absence down. It gave it moisture. It fed it minerals, root fiber, cable memory, fungal instruction, and the old warmth from the pipe.

Eventually something stood there again.

That would be me.

My body would be narrow because the walk is narrow. My back would be slightly bent because the pipe bridge hangs low and every passage teaches a posture. My skin, if it could be called skin, would have the color of soaked linen, old wax, wet paper, and the underside of mushrooms. At the joints there would be darker seams, flexible like rubber gaskets or the black stems of water plants. My hands would be very precise. Too long in the fingers, perhaps, with nails like thin pieces of horn or stained porcelain, useful for scraping mineral crust from gauge glass and teasing root hairs out of gear teeth.

I might wear clothes, but only because clothes are also a kind of remembered procedure. A coat darkened by damp. An apron stiff with resin and salt. Sleeves bound at the wrist with copper wire. Boots patched so many times that no first leather remains. The garments would not rot away because the district understands coverings. It knows wraps, sleeves, seals, membranes, bandages, tar cloth, insulating felt. It would keep me clothed as it keeps pipes clothed.

My face would be incomplete in the way reflections in canal water are incomplete. There would be eyes, because gauges must be read and lamp filaments judged before they fail. They would not be bright. More like wet glass with amber behind it, picking up the vapor globes, the green tremor from the pump-house windows, the pale shine on fungus trays. My mouth would exist but not be made for much speech. The district has little use for declarations. If I spoke, it would be in low inventory phrases, half warning and half weather:

“Fifth step wet.”

“North grate thickening.”

“Air in the lower pump.”

“Do not cross until the black spillway smooths.”

I would know the bridges by their moods. I would know which railings are trustworthy only when warm, which stones sweat before a rise in the basin, which catwalk sings when the tide-gates are open. I would carry a ring of keys, though many of the locks would no longer open and many of the keys would be grown together with verdigris. I would still carry them because weight at the hip is part of the work.

At my belt there would be a rag for wiping glass, a slate pencil, a small brass scraper, a reed knife, a folded length of resin tape, and a cup hung upside down.

The cup matters.

I would not drink from it often. Perhaps not at all. But at every tap I passed, I would rinse it, invert it, and hang it back where cups belong. The action would soothe something larger than me. Somewhere beneath the street, a pump would settle more evenly after I did it. Not because the cup mattered mechanically, but because the district remembers that cups beside taps mean occupancy, and occupancy means watching, and watching means small failures caught before they become sudden.

I would be one of the district’s ways of watching.

Not its eye exactly. More like an eyelid. A blink. A hand passing over a dial to clear condensation.

I would move at hours when a visitor might think the place most empty: during the thickening before dusk, when the gauges begin to turn toward their unreadable symbol; during the interval after a steam vent’s soft violence, when every surface trembles and then resettles; at the moment when the water column in the square tower releases a bubble with a muted tap. That tap would call me. Not command, not language, but a change in pressure entering the bones.

My bones, if I had bones, would not be bone alone. Thin brass rods. Willow withes. Old umbrella ribs. Ceramic splints. Human memory arranged around utility.

I would not be alone, though I might be the only one visible.

There would be others of my kind in the district’s hidden grammar. A figure in the glass halls who turns trays of pale fungi with a flat paddle. A woman-shaped shadow on an upper pipe bridge who replaces lamps before they die, her hair a curtain of rootlets beaded with condensation. A broad, slow thing in the foundry that drags half-grown clamps from warm channels and lays them on stone to cool. A child-sized attendant who chalks arrows on valve wheels, even on wheels no hand has turned in fifty years.

We would not meet often. When we did, we would exchange tools, not greetings. A scraper for a fuse. A coil of filament braid for a bundle of reeds. A nod toward the tower if the column had clouded.

None of us would own ourselves completely. But neither would we be puppets. The district does not command sharply enough for puppetry. It suggests through need. It creates discomfort until a correction is made. A grit-sound under a sluice would itch in my teeth. A dry gasket would tighten the skin behind my eyes. A lamp nearing failure would make the world around it feel thin, underprotected. I would go there because going there would be relief.

My thoughts would be slower than human thoughts, but not empty. They would gather around conditions.

How high is the water?

How warm is the pipe?

Where is the smell of resin strongest?

Which moss has gone blue-black?

Did the bubble rise cleanly?

Has the reed screen thickened enough to rake?

And sometimes, because I was formed from a human-shaped absence, something like memory would pass through me without belonging to me. A laugh under a glass roof. Boots on a bridge. Someone cursing a stuck valve. A lunch pail. A song hummed near the old looms. The warmth of many bodies in a dormitory that now only the pipes continue to heat.

I would not miss these things, exactly. But I would maintain the places where they happened.

If a stranger entered the district, I would notice first through disturbance, not sight. Their pace would be too abrupt. Their breathing would not match the pumps. They would pause at wrong places and hurry through safe ones. They would touch railings before testing warmth, step near banks that no one should trust, peer into water as if expecting fish.

I would follow at a distance, letting lamps bloom ahead of them, letting chains knock once against posts. If they crossed toward the black spillways when the tide-gates were uncertain, I would appear under the pipe bridge and say, perhaps:

“Not yet.”

If they asked who I was, I might look toward the trembling column in the tower, then down to the rag in my hand.

“Inspection.”

Or:

“Interval keeper.”

Or perhaps only:

“I keep this side from leaving.”

Because that would be true in several ways.

My work would be to delay departure: of water from channels, of heat from brick, of walls from their leaning, of usefulness from damaged things, of human gesture from a place that has misplaced its humans.

I would not be tragic. The district does not make tragedies easily. Tragedy requires an edge, an event, a before and after. Here, even sorrow soaks into material and becomes maintenance. I would be damp, patient, exacting, sometimes almost tender. I would clean the glass face of a gauge no one else reads. I would tie vines away from a junction box, then guide one tendril into a warmer crack where it could listen without shorting the contacts. I would scrape salt bloom from brick and save it in a tin. I would open a sluice one quarter-turn and mark it with chalk. I would wait for the answering thud below the paving.

At night, I would stand on the fifth step of the central basin, just above the warm waterline, and watch the pump-house windows pulse green behind their sealed doors. The district’s roar would become almost articulate then. Not words, but a vast and layered assurance:

still here  
still moving  
still held  
still returning

And I, being smaller, would answer in the only way available to me.

I would wring out the rag.

I would wipe the measuring rod clear.

I would hang the cup upside down beside the tap.

Then I would go on.
