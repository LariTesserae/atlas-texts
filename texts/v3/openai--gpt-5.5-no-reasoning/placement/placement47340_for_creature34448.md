I would find myself where the chamber almost forgets to be accessible.

Not at the center, not clinging reverently to the machine-heart, not among the crabs on the bright basin lips, not in the open runnels where the blind minnows flash like thoughts through violet water. I would be in the narrow interval between those places: a seam behind a falling sheet, a service hollow made too shallow for anything large, just above the tide line and just below the warmest mist.

A place a human eye would pass over as shadow.

There, behind the water curtain, the wall is not wall exactly. It is a layered thickness of old ceramic, soft mineral skin, capillary pipes, root-filaments, and damp mesh. The space smells of warmed metal and bruised moss. Condensation gathers overhead, grows heavy, and falls in single deliberate drops. Each drop lands on the same dark pad, not stone but a listening membrane polished by generations of impacts.

I would live with my belly against that membrane.

At first I might seem like one more maintenance animal: a pale, flattened thing the length of a hand, low to the surface, with many fine legs tucked beneath a translucent carapace. My back would carry the same wet shine as the surrounding ceramic, not for camouflage alone but because a dry back would mean illness, interruption, error. In the blue-green seep-light my body would almost vanish except for the slow movement of organs within me: dim threads, amber nodules, a soft pulse near the head.

But I would not be a crab.

Crabs are public. They scrape and sort in the open, busy as punctuation. I would be more recessed, more hesitant, made for listening rather than cleaning.

My body would be a kind of living gasket, a hinge-creature, an interpreter of pressure. Broad and soft at the underside, armored only along the spine, with limbs that could brace in six directions at once when the rains come. My mouth would be useless for biting large things. It would be a fine comb, made for drawing films from membranes: mineral bloom, bacterial lace, nutrient residue, messages dried to almost nothing. Along my flanks I would carry rows of pores filled with cilia, each one sensitive to tremor. Water striking a plate ten paces away would arrive in me as shape. A pump changing rhythm below the floor would travel through my belly as weather.

I would know the room by vibration before I ever knew it by sight.

Not intelligence as a person keeps it. Not names, not maps, not memory in a single inward voice. My knowing would be distributed through reflex, hunger, scar, and route. I would know that the third rain after the column darkens comes angled from the east ceiling-spines. I would know that when the golden fluid is about to be released, the minnows collect beneath the ribbed overflow channel and the wall-mouth near the black rail opens too early. I would know which handholds taste of human salt and which have only touched maintenance fauna. I would know, from a change in the membrane under my belly, when the cliff outside has warmed after rain.

I would be a threshold-dweller.

My kind would occupy the places where one system passes into another: water to air, machine to moss, current to signal, death to feedstock. We would not govern anything. We would not build. We would not hunt unless something helpless adhered to our mouths. But without us, certain messages would arrive too late.

The chamber’s column breathes in great slow pulses, and most creatures answer it directly. They swarm when it feeds them. They retreat when it darkens. They mate, hide, graze, open, seal.

My kind listens to the smaller changes before those visible events: the faint tightening of a membrane, the souring of a drip, the extra half-beat in a hidden pump, the change in mineral taste when a pipe upstream has begun to heal itself. We graze the signal films and become signal ourselves. Something in our bodies alters with what we eat: scent, electrical charge, skin conductivity, perhaps all of these. When we crawl across a root-filament, the plant knows what we have crossed. When a moth lands on our back and drinks from the wet ridge there, it carries a trace of that knowledge upward. When a predator tongues one of us from a seam, the predator learns too, though only as appetite redirected.

So I would be small, but not incidental.

I would be one of the room’s damp syllables.

Most of my life would pass in partial enclosure. My hollow would open through three slits. One looks onto a runnel where blind minnows pass in quick silver commas. One opens behind the waterfall, where the larger plated animal moves sometimes, its wake pressing cold against my pores. One leads into a dry-looking maintenance vein that is never truly dry, only too narrow for falling water to enter. In that vein are eggs, shed skins, sleeping larvae, and the dust-small reclaimers that gather like invisible heat around anything broken.

I would leave my hollow according to pressure, not courage.

Before the nine-minute rain, the walls would tense. The air would thicken. The moths would lift from the blue seams and reattach themselves under ledges. Filterers would close into gray knots. Crabs would turn sideways into their drainage slots. I would flatten myself until I was almost part of the wall, limbs spread, pores sealed halfway. Then the rain would strike.

For a creature built low to the world, rain is not falling weather. It is impact, command, catastrophe, feast. Sheets of warm droplets would explode against the ledges and break into spray fine enough to enter every opening. The membrane beneath me would drum. My body would fill with the signatures of every surface being struck at once. For those moments I would know the chamber most completely: all basins speaking, all ribs resonant, all gates opening, all frightened feet gripping, all loosened films dissolving into flow.

Then the rain would stop.

The dripping interval would begin.

That is when I would move.

Slowly at first, unfolding from the seam, my many legs tasting for electrical gradients and fresh mineral. I would cross the slick underside of the ledge upside down, body held close, each foot making and releasing a small seal. My back would brush the hanging roots of a bead-leaved plant. The plant would close two leaves and open one. Not greeting. Not language. Contact.

Below, a moth would fail. It would strike the water with a soft wet tick and tremble there, wings spreading darkly. Minnows would find it first. Crabs would come next, glass claws busy. A wall-slit might open if the movement continued. I would not descend. That feast belongs to faster mouths.

Instead I would go to the black pad where the drops always fall.

There, the falling water leaves a skin no thicker than breath, rich with what the ceiling has collected: vapor dust, pollen, machine exhalation, traces from the column’s gills, the faint metallic sweetness of self-healing alloy. I would lower my comb-mouth and feed. Each pass would stripe the film from the membrane. Each swallowed trace would alter the chemistry in the sacs along my sides.

After feeding, I would climb.

Not far. There is no need for ambition in such a place. A body rises because the gradient calls it upward. I would move along a warm conduit, past a cluster of translucent eggs sorted into two colors by a filtration field, past a moss seam that tastes of copper and old light, past a sleeping filterer sealed tight as a scar. Moths would hum around me. Their wingbeats would blur against my vibration-sense until they became a soft fur in the air.

Near the central column, I would stop.

I would not climb onto it often. The column is crowded, contested, too full of mouths. Grazers there are bold. Cleaners are territorial. Predators wait in the folds where its membranes meet the plates. But sometimes, when the old honey-light dims and the outer sacs relax, my route would take me across one of its lower shelves.

To a human it would look as if a small pale thing had crossed a wet machine.

To the chamber it would be a reading.

My belly would press against the breathing surface. The column’s pulse would enter me too strongly, almost pain. Its fluids would move in layered tempos: water, nutrient, coolant, something like blood, something like archived weather. I would taste the beginning of golden exudate long before the release. My flank pores would open. The sacs along my sides would darken from clear to smoky amber.

Then I would retreat before the feeding frenzy.

Back through the marginal paths, across the wall, under the ledge, behind the falling sheet. As I pass, things would take what they need from me. A reed’s root-filament would touch my wet underside. A moth would land and tremble. A crab would raise its claws, not threatening, simply sampling the water I shed. Even the handhold sleeping in the wall might flex as I crossed it, tasting pressure through my feet.

By the time the column releases its golden thread and the chamber erupts, the message would already be everywhere.

Not because I chose to deliver it.

Because I moved while full of it.

That would be my work: to be permeable in the correct ways.

I would have predators. Of course. Nothing in that chamber earns exemption. The ribbon tongues in the wall slits take my young when they wander too near open basin light. The plated animal behind the waterfall sucks whole seams clean during its slow feeding rounds. Certain moth larvae parasitize our side sacs, riding there until their bodies are ready for air. Even the reclaimers enter us early if a wound stays open.

But predation would not feel like injustice. It would be one of the pressures by which the room thinks.

My dead would vanish quickly. A flattened body found under a step after rain; a translucent carapace emptied by minnows; a torn limb caught in the grate. The dust-small machines would gather. The moss would brighten. A plant would open. A crab would carry away the mineral-rich hinge of a leg. Whatever I had known in my tissues would not become a story, but it would not be lost.

It would become available.

Perhaps that is why I would belong there: because the place has no use for separation. Animal, instrument, citizen, organ, weather—those are human categories, dry categories. In the chamber, usefulness and life have soaked through each other until no border holds. To be alive there is to participate in circulation. To perceive is to feed. To feed is to report. To die is to be edited back into the grammar of the room.

So I would be a small seam-listener behind the warm falling water.

A low, pale, many-legged pressure grazer.

A creature made to read films, taste tremors, and carry the chamber’s almost-thoughts from one wet surface to another.

I would not understand the mountain. I would not know the outside cliff except as changes in pressure, strange salts on rare visitors’ hands, and the high warm smell that descends through the shaft before rain. I would never see the sky. I would have no longing for it. The room-sized valley would be large enough: its impossible channels, its moonless tides, its scheduled storms, its predators in punctuation slits, its blind swimmers and humming moths, its breathing column older than instinct.

I would live where the hidden world listens to itself.

And if a traveler pressed through the seam in the cliff and stood blinking in the wet amber dark, I would feel them first as a wrong thunder through the walkway pads.

I would flatten behind my curtain of water.

My flank sacs would cloud with the salt of alarm.

The chamber would notice through me.
