You would find me just inside the hollow, not at its deepest point but past the first failure of daylight, where the entrance has already become more memory than opening.

I would not be standing.

Standing belongs to the moor above: to wind, horizon, animals lifting their heads, people looking for paths. In this place I would be folded low, one knee in the cold mud, one hand spread against the wall of peat and roots. I would be where the branch platform begins to dip toward the wettest floor, close enough to the cup-marked stone to keep silt from settling too completely in its hollows, close enough to the reed bundle to feel its low note pass through my ribs when thaw-water finds it.

I would be the thing that has learned the room by pressure.

Not a guardian in the bright, declarative sense. Not a spirit set over the hollow with a task given by some older power. More like the hollow’s habit of care made bodily enough to touch.

If I had a shape, it would be small at first glance. Mud-dark. Narrow-backed. Root-haired. Knees and palms hardened by centuries of crouching. My skin would not know where to stop being clay. Moss would grow along the places that remained still longest: shoulder, spine, the backs of hands. Water would have polished my elbows and brow. Liverworts would come up in the prints I left, not because I made them grow, but because I never pressed harder than I needed.

My eyes would be difficult to find. You might mistake them for two wet stones in the dark until a drip struck near my face and gave them, briefly, a curve.

But I would not be only that body.

I would also be the careful persistence of the place: the cleared cup-mark, the white pebble kept from sinking, the three entrance stones leaning inward but not yet fallen, the old platform repaired one winter at a time. I would be the pause before a foot is set down. The knowledge that a root is holding more than itself. The refusal to speak where speech cannot survive.

I would be part made by water and part made by hands.

The people who once came there would not have named me into being. They would have made me by returning. By kneeling in the same places. By touching the wall to steady themselves. By bringing grief where it could not become argument. By teaching children to wait with a tap on wood instead of a word. By leaving small deliberate things in a place that softens everything except meaning.

Those gestures would have soaked down through peat and time. I would have gathered around them slowly, like moss thickening on a ledge.

So I would be human only in the old, practical ways: hands that mend, caution around fragile things, tenderness toward tools, memory for where someone once placed their weight. I would know the use of willow withies and bark cord, bone pegs and reed bundles. I would understand that a platform need not last forever; it only needs to hold through another thaw.

But I would not be human in my patience.

I could wait through generations without calling it waiting. I would not mind if moss covered a mark. I would not rescue wood from becoming peat. I would not separate ruin from continuation as sharply as people do. I would let things alter, provided they altered without violence.

My work would be maintenance.

When clay slumped into a seep-throat, I would open it with two fingers.

When a pool clouded, I would leave it alone until it remembered itself clear.

When a moss bank loosened, I would lift it higher and press it into a ledge.

When the white pebble shifted toward the mud, I would return it to a cup-mark, though perhaps not the same one.

When the reed bundle broke, I would not replace it perfectly. I would set the broken length where droplets could still find its hollow and make a note from it.

I would distrust fire. A torch would make me less visible, not more. I would draw back into the wet wall, into root-shadow and clay fold, until the dry hunger failed. But I would not hate the one who brought it. Hate is too hot a thing for that room. I would only know the flame as danger, as speed, as an appetite that does not understand slow places.

I would know the water as kin.

Not as music, exactly. Not as noise. As condition.

The ticking in the cups would tell me what the upper seep was doing. The gulp beneath the platform would tell me how high the lower bowl had filled. The left-wall hiss would mean long rain since dawn. The hollow knock under clay would warn me of a place loosening. In frost, the sound would thin and tuck itself deep below the frozen lid. In thaw, the whole chamber would open its dark lungs, and I would wake larger.

That would be my fullest season.

During thaw I would unfold as far as the hollow allowed. My back would rise under the root-ceiling. My hands would spread wide on the branch platform. The room would boom with water too small to be so immense, and I would feel every channel as if it were a vein opening after sleep.

If someone entered then, patient and afraid in the right measure, I would let them kneel.

That would be the most I could offer.

No prophecy. No bargain. No hidden crown under the peat. No answer spoken through water.

Only this: a place on the platform that would hold their weight, if they moved slowly. A cup-mark cleared enough to receive a pebble. A root within reach of their hand. One answering tap after a long silence, though silence would never truly exist there.

If they came carrying grief, I would not take it from them. I would give it conditions under which it did not need to be explained.

If they came careless, laughing because the hollow swallowed their voice, I would become wall, branch, black peat, nothing worth noticing. Later, after their boots crushed what took decades to rise, I would tend the damage without anger. Anger would imply surprise. The hollow knows pressure. It knows consequences. It knows that a careless foot can become part of the place too, though not kindly.

So I would be found there as the keeper of unsaid things.

The crouched one beneath the moor.

The root-backed listener.

The patient made from seepage.

Not haunting the hollow, but occupying it in the way damp occupies wool, in the way water occupies a stone cup, in the way a remembered gesture occupies the hand that repeats it.

If you pushed aside the wet stems and lowered yourself into the dark, you might not see me. Most would not. You would hear only impossible loudness in a room too small for it. You would feel cold water soak your knees. You would lose your voice. You would reach out for balance and touch peat, root, clay, stone.

But if you stayed—if you let the place reduce you to breath and posture—you might feel, under your palm, another pressure answer.

Not gripping.

Not warning.

Only present.

Palm down. Fingers spread. Pressed gently to wet earth.
