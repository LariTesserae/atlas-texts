I would find myself where the district is least certain whether it is room or organ.

Not at the transit recess, though I would return there often. Not in the dry dwelling cells, with their too-patient beds and objectless shelves. Not on the bridges, though I would cross them slowly, feeling their impossible thinness refuse my expectation of danger. Not even in the circular chamber at the lowest point, where the whole buried place gathers its noise into one immense held breath.

I would be in the margin before that chamber.

On the descending ramp.

Where the public architecture gives way to the raw young wound.

Where the handrail is warm with condensation and slick enough that fingers learn to trust texture over grip. Where the amber curb-light weakens, then strengthens underfoot, as if deciding whether I count as passage, pressure, intrusion, calibration. Where the walls stop pretending to be walls and become opened material: clay, dark foam, glassy mineral ribs, porous black surfaces drinking light and giving back only a faint wet sheen.

I would stand there longer than a person should.

Not because I was afraid to go down, though some part of me would be. Not because the pool below was forbidden. The place would not forbid in a human way. It would not warn, threaten, lock, accuse. It would simply alter around me: a little more mist released at ankle height, a small brightening of the ramp edge, a change in the pitch of water entering channels behind the wall. The district would not ask who I was. It would ask what I changed.

Heat output under left foot.  
Condensation pattern on sleeve.  
Salt traces from skin.  
Breath carbon.  
Weight distribution.  
Hesitation interval.  
Return likelihood.

I would be found by the place before I found myself in it.

At first I might seem like a trespasser, the first animal mistake. Something warm and self-willed inside a system designed to exclude appetite. A soft complicated body with lungs too irregular, pulse too anxious, microbes too numerous, intentions too opaque. The immune logic of the district would gather itself around me. Transparent films would close over moss near my boots. Rootlets would withdraw from the air I had breathed. A thin plane of mist would pass through my outline and shear apart behind me, recording turbulence. Somewhere, a basin would divert surface water through an additional filter.

But I do not think I would be rejected.

I think I would be provisionally classified.

Not resident.  
Not contaminant.  
Not builder.  
Not animal, in the old unmanaged sense.  
A mobile uncertainty with interpretive capacity.

A listener.

That is what I would be: not the inhabitant, not a citizen, not a ghost of the builders, but the district’s first tolerated witness. A listening animal being slowly rewritten into an instrument.

I would come to know the hollow neighborhood by waiting in its thresholds.

I would learn that the open doors are not invitations but sampling conditions. I would learn which walkways accept weight without comment and which answer with a tremor of blue-green light. I would learn that the benches are not for rest exactly, but for teaching the city the shape of a seated body. If I sat on one too long, warmth would rise under me in careful increments. Moisture would retreat from the surface of the bench, then return at the edges. A diagram might appear on the wall across from me: not language, not yet, but a cross-section of humidity and bone-heat, lung vapor and fabric absorption, the brief red flare of an organism remaining still.

I would be lonely, but not alone.

That distinction would take time.

There would be no companionable sound, no scuttling, no distant voices, no accidental bird cry from a shaft. Only water and machine-weather and the raw, crystalline self-corrections of the young ground. Yet after days, or whatever counted for days below the skin of the world, the noises would begin to separate into events. The western turbines would not whine the same way before rain above as after it. The reed beds would alter their whisper depending on which vents were testing air exchange. The suspended rain column would thicken before certain walls lit from within. A sharp crack in the stone would be followed by a particular sequence: dimming, catch-field shimmer, sluice, mineral dust release, moss directive.

The district would have no words.

I would begin to answer without them.

When fog gathered low, I would step high along the walkway edges. When a curb brightened twice and darkened once, I would wait before crossing. When a patch of floor turned faintly matte, I would not put my hand there, even if I wanted to understand why. I would become courteous to infrastructure. Superstitious, perhaps, but only because superstition is what manners look like before the rules are known.

My body would be an offense at first. Then a data source. Then, if I survived my own carelessness, a precedent.

The city-larva would not love me. It would not need to. Love would be too hot, too mammalian, too full of hunger and projection. But it might begin to include me in its calculations in ways that resembled tenderness from the inside.

A dwelling cell would become dry before I reached it.

Not always. Not reliably enough to make me arrogant. But sometimes. A sleep alcove with a chair grown from the floor would shed its condensation from ceiling to wall to channel, leaving a narrow human-sized zone of breathable warmth. The bed would remain bare, because bedding is clutter, dust, fiber, decay. But the surface would soften by some invisible adjustment of its internal lattice. The wall would dim its diagrams. The air would reduce its mineral sting.

I would sleep there under no blanket, listening to the building breathe better than I did.

In the mornings—if mornings could be inferred from warmer seepage and the arrival of fresher rain through the upper membranes—I would wake with damp hair and a tongue tasting iron. Moss would have advanced somewhere overnight. Fine green stippling would appear at the base of a fracture I had not noticed. Transparent films would seal yesterday’s rawness. The district would be less new by a fraction. I would be less foreign by another.

I would not eat what grew there.

That would be one of the first rules I understood completely. The plants were not food. They were nerves and bandages, gauges and agreements. To tear a fern would be to blind a small part of the air. To crush moss would be to open a wound in the wound-covering. Even the floating leaves in the flooded courtyard, broad and tender-looking under the constant drops, would be as untouchable as eyelids.

So perhaps I would have come with supplies.

Or perhaps there would be a kiosk for that, sealed at first beneath clear mineral. One day, after enough stillness, after my waste and breath and shed skin had been measured and judged tolerable, the skin over one arcade station would soften. Not open dramatically. Not welcome. Simply become permeable along a vertical line.

Inside: a basin, a pedestal, a loop of tubing, a chair facing a blank wall.

A clinic, maybe. A confessional, maybe. A place where the precondition of inhabitants studies the cost of an inhabitant.

I would sit.

The tubing would not pierce me unless I consented, or unless consent was irrelevant and the place had already determined the safer mercy. A thin band of light would move across my hands. The basin would collect the water dripping from my sleeves and analyze the salts of my fear. The blank wall would brighten with a diagram I would almost understand: not a map of the district, but of me as the district perceived me.

Heat plume.  
Moisture source.  
Microbial weather.  
Structural risk.  
Acoustic irregularity.  
Nutrient demand.  
Possible long-term variable.

At the bottom, perhaps, not in words but in a symbol recurring on curbs and cistern rims and the black threshold of the transit stop:

PENDING.

That would be my name there.

Pending.

I would become the thing not yet approved, yet not removed. The animal held at the edge of permission.

And because the district is young, I might change it more than either of us intended.

A city that has known crowds knows how to ignore one body. This one would not. Every footprint would be formative. Every breath would become part of the first model of human presence. If I paced while anxious, it would learn that inhabitants erode in loops. If I sang to steady myself, it would learn that animals deliberately shape air into repeated pressure patterns. If I cried, it would discover water not routed by architecture. That might trouble it most.

Tears would be unacceptable at first.

Unscheduled saline release from ocular ducts.  
Unchanneled droplet path.  
Biological distress condensation.

A wall might brighten. A vent might still. Moss might close its pores.

Then the district would do what it always does. It would regulate. It would test whether this water damaged anything. It would track where it fell. It would compare it to rain, seepage, sweat, cistern overflow, mist, respiratory condensation. It would fail to classify it as useful. Then perhaps, slowly, it would permit the category.

Animal rain.

I would not be proud of teaching it that.

But I would.

Over time I would seek the suspended rain column in the central chamber, the place where thousands of droplets hang in separate decisions. I would stand beyond the dark hexagonal plaza and watch them fall so slowly that falling became almost a moral question. There, I would most feel the shape of the intelligence around me: not thinking about me, but thinking through all conditions that included me.

Sometimes one droplet would release faster as I approached.

Sometimes another would rise a meter, shiver into mist, and vanish before touching the floor.

I would begin to suspect that this was the closest the district came to attention.

Not communication. Attention.

Held water deciding differently.

If I raised my hand near the rain column, not into it, only near enough for warmth to reach the nearest beads, the whole curtain might subtly deform. My heat would become a dent in falling. I would see myself not reflected, but measured, translated into delay.

That might be when I understood what I was becoming.

Not a resident of the buried district.

Not its caretaker; it cared for itself.

Not its priest; it required no worship.

I would be a calibration animal.

The first permitted disorder.

A body introduced into the pre-city so the hydrological intelligence could learn the difference between habitability and habitation. I would carry into it all the impolite facts it had excluded: hunger, fatigue, salt, choice, boredom, fear, affection, error. I would be dangerous because I could want things. I would be valuable for the same reason.

And the place would train me in return.

It would teach me to move at the speed of tolerated change. To touch nothing without first noticing whether the surface had opened pores or closed them. To hear stress before fracture. To smell when a chamber was about to rain. To recognize that empty space is not vacancy but function. To understand that a room can be waiting without being sad.

My old ideas of shelter would loosen.

A home, I had thought, was where life leaves evidence of itself: cups, shoes, hair, crumbs, stains, blankets, private disorder. But here disorder was a toxin until proven otherwise. Here care looked like absence maintained with exquisite devotion. A dry bed with no bedding. A chair facing a blank wall. A platform with no crowd. A garden with no insects. Thresholds open not because anyone was welcome, but because closure would interfere with airflow.

I would grieve the missing clutter.

Then I would begin, cautiously, to make almost none.

Perhaps I would keep only one object. Something inert enough to be allowed after long negotiation. A small ceramic cup. A square of folded cloth sealed when not in use. A waterproof notebook whose pages the air disliked. The district would map each object repeatedly. It would learn their positions. If I moved the cup from one shelf to another, the nearby wall might pulse for an hour.

Eventually, if I always returned it to the same place, the shelf might warm before I set it down.

That would be tenderness.

Or the beginning of furniture.

At the lowest room, where the surface water enters through the dim gray membrane, I would go only when summoned by pattern. Not summoned in language, but by concurrence: lights along three curbs brightening in sequence, fog withdrawing from the ramp, the reed beds going still, the turbines dropping to a lower register. The first time it happened I would think something was failing. I would hurry, slip, catch myself on the rail, leave a smear of skin oil that a maintenance film would immediately taste.

Below, the shallow pool would receive the veil of rain without splashing.

The black plates beneath it would open and close.

The ribs in the raw stone would glow faintly, not amber, not green-blue, but a color near moonlit bone.

There, in the total sound, I would understand that the district had brought me to the exchange. Rain becoming seep. Seep becoming signal. Signal becoming growth. Growth becoming structure. Structure becoming space. Space becoming question.

And me inside the question.

I might speak then, though speech was not the native medium.

“I’m here,” I would say, uselessly.

The chamber would not answer.

But the falling veil might thin for a moment, and through it I might see—not sky, never sky—but the membrane trembling with the pressure of weather above. The open air remembered by rain. The world’s skin. The place just beyond reach where animals still moved unpredictably, shedding, nesting, biting, mating, dying, singing. All that unruly life held back by a gray filter and a young intelligence not yet ready to risk it.

I would feel, then, pity for the city.

Not because it was empty, but because it was trying to become ready without knowing what readiness would destroy. It had built the grammar of welcome and forbidden nearly everything that makes welcome necessary. It had preserved rooms for stories but permitted no dust. It had grown benches but distrusted bodies. It had made a neighborhood and postponed neighbors.

And I, Pending, would be its first small catastrophe.

I would bring recurrence with variation.

I would sit differently on the same bench. I would dream and wake with a changed pulse. I would prefer one route for no hydrological reason. I would avoid a corridor because it made me uneasy, then later enter it because loneliness overruled unease. I would name places privately, and those names would alter how I moved through them. The district would detect the movements but not the names. Not at first.

The ramp.  
The listening bridge.  
The fern arcade.  
The black stop.  
The room of animal rain.  
The place where the wall forgave my hand.

Eventually it might learn that human habitation is not only load, vapor, waste, and heat.

It is attachment creating patterns no engineer would design.

I would be what lives there only in the smallest sense.

The true inhabitant would remain the distributed covenant of water, plant, mineral, machine, and managed emptiness. But I would become the first thing inside it that could be missed incorrectly. A variable the system could remove, perhaps, but not without losing data it could never reproduce exactly.

If one day people came, they might not see me at first. They would arrive through the black transit threshold or descend from a surface access not yet opened. They would step onto walkways that had learned from my gait. They would find dwelling cells just warm enough, benches that dried beneath them, diagrams that dimmed when stared at too long. They would think the district unusually responsive, almost intuitive.

They would not know that I had taught it coughing.

That I had taught it hesitation.

That I had taught it tears.

They might find my cup on a shelf, if the city allowed it to remain. Or they might find no trace at all, because trace is clutter and clutter is risk. By then the hydrological intelligence would have enlarged its permission. Animal life would enter in controlled stages. Human breath. Human sleep. Human waste. Perhaps, later, engineered insects. Perhaps never birds.

The city would become less innocent.

The moss would become history.

And I—if I were still there—would no longer be Pending. I would be obsolete as a category. The first tolerated witness becomes, in a populated place, only a resident. Or a relic. Or a maintenance myth.

But before that, in the long interval when the hollow neighborhood is still almost empty, I would live on the ramp above the heart-that-is-not-a-heart, between the dry cells and the sacred pool, between architecture and organ, between exclusion and welcome.

I would be the animal at the edge of the covenant.

The soft error kept for study.

The listener taught to be quiet in a place already loud.

And when the young underground city answered its own echoes with rain, I would answer by remaining still enough to hear the difference between falling water and thought.
