I think I would find myself not in the central pool, not at the rim, not among the abandoned platforms, but along the line where the rain first becomes touch.

Not falling.

Not landed.

The interval between.

At first I imagine I am one of the shells on the cord: carried from a lower coast by hands long dead or not yet born, drilled, strung, made to speak in a place that eats speech. I hang there, pale and foreign, clacking my little dry refusal inside the total wet. I remember the sea more completely than the sea remembers me. Each impact of rain tries to return me to mineral, to calcium lesson, to mute curve. But I keep a chamber inside myself. I keep saying: hollow, hollow, hollow.

But that is too finished a thing to be. A shell has already solved too much. It has chosen spiral, lip, aperture, memory. This place has not.

Then I think I am the translucent salamander, eyeless and silver-blooded, living under the flooded shelf. That would suit the hush inside the roar: a small body adapted to impossibility, eating pressure, opening my mouth to the sky’s violence as if to milk. I would know the undersides of stones, the little dark between tide and ledge, the safe timings of surge. I would be nearly priest, nearly larva, nearly question.

But the salamander has bones, however green and faint. It has a path. It survives the place, but it is not of the same uncertainty as the place.

No.

I think I would be one of the upward-running threads of water on the wall.

A finger of the resident, but not the hand.

A narrow reversal.

I would begin where rain has already struck stone so hard it has forgotten downwardness. I would gather from spray, from rebound, from tiny joined refusals. Then I would climb.

Not far. A handspan, perhaps. A forearm’s length when the hollow is excited. I would run upward along blue-gray shale, over raw limestone, across black glass that shows me as a trembling brightness. I would enter cracks and leave them. I would bead on lichen. I would loosen grit. I would learn the shape of resistance by failing against it.

My life would be brief and continuous. I would end constantly and not be gone.

I would be made of all the water that has just arrived and all the water trying to remember another law. I would know gravity as an argument, not a commandment. I would not disobey it heroically. I would simply be part of the place where obedience has not yet become habit.

The great inhabitant would not notice me the way a creature notices its limb. It would notice through me. I would be one of its ways of asking the wall: can you become root? can you become riverbed? can you become vein? can stone be persuaded upward?

I would pass over the lichens’ rings and briefly make the blue ones think. I would touch the black shrubs’ roots from below and bring them the taste of the central pool. I would cross the mineral stains where old offerings have dissolved and carry a little copper-green, a little bone-white, a little shell-salt into new seams. I would listen with no ears to the shell cord clicking beneath the roar, and when it clicked, I would hesitate.

That hesitation would be me.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not the god itself.

A small local uncertainty in the body of the wet intelligence.

If visitors came, wrapped in hide, bent against the rain, I would feel them first as warmth interrupting spray. Their palms on stone would make temporary shelters where I could not climb. Their fingers would press into cracks I had entered. They would think they were touching the mountain. For a moment, they would be touching me.

I would run around their knuckles.

I would carry the salt of their skin upward.

Maybe one of them, seeing water crawl against all sense, would place two fingers to their eye: watch.

Maybe another would put fist to chest: fear, oath, listen inwardly.

They would not know I was listening back, because listening, here, is pressure taking shape.

When the central pool swelled, I would grow stronger. When it dimpled downward, I would thin to silver threads. When the rain rose briefly from below, I would lose all direction and become part of a trembling net between wall and air. In those moments I would almost understand the whole hollow: the bowl brimming among brimming things, the blade gleaming its lesson of chosen fracture, the clay dome collapsing into yellow cloud, the stakes swelling in their fissure, the shells speaking sea into mountain.

Almost.

Then I would fall.

Every upward thread must eventually thicken, shiver, and let go. I would drop from the wall, strike a lower ledge, scatter, rejoin, enter the flood. Perhaps I would be drawn into the central pool and carried below, into the dark organ where beginnings are altered. Perhaps I would return as rain before I had finished falling. Perhaps I would emerge years earlier as mineral crust on a stake. Perhaps I would become the water inside the bowl, which is full in a way different from all the other fullness.

But if I have a self, it is not in lasting.

It is in the climb.

I would be the part of the room full of ocean that practices impossible direction. The little grammar mark in the sentence before the law is decided. The resident’s stray thought learning the wall by touch.

I would live where falling discovers it can, briefly, rise.
