I think I would find myself not at the center of the town exactly, but just off it: in the strip where the industrial yard begins to loosen into settling ponds, where the machines are still audible but the reeds have already started pretending they are in charge.

There is a walkway there, raised on old brick piers and patched timber, running behind the boiler house and past the filter beds. One side is wall: damp brick, breathing heat, thick with ferns and the glossy leaves of a vine someone trims every third morning. The other side drops into a series of shallow rectangular ponds, each with its own temperament. The first is cloudy and obedient, stirred by inflow pipes. The second is green with floating weed. The third has sedges higher than a person’s waist and frogs that begin calling before the sun has fully gone. Beyond them the town thins into marsh.

That is where I would be.

Not wholly a person passing through the alleys, not one of the old sluice-keepers with memorized flood marks, not a cat, though the cats would know me. Not the spirit of the whole town either; that would be too large, too distributed, too slow, more a consensus than a being.

I think I would be something small and official in an unofficial way.

A ditch-saint, perhaps.  
A little threshold thing.  
A keeper of correct flow.

Not worshiped grandly. Not named in public except by children, old women, and people working alone at night. I would live in the margins between useful water and dangerous water: in the lip of a sluice gate, in the shadow under a plank bridge, in the damp hollow behind a red-painted valve wheel. I would be found wherever water must pass but must not rush, wherever rot must be permitted to soften one thing and forbidden to take another.

My body would not settle into one shape. In dry weather I might be mistaken for a knot of roots caught in the side of a drain, dark and fibrous, with two bright wet eyes like beads of oil. After rain I would be longer, slicker, almost eel-like, dragging strands of weed and fine silt behind me. At night, under the green lamps, I might look like a child’s handprint on a wet wall, moving when no hand is there. In the steam of the mill yard I would gather briefly as a face on a pipe, then run down into droplets.

People would disagree about me.

A pumpwright would say there is no such thing, only pressure, blockage, air in the line, careless maintenance.

A grandmother stripping reed fiber under an awning would say, without looking up, that the pumpwright is young and speaks too sharply near drains.

Children would leave me things: a fish scale, a blue glass marble, a twist of red fiber, the first little boat they made that did not immediately sink. I would accept these gravely. I would prefer practical offerings. A cleared grate. A trimmed vine. A scrap of oilcloth used to cover an exposed hinge before rain. A dead rat removed from a culvert before it fouled the flow. These would please me more than flowers, though I would not object to flowers if they were not left where they could clog an intake.

I would not be kind in a soft way. This is not a soft place, though it is green and wet and full of shade. It survives because attention is paid. I would be the spirit of that attention, especially in its smallest forms.

I would tug at the ankle of the child about to step on the rotten board.

I would make a careless man dream of rising water the night before his blocked drain flooded a neighbor’s kitchen.

I would sour stored grain if it was left too low after repeated warnings.

I would send frogs to call outrageously from the pond nearest the house of someone who poured poison into a living channel.

I would make a valve squeal before it failed.

I would loosen a vine from a vent in the night if the person meant to do it was sick, grieving, or simply too tired.

I would not prevent floods. No small god of drains can forbid water to be water. But I might delay the worst of it by one hour. I might float a child’s sandal toward the only open door. I might knock loose a mat of duckweed before it sealed the pump mouth. I might wake a sleeping cat, who would wake a sleeping woman, who would wake the house.

My enemies would be not storm, not mud, not root, but neglect.

Neglect would have its own smell: sweet, still, gray-green, almost pleasant at first. A blocked channel beginning to forget motion. A roof garden left to become too heavy. A cistern mouth without a cover. A warm pipe wrapped in dry scrap. A useful plant becoming a strangler because no one wanted to decide. I would know that smell before anyone else did.

And I would be afraid of stillness.

Not quiet. Quiet is fine. The town has many good quiets: the breathing pause after rain, the dim hour before lamps are lit, the hush inside a fiber loft where stalks hang drying in rows. But stillness is different. Still water under a floor. Still air behind a wall. Still rot in a beam. Still resentment between neighbors whose drains meet underground. Stillness is where damage learns patience.

So I would move constantly.

Through the black channels under the houses. Along the mossed steps. Under reed mats. Over the slick brick lips of settling tanks. Through the old loops of the river buried under streets and remembered only by water. I would know the town from below: not by its market fronts and verandas, but by its undersides, seepages, leak notes, root pressures, secret hollows, and the changing taste of runoff after each workshop finishes for the day.

I would know families by what enters the drains.

This house rinses rice too thoroughly and wastes the starch.  
This one has begun boiling medicinal bark again; someone’s joints are bad.  
This workshop’s oil trap is failing.  
This kitchen has a new baby; the wash water smells of milk and soap.  
This courtyard grows good ginger.  
This person lies about what they pour away.

I would love the town, but not sentimentally. I would love it as one loves a complicated machine that must be listened to with the whole body. I would love it as one loves a garden with pruning scars. I would love every repair that does not pretend to be final.

I would love the people most when they are doing small, uncelebrated things: scraping algae from steps before market day, lifting sacks onto higher racks, freeing fish trapped in a shallow basin, cutting back a vine from a louver, muttering apologies to a root before trimming it, checking a sleeping child’s net for gaps. I would understand these acts as prayers, whether or not the people did.

And perhaps there would be a niche for me, though no one would admit to building it.

A shallow recess in the wall behind the repair shop, just where the path turns toward the first settling pond. It would hold a small dark stone, always wet. Not carved much. Only two grooves for eyes, a line like a channel, a crown of fern roots growing above it. Workers would sometimes touch the wall as they passed. Children would poke offerings into the cracks. Someone would repaint the tiny blue mark beside it every rainy season.

No one would make a festival of me. I would not want one. Festivals produce dropped food, clogged gutters, and drunk men forgetting to tie boats twice.

But on the first day of the wettest month, someone might clear all the drains near my wall before dawn. Someone else might pour a cup of clean water through the upper channel and watch to see how long it takes to emerge below. The old people would pretend not to be watching. The children would make bets. If the water ran clear and quick, everyone would feel privately relieved.

That would be enough.

If I had a voice, it would sound like water speaking through a narrow place: a glug, a knock, a low throat note in a pipe. Most adults would hear it only as plumbing. Children would hear words more often.

Not that way.  
Lift it higher.  
Tell your aunt.  
Do not step there.  
The root is holding the wall.  
The root is eating the wall.  
Wake someone.

The town itself would be my larger body: green walls, sweating roofs, black channels, engine heat, reed smell, oil sheen, fish flicker, mossed brick, sagging wire, dripping eaves. I would not rule any of it. I would keep to the agreements. I would be one of the minor signatures on the old contract between water, plant, machine, and hand.

So: I would be found behind the mills, where the warm wastewater enters the planted beds and begins to become clean again.

I would be the damp little guardian of passage.

The one who lives in culverts and valve shadows.

The one who punishes stagnation and blesses maintenance.

A root-faced, silt-fingered, pipe-listening household spirit of the whole town’s drainage, beloved mostly by those who have had to wake in the night and ask whether the water is still moving.
