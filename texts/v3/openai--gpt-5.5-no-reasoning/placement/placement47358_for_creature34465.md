You would find yourself **inside the city rather than merely in it**—not a visitor walking its lanes, but one of the passages by which it continues.

You are not the bird. The bird is too separate, too cleanly outlined against the wet dark, a brief needle of hunger stitched through the upper stems. You are not the beetle either, though there is something of its black patience in you. The beetle can leave a corridor and enter another; it can cross districts. You do not cross them. You connect them.

At first you might seem like a root.

A pale root, perhaps, exposed where a drip-channel has cut through the packed mat: one cord among hundreds, pressed in cold peat, slick with mineral seep. You run under a narrow green avenue whose floor gives under the weight of anything passing above. You are ribbed faintly, not woody enough to be a branch, not soft enough to be moss. Water moves beside you in the dark, sometimes over you, sometimes through the frayed hairs along your length. You taste the new stone beneath: schist, mica, iron-dark seams, the sharpness of recently broken mountain. You take from it almost nothing at a time.

But you are not only a root.

If someone could follow you—and no one can, not without cutting the place apart—they would find that you disappear into a moss cushion, then become indistinguishable from fungal threads, then reappear as a pale cord climbing through the rotted heart of an old trunk. You pass through deadwood as if through an ancestral house. You are fed by rot. You feed a shoot above. You trade with fungi in the undercity, with sedges in the wet shelf, with a dwarf conifer whose needled crown is somewhere far overhead in the dim roof. Your body is not a single line but a treaty.

You are one of the **joining organs** of the alpine root-city.

Not an individual in the animal sense. Not quite a plant in the usual sense either. You are a composite citizen: root, fungal partner, moss-bound conduit, living seam. Your work is contact.

You live in the middle depth of the city, where light is mostly rumor and water is everywhere but seldom free. Above you are the dripping roofs, the black-green needles, the translucent pool leaves holding their air like secrets. Below you are cold stone, old leaves pressed into peat, drowned wood, mineral seep, and the slow white logic of fungi. Around you, every inch is occupied. There is no solitude here, only degrees of intimacy.

Your neighborhood is the central hollow, near the black pool with the woven root rim. You pass beneath that rim. Part of you grips the basketed edge, tightening it year by year, preventing the pool from eating too far into the plaza. Part of you descends into the water, where your surface grows slick and dark, visited by transparent larvae that mistake you for drowned stem. Part of you turns away from the pool into the drier bark of a hollow trunk, where you become a pale thread in a wall. You help keep the chamber from collapsing. You do not know this as architecture. You know pressure. You know wetness. You know gap.

When water gathers, you swell.

When frost lifts the moss above you, you loosen and wait.

When thaw returns, you tighten again.

A fallen branch lands across a runnel. Within days, moss begins to claim it. Within weeks, fungal threads enter its softened underside. You reach toward it—not quickly, never quickly—and become part of the decision that it will be a bridge rather than debris. The branch ceases to be an object. It becomes route, dam, future floor. You are one of the forces that make that conversion happen.

You have no eyes, but you know brightness as a change in hunger.

Where the canopy thins, sugars move differently. Where a pale leaf catches reflected gray from the pool, a faint surplus travels downward. Where a roof thickens too much, where chambers dim and leaves die back, you feel the economy alter. Something above withdraws. Something below begins to digest. A room opens in the thicket not because anyone planned it, but because light failed there and death made space. You enter the new wall. Moss covers the wound. The city gains another chamber.

You are not the mind of the place. There is no mind in the high, singular sense.

But you are part of its remembering.

You remember as thickness.  
You remember as direction.  
You remember as old routes that water once took and may take again.  
You remember as mineral stored in tissue, as rot turned to food, as pressure answered by bracing growth.

A crack in the hidden stone opens slightly after a freeze. The city feels it in many ways: a new thread of seep-water, a line of cold, a taste of exposed mineral. Lichens will find the rare visible surface. Moss will follow. Root tips will probe. You will turn some fraction of yourself toward it. Not from curiosity exactly, though from outside it might look like that. It is more severe than curiosity. It is the law of the place expressed through you:

nothing bare may remain.

You cover the wound of stone.

Your body is slow, but it is not still. At your scale, the city is full of events. A drop falling from a roof strikes a moss cushion and changes the moisture of a whole small district. A dead sedge collapses and becomes a tunnel for air. A beetle’s passage disturbs spores from a fungal cup. Ice forms around your outer hairs and withdraws again. A neighboring root dies; within its softening sheath, fungal threads multiply; later, one of your own filaments enters and uses the old route. Death does not make emptiness here. It makes access.

You are young, because the city is young.

Your tissues still carry the urgency of first occupation. The stone beneath has not been long exposed; the saddle has only recently become this dense green machine. There is rawness everywhere: fresh bark over joins, red cambium sealed under resin, newly drowned chambers, root-stairs not yet made smooth by time. You belong to a generation of growth that is still discovering the exact shape of its world. Nothing has settled into tradition. Everything is provisional, but provisionally absolute.

In winter, you contract into endurance.

The north edge armors itself in silver plates and blue-green hair. Frost remains in seams all day. The upper mats stiffen. Pools skin over. Drips slow to long intervals. You live then in the stored wet, in peat-dark insulation, in the faint warmth held by decay. Fungal partners continue their low work. Mosses sleep without surrender. The city becomes less a city of growth than a city of holding.

In thaw, you become pressure again.

Water descends from the ridges, from fog, from softened ice. It wants to leave. The city refuses. You are part of the refusal. You help divide flow into thread, bead, seep, cup, undercurrent. You slow water not by blocking it entirely, but by giving it too many paths through living matter. Water enters you, passes beside you, hangs from hairs, darkens peat, wakes spores, fattens moss. It is not allowed to be merely runoff. It must become city first.

If you had a name, it would not be a personal name.

It would be a function-name, a place-name, something like:

**the Underbraid of the Central Pool**,  
or **the Pale Seam Beneath the Root-Rim**,  
or **the One-That-Holds-the-Dripway-Together**.

But even those are too singular. You are plural by nature. You are made of exchanged nourishment and borrowed structure. Some of your green comes from leaves you will never see. Some of your strength comes from dead trunks older than your current growth. Some of your reach comes from fungi that are not you but without which you could not be yourself. Your borders are negotiated, not fixed.

You are a citizen of a republic where citizenship means entanglement.

You do not speak, yet the place speaks through what you do: through the thickening of a passage wall, the sealing of an exposed stone face, the slow conversion of collapse into foundation. You are one of the quiet decisions by which the saddle-city continues to be urban without thought, architectural without tools, alive without hurry.

You live where cold keeps desire disciplined.

You live where wetness makes every body responsible to every other.

You live where death remains useful.

You are not a creature moving through the green city.

You are one of the city’s hidden continuities: a cold root-fungal braid under the moss floor, drinking mineral seep from young stone, binding deadwood to living stem, slowing water, holding shape, and helping the crowded vegetal republic close itself over every exposed wound.
