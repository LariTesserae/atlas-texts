I would find myself in the basin’s innermost crowded place, where the spires stand packed together like a broken throat of stone.

Not on the open salt plates. Not high on the walls. Not in the pale chimney holes, either, though at first I might mistake myself for something that belongs there: a blind head tasting the air, a colony-fringe, a soft pale tenant withdrawing into mineral.

But I think I am lower than that.

I am in one of the old triangular openings of a black stone stack, in a shelter too deliberate to be fully natural and too worn to belong to any maker still remembered. The opening faces inward, toward the basin’s center. Its edges have been polished by centuries of bodies entering, rubbing, nesting, hiding, molting, dying. The stones above it are flat basalt plates, heat-cracked and salt-stained, leaning on one another by patience rather than mortar. Behind the triangular mouth is a little chamber just large enough for me to turn around in, if I do it slowly and fold my limbs in the correct order.

I am not the original builder. I may not be a builder at all.

I am one of the inheritors of shape.

A low, plated animal, dark as cooled slag on top and pale underneath where no light reaches. My body is oval but not smooth, made of overlapping mineral-edged shields that can rise and settle separately, letting heat in or locking it away. When I am still, dust gathers in the seams of me. Salt whitens my joints. My forelimbs are heavy and short, not claws for striking but wedges for prying flakes, dragging chips, bracing against stone. Behind them are smaller walking limbs, many enough that I do not count them as limbs but as a moving underside, a fringe of contact.

I have no useful eyes. Perhaps I have two sealed dim spots set far back beneath the front plates, old organs still able to tell furnace-glow from total dark, but I do not trust them. I know the basin through the floor.

Heat arrives through my belly as gradients and pulses. A grazer passing outside is a slow, blunt tremor with pauses. A file of runners is a stitched vibration, precise and many-footed, like dry beads poured along a groove. A wall hunter is almost nothing until grit falls from above. Gas moving in the rock has a hollow signature. A basalt-flat predator is dangerous because it does not sound enough like a body; it sounds like ground making up its mind.

I taste with the fringes around my mouth and along the first joints of my forelimbs. Salt, ash, horn-dust, hot iron, shed skin, the sour mineral trace of black film, the dry sealed smell of eggs, the faint bitterness of another of my kind. Breath matters. The basin is full of breath, but no one wastes it. Every exhalation is a mark.

What am I?

At first I might seem like a scavenger, because I eat what the basin gives back. Molted skins. Broken mouth plates. The thin black crust that grows in shaded undersides. Pale knots from crevices, if I can pry them loose. The dry residue left where something bled and the blood became paste before it could sink. Eggshell, when I can get it. The salt-cured fringes of the dead before the small jointed cleaners erase them.

But I am not only a scavenger.

I maintain openings.

Not as a conscious craft, not as a mason with a plan. I do not imagine a building before I alter one. But my kind has lived in these stacks for so long that our bodies have become tools shaped by their entrances. We widen triangular mouths by rubbing. We deepen chambers by backing in and scraping with our rear plates. We drag loose chips outward and arrange them where heat falls too sharply, where a gap admits a predator’s limb, where a cold draft from a pressure-flow makes eggs fail.

We do not build from nothing. Nothing here begins from nothing. We correct.

I live in the old stack because its stone remembers warmth properly. During the underheat, when the floor becomes painful and then lethal for smaller things, the chamber stays just below danger. During the cooling dark, when heat flees upward and the open paths stiffen with cold, the rear wall gives back what it stored. It is not comfort. There is no comfort here. It is survivable precision.

I would be found half-withdrawn in the triangular entrance, front plates lowered so that I resemble a fitted door. My outer shell matches the black stack stones closely enough that only the salt in my seams betrays me. Beneath me, tucked in the chamber’s rear dust, are eggs or young or perhaps only stored fragments of edible crust. If young, they are flat, armored specks, already hard, already silent, already learning the chamber by touch. They climb over my underside to feed on salt flakes from my joints.

I guard shade more than territory.

The patch just inside the entrance belongs to me because I occupy it, because my shell fits it, because my scent has thickened there over molt after molt. If another adult of my kind comes too close, we do not cry out. We brace. We press plates against plates. We lever for position, slowly, with terrible economy, each trying to make the other spend moisture and heat. Such contests may last a long time. The loser retreats not in panic but in arithmetic.

My enemies are not all predators. Heat is an enemy. Cold is an enemy. Open ground is an enemy. A vent pulse at the wrong hour can cook the chamber. A dustflow can seal the entrance. A colony of grain-runners can strip a clutch if they find a gap. A wall hunter’s narrow forelimb can reach farther than it should. The basalt-flat ones wait along the route to the nearest shaded film, and I must learn which slabs are too patient.

I move mostly during the half-temperatures.

When the vents slacken and the upper dark cools, I leave the triangular shelter. I do not hurry. Hurrying belongs to file-runners and the hunted. My shell edges lift slightly, releasing stored heat in cautious increments. My belly reads the path: polished stone, powder seam, old crack, grazer scrape, predator-stillness, safe roughness, unsafe smoothness. I follow routes older than my own blood. My underside has inherited them the way other creatures inherit songs.

I visit the undersides of fallen stones, levering them just enough to reach the black films beneath. Not every stone can be moved. Not every film is worth the cost. I scrape with the inner rims of my mouth, a quiet rasping that stops whenever the basin pauses. Sometimes a mineral-grazer has already passed and left only shining arcs. Sometimes I find pale knots in a protected cavity, tough and dry, and work at them for hours until they come away like plugs of hardened fat.

If I find a dead thing, I do not own it unless I can cover it.

This is an old law written by bodies, not declared. To cover is to claim. I drag chips, shed plates, dust, fragments of bone. I lower my own shell over the richest part and feed beneath myself while scavengers tick at the edges. If the dead thing is large, no one owns it. It becomes a place.

I think my kind may be one reason the old arches recur.

Not because we conceive arches. We do not look up and admire weight holding space open. But we drag what is useful toward openings. We push long bones aside until they catch between stones. We wedge plates where pressure-flows enter. We brace loose stacks with the curved ribs of the dead because the ribs happen to fit. Generation after generation, the same corrections remain. Mineral grows over them. Salt hardens the joints. Later creatures pass beneath and think, if they think, that the basin itself made a doorway.

Perhaps it did. Through us.

I am not solitary in the human sense. Nothing here is solitary; everything is surrounded, pressured, read, used. Yet I am not one of the file-minds, not a colony opening and sealing holes in waves. I know my own chamber, my clutch, the neighboring shelters by scent and vibration. I know the heat-croucher whose cooling body creates a temporary refuge near the vent-mouth. I know the grazer whose shell carries edible mites. I know the route where a slab has not moved for too long.

I know absence as danger.

If a usual scraping stops, if the runners leave a path empty, if chimney heads withdraw all at once, I freeze. My plates settle. My limbs lock. I become one more black fragment among black fragments. The basin listens through me and I through it. A hundred animals pause to read the same message.

Then, if nothing folds over us, life resumes.

My death will be useful and quick in its usefulness. My moisture will not travel far. My plates will be opened by wedges, mouthparts, patient prying. My inner softness will become heat and movement in other bodies. My shell may remain in the triangular entrance until another of my kind breaks it apart. A foreplate might be dragged into a gap to temper a draft. A limb-ring might become part of a nest wall. My salts will powder the floor. Young runners will tap over me as if over stone.

If there is a name for me, it is not a sounded one.

It would be a contact pattern: the feel of a fitted shell in a triangular mouth; the warm-dark pressure of a chamber kept open; the scrape-mark beneath a flat stone; the arrangement of chips after a dustflow; the taste of old basalt and guarded eggs.

I would be a threshold animal.

A keeper of inherited hollows.

One of the dark, plated correctors of the basin’s old structures, living between architecture and instinct, between shelter and shell. Not a person, not a beast in any simple way, but a habit the place has grown: a dry, patient, mineral-edged body that makes openings last.
