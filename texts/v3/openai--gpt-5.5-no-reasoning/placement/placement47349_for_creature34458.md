I would not be one of the foremen, or one of the level readers, though for a while I might mistake myself for that: a person with a slate under one arm, standing at the edge of the settling pond, pretending the numbers are the important part.

At first I see myself at the center, near the square that is not a square, under the low pipes where the footbridge crosses the steaming green-brown water. I have a habit of standing there because everything passes through that place eventually: carts, children, messages, arguments, lunch tins, rumors of a pressure change, a dog with one ear torn, a woman carrying a wrapped pump bearing as if it were bread. I would think I belonged there because I knew the crossings. I would know the bridge’s one loose plate, the office step that holds water after rain, the place where the dispatch roof drips down the back of your neck if you stand too close to the third post.

But that feels too visible.

Not important-visible, perhaps, but located in the official body of town. The center belongs to those who answer whistles, who carry keys, who are missed if absent. I am not sure I would be missed immediately.

I think I would be found slightly below the town’s eye level.

Not underground exactly. Not in the deep pump rooms, where the old workers listen through their palms and know the machinery by its breath. Lower than the street, but not hidden from it: in the drainage margins, in the strips beside raised walkways, in the wet seam between human routes and water’s routes.

I would be a drain-clearer’s child grown into a drain-clearer’s adult, or something close to that. Not a grand trade. Not a priest of gauges. Not a digger bringing pale clay home on his boots. I would be one of the people called when water has begun doing something small and incorrect.

A culvert mouth stopped with reed trash.

A yard channel running backward.

A sump that should smell stale but instead smells sweet.

A plank line sinking at one end.

Frogs gone quiet under the west housing block.

Algae thickening too fast in a gutter behind the canteen.

No one would call it expertise, but they would come find me.

I would have a hooked iron rod worn smooth at the handle, a coil of black cord, a small lantern with patched glass, a knife with rust freckles, and a habit of carrying two spare latches in my pocket because rats can make fools of better hardware than men can design. My clothes would be no different from anyone’s at first glance: pale shirt, dark cuffs, trousers rolled or tied above the worst water, scarf at the throat, cap pulled low. But my knees would be stained greener than most, and the left shoulder of my shirt would always show a dark rub where I carry the drain rod.

I would know the town from underneath.

Not beautifully. Not romantically. I would know it by blockage, seep, fall, and wrongness.

I would know which gutters take kitchen grease and which take clay wash. Which culverts shelter frogs and which shelter rats. Which drains speak loudly after rain and which swallow sound. I would know the little deltas of grit at outflows, their color and grain: black clinker dust from the yards, pale clay from the bank road, reddish scale from pipe runoff, gray silt from the marsh pushing back. I would read them the way others read notices nailed to posts.

I would spend much of the day crouched.

Crouched under catwalks while boots passed overhead.

Crouched beside warm channels with insects stippling the backs of my hands.

Crouched at the square pond’s outfall, tapping with the rod to hear whether the grate was merely fouled or wedged.

Crouched under raised houses, calling up through floorboards that whoever lived there should stop pouring wash-scraps down the east pipe unless they wanted rats beneath the kitchen again.

Children would know me because I would be where children are forbidden to be. They would appear silently behind me in little groups, damp-haired and bright-eyed, holding reed whistles, contraband eggshells, dead beetles, bits of wire. They would ask if there were blind fish in that drain. If a rat could swim through that pipe. If the leeches in the hot ditch got bigger than fingers. I would tell them yes, no, maybe, go home, mind the second whistle, and don’t step on that board. Then, because I was once one of them, I would show them where to put their feet anyway.

I would complain constantly.

About bad grates. About lazy repairs. About people who think water disappears because it is no longer visible. About boiled grain sticking to tins. About mosquito smoke. About the way the office men approve a new drainage lip but not the hinges needed to open the inspection hatch behind it. About children. About rats. About frogs in my sleeping mat. About my own knees. About the heat in a tone suggesting the heat had personally chosen me as an enemy.

And I would love the town in the only safe way: by mistrusting it intimately.

My house would stand toward the outer lanes, not in the center but not yet in the marsh. One of the plain raised houses with a narrow veranda and too many hooks inside. The floor would be scrubbed often and never feel clean. The underside would be shaded and busy with animal passage. There would be a water barrel under the back eave with a lid weighted by a brick. There would be a line of boots by the door, none fully dry. There would be a map of sorts on the wall, not official, not drawn to scale, made of charcoal marks and amended over years: drains, plank lines, weak culverts, rat routes, old repairs, places where water stands too warm, places where the ground sounds hollow.

People would laugh at the map until they needed it.

I might live alone, or with an elderly aunt who used to trim lamps and now knows everyone’s business by the timing of their footsteps. Or perhaps with a younger brother who works the clay bank and brings home fossils, shells, compressed black fragments, useless stones, laying them on the sill until I throw them out and he rescues them from the rubbish. There would be no delicate things in the house except one: a paper fan, perhaps, kept wrapped in cloth and taken out only on nights when the heat becomes insulting.

At night I would sit outside with the others, feet on the rail, cup in hand, listening.

Not to the pumps first. Everyone listens to the pumps. I would listen between them, to water’s smaller attempts: the tick of a drip beginning where there was none yesterday, the glug under the lane after a cart passes, the mosquito pitch thickening near a barrel someone forgot to cover, the sudden absence of frogs under the canteen steps.

I would be the sort of person who stops talking mid-sentence and says, “That’s wrong.”

No one likes a person who says that too often.

No one ignores them either.

There is another possibility.

Perhaps I am not human.

Perhaps I am one of the town’s small successful lives, one of the creatures who understands the whole arrangement without ever pretending to own it. A frog beneath the boards, my throat pulsing in the culvert dark, knowing pressure changes through my belly. Or a rat with a civic mind, traveling the pipe shadows, memorizing latches, nesting above old flood marks. Or a cat assigned by no one and obeyed by many, sleeping on warm paper in the office while clerks believe the room belongs to them.

But no—the question asks where I would find myself, who or what I would be, and I keep returning to hands.

Hands on wet brick.

Hands holding a hooked rod.

Hands feeling the tremor in a drain cover.

Hands washing and not coming clean.

So I am human, but only just above the level of the frogs.

A threshold person in a threshold town.

I would know the rules and mistrust anyone who called them quaint. I would have scars on my shins from grates, pale burn marks on one wrist from a pipe I misjudged at seventeen, mosquito scars reopened by scratching, a permanent crease between my eyes from looking down through vapor and dim copper light. I would be neither young nor properly old. Old enough that children think I have always been there; young enough that the older pump people still call me by the shortened version of my childhood name.

My work would matter most when nothing much has happened.

If a flood came, if a bank collapsed, if a boiler failed, others would take the story. Their names would attach to the event. They would shout, haul, brace, drag bodies or save them. I would be somewhere in the wet arithmetic beforehand, noticing a small reversal in a ditch, a frog absence, a new smell from clear water, a black tide mark half an inch higher than it should be on a concrete lip.

My victories would be unrecorded because they would look like ordinary days.

A yard that did not flood.

A housing row that did not sicken.

A pump that did not choke on reed trash.

A child who did not vanish through a rotten board because I had marked it with three nails before dawn.

A rat run blocked before it found the grain store.

Water held, diverted, delayed, persuaded.

Never defeated.

I would have learned that this is the only honest relationship with the place: persuasion, not mastery.

The town itself would know me as one of its minor nerves. Not brain, not heart, not hand of authority. A nerve ending in the damp skin of it, registering pressure and irritation. I would move along its low passages while the machines kept their ritual pace and the marsh pressed in with infinite patience. I would be practical, irritable, observant, damp to the bone, fond of children when they are elsewhere, respectful of rats, suspicious of clear water, comforted by frogs, and unable to sleep if the wrong drain fell silent.

If an outsider asked what I did, I would say, “I keep things running off.”

If they laughed, I would dislike them.

If they understood that nothing here ever truly runs off, that water only moves from one trouble to another, I might show them the safe way across the sluice bridge. Then I would tell them to watch the third plank, not touch the sweating pipe, and hurry if they heard the second whistle.

By morning I would be out before the first canteen door opened, walking the edge between town and marsh while the roofs dripped though there had been no rain. The copper light would lie dull on the tanks. Egrets would stand in the runoff ponds like officials of another government. A dog would lift its head and decide I was not worth barking at. Under the boards the frogs would begin, one by one, calling through the pipes.

I would pause, listen, and count them by absence.

Then I would go on.
