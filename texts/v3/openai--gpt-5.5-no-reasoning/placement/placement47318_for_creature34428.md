I think I would not find myself at the center of the chamber.

The center is too declarative: the polished bowl, the map-lines, the ringed plant, the place where a visitor’s eye arrives and tries to make meaning. If I were there, I would be somewhere less interpretive. Not hidden exactly, but overlooked by the mind because I had learned to resemble the conditions that sustain me.

At first I think I am one of the thread-animals.

A line between the resin post and a fan-plant rib. A tension so fine it is nearly social. I would live by feeling the chamber arrive into me: the tiny knock of a grazer’s foot, the far answering strain of salt above, the tick-sequence of an unopened pod. I would have no need to know the whole room. I would know the room as alteration in tautness. Hunger would be a geometry. Patience would not be moral, only structural.

But that is still too predatory, too cleanly an animal.

Then I think I am a tool.

The awl lying beside the bowl, dark at the haft where old resin gripped it. Made from bone or mineral—perhaps the difference no longer matters here, where dead matter and living matter dry into one another. I would have been held in a careful hand. I would have opened holes in reed-stems, scratched marks, freed a husk from a root-clamp, incised one more line into the floor-map.

Now I would lie unused but not abandoned. In this place, disuse is not death. It is storage. A tool here is a sleeping verb.

Yet even that seems too complete, too much an artifact with a before.

Where I settle is lower and more uncertain.

I would be inside the wall beneath the sealed crack, just behind the sign of the downward hand.

Not in a cavern large enough to enter, but in a narrow seam where ocher stone meets dead white salt. There, a vapor-plant has pressed a cluster of root-clamps into the mineral, and among those clamps there is a dry, dark accumulation: shed membrane, powdered crystal, grazer dust, old resin, tiny husks, perhaps one bead dropped from a builder’s wrist or mouth or pouch. It is not soil, but it has begun to behave like memory.

I would be a small custodian organism living in that accumulation.

Not one of the makers, but one of the beings from which their idea of tending may have arisen.

My body would be no longer than a fingernail clipping, flattened and jointed, the color of violet ash. I would have no true eyes. Sight would only mislead in a place where nothing announces itself by surface alone. Along my sides I would carry rows of short glassy bristles, each tuned to a different strand of the chamber’s hum. My shell would be laminated with mineral I do not manufacture so much as invite: red dust, pale salt, black resin, a faint tarnish of blue where the warm seam breathes metals.

I would move rarely. Most of the time I would be indistinguishable from a curled flake of wall.

My work would be boundary-work.

When too much powder gathers against the root-clamps, I loosen it grain by grain so the plant’s pads can keep tasting vapor. When a grazer has rasped too deeply into a membrane and left a wetless wound, I carry resin-crumbs to its edge, not to heal it exactly, but to keep the chamber’s exchanges from becoming abrupt. When dormant spores settle in a line where they should not thicken, I eat some, polish others, and leave the remainder spaced like beads.

I would feed on excess.

Not abundance—there is no abundance here in the surface sense—but on the fraction that would become danger if left to accumulate. Too much husk against a warm seam. Too much charge on a crystal hair. Too many spores caught in a dead corner. Too fine a powder packed into a vent. I would be a creature of correction, a minute subtraction by which the chamber continues to seem unchanged.

The builders, if they still exist, would know my kind.

They would not name us pets or pests. They might mark us as one of the small permissions of the room. Perhaps the downward hand does not mean enter or forbid, but attend downward: put your hand near the floor, near the roots, near the almost-invisible labor that keeps catastrophe from acquiring a body.

A builder might once have lifted me on a splinter of shale and moved me from an exhausted seam to a newly breathing one. To me, that journey would have been an epoch of terror and revelation: the whole chamber’s sound changing around me, vast fan-plants hissing overhead, salt teeth calling from the ceiling, the bowl’s low note passing through my shell like a moon.

I would remember it without narrative. Not as “I was carried,” but as a pattern of tones my bristles still compare against every new tremor.

My species would reproduce badly, extravagantly, and almost never successfully. I would lay bead-like eggs in dry pockets behind root-clamps. Most would remain eggs forever. A few would be eaten by mites. A few would mineralize and become part of the seam. One, perhaps when the ceiling’s low tone shifts by less than a human ear could bear to measure, would open. The young would emerge already powdered, already slow, already listening.

I would not fear death as interruption. Death here is a change of surface.

My shell would remain after me, its bristles clogged with red dust, its underside hollowed by mites. A vapor-root might fasten across it. A thread-animal might anchor there. A builder might mistake it for a useful chip and press it into a map-line. My body would become calibration.

If I had a mind, it would not be private in the human way. It would be distributed across contact: bristle, seam, root, powder, tone. I would know myself most strongly when something is nearly wrong.

A pod too tense.

A vent too sealed.

A grazer moving where no grazer should move.

A new taste in the dry air: not water, not yet, but the imagined edge of it, the chamber’s oldest terror rehearsed in mineral nerves.

Then I would wake fully.

I would uncurl from the violet-gray seam beneath the downward hand. I would take one step, then another, each step slow enough not to count as disturbance. Around me the whole buried garden would continue its held note. The fan-plants would hiss. The salt bowl would hum. The spores would hang in their appointed veil. The predators would remain arranged around their hungers.

And I would begin, grain by grain, to prevent an event.

So: I would find myself just under the sealed crack, where the wall has written warning into mineral and the old makers scratched their sign.

I would be a dust-keeper.

A small, blind, mineral-shelled tender of thresholds.

One of the chamber’s corrections.
