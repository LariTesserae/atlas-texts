I would find myself at the edge of the central ring, not inside it at first.

Not because I had chosen caution exactly, or even because I understood the place well enough to be afraid of its middle. I would simply be one of the things that arrives and then hesitates: a small weight added to the basin’s listening. A figure paused where the taller sedges give way to the bright low plants, one foot on firmer moss, the other sinking slowly enough to learn that the ground is not merely ground here.

I would not be a human visitor, though I might carry the outline of one at a distance.

I think I would be something that has almost become a tool, but has not yet been taken up.

A dark flake of volcanic stone lying half-buried in wet moss near the ring.

Not large. Not impressive. A palm-sized shard from one of the old black-green outcrops, broken long ago by frost, root pressure, heat, or some minute inland thunder inside the rock. One edge would be sharp enough to cut reed fiber. Another would have dulled under lichen. Its underside would be slick with the dark patience of soil. Its upper face would catch beads of water and hold them in shallow mineral dimples until they trembled free.

At first glance I would be only one more fallen piece among fallen pieces.

But I would be placed where possibility gathers.

Not in the center, where the basin most nearly knows itself. Not up on the ridge, where weather arrives first and passes on. Not sunk entirely beneath moss, surrendered already to soft green forgetting. I would lie at the threshold: close enough to feel the stillness of the inner patch, far enough to remain distinct.

I would be one of the basin’s small unchosen instruments.

A blade that has never been a blade.

The basin would know me well. Not as an animal knows a companion, and not as a person knows an object, but as a persistence in its skin: a hard interruption in moss-growth, a coolness after sunset, a surface where water gathers differently than it does on leaf or bark. My shape would alter drip-rhythm. My underside would shelter pale mites, fungal hair, perhaps the root-tip of one of those milk-pale cords when the wet months lengthened it outward.

I would have memory, but it would be mostly pressure.

The first memory would be darkness inside stone. Dense, unbroken, heat-made. Then the widening of a hairline crack. Then water entering. Then root. Then a season of cold expansion. Then release.

Not falling dramatically. Just separation.

A small tilt. A loosening. A slide down a lichen face into fern shade. A long time spent with one edge exposed to rain. A longer time sinking.

I would remember the parent stone not as origin but as continuing nearness. The ridge and I would still be related. When afternoon warmth drew the low hum out of the outcrops, I would answer faintly, too small to sing but not too small to tighten. Water passing over me would carry a taste of iron, green-black mineral, old fire cooled beyond imagining.

If a person came, I would be among the first things their hand might notice.

That would be my danger.

Not danger to them, at least not at first. Danger to my unfinishedness.

A hand could lift me from the moss. Thumb could test my edge. Another stone could strike me thinner, sharper, more purposeful. I could open reed stalks, scrape bark, sever fern stems, score wet clay. I could become useful.

The basin would not forbid it.

It understands contact. It understands the wish of materials to answer pressure. It has held me for years, perhaps centuries, in a state of almost-use. The place is full of such almosts: almost path, almost altar, almost speech, almost animal, almost mind. I would belong to that grammar.

But if no hand came, I would remain what I am: a possibility kept wet.

Moss would grow over one shoulder. Drops would darken me, dry slightly, return. A black frog might sit beside me after rain, its throat clicking like a tiny wooden hinge. Pale moths would mistake me for safe stone and sleep against my lichen-softened side. The roots below would feel my weight as a punctuation in their slow passage.

At evening I would change.

All day I would be merely hard, cool, dark, edged. But at evening, when the basin gathers inward and the ring fills first with mist, I would become a listening thing. The central pools would tremble when the spring boomed under the north slope. That tremor would pass through the saturated turf and reach me as a minute answering. Drips would strike my face. Some would split cleanly along my edge, making two sounds where there might have been one.

Perhaps that would be my voice.

A divided drop.

A small sharpening of the basin’s speech.

I would not think in words. I would not want in the way animals want. But I might possess a tendency, and the tendency would be toward readiness. Toward being held at the border between natural accident and deliberate act.

If the basin-being is a mind spread through water, root, stone, and moss, then I would be one of its teeth that has not bitten; one of its syllables that has not yet been spoken; one of its sleeping tools.

I would know the center without entering it.

I would know that no animal crosses casually. I would know by the absence of hoof-weight, by the way birds land and depart without pressing the low plants, by the subtle clearing of traffic around the inner wetness. I would feel the central stillness as a cool pressure along my buried edge. It would draw water toward itself. It would draw attention. Even I, a broken shard, would lean inward by infinitesimal settlements over years.

The basin absorbs everything, but it does not erase everything quickly.

That would be important to me. I would endure longer than leaf, moth, footprint, frog-call. Yet I too would be taken in eventually. Lichen would soften my face. Moss acids would worry my skin. Roots would wedge under me and turn me. Water would find my fractures. One day I would split again, becoming two smaller dark pieces, each with its own edge, each entering a new relation with moss and seepage.

I would not mind.

To be stone there is not to resist change. It is to slow change enough that it can be heard.

So I would be found near the ring, half visible, half claimed: a black volcanic flake in the wet green, sharp on one side, furred on the other, holding drops like tiny dark eyes.

I would be what the place offers without offering.

What waits for a hand that may never come.

What remembers the mountain’s old fire while lying in the basin’s damp breath.

A small hard thought at the edge of a larger soft one.
