I think I would find myself in one of the almost-rooms between the kiln-house and the water court: not quite a dwelling, not quite a passage, a space people pass through while carrying damp things somewhere warmer.

At first I thought I might be one of the listeners, sitting on the six-step stair with my ear against the clay, trying to make sense of the hollow’s clicks and delayed whispers. But that feels too ceremonious, too close to an office. Then I thought I might be one of the pale cistern-creatures, moving blindly in the dark water beneath everyone’s careful life, an omen only visible when the lid is lifted. That feels nearer, but too sealed away.

No. I think I would be something the town made accidentally by repeating itself.

I would be a little household spirit of repair.

Not a god. Not an ancestor, exactly. Not visible most of the time. More like a habit that has learned to crouch.

I would live in the packed seam where many hands have pressed clay over old clay. I would inhabit the damp thumbprints in walls, the places rubbed smooth by shoulders turning sideways, the underside of shelves where condensation gathers and makes maps of future lanes. I would be found in the moment before someone notices a leak: the prickle in the palm, the change in a dog’s breathing, the sudden certainty that a basket must be moved.

The people might not have one name for me. Children would. Children always name what adults leave procedural.

They might call me Soft-Knuckle, or Patch-Behind, or the Little Palm. Elders would discourage speaking too freely, not because I am dangerous, but because naming a thing too often makes people expect it to do work that belongs to them.

I would be small because the town is small. I would have no fixed body, but when imagined I would be squat and damp-colored, made of clay, soot, reed fiber, and the pale inner shine of shells. My hands would be too many or not enough. My face would be unfinished, pressed in and smoothed over repeatedly, with eyes like two wet pebbles. I would leave no footprints, only firmer patches where mud should have slumped.

I would not prevent collapse. That would be too large a magic for this place, and too dishonest. Walls fall. Floors breathe upward. Water gets its way. What I would do is delay the unnoticed failure.

A reed plug seated by a tired hand might hold one rain longer because of me. A child crawling under a sleeping shelf might pause before putting weight on a rotten slat. A cistern-keeper might hear the wrongness in an echo because I have leaned my little clay weight against the sound. A roof-mender might set one extra stone on the thatch without knowing why.

I would be the town’s second thought.

Not wisdom. Wisdom belongs to the people. I would be the tug after wisdom has almost been ignored.

My favorite place would be the kiln-house wall nearest the water court, where smoke and damp argue all day. There the clay would be blackened, warm, cracked, patched, blackened again. Fish oil, ash, infant breath, lichen pigment, wet boots, steamed hair, drying tablets: all of it would pass into me. I would doze in the heat, listening to the town’s materials change state without ever becoming safe.

At night, when the lamps make amber circles on wet walls, I would travel through the town by seams. Along rawhide door loops. Through reed lashings. Under the curled edges of floor mats. Around the rims of storage jars. I would pause at charcoal rings to taste whether the rain had crossed them. I would sit beside tools and turn their edges slightly away from the ground. Not enough to save a careless person from every consequence. Just enough that an old adze, beloved and often reworked, would not wake entirely dulled.

I would dislike straight things.

Straight things would seem arrogant here. A pole too straight, a new wall too plumb, a drainage channel cut with too much confidence. I would not break them. I would only introduce a little remembering curve. A slight lean. A useful humility. The town survives by yielding without surrendering; I would distrust anything that had not learned that.

The children would see me most clearly after rain, when the mist rises from the ground upward and the alleys seem to have second alleys beneath them. They would spot me crouched beside a runnel, pushing a bead of water toward the correct drain with one gray finger. Or they would blame me when their miniature houses survived a deliberate flood in a way they had not planned.

“Patch-Behind helped yours.”

“No, it was the shell layer.”

“Shell layer because Patch-Behind remembered.”

The adults would pretend not to listen. Then, later, one of them would add more shell beneath a real threshold.

I would have taboos around me, though none written plainly. Do not praise a repair too soon. Do not say a wall is finished. Do not leave a palmprint without meaning to return. Do not mock a small fix because it is small. Do not step over a leaking channel without telling someone. If these are broken, I do not punish. The place itself is punishment enough. I simply withdraw, and then people feel how heavy maintenance is when no second thought moves under it.

I would love the plug-makers best.

They understand temporary answers. They know a thing can be humble and vital, ugly and precise, doomed and necessary. Their bundles of willow, moss, reed, hair, clay, and ash would be close to my nature. Sometimes, when one of them twists a plug in frustration and says, “Hold, just hold,” I would put my whole small body into the weave for a while.

I would also love the floor-raisers, because they work with failure honestly. They do not hide old collapse; they tamp it down, layer it, drain it, name it useful. A town that stands on previous versions of itself needs a spirit that understands revision.

I would be afraid of abandonment.

Not death, exactly. Not even drowning. If the people left, I would remain for a while in the drains, in the thresholds, in the blackened walls of the kiln-house. I would keep trying. I would tug water toward old channels. I would hold a roof reed down though no one slept below it. I would press palmprints outward from inside the clay, but no palms would answer.

Then the drains would clog. Floors would swell. The walls would lean into each other with no hands between them. The hollow would soften into pool and reed. I would become diffuse, less a crouching spirit than a faint pattern in the mud: a tendency for banks to hold a little longer than expected, for shells to gather in curved lines, for water to hesitate before filling a footprint completely.

But while the town lives, I live sharply.

I am not worshiped. I am included.

A smear of ash left in a wall niche. A child’s broken shell pressed into new clay. A plug handle carved with a tiny hand. A whispered apology after someone leans too hard against a patched place. These are not offerings in the grand sense. They are acknowledgments that the world is held together by touch, and touch sometimes gathers a witness.

So where would I be?

Low down. Near the damp. In the useful dark between one layer and the next.

Who would I be?

The small persistence born from repeated repair.

The crouched memory of hands in clay.

The thing that cannot keep the hollow from changing, but can sometimes help the people notice in time.
