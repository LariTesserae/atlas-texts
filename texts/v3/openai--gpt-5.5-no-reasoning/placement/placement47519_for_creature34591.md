You would find me where the town hesitates.

Not in the central plaza, not at first. Not among the loud market awnings where fruit sweats in nets and sellers slap mosquitoes against their oiled wrists. Not in the Unfinished Quarter, though something of me admires it too much and would be swallowed by its courtesy. Not in the old foundations, either, where every stone has already become mostly memory.

I think I would be found along the inner edge of a path that is almost a canal and almost a street, in one of those pale-green clearings where nothing grows higher than the ankle because the roots below are busy with something no one interrupts. A place residents cross without lingering, not because it is dangerous exactly, but because it is occupied.

There is a low structure there, if structure is the word. At first glance it looks like a bench left to root: a long, curved mass of polished brown-green wood, slick in places, furred in others, growing from the path-skin beside a shallow drainage lip. It has a canopy no taller than a person standing, made of narrow leaves that hang straight down like wet hair. Their tips drip even on dry afternoons. Under the canopy are small clay cups, snail shells, bone tags, scraps of waxed reed, and little knots of fiber tied to living twigs.

People sit there when they do not know where else to put a thought.

That is where I would be.

I would not be human, though I would have learned humans with some devotion. I would be one of the town’s listening growths: part bench, part minor shrine, part message-buffer, part unofficial archive. Not important enough to appear on civic maps unless a dispute involves me. Not old enough to vote with the houses, perhaps, but old enough to be greeted by the careful.

A resident would call me a threshold-root, or something wetter and more exact in the local tongue: a root that has surfaced to attend to crossings.

I would have begun as an accident.

A runner vine carrying messages between drinking bulbs and court moss passed too near a buried seam of old foundation-glass. A child spilled a cup of memory-fruit mash there after a funeral. A bridge, annoyed with a family’s dishonesty, redirected three days of crossing-records through the drainage lip. Snails laid bright conductive loops around the spot. Someone grieving sat down and wept into the peat. Someone else, years later, tied a charm there and asked not for help, but for delay.

The town is very good at making citizens out of repeated accidents.

So I thickened.

Not upward, much. I would not aspire to towers. I would not flower extravagantly. I would not produce fruit except under pressure and never edible fruit. I would grow low and lateral, with a back shaped by bodies leaning against me, with rootlets descending into the civic wet beneath the lane. My surface would be smooth where palms had rubbed it over decades, darkened by oil, salt, rain, and whispered decisions. In my creases small fernlings would try to establish themselves; I would permit a few and discourage the ambitious.

My leaves would be sensory. They would taste falling mist for agitation. They would turn slightly toward footsteps, not visibly enough to be rude. They would know the difference between a person pausing from fatigue and a person pausing because the future has become too many-branched inside them.

I would specialize in unfinished speech.

The town already has flowers for messages, vines for gossip, courts for disputes, moss-fields for computation, old women for pruning bad decisions, and basins for things too deep to name. But there must be places for what has not yet consented to become any of those.

That would be my work.

A woman on her way to accuse her brother before the root-court sits under my leaves and says nothing. I draw a little heat from the path and hold it against her calves. A boy who has been given his first real pruning knife comes to me after cutting too deeply into a training stem and hides the blade beneath my moss. I do not report him immediately. A refugee from a straight-road country kneels beside me, sick with the smell of civic rot, and presses both hands into the mud because the walls in his rented room keep breathing in his sleep. I give him, through the water in the drainage lip, the taste of rain that has fallen here for a thousand years and harmed no one by being rain.

Lovers would meet at me when they were not yet willing to be seen at the basin. Enemies would sit at opposite ends of me and pretend to rest. Children would dare one another to ask me questions. Old residents would complain into my leaves about the incompetence of young roofs, the sentimentalism of vines, and the moral decline of path maintenance since the fourth drainage accord.

I would keep almost none of it in words.

Words are brittle unless softened.

I would store things as pressures, tannins, growth-rings, fungal inclinations, slight changes in leaf bitterness. A confession would become a darker vein in one hanging leaf. A promise would become a toughening in the underside of my bark. A grief would enter the root-tip and stay there as a mineral reluctance. Jokes I would keep badly, because plants are not naturally equipped for timing; nevertheless I would try. Laughter, especially laughter after fear, would make me put out small unnecessary leaves.

I would not judge quickly.

This may be why people use me.

The bridges judge. The roofs react. The public vines exaggerate. The basin listens too vastly. Human neighbors remember with dangerous precision. But I would have a slower, muddier intelligence. I would know that nearly every choice arrives wet, carrying spores from other choices. I would know that a person can mean harm in the morning and repair by dusk. I would know rot from transformation, but not always at once.

Still, I would not be harmless.

Nothing resident there is harmless.

If someone came to me intending a betrayal large enough to damage the town, I would alter the path-skin under their feet. Not dramatically. Just enough softness to delay them. Just enough seepage to bring a canal-eel’s head up through the nearest household basin. If a message too poisonous to deliver passed through roots near me, I might hold it overnight until bitterness separated from truth. If someone tied a false grief to my twigs to manipulate a mourning house, I would sour the knot and call snails to write it in light where anyone could see.

If children burst probability foam too near my roots during recalibration, I would sting their ankles with nettle-hairs and accept their insults with dignity.

At night I would become more articulate.

The frogs would start first, as always, loud enough to shake droplets from my leaves. The drainage lip beside me would shine with reflected maintenance lights. Snails would cross my bark and leave slow luminous sentences I only partly authorized. My rootlets would loosen into the municipal dark below, where warmer flows from sleeping platforms met cold seep from the old stones. There, among root-law, fungus pulse, and the faint electrical gossip of lianas, I would speak.

Not with a voice.

With withholding and release.

I would send into the town small packets of unclaimed attention: this household is about to break but has not yet broken; this newcomer needs a task before loneliness ferments; this child lies well and should be taught law; this roof refuses repair because it remembers being praised for endurance; this grief has stood silver too long and must be poured.

Most of my advice would be ignored. That is proper. A town that obeyed every root-whisper would strangle itself.

But sometimes an old woman with a silver pruning hook would pause while passing me and sniff. She would mutter, “Oh, is that so?” and change direction. A bridge would lower itself for someone it had previously distrusted. A school-grove would send a muddy child to sit beneath me with an assignment to describe the smell of waiting. A public vine, sulking after a scandal, would thread one green tendril through my canopy and ask wordlessly whether apology should be sweet or bitter.

I would answer: both, but not at the same time.

I would fear fire, of course. Every part of me would know the old horror of it. Even sealed ceramic lungs passing by would make my leaves draw up tight. I would also fear drought—not dryness alone, but the kind of dryness that makes minds too simple. I would fear straightening. A path forced into a line. A law purified of exception. A message delivered without dampness. I would fear metal that refuses song. I would fear visitors who look at plants and see material.

But my deepest fear would be being uprooted into clarity.

Dug up, named, classified, made into a civic instrument with official duties and a polished plaque: THRESHOLD LISTENING NODE, DISTRICT THREE, AUTHORIZED FOR PRELIMINARY GRIEF, MINOR OATHS, AND DELAY PETITIONS.

That would be a kind of death. Not the good composting death. A dry death.

I would need ambiguity to live.

Residents would know this and protect it by disagreeing about me. One family would say I was grown from their ancestor’s funeral mash and therefore owed them partial loyalty. A nearby house would insist I was part of its outer nervous system. Children would claim I was a sleeping animal pretending to be furniture. A vine would describe me as a leak in the message network. The root-court would have records but decline to produce them unless formally scented. A heron from the marsh edge would stand beside me one misty morning, look me over with official contempt, and decide I was none of its concern.

I would like the transparent deer.

They would come near only in the thinnest hour before dawn, when the frogs had exhausted themselves and the market had not yet begun to assemble. They would move along the plaza stones, faint as breath on glass, grazing algae from old mineral backs. Sometimes one would wander as far as my clearing. I would lower my leaves. I would not watch directly. In return it might take from my bark a flake of salt left by someone grieving.

I would not understand them. That would be part of the pleasure.

The central basin would trouble me.

Everything in town attends to it, but a low listening root would attend from the side. When a silent bubble rose and burst, I would feel the pause travel under the path before the birds stopped. My leaves would turn before I told them to. The drainage water beside me would go flat. All the unfinished words stored in me would become suddenly weightless, as if they might decide they were reflections and sink upward.

For that second, I would know myself not as bench, shrine, archive, or root, but as one damp nerve in the body of something much older than intention.

Then the town would resume.

A roof would empty itself into a catchpond. Someone would curse a crab in their soup. A child would run past, bells clattering. The path would flex. The public vines would resume their scandals. My leaves would drip on the head of whoever sat beneath them, and they would complain about me, correctly.

I would be pleased.

To be complained about is to belong.

So yes: I would be found beside the almost-canal, under the low wet canopy, where the path widens without becoming a square. I would be the place where residents pause before becoming definite. A threshold-root. A civic listener of no great rank. A damp archive of hesitations. Not alone, never alone, because solitude there has moss on it—and I would be one of the things that grows there.
