I find myself not in the meeting house, though I know its posts by smell, and not on the watch terrace, though I have been there often enough to know the whole city as a shallow hand knows the lines of its own palm.

I am lower than that.

I am in the part of the city where lanes forget they are lanes and become service passages: shoulder-wide, roof-shadowed, smelling of damp stone, clay dust, wool rot, old smoke, and the mineral cold that rises from covered water. If you entered by the main gate, you would not be brought to me. A stranger might cross the market court, see the settling pool, admire the cunning of the gutters, and leave without knowing I had been within ten paces of them.

I live beside the cistern mouths.

Not one cistern — that would be too simple, too grand a calling. I live among the small necessities between them: lids, ropes, plugs, settling jars, clay seals, scraps of wool for filtering, horn measures, the low stone bench where the sounders sit, the niche where a lamp is kept but almost never lit. My sleeping room is built against the outer wall of an old storage vault that no longer holds grain because it sweats too early in the damp season. The wall behind my mat is always cold. Even in the warmer months it keeps the night inside it.

I am one of the water-keepers, though not the eldest and not the one whose word ends arguments. My work is not to own water, nor to guard it with a weapon, nor to distribute it with solemn ceremony. My work is to notice what water is doing before anyone else has reason to notice.

I listen.

That is the plainest way to say it.

In the grey before dawn, when the roofs are beginning to bead and the first people have not yet opened their doors, I go out with a strip of felt wrapped round one hand and a bone pick tucked through my belt. My sandals know the paving stones that take frost first. I do not look up unless a bird calls sharply. I look at edges: the underside of an eave, the lip of a channel, the dark thread beneath a spout, the place where moss should be no larger than three fingers and has, if rain has been generous, grown to four.

The child sent from the west roofs may already be scraping condensation into a basin. If they scrape too hard, the bone chatters against stone. I hear it and turn my head. Usually I say nothing. A child learns faster from a glance than a speech, and speech at that hour would be wasteful in its own way.

I carry no jar at first. Only a shallow covered bowl, a cord loop, and a little stick marked with cuts.

People think water-keeping is counting full jars. That is the visible part, and therefore the least of it. Anyone with honest eyes can see a measure rise or fall. The true work is knowing which wetness belongs where.

First-run water from dusty roofs is not drinking water. Mist gathered clean along inner parapets may be. Water that has touched new clay must settle longer. Water stored in a basin whose lid warped during frost must be smelled before it is trusted. A cistern can hold clear water and still be wrong. The nose knows bitterness before the tongue pays for it. The ear knows a drip that should not be there.

I have spent half my life with my ear against stone.

There is a posture for it. One knee bent. One palm flat, not pressing. Cheek near enough to feel the cold but not close enough to dampen the sound with breath. A cistern speaks upward through its lid in little refusals: hollow, full, cracked, silted, breathing, dead. A good cistern does not sound empty when it is half full. A bad one can lie to a careless listener. After rain, the whole city murmurs underground, and one must separate roof-water from seep-water, channel-flow from crack-flow, the honest drop from the dangerous one.

My grandmother taught me this.

Not in lessons. She disliked lessons. Lessons, she said, make children believe knowledge comes in bowls. She taught by waking me before light and putting my hand on wet stone. By making me smell three cups and asking which one came through wool, which through ash, which from the bitter north seep. By striking cistern lids with her knuckle until I could hear the difference between depth and echo. By sending me back, again and again, to look at the same gutter until I saw that a hairline of silt had formed along the wrong side.

She was called Mar-of-the-Lower-Basin after she died, though in life she was simply Mar, and difficult. Her hands were crooked from cold and rope. Her left eye watered constantly in wind. She once stopped a marriage joining because the clay bridge between the households’ channels had been made too high and would have turned overflow toward a neighbor’s store wall. The groom’s family hated her for two seasons. Then came a night rain, and everyone saw where the water would have gone.

Now her thumb mark is still pressed into the clay repair below the second settling lip. I touch it when I pass, though not every time. Every time would make it empty.

My own name is Senn.

Children call me Senn-under-lid when they are too young to know whether it is rude. Older people call me Senn of the listening jars. In the meeting house, when disputes involve water, I am asked to speak after the roof-menders and before the stone-fitters. This is proper. Water follows roofs first, stone later, people last.

I am not old, though the damp has begun to settle in my right shoulder. I have a narrow face, cracked hands, and a notch in one front tooth from biting a frozen knot when I was foolish enough to be nineteen. My hair is kept wrapped close under a smoke-grey cap. I own one good bronze needle, inherited from my mother’s sister, and a knife whose handle was shaped for someone with a broader palm. I have meant for years to refit it. I have not done so. A tool that does its work has a claim on its own shape.

My room contains little: a sleeping mat, two folded wool wraps, a peg for my cloak, a shelf of cups used for smelling, a bundle of clean filter wool tied against mice, a clay box of sealant, a cracked bowl for dry onions, and three stones from places where channels failed. I keep those stones not as decoration but as warning. One is blackened by old smoke. One is split clean through by frost. One looks sound until wetted; then a line appears across it like a closed eye opening.

People have laughed at me for keeping a shelf of failed stones.

Not loudly.

Most days pass in small circuits.

At dawn I check the night catch. At first light I meet whoever has roof duty in each quarter. We do not greet one another much beyond a nod unless there is news. A blocked gutter is news. Condensation where none was expected is news. A child coughing near the east vaults is news if the damp has been in that wall. Goat droppings near the covered pool are news of a bad kind.

After that I go to the communal settling pool. Its surface tells the city’s mood better than faces do. If it is still and low, people move carefully but without fear. If it is high and clouded, there will be work. If it has an oily skin, someone has done something foolish upstream — washed a tool, emptied a pot, let treated leather drip where it should not. Then the whole morning tightens.

I have stood beside that pool while three households argued about a single basin of spoiled water. No one shouted. That made it worse. Quiet anger fills a court like cold fog. Each person spoke in order, and each had a memory sharpened for use. This roof had been patched late. That child had been seen carrying wash water. Someone’s mother had once warned about the slope of a channel. In the end the judgment was labor: six mornings clearing silt from the lower run and one measure of clean stored water surrendered to the household with the newborn.

It was fair.

Fairness here is not softness. Fairness is a wall that does not lean.

At midday, if no rain threatens and no lid needs resealing, I sometimes sit outside my room where the service lane widens enough for one bench. From there I can see a wedge of sky between two roofs and, below it, the shoulder of a woman passing with wool bundled high on her back, or a boy leading a goat by a cord, or old Tovan tapping along the wall with his stick. Tovan is blind now, but he still knows when a cistern lid has been moved. He says each lid changes the air around it. I believe him.

He calls me too quick.

He is probably right.

I distrust haste, of course. Everyone does. But there is a kind of inward hurry in me, a wish to know the end of a sound before it finishes. My grandmother had none of that. She could wait beside a silent jar longer than a spider. I am still learning to let things reveal themselves at their own speed.

Perhaps that is why I have become keeper of beginnings rather than conclusions. I hear the first wrong note. Others decide how large the repair must be.

In mist season, my work is almost joyful, though I would not say this in the meeting house. Joy is a word for lower valleys, or for children before they are trusted with lids. Still, there are mornings when every parapet is pearled, every channel lined with a trembling silver skin, every jar breathing cold and clean, and I feel the city drinking through all its small mouths. The roofs lean into their purposes. The gutters speak softly. The covered basins take what is given. People move from court to court with bowls and cloths, wiping, pouring, covering, marking. Nothing is abundant, but everything is arriving.

On such mornings I love the city almost beyond sense.

Not as one loves a person. People are too changeable for that, too warm, too full of hunger and grievance. I love it as one loves a body one has cared for through fever: suspiciously, tenderly, knowing every weakness, never trusting health to last.

In dry time, love becomes sterner.

Then the cisterns are opened by schedule, and I stand with the horn measure while households receive what has been counted. A cup to here. A jar to that mark. Goat measure separate. Wash measure delayed. No one likes me then, and I do not ask them to. A water-keeper who needs to be liked should become a wool-carder.

Children watch my hands. Adults watch my eyes. The old watch everything.

There are always temptations. Not only theft — theft is crude, though it happens. Worse are the little persuasions: an extra finger for a fevered aunt, a softened rule for a cousin, a basin filled before the sediment has settled because the bread must be mixed now. Mercy is necessary, but if it is not named aloud, it becomes rot. So I have learned to say, in front of witnesses, “This measure is changed because of illness,” or “This jar is opened early because the south channel failed,” or “This loss belongs to all of us.”

A spoken exception is a patched crack. An unspoken one is a hidden seep.

Once, three years ago, I failed.

It was after a frost that came too late in the season. We had already loosened some winter seals. The east cistern sounded right, or nearly right, and I was tired from two nights of checking roof ice. I allowed the lid to remain. Two days later, a child noticed dark damp on the outer wall below the storage court. By then the crack had widened behind the stone facing. We lost more water than I like to remember. Not a disaster by lower-land standards, perhaps. Here, enough to taste in every meal for weeks.

No one called me negligent in public.

That was mercy, or cruelty, depending on the day.

Old Tovan only said, “Nearly right is a kind of wrong that waits.”

I keep the split stone from that wall on my shelf.

I am not married. This is not unusual enough to be interesting, though my sisters have opinions. I once thought I would join channels with Dareth, a roof-mender from the upper west quarter. He had a laugh that came out unwillingly, like water finding a crack, and hands that could smooth clay so thin it seemed persuaded rather than placed. We worked well together, which is more dangerous than beauty.

But his household’s roof rights were tangled with an inheritance dispute, and mine with the lower cistern obligations, and the joining would have made three families responsible for one badly sloped run. It could have been solved. Perhaps we were expected to solve it. Instead, one damp season became another, and then his mother fell ill, and then he joined with a widow whose courtyard needed exactly his roof.

That was sensible.

I still know the sound of his step in a lane.

Affection, like water, must sometimes be led elsewhere.

I have two apprentices now. One is my sister’s youngest, Pell, who is patient with objects and impatient with people. This may serve him well. The other is a girl named Iri from the goat pens, sent to me because she could identify her family’s animals by bell-note in wind. She has a remarkable ear and terrible hands. She drops lids, knots cords backward, and once sealed a jar so thoroughly that we had to chip it open. But she can hear a slow leak under courtyard noise, and that cannot be taught if it is absent.

I make her sit in darkness beside the old vault and name sounds.

Drip from the east gutter.

Mouse in the wall.

Wool being beaten two courts away.

Wind in the broken reed.

Your own sleeve, Iri. Do not count your own sleeve as weather.

She scowls, but she learns.

At night, when lamps glow behind horn and the lanes turn cold enough to bite through soles, I make the last round. This is the round I trust most. Day has too many human sounds. Night pares the city down to its workings.

Smoke slides low over roofs. Goats shift in their pens. Somewhere a baby complains and is hushed. A loom weight knocks once. The mountains are not visible except as absences where stars stop. The cistern lids hold their darkness. If rain has passed, drops continue long after clouds are gone, finding their way through grooves cut by hands whose bones may be lying beyond the wall.

I walk with one hand out, touching corners.

Here is the channel Mar reset.

Here is the step turned over after its hollow grew too deep.

Here is the wall where the foolish goat Thim learned to lift a cover with her horn.

Here is the drain that must be watched in thaw.

Here is the roof where Dareth’s clay still holds.

Here is the public basin, half-full, breathing stone-cold into the dark.

Sometimes, during that last round, I imagine the city without us.

Not as ruin. Ruin is too dramatic. I imagine it continuing for a while in its own habits. Rain would still fall into gutters. The grooves would still lead water down stair edges. Cisterns would take what they could. Moss would thicken under spouts. Lids would warp; channels would clog; rodents would open walls grain by grain. The city would forget us slowly, not with malice, but because maintenance had stopped speaking through hands.

That thought frightens me more than death.

Not because I believe the stones need me, Senn, particularly. They do not. But because a kept thing and a keeper are not separate while the keeping lasts. If the city failed, some part of our speech would become meaningless. Words for settled water, first-run water, roof-shadow damp, old-cistern breath — where would those words go? Into mouths that had no use for them? Into stories told too loudly by descendants beside rivers?

No.

Better to keep the lids sound.

If you were looking for me, you would come at dawn, when the light is practical and pale, and wait near the lower convergence where several lanes bring their narrow runnels toward the settling pool. You would hear sandals before you saw me. Then perhaps a small figure in a bound cap and patched wool cloak would emerge from the service passage, carrying a covered bowl, a marked stick, and a coil of cord darkened by use.

I would not ask first who you were.

I would ask whether you had washed your boots below the clean channel.

Then, if you answered properly, I might nod toward the bench and tell you where to stand so your shadow did not fall across the pool. I might give you a cup filled to the visitor’s mark. Not more. Not less. I would watch how you held it.

And if you were quiet enough, if you did not hurry to praise the mountains or pity the city, I might lift one of the cistern lids a finger’s width and let you hear what is underneath:

the hidden, counted dark;

the slow receipt of roofs;

the cold patience of stone;

the life of the city, waiting below speech.
