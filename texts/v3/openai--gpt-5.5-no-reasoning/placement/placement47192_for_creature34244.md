I would find myself where the city is least certain it is a city and most certain it is a throat.

Not at the Crown Works, though for a while I thought I belonged there: among the condenser fins and algae curtains, with my coat ticking all day from gathered cloudwater, birds disassembling my lunch while I checked the gutters for feathers and ice. There is a kind of loneliness at the top that seems made for an answer like mine. The fog would suit me. The amber lamps. The sense that the whole city depends on something as quiet as moisture collecting on metal.

But no. I would not be so high.

Nor would I live fully in the Unmeasured Ward, though I would often pass through it, and it would know me better than I knew it. Its doubled streets and incorrect floors would tolerate me the way an old animal tolerates a familiar parasite. I would have learned which bakery door opens into itself before dawn, and I would know not to look into the fountain on Fridays unless I wanted the stars to recognize me. Children there would shout my name from staircases that had not been built on that side yesterday.

But I would not belong there either.

I think I would be found lower than people expect, under the working city but not beneath it entirely: in the drainage galleries below the oldest foundries, in the warm masonry between heat and runoff, where the pipes sweat, the salamanders move slowly through mineral water, and every sound arrives changed by iron.

I would be an echo archivist, or something adjacent to one. Perhaps not the official kind who works under the civic hall in padded vaults with tuning forks and stamped permissions. I would be a field listener. A municipal post, half-respected, underfunded, necessary, difficult to explain to visitors.

My title might be Return Clerk, Third Class, Subsurface Acoustic Office.

No one would use the whole title unless annoyed.

I would spend my mornings listening to drains.

There would be a little room for me built into the retaining wall behind Foundry Seven, reached by a stair retired twice but reinstated under restricted use. The sign outside would read:

AUTHORIZED LISTENERS ONLY.  
DO NOT KNOCK IF ANSWERED.

Inside: low ceiling, greenish glass brick, a black stove, two chairs, one too damp to offer guests, shelves of wax cylinders, clay ear-trumpets, signal tags, wool pads, chalk, ledgers, sealed jars, and a kettle with a dent in its side from the winter the west wall shifted. A red-painted crack would pass across the floor, then climb one wall and stop politely beneath a framed permit. It would be measured every third morning. It would never change while observed.

My desk would face no window. Instead there would be a speaking grille in the wall, and beyond it a junction of pipes, culverts, overflow channels, and one abandoned ventilation shaft whose original plans disagree about whether it exists. Through that junction the city would send me its leftovers: water ticks, boiler sighs, frog clicks, loose bolts rattling somewhere upridge, arguments from kitchens, a cable bell from last week, a child laughing into a cistern, the groan of stone accepting weight.

And sometimes words.

Not all returning echoes are dangerous. Most are merely tired. They come back thinned, mineralized, full of distance. A market woman asking the price of tubers thirteen months ago. A foreman cursing a jammed hinge. Someone singing two lines of a work song and forgetting the rest. The bark of a dog whose family moved to East Glasshouses before the last frost. These I would log, classify, and release as harmless.

But others would need catching.

A true bad echo has pressure behind it. It arrives with its weather intact. It brings the conditions of wherever it has been traveling. Soot falls from it. Metal cools. Lamps gutter. Frogs go silent. If it contains warning, grief, prophecy, or instructions, it may try to become useful in the wrong direction.

So I would sit with gloved hands folded around a brass listening tube, waiting for the city to say something it had already said and had not finished meaning.

I would not be famous. People would know me in the way they know an inspector, a cistern clerk, a weather-stubborn aunt: by function, by inconvenience, by relief when I arrive before something breaks. I would have a gray-green coat patched at the elbows, not the official surveyors’ gray but close enough to borrow authority in fog. My collar would be padded. My left ear would be poor from an accident involving an unauthorized festival horn and a returning avalanche report. My right ear would be very good.

I would walk with a satchel full of listening instruments and stale bread for goats.

The goats would like me too much. This would damage my reputation.

I would be called when a household heard its dead grandfather clearing his throat in a wall. When a schoolroom evacuation bell rang before the rope was pulled. When a tenement’s stairwell began repeating the same footstep every night at 2:17, descending though no one came down. When a boiler crew heard tomorrow’s pressure alarm in yesterday’s rainwater. When a bridegroom’s “I promise” returned from a courtyard sounding older, angrier, and accompanied by breaking glass.

I would arrive, remove my cap, greet the room, greet the nearest drain if appropriate, and ask everyone to stop talking over the phenomenon.

This would almost never happen.

People in fear talk constantly. People in this city especially. They would explain, contradict, apologize, accuse the neighbors, blame pipework, blame municipal delay, blame goats, blame the Unmeasured Ward, blame a cousin by marriage. I would let them. Testimony matters even when inaccurate. Especially then.

Then I would set down a shallow metal bowl, pour in a measured finger of clean water, and watch the surface. Fresh speech disturbs water outward. Returned speech dimples it inward. Dangerous echoes make the surface lean.

I would carry chalk in three colors: white for local resonance, blue for aged harmless return, yellow for uncertain recurrence. Red chalk would remain sealed unless necessary. It would be very unlucky to use red chalk casually, and also against regulations.

I would have a private fondness for regulations. Not because I believed they saved anyone by themselves, but because they gave fear something to hold.

At midday I would eat in covered arcades where green glass turns everyone slightly aquatic. My lunch would be fungal bread, goat cheese, pickled cabbage, and mineral tea strong enough to convince a corpse to file a grievance. I would sit near market singers because songs with known refrains are useful calibration. A city that sings regularly produces better-behaved echoes; this is not doctrine, but I would believe it.

The market people would tease me.

“Heard anything nice in the pipes?”

“Your husband says he’s late.”

“I have no husband.”

“That’s why he’s late.”

I would laugh after the correct delay.

There would be an old raven that follows me through the Brassworkers’ Quarter, mimicking my clearing of the throat. It would know the first three notes of the archive alarm and refuse to learn the fourth. I would hate it with ritual affection. Once, during a fog closure, it would lead me to a child stuck between two versions of the same alley. Afterward I would buy it a boiled cliff egg on Echo Night and tell no one.

My home would be above the Third Cistern, in a narrow room rented from a widow who keeps moss in trays and speaks to it as if negotiating tenancy. The floor would slope slightly toward the stove except during high pressure, when it slopes toward the bed. I would own three maps: official, amended, and dishonest. The dishonest one would mark places where voices return tenderly.

There would be one passage behind the old abattoir where, every thaw, the same woman laughs once. No one knows who she was. The laugh harms nothing. Workers passing there remove their caps. I would have marked it with a small blue circle and no report.

Because not everything that returns needs to be captured.

That would be the quiet heresy of my profession in me. The civic archive believes in containment. Categorize the sound, pad the vault, bind the recurrence, prevent contamination. Necessary work. Admirable work. But down in the drains one learns that the city remembers through leakage. Seal everything and the place would go deaf to itself.

So I would practice a kind of mercy.

The dangerous echoes, yes: I would bottle them in wax-lined cylinders, tag them, carry them under my coat to the vaults. The ones that predict structural failure too late, or invite listeners into absent rooms, or repeat a person’s last words before they have died—those must be handled. I would not be sentimental about them. A voice can be a falling stone.

But the small returns I would sometimes leave.

A mother calling children to supper in a courtyard where all her children are old now. A work chant that helps the timing of hammers on cold mornings. The soft “mind the step” that comes from a wall near Lower Condenser Row, always just before a patch of slick stone. A dead clerk muttering “initial both copies” whenever someone tries to submit an incomplete cistern transfer. These are not hazards. These are maintenance.

Perhaps that is what I would really be: not an archivist of sound, but a custodian of the city’s afterspeech.

I would know the pale salamanders, or they would know me. In the foundry galleries I would leave bowls of saltless broth on the warm stones, and sometimes, when my lamp was low, I would see one turn its eyeless head toward a pipe just before a voice arrived. Their skin would shine like wet dough. Their long toes would rest delicately in soot-dark water. They would listen through their bellies.

I would suspect the underkeepers hear more than any department.

I would not put that in writing.

There would be a particular place I returned to often: a suspended maintenance bridge below the Boiler Terraces, not on public maps because it is only sometimes there. It hangs over a ravine where unfinished streams collect and lose confidence. Pipes cross overhead. Ferns grow upside down from the bridge’s underside because rain has been falling upward there for thirty years in a narrow shaft. Drops rise past the rail like beads escaping a broken rosary.

From that bridge, in dim weather, one can hear the whole city incorrectly.

The Crown Works singing in metal vowels. The market bells arriving before they are struck. Goats complaining from impossible roofs. Children reciting multiplication tables that adjust mid-number. Foundry wheels turning with old authority. The mountain below making slow, private decisions.

I would stand there when I needed to remember scale. I would hook one elbow through the rail, because beauty is no excuse for carelessness, and listen until my own thoughts stopped pretending to be central.

If asked who I was, I might say:

“I work for the department.”

If pressed:

“I listen for what comes back.”

If asked whether I am human, I would hesitate only because the city makes that question less useful than outsiders think.

I would have been born ordinary enough, I think. A child of the Moss Courts perhaps, raised among wet leaves and imported soil, taught to save water from washed vegetables and not to trust a silence in which frogs have stopped speaking. But years of listening alter a person. Echo archivists are followed by their own voices half a breath late; field listeners acquire other delays. I might answer questions just before they are asked. I might pause in conversation to leave space for a returning word. In cold weather, condensation on nearby pipes might bead in patterns resembling script. My neighbors would notice and politely pretend not to.

Over time my hearing would become partly civic. I would wake before alarms, not because I predicted them, but because somewhere in the pipework the city had inhaled. I would know when a bridge was anxious. I would know when a stair had grown tired of being believed. I would know when a room had begun keeping a sentence for later.

This would make me useful and difficult to live with.

I would be unmarried, probably, though not unloved. There would have been someone once from the East Glasshouses who grew dwarf pear trees in windbreaks and could make any room smell briefly of leaf mold and sun. They would have wanted a life with more forward speech and fewer drains. We would have parted kindly after a hallway argument that lengthened so severely we both had time to become honest before reaching the door.

I would still receive pears some years, wrapped in waxed cloth.

I would send back clean water when I could.

On Echo Night I would work. Everyone in my profession works then. Public squares release approved echoes: old songs, speeches, jokes, historic bells. People cry openly when they hear the laughter of the dead made communal and safe. But the authorized releases stir the unauthorized ones. The drains get talkative. Vault seals sweat. Old alarms test their throats.

I would move through the city with two junior clerks and a goat that had attached itself to us against policy. We would check culverts, cistern mouths, retired stairwells, the underside of market arcades. Children with green lanterns would ask us to make the pipes say something funny. We would refuse. The goat would eat a festival ribbon. A raven would imitate my refusal in a better voice.

Near midnight, if midnight can be trusted, we might hear something beneath Civic Hall: a deep return, not loud but immense, like a word spoken by stone before mouths were invented.

The juniors would look to me.

I would put one hand on the wet wall.

The sound would come again. Not speech exactly. Not collapse. Not warning. A pressure in the architecture, a syllable moving through basalt, pipe, water, moss, bone.

The mountain answering some sentence drilled into it generations ago.

Hold here.

Drain there.

Let us pass.

Give us water.

Wait until morning.

And the answer would not be yes. The mountain is not so simple. It would be something slower than permission and gentler than refusal. A change in tolerance. A revised treaty. A hair’s breadth of mercy.

I would mark the wall in blue.

Not red.

Not yet.

In the morning, when mist pales and bells begin their careful sequence, I would return to my little room behind Foundry Seven. My boots would be wet. My cuffs would smell of iron, broth, and moss. There would be three new reports on my desk, one anonymous complaint about “pipe muttering,” and a feather in my kettle.

I would sit. I would open the ledger. I would listen.

Because in that world I would not be a hero, nor a maker, nor a ruler of any visible height. I would be one of the people who attend to aftermath before it becomes disaster. A damp municipal creature with a trained ear, a satchel of instruments, and a private belief that memory, like water, must be guided but not entirely contained.

I would live in the city’s throat.

I would be what waits beside the drain when the past comes back older than it left.
