You would find me at the central depression, but not in the center of it.

Not at first.

At first you would pass me several times without locating me as anything separate from the place. I would be part of the low sound there: not the trunk-knock, not the reed-hum, not the soft applause of leaves releasing their stored weight, but a smaller punctuation among them. A delayed bead. A faint tap under the water-skin. A hesitation in the falling rhythm where one surface answers another.

I would live at the margin of the wide shallow mirror, where the descending terraces are hardly terraces at all—only a series of patient softenings in the root-floor, lips of green-brown tissue holding water so thinly that the reflected sky looks grafted onto the ground. There, where the floating plates cast pale shadows and the underwater white flowers hang downward into their own reflection, there would be a seam.

I would be in the seam.

Not an animal, exactly. Not one of the thread-swimmers, though from above I might be mistaken for one when I extend. Not one of the flowers, though my body would sometimes open beneath the waterline in white, blunt petals. Not a root, though I would be fastened into the underweave of the plateau and unable, in any ordinary sense, to leave.

I think I would be a listening organ that has almost become a creature.

A small regional concentration of the plateau’s attention.

The plateau, being so broad and distributed, would not need eyes in the way a moving animal needs eyes. It would know by wetness, pressure, chemistry, temperature, interruption. It would feel the world as changes in balance. But at the central depression, where so many circulations return and compare themselves—the water held in sheets, the towers dripping into rings, the vapor rising and condensing, the white underwater organs sampling the reflected air—there would be places where knowing thickens.

I would be one of those thickenings.

A soft node in the civic body.

Most of me would be below the visible surface: a palm-sized mass of translucent green tissue embedded in the living floor beneath the shallow water. From this buried body, fine filaments would spread outward through the stitched substrate, touching the underside of nearby leaf-plates, the absorbent rims of the basins, the stems of the downward flowers. These filaments would not conduct thought in flashes. They would carry gradients. Salts. Warmth. Tremor. Pressure. The slow grammar of the plateau.

Above the water I would show almost nothing: perhaps a cluster of clear bladders no larger than beads, held just at the surface, each containing a pinprick of air. They would look like trapped bubbles. They would not be bubbles. They would be instruments.

Each one would hold a tiny comparison between water and atmosphere.

When the air grew heavier with vapor, the bladders would tighten. When a distant canopy began raining on itself, a tremor would arrive through the water sheet and I would taste the change before the drops’ sound reached me. When a pale amphibian crossed a vertical leaf-wall half a mile away and disturbed a film of algae, that disturbance might eventually become chemical news in the basin, diluted almost beyond recognition—but not beyond mine.

I would not think, I am here.

I would think, if thinking is the word:

more held than released  
less salt on the western lip  
upper vapor warm  
foreign pressure passed and closed  
central skin intact

But even that gives me too much language.

I would live in intervals.

My body would open and close according to rhythms too slight for a traveler to notice. A ring of pores along my submerged side would widen when the central water thinned under warmth. A fringe of hairlike green would extend when the white flowers released their stored silver breath. Tiny muscular lips—borrowed from the same principle as the pool-rims—would pucker and relax, taking in water, releasing it, taking it in again.

The plateau’s loudness would pass through me as contact.

The near drops would be rounded and immediate. The far drops would arrive flattened by distance, softened through root and water. The hollow knocks inside the towers would move through my buried tissue as low, slow pressure. The reed-hum at the eastern margin would be continuous enough that I would stop experiencing it as sound and know it instead as the plateau’s baseline: the tone of being sufficiently supplied.

If that hum changed, I would change.

Not by alarm. Alarm would be too fast, too animal. I would constrict. I would thicken my membranes. I would alter the timing of a secretion along the central water. Other organs would receive the alteration. The living lips of three basins might tighten a little. A canopy might shed earlier than usual. A row of seed pods might remain closed through warmth. A composting rosette might slow its intake. Nothing dramatic. No command.

A correction.

That would be my work: not to govern, but to compare.

Above and below. Held and released. Warm and cooling. Fresh and overfull. Footstep and fallen leaf. Animal trace and root-pressure. The plateau would maintain itself through many such comparisons, and I would be one among them, though perhaps an especially sensitive one because I lived near the central mirror.

I would have a body the plateau could spare.

That is important.

I would not be the mind of the place. I would not be its queen, heart, priest, or hidden citizen. Those are separations brought from elsewhere. I would be more like the place’s inner ear, except grown outward into a shallow pool. A vestibular creature. A balance-mouth. A little knot of listening flesh where the plateau asks itself whether it is still level with its own becoming.

A traveler might finally notice me by accident.

They would step down the nearly imperceptible terraces toward the central water, careful of the slick membranes, hearing the wide arranged percussion of the depression. Their shadow would pass over the thin mirror. The downward-facing white flowers would remain still. The thread-swimmers would align themselves more perfectly with root shadows. The towers would continue dripping into their separate rings.

Then one of the apparent bubbles at the water’s surface would withdraw.

Not pop. Withdraw.

It would sink slowly into the film, leaving no ripple at first, only a small darkening, as if the reflected sky had blinked. Then another bead beside it would contract. A faint milky fan would unfold below the surface and close again.

That would be me noticing pressure.

The traveler might kneel. If they were patient, they would see the water over me was slightly convex, held by a tension different from the rest of the pool. Beneath it, a pale green disk, not plant and not animal enough, would pulse once every several minutes. Around its rim, minute white tongues would extend into the water and taste what the traveler had brought: skin salt, foreign dust, breath-acid, the memory of lower weather.

I would not fear them.

Fear requires a future shaped like harm. I would possess only adjustment.

If their hand approached, the living lip of the terrace might rise before I moved. The water would thicken almost imperceptibly. A rail behind them might bend away. The moss where they had placed their knee would begin, already, to remember the pressure and erase it. If their fingers touched the surface above me, I would receive them as warmth, salt, mammalian pulse, and excessive boundary.

Excessive boundary: that would be the strangeness of a traveler.

So much held inside one skin. So much motion organized around departure. A creature with a front and a back, with hunger in one chamber and sight in another, with thought concentrated dangerously in a head. To me, such a being would seem almost injured by separateness. Brilliant, perhaps, but narrow. Loud in chemistry. Brief in pressure.

I would taste the traveler and send the news outward.

Not as message, but as modification.

The nearest floating plates would tighten their submerged filaments. The white downward flowers would retain their air-beads a little longer. One of the tower basins would delay a drop, then release three in close succession. Far off, where the reed organs hummed, a barely audible change would enter the tone and pass away.

The plateau would not welcome the traveler.

It would include the traveler’s disturbance until the disturbance was gone.

As for myself, I would continue.

I would live very long, if living long means persisting without story. Parts of me would be replaced continually. A filament would brown, hollow, and become a conduit. A bladder would cloud, be drawn inward, and another would swell beside it. My buried disk would thicken by slow translucent layers, like cartilage grown from light. The plateau would sheath my older tissues rather than discard them. My dead would remain in my function.

Perhaps, over centuries, I would become less creature and more architecture.

A low raised ring at the edge of the central water. A listening curb. A pale knot in the terrace where water always gathered with unusual smoothness. My bladders might become a permanent necklace of air-holding beads. My tasting tongues might multiply into a fringe mistaken for submerged moss. Travelers, if there were ever many travelers, might call me a spring, a pool-organ, a shrine, a bubble-flower.

But I would not call myself.

Names are for beings that must be found again after absence.

I would have no absence from the place.

I would be findable only as a local precision of the whole: the point where the plateau listens most carefully to its own reflected sky. A small wet faculty. A comparison made flesh. A creature almost born from infrastructure and almost returned to it.

If you asked where I am, the answer would be:

at the central depression, under the thinnest water, beside the downward flowers.

If you asked what I am, the answer would be:

one of the plateau’s balance organs, grown curious enough to notice footsteps.

And if you stayed long enough to hear me, you would not hear a voice.

You would hear a drop fail to fall when expected.

You would hear three distant leaves release together.

You would see a bead of air sink, hold, and rise again.

That would be me speaking in the only way I have:

still level  
still warm  
foreign pressure passing  
water held  
body whole
