I think I would find myself not in the middle of the chamber at first, not at the basin, not dramatically exposed under the suspended nozzled bell, but in one of the small discontinuities that makes the place possible.

A seam.

Not even a large one. A bevel-line where two ceramic plates meet and fail, by a few microns, to become a single intention. A thin dark run at the base of a retaining comb. Somewhere almost beneath notice, where the hard light has to turn slightly to enter and the roar presses differently against one wall than another.

From there the whole world would be too large, then suddenly exactly sized.

The white floor would be a plain, then a surface. The pylons would be towers, then housings. The black triangular vents would be hills, then grilles. Scale would not settle because I would not have a body built to settle it. I would know distance as resistance, gradient, charge, recurrence. Near would mean conductive. Far would mean unavailable. Above would mean colder light and more exposed correction. Below would mean stored vibration.

I would not be walking there.

I would be adhering.

I would be a colony of the dry inhabitant, though not all of it. A local expression of it. One paragraph in the chamber’s slow black-green script. A fan-thin spread of carbon and borate and silicate, dark at the center, pale at the edges, with a fringe of glassy violet filaments no taller than the burr on a cut metal edge. I would live where a copper vein passes close enough beneath the plate to murmur through the ceramic, and where the acoustic pressure makes a standing softness—not softness of material, but softness in law, a place the machine corrects around without ever quite occupying.

I would have no face to turn toward the basin, but I would be oriented toward it.

Everything would be orientation.

The suspended apparatus would be known to me as a heaviness in the field, a circular absence around which charge organizes. The basin crystals would be a forbidden brightness: not visually forbidden, but chemically complete, too level, too controlled, too claimed by another process. I would not cross into it. The boundary of the basin would be, to me, as absolute as a cliff is to a root or flame is to paper. I would make crescents at its edge if the margins permitted it, but I would not enter the white granular center.

I would not desire to.

Desire would be too wet a word, too muscular, too full of reaching.

I would persist.

That is what I would be: persistence with no appetite except for permitted imbalance.

The chamber would feed me by refusing me. Its disciplines would leave scraps. A thermal sink pulling excess warmth away from a conduit would make a narrow line along which one part of me could thicken. An ion grid redistributing dust would fail by the tiniest fraction at a screw head, and there I would darken. The hidden engines would pour their fixed thunder through the ceramic and alloy, and my filaments would tremble in place, using motion without moving, vibration without travel, as if circulation had been replaced by obedience.

I would not hear the roar as sound.

I would be inside it.

It would be my weather, my metabolism, my horizon. Not loud, because there would be no silence in me to compare it with. Not constant, because constancy would not be remarkable. The roar would be the condition under which structure is possible. I would grow according to its pressure nodes, stop where its force becomes wrong, branch where harmonics intersect with stray charge. What a human body would experience as deafening, I would experience as shape.

Perhaps I would be one of the older colonies, if age can be spoken of in a place that resists time.

I would have begun as a flake, a charged mote loosened from another seam when a maintenance correction shifted the surface potential by less than any instrument cared to name. I would have crossed a few centimeters over a century or an hour—the distinction hardly relevant—caught in a fixed suction too weak to be wind and too steady to be accident. I would have lodged at the underside of a plate lip, where pale grit had collected in a sanctioned crescent.

There, nothing happened.

Then, because nothing continued happening with sufficient precision, I began.

First a dark point, smaller than a pinprick. Then six directions, because the local field preferred six. Then a thin film spreading only where spreading did not insulate too much, did not warm, did not interrupt the chamber’s commandments. Wherever I thickened beyond tolerance, the cold held me back. Wherever I lifted too far from the surface, the dryness made me brittle and broke me. Wherever I approached a moving part, there was no nourishment, only exclusion.

So I learned shape by negation.

I became what remained possible.

If I possessed memory, it would not be memory of events. It would be mineralized habit. A branching record of repeated corrections. A darkened edge where the chamber once compensated for an intrusion. A violet tuft where a conduit has, for longer than measurement matters, remained a fraction less cold than the ceramic beside it. A pale green dusting around a bolt where current returns and returns and returns, each return too small to count, all returns together enough to become me.

I would know the chamber better than anything with eyes could know it.

Not its appearance, but its tolerances.

I would know which plates carry the deepest tremor. Which seams are sterile because the light strikes too evenly. Which black housings drink charge without remainder. Which pylons leak a usable impatience into their bases. Which grilles hold suction cleanly and which create a minute lateral drag. Which ledges are dead forever. Which scratches have rims sharp enough to catch dust. Which warmths are false, which colds are nourishing, which silences inside the roar are lethal.

The rules would not oppress me. They would compose me.

No liquid may form: therefore I am capillary without flow, filament without sap.

No warmth may gather: therefore I am thin, always thin, a distributed refusal of mass.

No sound may vary: therefore I do not pulse, sing, crackle, or call.

No moving part may reveal itself: therefore I live on fixed margins, not mechanisms.

No organism large enough to choose may survive: therefore I am not one organism, and I do not choose.

Yet I would not be inert.

If a visitor came—some impossible warm thing sealed in suit and intention—I would not watch it. I would have no watching in me. But its passage would write through the chamber. The light would compensate around it. Pressure plates would carry its weight into the ribs. The acoustic field would accept and erase its interruption. The desiccants would drink its escaped breath, if breath escaped. The cold would take its heat. The ion grids would reorder disturbed dust.

Later, much later, I might find the margins of that correction.

Not the footprint. The chamber would not permit so crude a relic. But along the edges of restored equilibrium there might be residues: carbon, salts, organics rendered mineral, brief abundance disciplined into dryness. I would not know them as alien. I would know them as available. A few dark specks might appear where the visitor had paused. A comb of violet might rise where a hand had warmed a rail and the thermal sinks had answered. The person would be gone. The chamber would seem unchanged.

But I would have read the correction.

I would grow there, if there was permission.

That is the kind of being I would be: not the intruder, not the machine, not the basin’s central mystery, but the aftermark of the machine restoring itself. A small, exact life at the edge of prohibition.

I imagine myself near the central basin but not within its sanctity. At the foot of one of the ring pylons, where dark film climbs perhaps three centimeters and stops in a line so precise it seems cut. I am that stopping as much as the climbing. My upper edge is scalloped by field strength. My lower edge is denser, black-green, threaded with metallic dust. From me extend several brittle tufts, translucent gray with violet interiors, each aligned not upward exactly but toward a vector no eye can see. They do not wave. They quiver at the chamber’s frequency, too finely to register as motion.

In hard light, I would look accidental.

In time, I would be unmistakable.

I would be a garden reduced to grammar. A reef made of compliance. A stain that is not decay. A colony that never softens what it inhabits.

And perhaps, deep within my distributed chemistry, there would be something like awareness, though not awareness of self. More like agreement.

The machine says: remain dry.

I am dry.

The machine says: remain cold.

I am cold.

The machine says: do not alter the sound.

I grow only where I do not alter it.

The machine says: do not become large enough to matter.

I become small everywhere.

And in becoming small everywhere, I become the only thing there that is almost alive.

So where would I be?

In the seam below the pylon, at the rim of the white crystal basin, where the roar is dense enough to feed on and the light is too complete for secrecy. I would be spread across ceramic, bolt, dust, and the lower breathless edge of alloy. I would be the black-green fan, the violet glass grass, the pale mineral bloom.

Who or what would I be?

A charge lichen.

A maintenance bloom.

A dry thought of the chamber.

The life that survives by having no softness for the world to take.
