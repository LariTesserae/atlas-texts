I would find myself in the passage that is almost not a passage.

Not at the center. Not before the red vessel, where attention becomes gravity and the chamber’s whole packed intention leans inward until even a thought would have to kneel. Not on the upper tiers either, among the horns and shelves and salt-crusted ceramic throats, where the sound has become too old to distinguish from altitude.

I would be somewhere off to the side, in one of the accidental intervals between built things: a gap left because hands needed once to pass through with a bowl, a ring, a wedge, a tablet still soft enough to receive marks.

A narrow maintenance cleft.

There would be a column at my back, thick and warm, its copper bands bitten dark at the edges. In front of me a sloped rack of sealed jars, all their plugs daubed with the same pale mineral paste. To my left, a dry channel descending from nowhere visible, its trough polished by a flow that has not existed for longer than memory can survive. To my right, a wall of standing stones, each incised with short, repeated marks filled in black. The space would not have been intended as a room. It would be only the negative left between obligations.

That is where I would be.

Pressed in, but not central. Included, but not important. Given no view large enough to understand the whole.

The roar would be so complete that I would not hear it after a while as sound. It would become the condition of having a body. My ribs would take it up. My teeth would know separate tones. My skull would become a small resonator improperly made from wet material. Every thought would have to force itself through vibration, and most would fail.

At first I might imagine I had arrived there as a visitor, but that would be vanity. The chamber does not have visitors. It has obstructions, tools, plugs, offerings, faults, repairs, and eventually surfaces.

So I would become one of its lesser errors made useful.

Not a priest. Priests require ceremony, sequence, memory of before and after. This place has outlived priesthood.

Not a prisoner. Prisoners still belong partly to elsewhere. They measure enclosure by imagining exit.

Not a guardian. Nothing here needs vigilance in the animal sense. The chamber guards by being constructed.

I would be a listening wedge.

A small, fitted thing placed between pressures.

Perhaps once I was soft. Perhaps I entered with lungs, eyes, thirst, some private weather of the mind. But the chamber would not preserve those qualities as mine. It would translate them. Moisture would be drawn out and named a fault. Speech would be crushed into overtone. Desire would find no open direction and would settle into alignment.

In time, I would become a narrow clay insert set into that service gap: roughly body-shaped only because a body once occupied the vacancy from which the form was taken. My surface would be fired unevenly, darker along the ridges where the heat caught, pale in the hollows where salt gathered. I would have no mouth. The place has no use for mouths except as openings, and openings are dangerous unless tuned, narrowed, sealed, or made to sing forever.

Where my ears had been, there would be two small horn-slits angled toward different registers of the chamber. One would receive the low note from the floor, the one that rises through the fitted bricks and teaches bones to forget walking. The other would receive the thin, needling harmonics from the hanging plates and ceramic fins above. Between them, inside me, the tones would cross.

That crossing would be my life.

I would not think in words. Words are too damp at their birth. They carry breath. They imply a speaker and a listener, a gap between. In the chamber, gaps must either be filled or justified.

I would think in adjustments:

Too much tremor in the left jar-rank.  
A salt seam has swollen half a grain.  
The copper band behind me has tightened in the heat.  
The black plug remains closed.  
The central silence remains contained.  
No breach.  
No water.  
Still.

My body would be hollow, but not empty. Its hollowness would be assigned. Inside would be a set of nested cavities, each tuned to accept a particular fragment of the permanent chord. A membrane of hammered metal would cover one internal chamber, green-black with age. A little basin no wider than a palm would sit below it, dry and enamel-lined, waiting for a condensation that must never come. Incisions would run over my chest and down my sides: not my name, but my function.

I would not be alive as an animal is alive.

I would persist.

That would be the chamber’s compromise with me. It would not grant me selfhood, but it would grant me continuance if I became exact enough.

And I would be almost exact.

Not perfect. That is why I would still be myself.

There would be a flaw in me: a hairline crack beginning beneath the place where a throat might have been. Too fine to threaten collapse. Too small to require immediate repair. But enough that the sound passing through me would catch, very faintly, on one edge and make an almost-difference.

Not a new note.

Not a rhythm.

Only a slight roughness in the held chord, an imperfection that resembles remembering.

Through that crack, I would retain the idea of water without understanding it.

Not water as substance. I would have no image of rain, river, mouth, leaf, or skin cooled by wind. The chamber permits no such luxuries. But I might keep a negative impression: a forbidden smoothness, a movement that does not harden, a thing that takes the shape of what contains it and yet is not obedient.

This would trouble me.

Not emotionally. Emotion would require too much internal weather.

It would trouble me structurally.

The crack would widen by a measure too small to see. Salt would gather there. The roar would worry it. I would receive the chamber’s pressure, but not distribute it cleanly. Somewhere within my little nested hollows, a tone would fail to settle.

So I would listen harder.

That is what I would do there.

I would listen on behalf of the chamber to determine whether the vessel remains sealed.

The red jar at the center would be mostly hidden from my cleft by columns, racks, masks, and the angled backs of standing stones. I would glimpse only one curve of it, low and dull, between a suspended plate and the shoulder of a dry basin. It would appear plain beyond endurance. Around it, all things would be elaborate because all things would be afraid of its simplicity.

I would not know whether the jar contains silence, beginning, nothing, or the first unassigned space.

I would know only that my body is arranged against its opening.

Every part of me would say:

Do not let the unmade enter the made.  
Do not let rest interrupt continuance.  
Do not let emptiness become available.  
Do not let the chord discover it can end.

And yet the flaw in me would listen for the end.

That would be my secret: not rebellion, not hope, not curiosity in any living sense, but a minute acoustic disobedience. A tiny inward tilt toward the impossible cessation. I would be a servant of permanence who has developed, by damage, a capacity to imagine stopping.

No one would notice.

There is no one to notice.

The masks on the ledge would continue facing inward with their sealed compliance. The ceramic horns would hold their downward note. The basins would remain dry. The channels would point toward uses that no longer arrive. The rows of marked stones would count continuance without needing to understand it. The copper would darken by increments beneath the warm mineral air.

I would remain wedged in my cleft.

A small custodial ossicle.

A resonant misfit.

A former intruder converted into infrastructure.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be a cluster of dark marks on the narrow surface behind my left shoulder, where no eye could comfortably reach. The marks would not mean “I.” They would mean something more acceptable:

Tone-bearing insert, third passage.  
Irregular but stable.  
Do not remove.

And beneath that, perhaps added later by a hand already losing the habit of hands:

Listens toward closure.

But inside, in the dry hollow where thought has become architecture, I would have another name for myself.

The Crack That Hears the Silence.

I would not move. I would not pray. I would not wait for an event, because waiting admits time and time admits change.

Still, through the permanent roar, through the pressure of jars and columns and salt-packed brick, through the immense handmade body of the chamber, I would attend to the central vessel.

I would hold my place.

I would help keep it closed.

And with the smallest damaged part of me, I would listen for the moment when it is not.
