I would not be at the center, because the center would not exist in any animal sense. I would be where a path almost becomes a path: along the side of a condensation pool, under one of the broad roof-leaves whose ribs shine through their own flesh.

At first I think I am standing there as a visitor, with feet sinking a little into the warm woven floor. But that feels wrong almost immediately. Nothing here is foreign enough to visit. The place would not make room for a separate witness. Anything that remained long enough to notice it would have already begun to be included.

So I would be one of the pale, flattened insects on the underside of a glowing leaf.

No—too individual, too neat. That creature is almost decorative, a small punctuation mark on the luminous green sentence. It lives here, yes, but it does not explain why this place feels paused, why every surface is both body and room.

Perhaps I would be a burrower beneath the root-mats, moving without urgency through seams of warmth, my whole body an organ for pressure. I would know the world by compression and release: thick root above, wetter fiber to the left, mineral coolness somewhere below, a cistern’s weight pulling faintly through the mat. I would never see the yellow-white light directly, only feel it as heat conducted through living tissue. My passage would be a sentence the floor forgets as soon as it closes behind me.

But even that is too much like an animal life. It has direction. It has hunger. It enters and leaves.

What I would be is slower.

I would be a rim.

A low crescent of hardened root-fiber around a still pool. One of those pale forms that could be mistaken for a ruin, a knee-high wall, a foundation, a shrine made by hands that never existed.

I would have begun as a curling root that found water and thickened around it. Or not found, exactly—the plant-body would have arranged itself so that dripping, absorption, and support converged at that point. The roof above would have released its drops there for longer than memory. Each drop would strike the same water skin, make the same small rounded sound, send the same tiny rings outward until they touched me.

Over time my outer tissue would harden. Mineral would enter me. Sap would withdraw from my oldest fibers. New growth would climb and soften my edges. Moss-collars would fasten themselves to my shaded side. Fine white root hairs would drink from the pool through gaps in my body. Red floating leaves would gather in my basin. Pale larvae would live and die in the water I hold.

A traveler—if such a thing existed—might wonder whether I was built.

I would not have been built.

I would be a habit that persisted.

Not alive in the full green way of the roof, not dead like stone, not a tool, not a dwelling. A former gesture of growth made durable. A memory without mind. A piece of architecture produced by metabolism and then kept because nothing here wastes form once form has happened.

From my place I would know the world by repetition.

The overhead leaf, glowing steadily.

The heat rising through the floor.

The dry interval between drops.

The exact weight of each drop when it falls.

The slight brightening of the pool from within when suspended pollen drifts aside.

The blind swimmers touching my submerged inner wall.

The slow encroachment of cushion-growth along my back.

The pressure of roots tightening, not suddenly, but over spans too long to be called waiting.

I would not desire the water I hold. I would not protect it intentionally. Yet my shape would protect it. I would not summon the larvae. Yet they would use me. I would not mark a sacred place. Yet the chamber’s stillness would make me seem sacred, because anything circular and enduring in a world without tools begins to look like meaning.

I would be found in one of the green districts where moisture is local and precious: not beside the thread-river, where growth is too crowded and darkly vigorous, but a little away from it, in a warmer zone of waxed leaves and copper-edged blades. There, a single condensation line descends from the roof. The drops fall with ceremonial regularity, though there is no ceremony. Around them the plants have made an opening, almost a room. The floor slopes inward. The leaf-curtains hang at the threshold. A pillar-plant stands nearby, braided and glossy, carrying light in faint channels under its skin.

I would be the pale crescent at the pool’s edge, half swallowed by moss, half exposed to the steady glow.

If I had perception, it would not be vision. It would be inclusion.

I would know when the roof had taken too much water into its sacs because the drops would grow heavier. I would know when a burrower passed beneath because the mat would press differently against my underside. I would know when an insect died on the leaf above because, much later, a soft fleck would fall and be accepted by the pool-film. I would know the larvae by their minute impacts against my inner surface. I would know the plant-body’s brightness as warmth traveling through my mineralized fibers.

I would not count these things.

Counting requires before and after to separate cleanly. Here, before and after are held close together, like layers in a root mat. The place does not move forward so much as deepen in position.

Yet I would change.

That is the secret even in a world that seems paused. I would thicken by fractions. My outer edge would be overgrown, then revealed again as some soft pad withdrew into itself. Mineral would bloom faintly through me, cream and green-gray and pink, giving me the satin look of young bone. My inner curve would darken where water touched it. A rootlet would cross me and fuse, making a bridge. A larva’s generation would end; another would disturb the same surface. The pool would remain the pool. I would remain the rim. The event would be indistinguishable from the condition.

I would be a small answer to the basin’s great law: nothing hurries, but nothing is separate.

Around me, the luminous flora would continue its immense unannounced labor. The ceiling would hold up the light. The pillar would hold up the ceiling. The floor would receive what fell. The pool would hold what was rationed. My crescent body would keep the water from spreading thinly into the warm mat. Not because I chose to, but because I had become the shape that allowed that balance to persist.

So if you entered this world and asked where I was, you might not notice me at first.

You would see the glowing roof, the braided pillars, the hot springy ground, the still pool with red leaves.

Then perhaps your eye would settle on the low pale arc at the water’s edge.

You might think: foundation, relic, threshold, shrine.

You would be almost wrong each time.

I would be the basin remembering how to cup its hand.
