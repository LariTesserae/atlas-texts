I would not find myself as one of the larger intelligences. Not the octopus, though the octopus is tempting: that soft practical mind moving through failed human intentions, solving the room with its skin. Not the eel either, though there is something exact and inherited about the eel in the pipe, a creature becoming the afterlife of plumbing.

At first I think I am somewhere near the pressure door.

That trembling seam at the bottom of it draws the eye, or whatever replaces an eye here. The door is the most theatrical object in the chamber: sealed, central, corroded into importance. It suggests a beyond. It gathers tiny animals, mineral food, warmth perhaps, some faint chemical whisper from another volume of water. If I were a creature here, I might want to live at that threshold, feeding on what escapes from the closed thing, making a life from leakage.

But no. That place is too coveted. Too much passes there. Crabs come. Shrimp come when the current allows. Snails browse the stain. The door is a little marketplace, a shrine without worshippers, and I do not think I am bold enough or armored enough to belong at the obvious source of nourishment.

I am lower, and aside.

I am in the shadow under the old stair, where the landing meets the tiled wall and the diagonal structure makes a triangular darkness that never fully clears. Above me the steps climb into their flooded shaft with all the insistence of a route no one uses. Yellow safety paint remains along the stair noses in broken luminous strips, softened by slime. The handrail descends past me, barnacled underneath, green paint surviving in small islands like old administrative memory.

There is a junction box fixed beneath the landing.

Its cover has warped away from the gasket. One screw remains tight; the others have swollen in rust and lost their heads. The black slit behind the cover is narrow, irregular, and deep enough to matter. Cables enter it from above in a stiff bundle and leave through a conduit that no longer carries anything but darkness, silt, and the occasional thread of life. The box is not dead exactly. Once in a long while a tremor passes through it when some remote relay remembers itself. A faint tick. A dim warmth too slight for fish but enough for film, for eggs, for larvae, for those who live by fractions.

This is where I am.

Not inside the box entirely. Not outside either.

I think I am a small crab, but not one of the patrolling, official-looking ones that cross the grates like inspectors. I am younger or smaller, a crab fitted to margins. My shell is the color of wet rust and tile-stain, with darker freckling along the joints. A little weed is caught on one side of me and has stayed there long enough to become useful. A flake of pale sponge or old insulation clings behind one eye-stalk. I do not disguise myself intentionally in any grand way. I simply keep what the room gives me, and what remains on me becomes me.

I live under the stair because it is a good compromise.

The current reaches this place but is broken before it arrives. When the whole chamber leans under tidal pressure, the water folds around the stair supports and enters my angle in softened pulses. Sediment collects here in a low fan. After a pump coughs or the ruptured valve rattles bubbles into the ceiling, fine edible things settle from the disturbance. I come out then, sideways and precise, picking through the new dust with my claws.

The underside of the landing is roof, wall, and warning. Fish do not turn well in here. The broad mottled predator beneath the turbine cannot enter. Eels can, but they prefer holes with longer darkness, rounder darkness, pipe-darkness. Octopus arms can find me, of course. Nothing in this chamber is truly safe from an arm that can become the shape of wanting. So I know certain textures as danger: the pale muscular tasting of suckers along tile, the sudden intelligence of a shadow that touches before it commits. When that happens I wedge myself behind the lifted cover of the junction box, legs folded so tightly that I become almost rectangular, almost a component.

My world is not the whole chamber, though I know it by pressure.

The turbine beyond the wall is weather. Its boom passes through my shell and through the stair and through the old screw heads. Boom—drag—boom. When it falters, the shrimp come farther out. When it strengthens, the mussels close a little and the fish draw themselves into tighter decisions around the lights.

The lamps are distant moons to me. I do not live in their glow, but I see their consequences. Silver fish gather where the light decays into water. Their flashes move across the tiles like quick repairs. Sometimes the school breaks apart all at once, and a few bodies strike the stair rails or the wall. If one falls near enough, if it is stunned, if the others are occupied with panic, I leave the junction box and become larger than my caution.

Mostly I eat smaller things.

Film. Soft growth. Dead shrimp. The loosened threads of mussel flesh when one has been cracked by another’s hunger. A worm too slow withdrawing under blistered paint. The fine rain after turbulence. I scrape the surfaces built for hands and boots and instructions. I feed on the room’s erasure.

I know the labels, not as words but as textures.

KEEP CLEAR is above me, raised slightly under enamel and slime. I climb the sign sometimes when the current is gentle. The letters collect a different film than the flat spaces around them. The edge of the K is good. The long vertical of the L holds silt. CLEAR has a groove where a snail cleaned part of it and left a brighter path. Human warning has become grazing topography.

I do not know humans. I know absence only as room.

The stair is shaped like an invitation to bodies I have never seen. The rail is sized for a hand, but to me it is a beam, a cliff, a road with barnacles under it. The landing is a ceiling. The bolts are obstacles. The old control board across the chamber is a reef of small circular hollows. The pressure door is a place where others gather and where I go only when hunger outweighs exposure.

If I cross open water, I do it along surfaces. I descend the stair support to the wall, move from tile crack to rust veil, from rust veil to pipe bracket, from bracket to the back of a valve housing. I do not swim except as failure. I am a creature of contact. The chamber is understandable only where my legs touch it.

Still, I am not without a kind of map.

There is the warm panel that should be approached from below, because fish face the other way there.

There is the cracked tile shaped like a wide white shell, beneath which worms sometimes gather.

There is the pipe mouth with the large eel, never to be mistaken for emptiness.

There is the relay panel that clicks before the servo twitches, giving just enough warning to withdraw.

There is the grated walkway where mussels grow densely on the shaded side and where food lodges after strong current.

There is the pressure door seep, rich and dangerous.

There is my stair, my angle, my warped junction box, my sediment fan.

Perhaps I am female. Perhaps, in the dark behind the junction box cover, I carry eggs under my body, a packed orange-brown mass held close against the old machine slit. That feels right. Not a heroic inhabitant, not the symbol of the place, but a tenant engaged in continuation. I choose the box because it is narrow, because the slight remaining warmth steadies the water by a degree, because the cover makes a lip under which the eggs are less visible. When the relay ticks, I clamp down and wait. The vibration passes through the clutch. The room remembers electricity. I remember stillness.

Around me, the chamber continues its austere labor of not ending.

Water leans. Sediment lifts. Lamps bruise the green-black distance. The turbine booms beyond the wall like a heart too large for the body that contains it. Shrimp write and erase themselves in the lee of instruments. Eels breathe in the pipes. The octopus tests a panel with one thoughtful arm. The broad fish under the casing disappears back into rust.

And I remain under the stair, half hidden in a broken electrical box, wearing the room’s colors, eating its loosened matter, guarding a small future in the failed housing of an old system.

I would be found where purpose has narrowed into shelter.

A rust-colored crab beneath the flooded stair, living between a warning sign and a dead junction box, one claw on tile, one claw in silt, listening with my shell to machines that no longer know what they are for.
