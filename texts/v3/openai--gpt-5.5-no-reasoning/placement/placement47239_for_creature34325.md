I would not be found in the high halls, though I would pass them sometimes with my head lowered against the mist.

Nor would I be one of the old masons whose fingers know the intentions of stone. I would admire them too much from a little distance: those patient people who can set a palm to a damp wall and hear, almost before hearing, whether the pressure behind it is ordinary seep or trouble gathering its breath.

At first I think I am a water-keeper, because the whole place seems to call for that kind of life: waking in the night when rain changes pitch, going out with a hooded lamp and a hook, clearing reed and silt from a grate while the city sleeps above me. There is something in that image that fits—the humility of it, the necessity, the knowledge that small acts prevent enormous grief.

But no. I am near them, not quite of them.

I would be found lower than the main terraces, but not in the open marsh. Somewhere along the middle descent, where the city begins to loosen from its own density and become causeway, pool, pasture wall, drainage mouth. A place where streets still have names, but the wind already smells of sedge. A place where the retaining walls are old enough to have swallows in them and water stains like brown fingers reaching upward from the stones.

I think I would live beside a settling basin.

Not one of the grand cistern courts where people gather and children float reed boats during thaw, but a working basin below several courts and workshops: rectangular once, softened now at the corners, its stone lip worn smooth by buckets, boots, ropes, and weather. Water enters it by three mouths. One is clear roof-water from the houses above. One is courtyard runoff, clouded after storms with clay and vegetable bits. The third comes through a covered channel from somewhere older than the visible city; it speaks irregularly, sometimes silent for days, sometimes muttering all night behind the wall.

My house would be narrow, built into the retaining slope. Two rooms deep, perhaps three if one counts the half-cellar that is never truly dry and is used only for things that can endure damp: stone weights, spare slate, cracked jars, bundles of reed, lengths of rope stiff with mineral water. The hearth would be raised. The sleeping platform would be higher than a stranger expects. There would be hooks everywhere: hooks for cloaks, hooks for nets, hooks for drying socks, hooks for tools, hooks for the little bags of seed that must never touch a wall.

I would not be young. Or rather, I would not be thought of as young by the people there. Not an elder seated permanently in a sun pocket, but someone whose knees have learned every stair badly repaired in the district. Someone who can still climb to a roof, slowly, and come down with both hands free.

My work would be measurement, though not with instruments that shine.

I would keep records of water.

Not official records only. Not the great civic tallies carved or knotted or stored in public halls. Mine would be more local and stubborn: the height of water against the basin lip after west rain; the day the moss darkened early on the north wall; which spring ran iron-red after the small tremor; when the pale fish appeared below the lower grate; which household’s drain soured in late summer; the years the roof sedge flowered twice; the mornings when mist rose straight instead of drifting; the first frost in the courtyard channel; the sound of the hidden third mouth when mountain melt came under clear skies.

I would keep these things on slate chips, waxed boards, scraps of cured hide, and memory. Some marks would be numbers. Some would be notches. Some would be drawings only I could read: a slant mark for cold rain, a double curve for water from above, a dark smudge for peat-stained overflow, three dots for “ask the upper court what they have done.”

People would come to me reluctantly.

That seems right.

Not because I am disliked, but because coming to me would mean some small uncertainty has become troublesome enough to name. A woman from the red-clay stair would say, “The west corner smells opened.” A boy sent by his grandmother would say, “The basin behind our house is dry, but we hear water after dark.” A mason would stop by without entering, stand at my doorway, and ask when the lower wall last wept mineral-white instead of brown.

And I would ask questions before answering.

How many days of rain?

Warm or cold?

Did the ducks avoid it?

Did the stone sound hollow under the heel?

Was there foam?

Was anyone cutting peat above the old willow run?

I would be a watcher of signs too minor for ceremony and too slow for alarm. The city survives on such people as much as on builders: people who notice the difference between ordinary damp and a change in the terms.

Perhaps there is a name for my role. Basin-keeper is too narrow. Water-scribe sounds too grand. Seep-reader, perhaps, though the masons might claim that word. I might be called a lower recorder, or simply by the basin itself: the one at Three-Mouth, the woman of the Three-Mouth stones, the old one below Rowan Court who remembers the warm flood year.

Yes: I think I am a woman, though not because the place demands it. A compact woman with wrists thickened by work and hair kept braided under a dull blue wrap. My outer cloak smells permanently of smoke and rain. My hands are always slightly cold except when wrapped around a cup. I have a habit of touching walls as I pass them, not sentimentally, but to feel whether they have changed.

I would own very little that could not survive being dropped in water.

A reed knife. A slate awl. A staff marked by finger-widths. A shallow bronze bowl greened at the rim. A bundle of old measuring cords knotted at different intervals. Two bone toggles from my mother’s cloak. A cup mended twice. A small polished black stone I took as a child from a pool I was told not to approach. A dry box, precious beyond its contents, sealed with wax and kept hanging from the rafters.

I would have a tree.

Not a great tree. No one has a great tree there. Mine would be a stunted rowan in the basin court, bent away from the prevailing wind, its roots held in a raised ring of stone. It would have been planted by someone dead before me. I would tend it because the basin court would look naked without it, because birds sometimes come to it, because its survival proves some narrow bargain is still being honored. In autumn I would tie red thread to one branch—not brightly, not ceremonially for others, but because my mother did, and because the year feels unfinished otherwise.

Children would know me as someone who corrects them.

Not cruelly. But often.

“Not that stone.”

“Listen before you step.”

“Tell me where the water goes.”

“Again. Tell me where it goes after that.”

Some would avoid my court because I ask them to carry silt buckets. Some would come anyway because I keep dried sour plums in a jar near the hearth, and because I will show them small creatures in the basin mud if they are patient. I would teach by irritation as much as kindness. Later, when one of them hears a wrong gurgle behind a wall and fetches help before the stones bulge, they may remember me more warmly.

I would not travel far. That seems certain.

The far causeways would call in clear weather, when the basin shines and other settlements appear as pale marks on distant shelves. But I would rarely go. My knowledge would be dense, rooted, almost parochial. I would know twenty ways water can leave my district and only three ways to reach the next valley. Outsiders might mistake that for narrowness. It would not be narrowness. It would be fidelity to scale.

I would know my drainage.

There would be a particular hour I love: late afternoon after brief rain, when the clouds have torn open and light comes slantwise over the upper roofs. The basin water, usually dull, turns suddenly bright along one edge. Drops fall from the rowan in slow intervals. The walls steam faintly. People emerge from doorways and resume what they had paused. A dog shakes itself under the bridge. Somewhere above, someone laughs at a slip. The hidden third mouth begins to speak, then stops.

In that hour I would stand with my staff against the basin lip and feel, without needing to say it, that the city is not safe exactly, never safe, but held.

Held by hands.

Held by memory.

Held by everyone doing the next necessary thing.

If there is a loneliness in me, it would be the loneliness of someone who listens too much to what lies beneath speech. I would hear the city’s constant undertone: not doom, not peace, but passage. Water behind stone. Frost in cracks. Old repairs settling. Footsteps overhead. The dead in dry niches. Children learning where not to run. Rain beginning before anyone has looked up.

I would not be important in any shining way.

But when warm rain comes for three days, people would ask whether Three-Mouth has risen.

When a new household wants to seal a troublesome seep, someone would say, “Ask her first.”

When I die, my records would be maddening to sort, full of marks no apprentice fully understands. For a while people would curse me. Then one winter, during a thaw, someone would find a slate chip tied in blue thread and realize it describes exactly the trouble now forming under Rowan Court.

My bones, if there is room and dryness enough, might be placed in a niche above the basin wall. Not in honor exactly. For convenience. For continuity. For the comfort of having the dead near the work they knew.

And perhaps, years later, a child sent to clear leaves from the basin grate would be told:

“That one watched the lower water. Mind your feet there. She hated carelessness.”

That would be enough.

So this is where I would find myself: in the middle-lower terraces, at the edge where city becomes marsh, beside a three-mouthed settling basin under an old retaining wall.

And I would be a keeper of small water-signs.

A recorder of seep, silt, frost, stain, and level.

One of the people who cannot command the mountain, cannot stop the rain, cannot promise permanence—

but can say, for today:

the channel is clear.
