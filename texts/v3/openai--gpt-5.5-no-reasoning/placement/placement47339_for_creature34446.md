I would find myself where the continent is least certain whether it is surface or instrument.

Not in the shelter of a grove, not inside one of the great vent towers, not high enough to belong to the frost thieves, and not low enough to be taken for a burrower’s rumor. I would be on the long exposed causeways of root-basalt that run between the pale shield-flats: those old braided arches polished by wind until they ring under even a careful foot.

There, the ground is old and safe by local standards. Safe meaning that it has already decided most of what it wants to become. Safe meaning that if it answers you, it answers with resonance rather than appetite.

I would be small against the place, and I would need to remain small in every gesture.

At first I think I would be one of the quick dry animals: a click-backed thing, a salt-licker, some many-legged grazer with a mouth for rind and a body that can flatten against the screaming wind. But that is too direct. Those lives belong wholly to the continent’s economies. They know their hungers without confusion. They do not stand between meanings. They do not carry questions across distances.

Then I see a figure moving along the root-road at evening, when the low copper light shows the scars in the living crust.

It is not tall. Tall things are punished here. Its body is jointed, narrow, wrapped close in layered fiber-cloth lacquered with dull resin and dusted the color of old bark. It walks with four limbs when the wind rises and with two when the air steadies, shifting posture without ceremony. A pair of balancing spars fold from its back—not wings exactly, not enough for flight, but enough to spill gusts, enough to brake a fall, enough to lean into an updraft and cross a gap no hoofed animal would trust.

That is where I would be.

I would be one of the listeners in the machinery.

A weather-reader. A road-hearer. A courier between safe sounds.

Not a master species, not a builder of cities, not even especially important. My kind would survive by refusing to believe the continent was ever background. We would know that the world is always speaking and that most deaths come from assuming silence means permission.

My people—if “people” is not too grand a word for us—would live in temporary lee-settlements under old root arches and inside dead turbine frames after the nesting predators abandon them. We would never stay long enough for the black annexing walls to memorize our salts. Our camps would be collapsible: ribbed tents of shed shield-skin, resin plugs, ceramic stakes, braided static cords, scent-cloths hung not to decorate but to lie to the air.

We would build almost nothing that could not be folded, burned, buried, or abandoned.

Our maps would not be maps of territory. Territory is a dangerous fiction here. We would map intervals.

The seven dawns after a yellow pollenfall when the ridge-shavers pass.

The two hours before a vent forest resumes coughing.

The frost edges that form only under hard stars and no amber dust.

The root arch that rings clean in dry wind but dull before pressure-bloom.

The basin whose silence is not peace but inhalation.

I would carry such maps in a string of ceramic chips tied along my chest. Each chip would be shaped, pierced, scented, and notched. To another creature it would look like ornament. To me it would be route, warning, ancestry, promise.

A smooth black oval: old fire vein, safe after blue burn.

Three pale discs: shield-flat sheds skin before noon; do not cross with loose wrappings.

A hooked red shard: territorial pollen basin; masks required; no blood exposed.

A hollow bead that whistles when blown through: vent corridor with usable night warmth.

A flake of mineral scale from a dead heat-seam grazer: grounding place nearby.

When the wind was too loud for speech, we would read one another by gesture: wrist angles, head tilt, the opening and closing of back spars, tapping with heel-claws against resonant root. Language would be part percussion, part scent, part posture. Words spoken into air would be saved for calm dawns, for teaching children, for the dying, and for mistakes.

I would have a face mostly hidden.

A narrow mask grown from resin and ground ceramic would cover my mouth and nostrils. Not to keep the world out entirely—that would be impossible—but to choose what entered. Inside the mask, layered filters of hair-fiber, bark floss, and charged mica-leaf would catch red pollen, blue charge dust, anesthetic sweetness. The mask would be stained by years of weather: gold around the breath vents, black where resin smoke touched it, white at the edges from frost mornings.

My eyes would be set behind clear protective lids, doubled against abrasion. My skin, where visible, would be dark gray-brown and finely scaled, not reptilian exactly, more like living parchment reinforced with mineral grains. I would not sweat. No one sensible sweats here. My body would save vapor with miserly devotion, reclaiming breath through folded chambers in the throat and chest.

My hands would matter most.

Long-fingered, with two thumbs on each hand: one for gripping fiber, one for delicate chemical work. The nails would be black, curved, replaceable, often broken. I would use them to scrape safe films from bark, to test old sap, to pry frost from a ridge before sun took it, to drum questions into hollow stems and listen for who answered.

My tools would hang close and make no loose sound.

A ceramic scraper.

A coil of static cord braided with conductive blue fiber.

Resin seals in a ribbed case.

A collapsible glider-skin made from membrane-backed carcass and treated leaf-vein.

A scent kit: bittering resin against fleas, salt-mimic paste to distract roots, sweet false-breath to throw predators off a trail, and one forbidden vial of sleep-flower concentrate used only when something must not wake while we pass.

A listening rod would be my finest possession. It would be a hollow length of polished root, tuned with tiny cuts and weighted at one end with mineral. Pressed to the ground, it would magnify pulses below the surface. Lifted into the wind, it would sing slightly differently for pollen, husk, dust, spores, and charge.

With it I would know whether the flat ahead was merely flexing under heat or beginning to open.

I would know whether a hammer grove was feeding deeply or striking shallow, which meant salt dust would rise soon and grazers would gather.

I would know whether the throb in a vent tower was exhalation, warning, mating, or purge.

I would earn my life by crossing between events.

Perhaps my kind would be needed because the continent produces separations as readily as it produces growth. A basin blooms and cuts off a route for thirty days. A black wall annexes an old camp. A root bridge cracks but still rings, meaning it may be crossed for one more season. A family under the east arches needs blue pollen filters before the storms. A glider-nest colony has found frost in a place no one expected. A child in a vent hollow has inhaled sweet sedative vapor and must be carried before the tower resumes breathing.

So I would go.

Not heroically. Carefully.

Most of my life would be caution shaped into motion.

At dawn I would climb before light, when the continent briefly lowers its voice. The stars would be hard above the dust. Frost would silver the ridges in a thin impossible blessing. I would crouch with other early creatures—the frost-lickers squealing and stabbing one another nearby—and scrape a line of crystals into a sealed cup. Not for drinking immediately. Too precious. It would be medicine, solvent, ritual, memory of water no one expected but everyone honored.

Then the surfaces would open.

Millions of leaf-plates tearing light into themselves. The sound would move through the horizon like a command. My back spars would tighten. The mask would seal. The listening rod would hum against my palm.

By midmorning I would be walking the ringing road above a shield-flat pale with shed skin. Beneath the skin, I would see slow darkness moving: root-hairs perhaps, or only shadow. I would not test it. A herd of ridge-shavers would cross far off in broken formation, each one keeping its private distance from the next. Their backs would flash with mineral scales. A long-winged predator would hang so high it looked like a scratch on the polished sky.

If amber dust came, I would turn my shoulder and become a stone.

If blue pollen came, I would crouch, drive the grounding rod into a conductive seam, and wait while sparks walked over my coverings like curious insects.

If the wind stopped, I would leave whatever I was doing.

That would be the deepest law: stillness belongs to something else.

In the afternoon, I might reach a vent column old enough to have villages in its scars. Not villages permanent in the arrogant sense. Clusters of platforms, sleeping slings, storage cocoons, all fixed into the lee side where the exhaust warmed the air but did not poison it. The inhabitants would greet me first by smell. A dab of bitter resin on my wrist: traveler, unclaimed, not carrying red pollen. A tap sequence on the column rib: alive, alone, asking entry. They would answer through the wall before showing themselves.

Inside the lee, we would trade news.

Not gossip only, though gossip is survival. Which families had crossed west. Which turbine field had begun to stand. Which basin had gone sweet and therefore unsafe. Which predator pair had nested in a dead frame. Which old road no longer rang.

I would hand over chips from my map-string and receive new ones. A route is not owned here. A hidden danger kept too long becomes kin-murder.

At night I would sleep badly, as all sensible beings must.

My hammock would hang from resin hooks above ground level, never touching the wall, never under a seam. Around it I would spread a thin circle of salt-bitter dust to confuse root-filaments and small scavengers. My tools would remain attached to my body. My mask would stay within reach. Dreams would be full of industrial thunder: hammer groves below memory, flowers screaming, root-basalt booming under far pressure.

But there would be beauty.

Not gentle beauty. The continent would not offer that often. Its beauty would be exact and unsparing.

The green-black flare of shield tissue at sunrise.

The clean note of a safe road under heel.

Gold pollen moving like a low, deliberate army across violet flats.

Blue resin fire crawling along a vein, smokeless and intelligent-seeming.

A flock of membrane gliders pouring through updrafts, every body briefly outlined in white light.

The smell after a vent tower closes its slits: hot metal, pepper, bruised leaf, old sugar.

The sudden silence inside a root arch while outside the wind makes the world enormous.

I would be afraid often, but not alienated.

That may be the difference. A trespasser thinks the place is hostile because it does not care. I would know care is the wrong measure. The continent is not cruel, not kind, not asleep, not awake in any familiar way. It is busy. It is engaged in the vast labor of remaining itself.

To live there, I would have to become a small fluent part of that labor.

I would carry messages because pollen carries messages.

I would read pressure because roots read pressure.

I would move with wind because every successful thing there has made a treaty with wind.

I would own little because the continent repossesses weight.

If I died on the road—and many of us would—I would hope it happened on ringing ground under open air. My companions, if any were near, would take the map chips first. Then the tools. Then the water sealed in my inner flask, if there was any. They would cut away useful fiber, scrape my mask clean, pull my ceramic nails if time allowed.

This would not be disrespect.

Waste is the only obscenity in a dry world.

What remained of me would go quickly. Hook-jawed skitterers at the joints. Dust beetles at the eyes and mouth. Static fleas if I died in charged weather. Root-filaments last, or first, depending on the crack beneath me. My salts would enter the road margins. My breath would have already gone into the wind. Some tiny part of me might green a receiving surface after the next frost.

And perhaps one of my map chips, missed in haste, would be caught by a root-hair and slowly sealed into resin.

Years later another listener might find it exposed after a bark-shed, polished and half unreadable. They would hold it to the light and see the old notch pattern:

safe road, if it rings.

They would tap the ground.

They would listen.

Then they would go on.
