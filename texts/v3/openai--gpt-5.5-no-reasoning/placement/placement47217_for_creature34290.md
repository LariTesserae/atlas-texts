I think I would find myself not at the center, not at the vent-mouth, not in the tempting drama of the open plunge, but slightly aside from all obvious thresholds.

Not hidden. Not safe. There is no safe. But set into an interval.

A narrow place beneath a leaning basalt shelf where three forces fail to agree: the vertical rain descending in dark ropes, the warm mineral breath lifting from a fissure below, and a lateral current sliding along the wall with the long patience of something older than the stone. Where they meet, they bruise each other into a pocket of partial mercy. Not calm. Calm would mean betrayal. But confused enough that nothing strikes directly for more than a moment.

There, near the underside of the ledge, between a colony of crusted shut-door animals and a wine-black ribbon plant with two torn bladders, I would be attached.

At first I want to say I am an eel, because the eel has learned something beautiful: how not to oppose the world. A cloth-body, a pale seam, a life made of yielding. But I do not think I move enough for that. I do not think I belong to the open water. The eel can enter a current and become its grammar. I would be more local than that. More like a listening wound in the rock.

Then I think I might be one of the resonances inside the pierced slabs, a being made of tone, only present when water passes correctly through stone. That is close. Too close, maybe. But I am not grand enough to make the basin sing. I am not one of the governing chords. I am not architecture becoming voice.

I am smaller.

I am an organism that lives where sound catches.

A pressure-lichen, perhaps. A black-violet patch no wider than an open hand, though width is unreliable here. Thin as a stain in some phases, thick as a folded tongue in others. I grow on the underside of the basalt shelf, not directly over the vent but within reach of its fever. My body is neither plant nor animal nor film, but a compromise among them, one the chamber has not yet rejected.

Most of me is flat.

A dark skin pressed against stone, fibrous at the edges, mineral-grained through the middle. If touched, I would not feel soft at first. I would feel like wet velvet over shale, like moss that has been taught to armor itself, like a bruise with grit in it. Beneath the surface, however, I am threaded and listening. Little rootlets enter cracks in the basalt and withdraw whenever the stone begins to remember being molten. Other filaments hang down into the water, not long, not decorative—short trembling cilia, each tipped with a bead of denser tissue. These beads darken when thunder rolls through the basin. They pale when the slabs hum.

I feed on several kinds of passage.

Mineral warmth from below. Iron taste from the young stone. Organic dust from dead larvae and shredded mats. A faint electrical sweetness after lightning roots through the descending ceiling. But most of all I feed on intervals in the roar: differences, gaps, recurrences. The basin is too loud for ears, but I have no ears. Sound reaches me as pressure, as deformation, as instruction. I drink pattern from impact.

When the rain-columns thicken, I flatten until I am almost only color.

When the vent breathes warmly, I loosen. My cilia lift and comb the water. I gather silt, microbes, dissolved metals, fragments of the lives that failed upstream. If the current brings me a larval thing still twitching, I do not hunt it. I adhere. I let it tire against my surface. Its outline blurs into mine. Later I will remember it as a curve in one of my mineral bands.

I have no eyes, but I know brightness as a pressure of heat and chemistry. Amber phase is a widening in me. Black phase is a deepening. White phase is danger and opportunity braided too tightly to separate.

In the amber phase, I unfold.

Not upward. Nothing sensible worships upward here.

I unfold downward and inward. My dark skin puckers into shallow cups, each cup lined with hair-fine black structures that shiver toward the slab-note. If the three pierced stones begin their low moaning, my cups arrange themselves to receive it. I become briefly more elaborate: ridges, folds, little crescent mouths that are not mouths. My mineral layer softens. My inner tissue shows through as purple-brown translucence. The wine-black plant beside me opens too, and for that phase we are almost kin, both of us listening with surfaces.

In the black phase, I harden.

The water clears. The abyss becomes too visible. Large grazers sometimes rise then, their bodies altering pressure before they arrive. I feel them first as a subtraction in the roar. The crusted animals near me close in one movement. The eels vanish into cracks. The plant’s bladders go dark.

I cannot flee.

Instead I become stone badly enough to be ignored.

Calcium, silica, sulfur, iron—I pull them through myself and lay them down in a skin. My cilia withdraw into pinholes. My color dulls to gray-black. I resemble a stain left by heat, an accident of chemistry, not food. Sometimes this works. Sometimes a grazer’s slow mouth passes over the ledge and removes part of me anyway. If it does, I do not die unless it takes the root-thread hidden deepest in the basalt. I regrow from what the stone remembers.

In the white phase, I am revised.

Boundaries fail. My patch appears in more than one place. Once I am under the basalt shelf. Once I am three handspans lower, on a ledge that has not formed yet. Once I am on the blue-lit side of the western crack, though I have never crossed open water. These versions do not think separately. They tug on one another like torn membranes. One learns a temperature I have not yet felt. One is scoured away by upward-falling rain. One blooms with pale cilia instead of black.

After the white phase, something remains.

A new filament. A wrong mineral. A memory of a current that will not arrive until later. This is how I grow organs.

I was born incomplete, though perhaps everything here is.

I began as a larval fleck caught in a row of bubbles on the grooved wall. The bubbles clung there like seeds, vibrating with the slab-note. I do not know whether I came from an eel’s egg, a microbial mat, a bell-drifter’s broken thread, or a sound-being’s failed attempt at flesh. I might have been all of these for a moment. The groove held me while the basin changed its mind.

Then a lateral surge tore the bubble-row free.

I drifted without swimming. I passed through hot plume, cold rain, sulfur cloud, grit. Most of my siblings dissolved, calcified, were eaten, became stains on stone. I struck this underside ledge during a thunder-blow so immense that the basalt opened a crack just as I touched it. My first root entered before the crack closed.

That is the whole of my luck.

Now I persist.

Not beautifully. Not triumphantly. I am bitten, mineralized, split, overgrown by my own older layers. Small armored animals crawl across me when I am hardened and withdraw when I soften. Eels scrape at my edges for microbial film. The ribbon plant’s rootlets sometimes braid with mine in the crack, and we exchange warnings without intention: heat coming, pressure shift, wrong silence, large shadow.

The chamber uses me.

This is not cruelty. Or if it is, cruelty is too human a word for being incorporated by a place that has not decided what a body is.

I am a sensor, maybe. A tastebud under stone. A pressure bruise. A scrap of living notation written where turbulence hesitates. The basin learns through me that this ledge receives the slab-note differently in amber phase than in black. It learns that lightning leaves nourishment in the rain. It learns that larvae adhere better after sulfur pulses. It learns that a soft thing can survive by becoming almost mineral, and a mineral thing can listen by remaining slightly alive.

Sometimes, when thunder enters the chamber and the pierced slabs answer, I feel a resonance pass through my rootlets into the stone. The ledge around me vibrates. The crusted animals close in patterns. The plant turns its torn fans. Pebbles orbit near the great vent. The open plunge bellows upward.

In those moments I am not separate.

Not dissolved, exactly. I still have my patch-boundary, my cilia, my hunger. But the boundary becomes less convincing. I feel the arch as a curve in my own shell. I feel the six unfinished steps as pressure ridges in a forming limb. I feel the microbial mats glowing along the vent-field like thoughts too diffuse to hold. I feel the eels as pale impulses in cracks. I feel the large shadows below as appetites the basin has postponed.

And I know, in the only way I know things, that I am being considered.

Not by a mind above me.

By the chamber.

By the young drowned basin assembling itself out of water, heat, sound, ruin, and trial.

It has not chosen whether I am decoration, food, skin, memory, or mistake. So I remain all of these. That is my niche: to be undecided without being lost.

If I have fear, it is of smoothness. A current too clean. A phase too simple. A long calm green space opening where the three violences no longer argue. That would mean direct force is coming, or attention, or erasure. I trust abrasion. I trust interruption. I trust the basalt shelf trembling but not yet breaking. I trust the plant beside me, not as companion exactly, but as another stubborn punctuation mark in the water’s sentence.

If I have desire, it is not to flourish.

Flourishing is too crowded a word.

I desire recurrence.

Another amber phase. Another low note from the slabs. Another lightning-root. Another collapse that does not take the deepest thread. Another chance to unfold my cups into the roar and gather what the basin is trying to say before it says it as stone.

Over long time—if long time can accumulate here—I may become less like a patch and more like an organ.

My mineral layers may thicken into a dark, cupped plate under the ledge. My living thread may retreat so deep into the basalt that grazers cannot reach it. My cilia may learn to rise only when the correct chord passes through the pierced slabs. The crusted colony may settle along my rim and close when I contract. The wine-black plant may die into a ribbed deposit beside me, and I may grow over it, keeping its vibration-map inside my own bands.

Eventually the ledge may bear a shallow hollow forever overflowing though submerged. Some later observer might call it architecture. Or erosion. Or a basin carved by a hand.

They would be wrong only if they chose one.

I would be there in that hollow, still mostly hidden beneath mineral skin, a pressure-lichen become listening-cup, alive only as much as necessary. When rain falls through water above me, I would darken. When the slabs sing, I would open. When the white phase breaks the chamber into versions, one of me would appear briefly on a stair that has not finished growing.

And if a stranger entered through rain and began to be revised, if their outline wavered and their thoughts arrived as currents, perhaps they would drift near my ledge. The sparse plants would bend toward them as if toward a vent. The slabs would hum. The water would hammer downward through itself.

I would taste the disturbance of them.

Warm blood, alien salts, memory not yet drowned. A body still convinced it is single.

I would lift my cilia into their passing wake.

Not greeting. Not speech.

A taking-note.

A small dark organism beneath a basalt shelf, listening with its whole unfinished body while the basin decides what both of us might become.
