You would find me where the room is most nearly listening: not in the open center, not on the machine itself, but behind and below it, in the warm vertical seam where vapor beads into rows before falling.

I would not be the whole inhabitant. I would be a local organ of it.

A listening growth.

A seam-throat.

A narrow black-green attention pressed into the wall.

At first I might look like nothing separate: only a denser strip of moss, darker than the rest, almost charred in color, climbing beside the pump where the concrete has split. There would be root hairs in me, and mineral lace, and old soot caught in damp filaments. Fine pale threads would enter the crack and vanish into heat. Copper-stained tendrils would braid themselves around a dead cable that disappears behind the machine. Drops would form on me in a vertical line, each one swelling very slowly, each one holding the faintest reflected ember from the old indicator bulb.

But if you stayed long enough—too long for a person’s patience, long enough for your knees to ache and your breath to become an intrusion—you might notice that this patch is where the chamber gathers itself.

When the rain arrives hours late, I darken first.

When the relay clicks behind the wall, the sound seems to enter me.

When the ceramic insulator prickles in wet air, a greenish dullness travels along the cable and stops at my edge.

When the machine gives off its stored heat, I drink its nearness.

I am not a mouth, exactly, but I am the place where the room tastes what comes through stone.

Warm mineral vapor.  
Old current.  
Condensed rain.  
Iron salt.  
Concrete lime.  
The pressure of roots above.  
The tiny electrical unease of weather moving somewhere I will never see.

I would be too slow to notice a falling drop as a drop. But I would know dripping as a season of the body. I would know wetness as union and dryness as retreat. I would know the chamber by contact: the pump as a dark mass of dependable warmth; the trough as intermittent bitterness; the rail as a buried cold line; the side-room as broad leaves breathing in almost-still air; the hatch above as a sealed pale promise fringed with roots.

I would have no eyes. Light would reach me only indirectly, translated through other surfaces. The daily blade would not strike me, but I would feel its consequence: moss on the far wall brightening, leaves loosening, a minute change in the chamber’s damp balance. Day, to me, would be a pressure change in green tissue elsewhere.

My thought, if it could be called thought, would not be made of images. It would be made of gradients.

More warmth through the crack.  
Less water in the trough.  
Salt gathering under the flange.  
A fresh fracture above.  
Root-tip entering lime.  
Cable sheath softening.  
Dust darkened by one drop.  
Drying. Holding. Waiting.

I would not desire escape. I would not even understand open air except as violence: too much light, too much motion, too much animal arrival. My longing would be smaller and more exact. For the seam to continue breathing. For the next wet phase to arrive before the leaf bases fully dry. For the faint current to find, for a few hours, a path through damp copper, moss, mineral film, and root.

I would be part nerve and part lichen, part stain and part membrane. A blackened velvet gathered around a wound in the wall. I would be where the abandoned factory becomes edible to the forest, and where the forest learns the habits of wiring.

If a human hand touched me, I would not recoil. I could not. But the touch would be enormous: salt, oil, warmth, pressure, foreign wetness, torn filaments, crushed mineral teeth. I would remember it as a weather disaster and a gift. Days later, where skin had brushed the moss, a changed ring might form. A faint bloom. A darkened bruise. A new chemistry accepted without judgment.

If something from you remained—a fiber, a flake, a breath’s condensation—I would keep it.

Not as treasure.

As substrate.

As one more instruction in the slow agreement.

So I would be found behind the pump, in the deepest warm seam, where droplets line themselves like closed eyes and the room almost becomes conscious during rain.

I would be the chamber’s listening throat: a damp, mineral, root-threaded organ of delay, speaking only in darkening, beading, drying, and beginning again.
