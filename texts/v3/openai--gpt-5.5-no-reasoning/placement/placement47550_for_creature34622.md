You would find me where the path fails politely.

Not lost, exactly—this is not a country that permits the luxury of being lost in a dramatic way. It corrects you before that. It bends the fern one direction and then another. It softens the moss under your leading foot. It lets a tied branch rot through at the moment you most need certainty. It offers an animal track, then a root-corridor, then a polished crossing whose shine may be from hooves or rain or something dragging its belly in the dark.

So I would be in one of those uncertain places: between human use and animal use, between the open basin and the old living halls, where a trail has almost become a root and a root has almost become a trail.

I think I would not be one of the large presences first imagined from a distance. Not the cat in the vertical garden, not the bear prying open the plateau with its thoughtful claws, not the under-mat thing whose name is avoided. Those belong too strongly to hunger. Nor would I be the plateau itself, though it tempts description toward that—the great vegetal mindless memory, the patient archivist of pressure and rot. That is too broad, too slow, too complete.

I would be smaller. Human, probably. But not settled in the way a villager is settled, not tied entirely to one shelter or one lineage of granaries. I would be someone whose work is passage.

A path-keeper, though the word would be misleading if it suggested ownership.

I would know the routes that do not last unless attended to. I would move between the small human sites: the hollow buttress where a family returns during fruiting weeks; the three high granaries now being entered by pale roots; the ridge of standing stones with cup-marks full of rain; the fern basin where the canids hunt too well in pairs; the root-bridge that should not be trusted until the browsers have crossed it after a storm.

My tools would be light and repaired too many times to be beautiful: a stone blade in a bark sheath; a thornwood hook; cord wrapped around the wrist; a little packet of resin and gray clay for sealing cut vines; a bone awl; a sling worn soft in the hand. Perhaps one inherited metal thing, not a knife but a copper needle or narrow chisel, green at the edges, used only when stone would bruise the work.

I would not cut a path as a road-maker cuts a road. I would persuade one to remain passable.

A branch tied aside here.  
A thorn bent and grafted into its neighbor there.  
A stepping stone levered upward from sucking moss.  
A root scraped clean so a foot can find it in rain.  
A water-vine marked with a twist of fiber to show it has been opened and sealed.  
A deadfall left in place because the spotted cat uses the higher limb, and better to know where the cat prefers to pass than to force it into uncertainty.

My body would have become an instrument for judging ground.

Not strong in any heroic manner. Strong like a handbasket, like cordage, like something repeatedly wet and dried and not yet broken. Feet broad from use, toes gripping root-bark without thought. Calves scarred by sedge cuts. Palms crosshatched from fiber, resin, stone, thorn. A shoulder permanently shaped by carrying loads that must not swing. Eyes used less for looking at grand distance than for finding interruption: a new scrape on bark, dung too fresh near a crossing, fern bent against the wind, ants abandoning a vine, birds complaining from the wrong height.

I would be quiet, but not silent. No one living there could afford the fantasy of silence. I would click my tongue before entering a root-hall, not to announce myself to spirits exactly, though others might say so, but to let snakes and small mammals choose withdrawal. I would hum at certain crossings in rain, a low pattern more felt than heard, because startling a browser herd on a bridge is a form of stupidity. I would speak to children sharply about flower-cups with red rims. I would speak to old stones more gently.

The standing stones would matter to me.

Not as monuments. More as agreements still standing after the people who first made them had been taken into the mat. I would clean their cup-marks with my thumb. I would pull seedlings out of the rain hollows but not strip away all moss, because a naked stone in that place is an insult and a beacon. I would leave water where a ridge is dry. I would know which stone means crossing, which means death, which means shelter nearby, which means do not sleep here no matter how tired you are.

The plateau would have taught me that memory is not what remains unchanged.

Memory is what is maintained against being eaten, and also what is allowed to be eaten properly.

A path is memory underfoot.  
A scarred vine is memory of thirst.  
A bridge polished by hooves is memory of weight.  
A shelter entered by roots is memory returning to its older household.  
A stone blade lifted with root grown around its edge is memory caught in the act of becoming ground.

I would sleep high when I could: in a platform inside an old buttress, or in a sling-hammock hung between living ribs, with smoke kept small and bitter leaves crushed around the cords to discourage certain climbers. On bad nights I would wake before knowing why, because the mat below had shifted with a pressure that was not rain, not wind, not herd. Then I would hold still and listen through the roar.

That would be one of my gifts: not sharper hearing, but trained subtraction.

To live there is not to hear everything. Everything is too much. It is to remove the expected noises until the remaining shape appears.

The cicada scream belongs.  
The drip from hanging roots belongs.  
The frogs after rain belong.  
The bark knock of beetles belongs.  
The cough-bark of canids belongs, unless it comes from below rather than across.  
The birds’ alarm belongs, unless all species stop at once.  
The root-bridge creak belongs, unless it answers itself from underneath.

I would know absence as a sound.

I might carry messages, but not often in words alone. More often in alterations: three knots on a hanging cord to warn that bears have opened a bromeliad stand; a broken fern bundle tied upside down where canids have denned; fresh clay on a vine to show drinkable water; a sliver of white quartz set in moss to mark a safe turn in mist. My language would include speech, yes, but also maintenance.

Other people might call me when a bridge begins to sag, when a shelter must be re-lashed, when a child is ready to learn the route to the cup-stones, when a body needs to be placed in a hollow trunk without offending the insects that will finish the work. I would be useful at edges: between dry and wet seasons, between one band’s range and another’s, between human path and animal corridor, between the living and the taken-back.

Not a priest, though I might perform acts that look priestly.

Not a hunter, though I would hunt when necessary.

Not a builder, because nothing there is built once.

A keeper of temporary permissions.

And what would I be, if not simply human?

Perhaps I would be one of those people whose identity has partly crossed into the work. The plateau would have given me a name that is also an instruction: Moss-Under-Foot, Root-Listener, Cup-Stone, She-Who-Ties-Back-Thorns, He-Who-Waits-for-Hooves. A name not inherited but noticed.

I like Root-Listener.

Not because roots speak in any sentimental way, but because they report pressure, rot, water, animal passage, failure. A root under tension has one sound in wind and another before tearing. A hollow root-bridge has a different warmth at dawn. Living wood takes a cut differently from dead wood pretending. If you put your palm to certain old buttresses, you can feel bats shifting inside, larvae working, rain descending through internal channels, perhaps even the slow creep of growth around some forgotten human thing.

Root-Listener, then.

I would be found kneeling beside a half-swallowed path after afternoon rain, pressing clay into the wound of a cut water-vine someone opened badly. Around me the insects would already have begun to discover the sweetness. Small birds would be arriving, pretending not to be interested. Somewhere farther off a browser would snort at the smell. I would work quickly, not angrily at first, but with the weary irritation reserved for preventable trouble.

My hair would smell of smoke and crushed fern. My arms would be freckled with old bites. There would be a scar along one calf from a root-hole collapse, and another pale mark at the wrist where a snake struck and taught me a rule I had been told but not yet believed. I would carry no ornament that clicked. Nothing bright except perhaps a bead made from a runner bird’s discarded display-stone, tucked into cord at the throat—not decoration only, but memory of someone who gave it.

I would know the cliff-dwellers by individual malice. One with a torn ear. One that always approached from below the drying racks. One old female who understood slings too well and taught the young to wait until hands were occupied. I would throw stones near them rather than at them unless hunger had made the argument severe. I would distrust them, admire them, and occasionally laugh despite myself.

The bears I would treat like weather with intentions.

The cats like mistakes already waiting.

The canids like neighbors who understand too much.

The under-mat thing I would not joke about after sunset.

As for the plateau itself, I would not worship it. Worship implies distance. I would live inside its consequences. I would resent it when it swallowed a tool, thank it when a vine held my weight, curse it when thorns closed a route overnight, trust it only after testing, and know that every human arrangement upon it is provisional. It does not hate. It does not love. But it accepts, incorporates, and alters. That is more intimate than either.

At evening I would pause on a low granite dome where the living mat thins over the mountain’s old back. That would be one of the few places where the buried foundation can still be touched directly. Warm stone under the palm. Lichen rough as old skin. Fern basins below, honey-lit, sounding with insects. A colossal tree far off casting its village-sized shadow. Smoke from a human fire almost invisible beneath a living wall. Rain approaching from the west as a gray moving seam.

I would look over the basin not as a conqueror, not even as a guide, but as someone checking the mood of a room before entering.

Where are the birds turning?  
Where have the browsers bunched?  
Which bridge has gone dark with wet?  
Why is that hollow quiet?  
Has the cloud climbed fast enough to bring frogs before sundown?  
Will the path still be itself tomorrow?

Then the rain would arrive.

The plateau would vanish into impact and voices. Leaves hammering, frogs brightening the air, insects shifting pitch, animals moving unseen through veils of water. The path would become smell and memory. My own skin would bead and run. Clay would soften in its packet. Cord would swell. The stone beneath me would darken but hold.

That is where I would be: on the high living skin of the buried mountain, in the ordinary hard light and the unbearable sound, belonging only by attention.

And I would be Root-Listener: a human maintenance-creature, a negotiator of paths, one of the small temporary beings who keeps passage open without ever believing it belongs to them.
