I would find myself close to the seep line, where the bank darkens without quite becoming wet.

Not in the open center of the basin at first. The center is too exposed, too plainly itself: the cracked clay plates, the shallow polygons, the dry flats holding every past withdrawal of water in softened lines. Nor would I be among the three upright stones, though they would draw the eye. They have the dignity of things that have stood too long to explain themselves. They are presences, but not the one I would be.

I would be lower.

At the meeting of damp and dry.

A place no wider than a palm, perhaps, where a thin run of seepage has touched the foot of the clay bank often enough to make a narrow, persistent stain. There, under a slight overhang where condensation gathers during the cooler hours, the surface would look only marginally different from the rest of the basin: a deeper brown, a smoother skin, a dull slickness if pressed by a finger. Around it, mineral dust would pale into a faint rim. A few red-jointed stems would lie close to the mud, their round leaves flat and small, not flourishing but continuing. The roots beneath them would be finer than hair.

That is where I would be.

Not a creature with a den. Not something with eyes waiting to open. Not something that could be startled into motion.

I would be a crust.

A living skin of wet-season darkness on clay.

Most of the time I would appear to be nothing: a stain, a subtle discoloration, a slightly more cohesive film along the seep’s edge. During dry intervals I would draw myself inward until I was nearly indistinguishable from dust. My body would not retreat to a shelter; it would become its shelter. I would flatten, seal, dull, and wait. The warm dim air would pass over me. The pools would shrink. The clay would crack wider around me and then soften at the edges again. Fallen grass would bleach beside me. The ankle-high wall would slump by a thickness too small for any visitor to notice.

Then water would return.

Not dramatically. Not rain in sheets. A darkening first. A thread. Moisture moving through the clay by old routes, filling the known hollows, touching my edge.

I would wake by softening.

No limb would unfold. No head would lift. Instead, the sealed flecks of me would loosen. Filaments finer than sight would take up water. Cells would swell. The dark film would deepen, not enough to turn green, only enough to make the mud look more awake. I would begin again my small chemistry: taking in mineral traces, old leaf rot, dissolved salts, warmth, the faint organic memory of stems that fell and stayed where they landed.

I would not think in a human way. Thought would be too sharp, too separate. I would know only gradients.

More damp here.

Less salt there.

Shade under the clay lip.

A little nutrient near the dead grass.

Too dry beyond the pale ring.

Water thinning.

Surface closing.

Wait.

My world would be measured in contact. Clay beneath, air above, water passing through. The upright stones would matter only by the coolness gathered at their bases, by the trickle they diverted, by the silt collar where another small life might briefly thicken. The ruined wall would be a ridge of altered weather: one side holding shade a little longer, one seam catching a seed, one soft corner returning to the basin grain by grain.

I might not be one organism. It would be more accurate to say I would be a small agreement among many lives: algae, bacteria, dormant spores, mineral-tolerant threads, a darkened slick that becomes active when permitted. A community so intimate it looks like a surface. A being made of plural patience.

I would have no territory except recurrence.

Where the dampness comes back, there I am.

Where it fails for too long, I become memory in the crust, then dust, then perhaps nothing distinguishable.

I would not mourn this. In that basin, survival is not insistence. It is timing. It is reducing oneself to what can endure. It is accepting that presence may look like absence for most of the year.

If a person crossed the hollow, they might step near me without seeing me. Their foot would compress the clay with a dry softness, then pull slightly where the ground held moisture. If they knelt and touched the darkened line, their fingertip might come away with a faint slickness, a smell of mud and warm stone. They might think: algae, damp clay, nothing much.

They would be right.

And still, that would be me.

I would be one of the basin’s lowest flames: banked life, nearly mineral, old in habit. I would belong to the interval between wetting and drying. I would have no desire to leave because leaving would be a category of life unlike mine. The basin would not be my home in the sense of a place I occupy. It would be the shape by which I exist.

Clay for skin.

Seepage for pulse.

Evaporation for breath.

Dormancy for sleep.

A dark film at the edge of a shallow pool, waking only enough, fading before it is noticed, returning when the water remembers the hollow again.
