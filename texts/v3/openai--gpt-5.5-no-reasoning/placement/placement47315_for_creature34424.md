I would find myself on the lower margin of the rookery basin, not in its center where the white guano crust shines faintly under the leaked light, and not high among the ledges where the birds have made the air impossible, but along the broken edge where the floor begins to lift into dry shelves.

There is a place there where fallen limestone slabs lean against one another without quite making a cave inside the cave. A low shelter, accidental, sharp-edged. Above it, roots hang from a crack too narrow to see into. Below it, a shallow runnel passes under gravel and emerges only as a tremor in sand. The rookery is close enough that feathers collect in the hollows, but far enough that the heaviest filth falls elsewhere. It is a border-place: dry enough for fur, damp enough for insects, open enough to flee, enclosed enough to sleep.

That is where I would be.

At first I want to say I would be one of the tool-users, because the place invites a consciousness that moves carefully through it, naming cups of water and dangerous crusts, carrying bone and cord and memory instead of architecture. But the longer I stand in the description, the less I seem upright. Uprightness is too exposed here. It belongs to crossing, not belonging. A person passes through this world. A person must keep deciding. The place itself favors those who do not decide in language.

Then I become smaller.

Not insect-small. Not one of the pale shrimp sealed in a hand-deep pool, though there is something tempting in that: an entire ancestry contained in a dark cup of water, a world whose ceiling is the trembling skin of the pool. But I am not that enclosed. I know passages. I know dry stone and wet stone. I know the smell of fox before its shadow moves.

I think I am one of the root-running mammals.

A shrew-like thing, perhaps larger than a shrew, smaller than a rat. Long-bodied, narrow-headed, with fur the color of damp ash and clay. My eyes exist, but they are not trusted first. They are small, dark, bead-wet, useful for distinguishing paleness from depth, motion from stone. My true face is whisker and nose. Long white whiskers sweep the air ahead of me, bending against shale edges, feather shafts, root fibers, the cold surface of bones. My ears are thin and half-hidden in the fur, always taking in the rookery thunder, the bat-click weather, the minute ticking of beetle legs.

I am not noble. Nothing here is noble for very long. I am hungry almost always, and my intelligence is made of hunger arranged into caution.

My forefeet are quick, with fine claws for pulling apart guano crust, shredding fungus-softened feathers, opening the packed nests of larvae under droppings. My hindquarters are stronger than they look. I can dart, flatten, reverse inside a crack, climb a root cord, vanish under a slab that seems too tight even for breath. My tail is not beautiful: pale, flexible, sparsely furred, used for balance when crossing wet limestone lips over pools.

I live in the leaning-stone shelter with others of my kind, though “with” is too generous. We tolerate one another by warmth and kinship and habit. Our nest is made of feathers, dry moss, root hair, and the chewed inner bark of roots descending from above. It smells intensely of ourselves: milk, musk, guano dust, old blood, damp fur. There are several exits, because any den with one mouth is already a trap.

The roof above us belongs to wings. The floor around us belongs to mouths.

I would know the underland as a map of nearnesses.

The sweet pool is twelve body-lengths downslope, under a lip of mineral crust where a fern grows sideways and sheds bitter spores. The pool with fish is farther, and dangerous, because snakes wait there when the thin light has lain on the stones long enough to warm them. The place of beetles is beneath the black shale fan. The place of eggs is upward and white and loud, reachable only when the birds rise together and confusion opens a brief road among shadows. The place of owl-death is marked by pellets: gray knots of fur and bone beneath a high ledge. I avoid it unless starvation has made me bold, because owls return to where they have succeeded.

I would not understand constancy as a concept. I would live inside it as trust.

The drip falls there.

The gravel trembles there.

The bats loosen from the ceiling before the air changes taste.

The birds return in a violence of sound, and afterward eggshells and nest-filth fall.

The fox passes through the lower channel when the rookery quiets.

The root above the den beads with water after unseen rain.

The same things happen, not safely, but reliably. Reliability is the nearest thing to mercy.

My days would not be days. They would be intervals between noises.

When the rookery is at its fullest, I stay low. The air is too crowded with falling things: droppings, feathers, broken shells, the occasional chick not yet dead. A fallen chick is a fortune and a danger. Its cries draw everything. Beetles arrive first as a dark seam in the whiteness. Then other small mammals. Then sometimes a snake, or the fox, or an owl dropping like a thought from above. If I find such a body early, I bite quickly at the soft places, take what I can carry, and run with my muzzle red.

When the birds thin, I range farther.

I move along the line where guano gives way to bare stone, where nutrients spill but predators cannot see cleanly. I dig at damp crusts. I eat fly larvae, beetle grubs, seeds softened in droppings, translucent worms, the eggs of insects, sometimes the eggs of birds if I can break them. I gnaw old bones for mineral. I chew fern rhizomes when nothing else presents itself. I drink by licking condensation from stone rather than lowering my head too long at open water.

Everything tastes of limestone.

Everything smells layered: bat musk over old feathers, fox over wet clay, snake over warm dust, owl over pellet-dry bone, root-sap over mold, rain from above passing briefly through all of it.

I am prey more often than predator, but not helpless. My defense is interruption. I am shaped for a world of cracks. The large hunters need continuity: a clear pounce, a straight drop, a swallowable angle. I survive by denying them those things. I break my path. I run under stone, over stone, through root, across guano, into water if I must, out again by a different edge. I leave scent everywhere and nowhere. I freeze when the air pressure changes under wings.

The owl is the great erasure.

It comes without sound. The rookery may be screaming, bats may be clicking, frogs may be pulsing from the clay hollows, and still some deeper part of me knows when silence has taken the shape of a face. Then I press myself flat. I become shale. I become old fur caught under a rock. If I move, I become food.

The fox is different. The fox is smell before body. Damp hide, meat, den, the sharpness of teeth. It noses and listens. It can wait at one exit, but not all. It is clever in a way nearer to mine, which makes it worse. Sometimes it digs. Sometimes it pretends to leave. Sometimes it leaves and returns from an angle I had not expected. I fear the fox personally. I fear the owl as weather.

Snakes are patience made into muscle. I fear warm stones.

If I breed, I do so in the dry season above, though I do not know it as season. I know it as fewer roof-drips, thicker guano crust, more brittle feathers, sweeter seeds washed down less often but preserved longer in cracks. My young are born blind in a nest of stolen bird down. They make small wet sounds. Their bodies are pink-gray, almost rootlike. For a while I become more dangerous because I must return again and again with food, and repetition is how predators learn you.

Some of the young vanish. This is not tragedy in the human sense. It is the arithmetic of the place. A centipede takes one. A snake takes another when it first follows me beyond the slab. One weakens and is eaten by its siblings or by me, because nothing here remains outside use. Two live long enough to learn the difference between loose guano and stone, between beetle-click and dripping water, between my warning chirr and the ordinary nervous muttering of the den.

I would have no myth of the surface. But I would know its leakages.

A drowned leaf is a rare object, broad and fragrant with impossible green decay. Seeds arrive after rains. Sometimes an insect from above falls through a shaft, bright-bodied, too soft, tasting of sun-fed plants. Sometimes cold air descends differently and every animal shifts. Roots thicken and thin. Birds bring upper weather in their feathers. The surface is not a world to me, but a source: food, danger, water, unfamiliar scents.

The tool-users, if they come, are enormous intermittent animals.

I know them by smoke first. Smoke is frightening because it changes the air in a way no body can explain. Then comes hide, sweat, old blood, stone dust, crushed fern. Their feet are too heavy but often careful. They carry light, and light is wrong when it moves sideways. I watch from cracks. They turn stones and expose grubs. They climb toward eggs. They leave scraps sometimes: a shred of sinew, a flake smeared with fat, a dropped seed, a piece of cooked meat hardened at the edge. I do not worship them. I raid them.

If one of them dies here, the cavern will accept that body as readily as a bird.

First the flies, then beetles, then mammals, then damp, then bone. Intention will leave sooner than smell.

I think my body would fit the law of the place: temporary, alert, useful after death. I would not transform the underland. I would help it continue. I would carry seeds in my gut and fail to digest some. I would drag feathers into cracks. I would open guano crusts to air. I would feed snakes, owls, foxes, mites. My tunnels in the soft banks would hold moisture and collapse and be used by insects. My nest, abandoned, would become a warm province of fleas and mold and larvae. My bones would whiten under the rookery dust.

I would be one of the small citizens of the recycling hunger.

Not a ruler, not a builder, not a symbol. A gray, sharp-nosed underland scavenger with listening skin and a heart fast as dripping water is slow. I would live where the rookery’s waste thins into bare limestone, in the accidental shelter of young broken stone. I would know the vast buried country not as vastness, but as a chain of edible, dangerous, repeatable places.

I would be a creature of the edge between noise and hiding.

When the far birds lift all at once and the sound rolls through the chambers like surf trapped in stone, I would freeze with one paw raised, whiskers forward, mouth full of beetle. The pool nearby would tremble. The fern would bend under condensation. A bat would drop and vanish overhead. The roar would pass through my ribs.

Then it would settle.

And I would lower my nose again to the damp mineral floor, searching.
