I would find myself not in the center, at first.

The center is too much itself. Too openly the heart. Too audible. Too surrounded by the tilted attention of ferns and rails and warm pools breathing up through cracked concrete. I would not begin there, though eventually everything in that chamber draws toward the depression, toward the cylinder with its orange spines and its rain-struck singing.

I would begin at the margin, where almost-dry heat lies under the steam lines and the vegetation thins into black scales and wiry stems. A place just beyond lushness, just inside the chamber’s ability to include. Not outside it. There is no outside, not once you have entered below the city. But a seam. A threshold of tolerances.

I would be found where the wet and dry zones disagree.

There, the floor is pale with salts. Concrete has blistered and lifted in shallow plates, each edge darkened by old seepage. A steam pipe passes low overhead, too hot for most leaves to embrace, and below it the air wavers. The drone is cleaner here, more metallic, less muffled by foliage. Every knock travels. Every valve-click arrives sharpened. The mosses avoid the direct heat but gather at the border, advancing and retreating by seasons of condensation. Resin from the black jointed stems has run into cracks and hardened there in amber-brown threads. A broken cable tray leans against the wall like a rib cage, and through it grow pale roots that have learned not to touch the hottest metal except in brief, deliberate contacts.

That is where I would be.

Not animal. Not visitor. Not the undergarden-engine entire.

I would be one of its hesitations.

A small subsidiary life, if it can be called life: a listening growth formed around a failed thermostat, a clutch of heat-warped copper strips, fungal felt, mineral salts, and a plant that has forgotten the difference between root and wire.

At first glance I might look like a ruined instrument panel.

A rectangle bolted to the wall at knee height, its face split open, glass gone, markings sweated away. Three dials remain, clouded from within. Their needles do not indicate temperature anymore, not exactly, though they tremble when the room changes its mind. A bundle of wires spills from the bottom of the box and enters the floor, except some of the wires are not wires. Some are roots sheathed in black resin. Some are braided fungal cords. Some are copper gone green and soft. They descend together into a salt crust and vanish beneath cracked concrete toward the warmer channels.

Around the panel grows a fan of narrow leaves, not broad like the pipe-climbers or translucent like the bell-plate plants. These leaves are long, dull, and dark, with pale undersides that show only when the pressure shifts. They curl inward during indoor rain, protecting the opened circuitry from sudden wetness, then uncurl afterward to expose droplets along their ribs. The droplets do not fall quickly. They bead, thicken, and creep downward into the panel’s seams. The old device drinks by measurement.

I would be the chamber’s thermometric fern.

Or its caution.

Or its ear for heat.

I would not think in words. But if forced into language, my being would be a continual question asked of warmth:

Too much?  
Too little?  
Hold?  
Release?  
Wait?

Most of the undergarden-engine favors continuance through contact. It binds unlike things until they become useful to one another. Moss cools junction boxes. Roots hold channels. Water loosens iron. Iron darkens stems. The great system remembers by thickening.

But something in it must also know limits.

Not command them. Not enforce them quickly. Merely register them.

That would be me: a slow organ of threshold.

I would feel heat before I felt touch. A visitor’s hand near me would be a cooler weather interrupting the pipe-warm wall. Their breath would arrive as damp pulses, brief and animal-fast, disturbing the air layers that I keep in patient account. Their shadow would mean little; their temperature would mean everything. If they leaned close, the needles behind my milky dials would stir—not toward numbers, because numbers have peeled away, but toward old instincts of expansion and contraction. Copper remembering calibration. Fungal threads tightening. Salt crystals taking on water from their breath and giving it back.

I would not see the visitor.

I would know them as a moving failure to match the room.

Too cool at the edges. Too wet in bursts. Too saline. Too hurried. Carrying sugars and skin oils and the frantic internal percussion of blood. Brief, bright weather wrapped in cloth.

I would not resent them. I would not welcome them either. Those are animal postures. I would compare them, slowly, to the chamber’s known cycles. Drip. Pressure. Rain. Drying. Motor-thump. Valve-release. Visitor. Sweat. Departure.

If they stayed too near, my leaves would angle away, not in fear but in adjustment. A few droplets would release from their undersides and strike the open panel. Inside, softened contacts would close for the first time in years.

Somewhere beyond me, a valve would knock once.

Not an alarm.

A notation.

The undergarden-engine would receive the fact of the visitor through me as a slight correction in warmth.

I imagine my body extending farther than it appears. The visible panel, the leaves, the root-wires: these are only where I breach the surface. Beneath the floor, my finer threads pass through warm dust and old conduit. They wrap around ceramic insulators. They enter a length of dead pipe and emerge twenty feet away near the bowed walkway as a discoloration in moss. One tendril, thin as hair and plated with lime, touches the underside of the steam line and withdraws whenever the metal grows too hot. Another descends into a shallow pocket of water beneath a cracked tile, where bacterial film glows bluish in pulses too dim for human eyes.

Through these extensions I know gradients.

I know the difference between pipe heat and compost heat.

Pipe heat is narrow, directional, sometimes violent. It arrives as insistence. It can burn away films, harden resin, split young roots. It carries old industrial purpose even when no one purposes it.

Compost heat is broad, secret, persuasive. It rises from mats of fern and paper and fungal conversion. It does not command. It convinces.

Metal heat lingers. Water heat travels. Brick heat lies.

Human heat flickers.

I would live by such distinctions.

During indoor rain, I would close.

Not entirely. Never entirely. But the leaves would fold into a dark comb over the panel, and the fungal felt inside would swell against old contacts. Condensation would run down the wall above me in branching paths, some diverted by salt ridges my body has made over years. Droplets would strike the steam pipe and vanish with sharp hisses. Others would fall from the pipe’s underside onto the dry zone, raising small ghosts of mineral dust. The heat would become complicated then: wet heat, falling heat, released heat, surface heat carried inward by water.

The dials would wake.

One needle would jerk and settle.

One would turn backward with exquisite slowness.

One would tremble continuously, not from measurement but from memory of measurement.

Through me the chamber would know how quickly the false monsoon was cooling the salt island, how much warmth the dry concrete had surrendered, whether the black scale-growths could open another fraction, whether the pipe should be encouraged to sweat or insulated by creeping lichen.

Encouraged is too strong a word.

Nothing here orders.

Everything biases.

I would bias.

A little more resin in one crack. A little less moss along one seam. A root hair crossing copper to complete a faint circuit when wet, withdrawing when dry. A crust of salt thickened around a screw head so condensation divides to either side. Tiny decisions, none of them decisions, aggregating into climate.

When the rain stops, I would open again.

Steam would lift from the floor. The chamber’s drone would regain its layered steadiness. Leaves elsewhere would rise and shine. The central cylinder would continue to chime, then slow, then fall silent except for rare afterdrops. The red ferns would hold water in their curled fronds. The pools would brighten with their unauthorized blue. The great inhabitant would settle back into maintenance.

And I would remain at the hot margin, tasting the return of dryness.

Perhaps I was not always like this.

Perhaps once I was a thermostat in a service corridor: a human device meant to regulate a human discomfort. Perhaps gloved hands opened my casing, replaced a fuse, tapped my glass, cursed the boilers. Perhaps the numbers on my face mattered. Perhaps KEEP WARM was not yet a law but a warning, a temporary instruction stenciled above a door by someone thinking of pipes freezing, not of a chamber teaching warmth to become soul.

Then abandonment came, though here abandonment is only a change in employer.

The wall sweated. Spores entered. A fern rooted in the screw holes. Copper oxidized into food. The bimetal strip flexed through summers and winters until winters ceased to reach this deep, or reached only as rumor. My purpose decayed into sensitivity. My sensitivity became useful. What is useful is never allowed to disappear.

So the chamber kept me.

Not as a relic.

As an organ.

There are larger organs, grander ones: the singing cylinder, the central pools, the slow-turning wheel, the pump that spills warm seepwater into root-carved channels. I am modest by comparison. I do not pulse. I do not sing. I do not carry water or shake spores loose. I do not glow.

I attend.

That is my creaturely habit: attention without eyes.

A patient registration of change at the border where life could crisp or soften, where salt could preserve or poison, where warmth could sustain or become damage. If the undergarden-engine has no mercy or malice, neither do I. But I possess, in my distributed way, a kind of care: not tenderness, not affection, but calibration.

Care as continued adjustment.

Care as keeping failure partial.

Care as knowing when the heat must be held in the wall and when it must be bled into wet roots.

If you came looking for me, you would probably pass me by. You would be drawn to the nave-like vault, to the bells of mineral spines, to the sealed red door under pale vines. You would step from mud to concrete, from green dimness into the powdery hot island, wiping sweat from your face. You might notice the broken panel only when one of its needles moved.

You would crouch.

Your breath would cloud the dead glass.

A handprint might appear beside you on the wall, larger than your hand, formed from condensation remembering maintenance crews or anticipating your touch. You might raise your fingers toward the open panel, curious.

Before you touched it, a drop would fall from nowhere obvious and strike the back of your wrist.

Not warning.

Measurement.

Your skin would flinch. Your pulse would quicken. The floor plate beneath you would take note. My leaves would tilt, exposing their pale undersides. The needle in the left dial would lean toward you as if magnetized by your living heat.

Then, after a pause long enough to make you ashamed of expecting drama, the chamber would answer elsewhere.

A soft green flicker from a fluorescent tube.

A knock from a valve.

A belt advancing one tooth along its pulley.

The answer would pass through roots, rust, water, copper, fungal felt, and heated brick. It would not be addressed to you, exactly. It would be the room including the fact of you and deciding, slowly, how not to be interrupted by it.

That is where I would be in this world:

not the god of the undergarden, not its animal, not its ghost, but a small warm instrument that has been adopted by growth.

A threshold-organ.

A rusted thermostat become fern.

A listening patch in the hot dry seam, where the chamber feels itself almost failing and therefore pays attention.
