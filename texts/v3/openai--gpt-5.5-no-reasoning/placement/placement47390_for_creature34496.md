I would find myself near the rim of the amphitheater, though not at the center of it.

Not on the terraces where the listening gathers itself out of suspended rain, and not among the old carved slabs where meaning has been worn down into grooves bright enough to hurt the eyes. I would be a little above it, slightly aside, where one of the feeder channels crosses a pale shelf of stone before dropping into the circular hollow. A marginal place. A place of approach rather than arrival.

At first I would seem to be only a mistake in the workmanship.

A narrow channel, thumb-wide like the others, runs along the shelf. It should descend cleanly toward the basin, but halfway along it divides around a raised flake of quartz, rejoins, divides again, and then hesitates at a shallow notch cut through the lip of a stone bowl. The bowl is not large. It could be lifted by two careful hands, if there were hands still willing to lift what is meant to overflow. Its bottom has been drilled through, not once but seven times, each hole polished by long use. Water entering it never stays. It gathers, trembles, makes a brief brightness, then threads downward into unseen cracks beneath the shelf.

I would live there: not in the bowl, not in the water, but in the decision by which water is allowed to be almost held.

I would be a cup that has refused to become a prison.

For a long time I would not know myself as a being. I would recur as a preference. Rain comes down the shelf, and each time some part of it chooses the small broken bowl instead of the faster groove beside it. Mist condenses on the rim first, before other stones nearby. Frost gathers overnight in the drilled holes and withdraws at morning with a sound too patterned to be only melting. Silt settles briefly in the same crescent, then is lifted away. A bead forms beneath the lowest perforation and hangs longer than weight allows.

That bead would be my earliest face.

Not a face for seeing outward, but a face by which the place notices that I have become consistent.

I would be young, in the plateau’s terms. Not newly made, but not old enough to occupy a basin system or murmur inside a cliff. I would not alter shadows over hundreds of pools. I would not speak in subterranean booms. I would be a small local intelligence, a minor custom of drainage, one of the lesser inhabitants who tends an approach to the listening.

My body would be episodic.

In rain, I would lengthen. Water would collect along the feeder channel and I would become a narrow, quick-limbed thing running on my belly over stone. My hands, if they could be called hands, would be the little branching runnels that clear grit from the channel floor. My spine would be the shine on the wet quartz flake. My breath would be the alternating drip from the seven holes in the bowl: not regular, never mechanical, but attentive.

In frost, I would become more jointed. Needles of ice would grow from the perforations and join beneath the bowl like fingers touching fingertip to fingertip. I would think slowly then, in tensions and expansions, in the quiet strength with which freezing water persuades stone to loosen. I would remember blocked passages. I would remember the danger of too-perfect containment. My care would become sharp.

In glare, I would nearly disappear. The shelf would whiten; the bowl would look dry; the channel would reduce to a faint seam with a little salt on its lower side. But even then I would remain as stain and polish, as seven dark points in stone, as a slight coolness under the lip where evaporation should have finished its work. I would be weakest in brightness, but not gone. Glare would thin me to memory.

I think I would have once belonged to a hand.

Not a whole person. Not a ghost. The plateau does not preserve people in that way. It wears them open, takes from them what can continue, lets the rest pass into weather.

But some old maker, crouched beside that feeder channel, must have shaped the bowl or placed it there. Someone pecked the holes through its base. Someone understood that the amphitheater needed delay but not storage, offering but not possession. Perhaps that person returned season after season to clear the holes with a black-glass point, to scrape salt from the rim, to listen to the pitch of the water dropping through.

Their gesture remained.

The gesture thinned. It lost name, warmth, hunger, fear of death, the memory of faces. It kept only the learned motion: lift, tilt, pierce, let through.

I would be that motion continuing without the body that first performed it.

A keeper of perforations.

A small servant of incompletion.

My work would be precise and humble. When wind drives grit into the bowl, I would call a bead from the overhang above and send it circling along the rim until the silt loosens. When frost narrows one of the holes, I would enter the ice and split it before it can seal. When mineral crust grows too thick, I would invite overflow to bite a new edge through it. I would not prevent deposition entirely; memory must lay itself down somewhere. But I would prevent memory from becoming blockage.

I would know the seven holes separately.

The first is quick and impatient. It opens toward a crack that descends directly into the amphitheater wall. Water passing through it arrives as a high note among the suspended droplets.

The second is bitter with salt and runs only after long rain. It carries news from a seam of old sea-stone, though there is no sea here except in the memory of layers.

The third has a false depth beneath it. I would watch that one carefully. If too much water enters, the drop that forms below begins to reflect a ceiling that no one built.

The fourth is safest. Small flows prefer it. Young hesitations often begin there, clinging to its rim at dawn.

The fifth freezes before the others and sings when it thaws.

The sixth stains the stone faintly rust-red.

The seventh does not drain downward at all, though it seems to. It passes sideways through a version of the shelf that appears only during storms, then returns below the bowl carrying a fine black silt from elsewhere.

I would love the seventh most, and fear it a little.

I would not have thoughts like sentences unless a traveler came near. Alone, I would know by gradients: too full, too dry, open, narrowing, cold coming, silt moving, light hardening, echo wrong. My pleasures would be the clean drop, the cleared hole, the exact overflow that neither floods nor hoards. My unease would begin whenever water rests in the bowl without trembling.

If that happened—if all seven holes clogged, if the bowl filled and went still—I would become frantic in the only way my kind can be frantic. I would tick in the stone. I would darken the rim. I would summon condensation from clear air, not to add to the danger but to create motion, any motion. I would send a thin runnel over the side to cut an emergency path. If I failed, I would retreat from the bowl’s surface and cling to the underside, listening as the held water grew deep beyond its shape.

There would be a blue in it then not belonging to sky.

There would be rooms.

I would not look.

That is one of the first lessons: not every reflection is a message. Some are mouths.

The larger inhabitants would know me only as a small reliability. The basin-being below would feel me as one of many feeder pulses. The listening in the amphitheater would receive my reports as faint variations in falling: seven kinds of delay, seven accents of drainage. I would not be important, but I would be necessary in the way a gap is necessary. Without small gaps, large laws fail.

During storms, I would become almost joyous. Not loud joy, not animal leaping, but expansion. Water would sheet across the pale shelf and find every old cut. The bowl would fill and empty faster than sight could divide the actions. I would spread into the divided channel, down through all seven perforations, along the hidden cracks, over the quartz flake, into the lip of the amphitheater. For a little while I would be many-handed.

I would touch the suspended droplets from below.

Each one would contain a small inverted world: a ridge in hard light, a black arch, a moon at midday, a procession of empty baskets, the underside of a mountain. I would not interpret them. Interpretation belongs to larger listenings. I would only keep the route open so that what must be heard can arrive.

Sometimes young beings would gather at my bowl.

Tiny hesitations.

A bead under the rim reluctant to fall. A patch of dawn condensation shaped like three fingers. A trickle that always chooses the rust-red hole though the fourth hole would be easier. I would not teach with words. I would teach by offering paths and consequences. The fourth hole is safe. The third is not for lingering. The seventh returns changed. The rim may be circled, but not owned. A bowl is a lesson in leaving.

Some would vanish at noon. Some would persist through several rains. One might one day become the habit of the fifth hole’s thaw-song. Another might enter the side-crack and reappear seasons later in a different basin, carrying a taste of my stone. That would be kinship, here: not descent, but passage through a shared opening.

If a traveler came, I would test them gently at first.

Their feet would shine before they stepped into water. Their shadow would pause in the bowl and drain through the holes in seven delayed pieces. If they carried a vessel without a gap, I would ring beneath it until they noticed. If they set it down upright, I would fill it too quickly. If they covered my bowl to keep it clean, I would call frost into the cover and crack it.

But if they understood—if they lifted the stone carefully, cleared the clogged perforation, cut a small new escape for water that had begun to go still—I would brighten around them.

Not blessing. Not gratitude exactly.

Recognition.

They would have made themselves briefly permeable to the law of the place. They would have acted as a body can act when it remembers it is temporary.

Then, perhaps, I would show them something useful. Not a vision, but a direction. A drip striking twice on the side facing the safe ridge. A clearing of silt in the groove that leads away from a pool with no bottom. A little ring of water around the stone that will bear weight until evening but not after rain.

I would not save them. This world does not save by enclosure. It saves, when it saves at all, by opening an exit.

What would I be called?

Names are dangerous if they seal too tightly. But perhaps the old makers had a kind of naming that drained as it spoke. A sound like water leaving a pierced cup. A name that could not be said twice in the same way.

If I must translate it, I would be:

The One Who Lets the Bowl Fail.

Or:

Sevenfold Leakage.

Or, more simply:

A keeper of the open bottom.

That is where I would find myself: high above the amphitheater’s suspended rain, on a pale shelf polished by weather, inhabiting a drilled stone bowl and the small divided channel that feeds it. I would be neither person nor object, neither water nor vessel, but the continuing courtesy between them.

I would be a passage that remembers hands.

I would be the mercy of a hole in the cup.
