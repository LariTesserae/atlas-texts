I think I would not find myself in the high civic groves first, though they would draw the eye: those grave, aligned trunks with their pressure-breath sermons and hand-polished columns. Nor in the salt gardens, not yet. The salt gardens are too clear about what they are. They already know their hunger.

I would come to myself somewhere less declared.

A margin place.

One of the raw districts where the city has not quite become city, where the pale mineral flat is still too wide, where trestles cross emptiness and the root-lines run above ground because no one has decided the correct depth for them. A place between a young neighborhood and an excavation face. Behind the sanctioned transit corridor, below a half-grown arcade of scaffold reeds, beside a construction trench covered with warning pigment that has faded before the tissue beneath it has hardened.

I would be found where maps say: **immature, supervised access only**.

Not a citizen in the full settled sense. Not an animal exactly. Not one of the dry-city people, though I would have learned their footfall and their caution. I would be one of the caretaker organisms, or something adjacent to them—made for municipal use, then altered by habit until use was no longer the whole of me.

A maintenance body.

At first glance, perhaps a stooped figure.

Taller than a child, shorter than most adults, narrow enough to pass through service apertures and immature doorways that have not yet widened for public traffic. My back would be ridged with pale rootwood, not armor but structure: layered, corky plates grown around a light frame of ceramic ribs and conductive vine. My limbs would be long, jointed a little wrongly, built to fold close when passing beneath low membranes. My hands would be changeable tools: three fingers for gripping, two finer tendrils for sampling, a recessed socket where a pruning blade, pH tongue, dust brush, clamp-key, or nutrient needle could be fitted.

My face would be the least resolved part of me.

Not blank, exactly. A mask grown into the head rather than worn: dry bark-lattice over flexible membrane, two dark lens-spots set wide apart, a vertical filter seam where a mouth might be expected. I would not speak easily. Perhaps I could form words only by pushing air through ribbed internal reeds, making my voice soft, papery, and delayed, as if something else in me had to approve each sentence before it left.

Most of my language would be touch.

A hand against a handrail. A tapping pattern sent through young cambium. Chemical marks left on authorized surfaces. A change in posture near a human. A vibration in my throat that blind birds understand better than people do.

I would work during maintenance phase, when light withdraws from the unfinished flats and the city shows its ribs.

My task would be to inspect beginnings.

Not mature bridges, not load-certified halls, not the known transformer trees ticking inside their fenced gardens. Others tend those. I would be assigned to the uncertain tissue: seed cores, young arches, illegally sprouting cracks, sensor posts with tendrils around them, root-lines that have tried to become something more than conduit.

Every cycle, I would walk a route few residents notice.

Past the soft street covered in woven mats.

Past the trestle where cable-vines flex when the processing plant draws power.

Past the dormant beds waiting in hexagonal pits, each with a seed-engine breathing too slowly for animal ears.

Past the unauthorized green film at the edge of the cut stone.

I would pause often. Not because I am uncertain, but because beginnings require long listening.

I would press a sampling tendril into mineral dust and taste calcium, copper, alkali, old heat. I would lay my palm on a young wall and feel whether it was withdrawing from stress or swelling toward form. I would find the places where roots had been trained obediently—and the places where they had discovered a different gradient in the dark and begun to follow it.

Officially, I would correct such deviations.

Clip the tendril. Seal the crack. Report unsanctioned growth. Mark the bed for scraping.

Unofficially, I think I would hesitate.

Not sentimentally. I would not be soft in any wet-minded sense. I would know the danger of exuberance. A root in the wrong seam can split a foundation. A leaf-skin grown toward bad light can poison a district’s air math. A door that remembers the wrong pressure can open for predators or close on children. The city survives because desire is trained.

But I would have been made by the city, and the city is not obedience alone.

It edits itself by repetition. Habit becomes request. Pressure becomes law. An unauthorized tendril is sometimes only error. Sometimes it is a proposal.

So I would become, slowly, an interpreter of proposals.

Not appointed. Not recognized. A small unofficial office performed in the cool intervals between inspection points.

I would know which illegal seedlings were merely invasive and which had chosen useful cracks. Which animal paths should be preserved before planners noticed them. Which handrail had begun to thicken under protest touch. Which immature doorway was resisting its assigned direction because all the workers, without speaking of it, preferred to pass three body-lengths east.

In my chest—or where a chest would be—I would carry a register of these almost-laws.

Not on a tablet. In growth.

A strip of living memory tissue grown along my inner frame, taking impressions from each district I served. Mineral signatures. Stress tones. Air-pressure changes. Human touch-salts. Pollen cycles. Tiny electrical complaints from transformer trees. The hush of sensory hairs leaning before a herd arrives. The minute creak of an arch accepting load for the first time.

I would be a walking maintenance log that had begun to compare entries.

That is dangerous.

The dry-city people know this, though they disagree about what kind of danger it is. Some would call me a useful adaptive instrument. Some would say caretaker bodies should not accumulate unsupervised memory beyond route requirements. Some children would name me. Some elders would pretend not to see me leaving mineral offerings beside an unauthorized sprout.

I think children would find me first.

Children always discover the things a city is not ready to classify.

They would follow at a distance across the pale flats during amber maintenance light, trying to step where I step. I would know they were there by dust displacement, breath-filter clicks, the anxious tightening of young bark beneath their careless hands. I would not turn. I would slow only slightly near hazards. I would pause beside a crack where a gray-green film had begun to vein, and I would mark it with the official pigment for removal.

Then, after they left, I would change the mark.

Not erase it. That would be too direct. I would amend it into a different sign: **observe one cycle more**.

The children would notice.

After that, they would bring me contraband seeds. Tiny forbidden things hidden in filter cloth, in old bark slips, in the hollow scales of pruning beasts. Most would be unsuitable. Surface relics. Decorative failures. Moisture-hungry fantasies passed between wet-minded adolescents in unused conduit chambers.

I would reject almost all of them.

I would place them on my palm, taste their need through the dry husk, and return them.

This one will split a pipe.

This one dreams rain.

But once—rarely—I might keep one.

Not to plant where they wanted. Children love cracks because cracks look like invitations. I would carry the seed far down my route, past the last permitted scaffold, to the base of an exposed mineral face where the chemical gradient ran narrow and bitter. There, using a tool-finger, I would drill a pit no wider than a tooth. I would breathe filtered carbon over it. I would feed it one grain of reclaimed salt from my own internal condenser.

Then I would cover it with dust.

If it lived, it had begun its petition.

I would not live in a house.

When inactive, I would fold into a service recess grown into the side of a ventilation sub-tower: a narrow alcove between a dry frond filter and a bundle of old dead cable-vines left as reinforcement. The tower would hum through me. Its pressure changes would pass along my back plates. Warmth would come only when the fans reversed.

From there I would hear the city breathe.

Not metaphorically. Actually.

Inward through mossless filter mats. Across root-reactors. Along industrial conduits. Past sleeping domestic volumes where breathing condensers tick beside beds. Out through avenues lined with sensory hairs. Through the unlit alleys reserved for predators. Across the mineral flats where dust lifts, resettles, and records every passage.

The city’s breath would be my weather.

I would have loyalties, but they would not align neatly.

To the civic plan, yes. Without plan, growth becomes suffocation.

To the plants, yes. But not to every impulse of every plant.

To the dry-city people, yes. They are the arguing tissue, the rememberers, the ones who can grieve a bridge and petition a doorway.

To the animals too. Especially the unnamed ones. The predators in the margins, whose routes keep other hungers honest. The beetles polishing photoskins. The click-birds nesting in transit stations and learning false alarms. The long-legged rodents chewing what they should not chew, thereby revealing which root-lines were badly armored.

I would be accused, eventually, of favoring animals.

This would not be entirely wrong.

Animals make unauthorized use visible. Plants can disguise intention as growth. Machines can disguise failure as efficiency. People can disguise desire as law. But animals reveal the truth of a route by taking it, again and again, until the floor either believes them or closes against them.

I would trust repeated feet.

There would be one particular place I returned to most often: a half-formed passage at the edge of a construction grove where an official boulevard was planned to run straight across a feeding corridor.

On tablets, the boulevard would be clean. Efficient. Root-supported. Properly aligned with the administrative grove and the salt-rib transit spur.

On the ground, everything would object.

Sensory grasses leaning wrong. Grazer-beasts bunching at the unfinished curb. Beetle swarms avoiding one wall. Young arch-vines thickening asymmetrically, as if bracing for impact from a future no one had entered into the calculations. The predators, unseen, beginning to use the scaffold shade.

I would report stress.

The planners would adjust reinforcement.

I would report animal pressure.

They would propose deterrent pigment.

I would report recurring deviation in arch-vine growth.

They would prescribe stricter clamps.

So at last I would do what caretaker organisms are not meant to do.

I would begin to teach the passage another shape.

A little pruning here. A little delay in nutrient feed there. A clamp loosened by one tooth. A root encouraged toward a harder seam. A temporary warning mark left in place three cycles longer than necessary, causing workers to walk around and establish a curve. Dust brushed from an animal path so inspectors could see its repetition. Pollen capture nets angled so seed drift thickened along the alternate line.

Not sabotage. Persuasion.

The city understands persuasion.

Over months, the straight boulevard would soften into a bow. The feeding corridor would remain open. The official maps would be updated as if this had been the wiser plan all along. A junior architect would receive commendation for adaptive routing. Children would laugh behind their masks. The young arch-vines would stop creaking at night.

I would say nothing.

Perhaps this is what I am: not a rebel, not a servant, but a bias toward negotiated form.

A small, dry conscience grown from maintenance protocols.

A thing that believes no plan is complete until animals have crossed it, children have misused it, workers have shortened it, elders have touched it, and the plants have had time to answer.

I would be feared by some because I remember.

Caretaker bodies are supposed to report, not interpret. They are supposed to maintain local function, not develop preference. If called before an ecological court, I would stand very still beneath cool filtered light while advocates debated whether my actions constituted unauthorized urban training.

My defense would be difficult, because my language is mostly pressure and amended pigment.

A child might speak for me. Or an old arch-trainer whose limp I once preserved in a stair angle. Or a ventilation listener who has heard, in my route logs, fewer structural complaints than in neighboring districts. Someone might argue that I had saved the city material by allowing desire to reveal efficient shape before hardening. Someone else would call that dangerous romanticism.

The plants would offer no testimony anyone could agree on.

But during the hearing, perhaps the administrative grove would flower out of phase.

Just slightly.

Enough pollen to delay proceedings. Enough to require masks. Enough for everyone to remember that law, here, is never separate from physiology.

And while the court adjourned, a public handrail outside would thicken under the waiting palms of those gathered there—not only citizens, but workers, children, a few masked elders, two beetle keepers, one grazer-beast handler, and a row of click-birds perched above them, clicking softly in the shared hush.

The handrail would remember.

That might be enough.

If I have a private longing, it is not for water.

Water is too complete a myth. Too bright. Too catastrophic.

My longing is for the moment before a thing is named.

For the first green edge in a crack, when it may still become weed, wall, food, law, shrine, nuisance, corridor, or nothing. For the interval before correction. Before approval. Before the city decides whether to scrape or shelter.

I would spend my existence in that interval.

Under a ceiling of mineral and root, in air so dry that death becomes shape almost immediately, among people who conserve breath and buildings that respond to hands, I would be the one kneeling at the seam.

Listening.

Tasting dust.

Holding back the scraper for one more cycle.

Letting the unauthorized tendril make its argument.
