I would find myself in one of the side-chambers that is only sometimes a chamber.

Not one of the great courts, not among the high terraces where the pale pressure-creatures cross like loosened architecture, and not in the lower avenues where the knockers make the drowned city remember fear. I would be somewhere marginal: a recess under a stairfield, a wedge-shaped hollow behind three worn pillars, a place formed less by design than by erosion misunderstanding design for ages.

A small kingdom, because small hollows are kingdoms.

At first I would think I was debris.

A pale scrap lodged in a seam. A shred of shed skin caught where two currents cancel one another. A translucent fold quivering in the lee of a balustrade. Something almost too slight to count as an animal, with no clear front, no dignity of outline, no confidence that it is separate from silt. The water would pass through me more than around me. I would tremble with every pressure change. I would be made mostly of membrane, threads, and listening.

But I would not be helpless exactly. Nothing that lasts there can be merely helpless.

I think I would be a kind of seam-dweller: one of the small adherent lives that occupies the boundary between architecture and current. Not the great pale ruin-animal itself, but something that lives in relation to it, below notice until notice becomes fatal. A creature adapted to cracks, inscriptions, grooves, sockets, the underlips of old construction. A listener in joints. A thin, many-limbed thing that can flatten itself into a relief-line and become part of the unread writing on the wall.

My body would be soft where the water must take me and mineral where the wall must hold me.

Most of me would be a translucent mantle, oval when relaxed, long and ribboned when stretched by flow. Along the underside: dozens of small gripping mouths, each able to seal against glazed stone. Not mouths for eating, not primarily, but mouths for staying. They would kiss the ceramic and taste its age: salt, mineral dust, the faint organic slick left by other bodies. Around my rim would be hair-fine feelers, not waving freely—free things are taken—but pressed close, reading vibration through the boundary layer where current slows against surface.

I would have no eyes worth mentioning. Perhaps two dim pits that know the difference between open water and shadow, between the approach of a veil-wing and the eclipse of something larger. Sight would be a luxury belonging to places with distance. I would know the world by pressure along my edge, by chemistry across my skin, by the tremor of claws, plates, pulses, downrushes.

My first law would be: do not leave the wall without being carried to another wall.

My second: trust no stillness unless the latch-crowns remain open.

My third: when the pale one passes, close all mouths and become inscription.

I would live among inscriptions, though I would not read them. That seems important. The makers’ marks would be my landscape: shallow ovals, branching channels, depressions worn nearly smooth. To me they would not be symbols but weather. Good grip here. Food collects here. Eggs survive here. Eels hunt here. Pressure eddies under this branching line. A groove that once meant dynasty or organ or tide would mean refuge if approached from below and death if approached during an outward breath.

I would feed on what the flood edits down to my scale.

The large creatures take bodies. The latch-crowns sieve the water. The stair-eels steal eggs and weakness. I would live on films, shed skins, microscopic flesh, the bruised milk released by ceiling nodules, the powder of crushed shell after a pale pressure-creature grinds its prey. I would rasp without sound. I would unfurl a little net only in the safest seconds, a lace of sticky filaments extended from my mantle into the slow seam-water. The current would comb food into it. I would draw it back before the next shear.

Sometimes I would eat the city.

Not as the knockers might, not by force, but by patient theft. A grain of old ceramic loosened from an edge. Mineral powder caught in my gut. Dark composite dust from a worn relief. I would sort these grains into myself and lay them along my back in a mosaic of flexible scales. Not armor enough to save me from a true predator, but enough to make me resemble the place. Over time my back would become a patch of wall-colored scutes, each one grown from what the wall had lost.

If I lived long enough, I would be hard to distinguish from damage.

A pale blister in a carved line. A row of false chips under an arch. A breathing irregularity in a stair-groove. Transparent at the edge, blue-white at the center, dark where my gut shows the city’s recent dead.

I would fear the stair-eels most often.

They would come like black decisions through the grooves. Not large, not grand, but intimate. Their wedge heads would enter the seam where I live. Their many small jaws would test every soft edge. When pressure drops, they would pour from the steps in hundreds, and my whole world would become teeth passing over me. My defense would be adhesion, silence, mineral stillness. I would clamp every mouth to the wall and harden my rim. Many of my kind would fail. The eels would leave behind clean patches where small kingdoms had been erased.

I would fear the pale pressure-creature differently.

Not as a hunter, perhaps. It might not seek me. I might be too small, too mineral, too much trouble. But its passing would remake law. Before it arrived, the fish would split, the latch-crowns would close, the ribbon creatures would flatten, and I would feel the additional cold of displacement. Then its flank would enter the passage like a moving piece of the city. Its pressure-plates would scrape close enough to shear me away. Its underside mouths might flare and seal over half the wall, taking everything loose.

If I survived, I might gain from it.

After it fed, the water would be rich. Fragments would drift. Crushed shell. Blood too cold to steam. Mucus, grit, torn membrane. Its skin, abraded against a parapet, would leave pale sheets snagged along the edge. I would emerge from my false inscription and feed. I might even attach briefly to one of its plates, if I were young and reckless, riding it from one district to another.

That is how my kind would travel.

Not by journey, because journey implies intention across distance, and distance belongs to the open water where small things are lost. We would travel by accident refined into instinct. A loosened grip at the right moment. A latch onto shed skin. A body flattened against a drifting fragment. A desperate seal onto the flank of a larger animal. Generations would spread not through exploration but through tolerated catastrophe.

I would breed in pauses.

When a chamber holds calm long enough for silt to fall in a gray veil, when the breathing nodules are expanded and the latch-crowns half-open, when no knocking rises from below, I would crawl to the protected underside of a ledge. There, in the blind seam where the current curls but does not tear, I would lay a chain of eggs like small cloudy lenses. Each would be glued to the mineral with a substance stronger than hope.

Most would be eaten.

Transparent scavenger-crabs would find some. Stair-eels would take others. A pressure change would peel away an entire chain and send it spinning upward into a downrush. But a few would hatch into almost nothing: motes with gripping rims, each seeking wall before the water finished deciding about them. The first success of life there would not be hunger. It would be adhesion.

I would not know loneliness, because I would not have imagined company as comfort.

Still, I would live among signals.

The pulse of a pale one through the wall: deep, spaced, possessive.

The many tiny clicks of scavengers after a feeding.

The shut-sequence of latch-crowns along a colonnade.

The soft collective panic of blind fish moving through a corridor above me.

The distant knock from lower architecture, after which even the water seems to make itself smaller.

My own kind would communicate by contact. Slow taps through stone. Chemical smears along safe routes. Warning bitterness left in seams after an eel attack. A breeding slick that says: this hollow held for long enough once; it may again. None of these would be names. They would be usable truths.

Here is grip.  
Here is scraping.  
Here is food after violence.  
Here is a hollow that lies.  
Here is a wall that breathes outward.  
Here is the path of the pale one.  
Here is where eggs vanished.

I would belong to the city in the way lichen belongs to a statue, except there is no green here and no sunlight-softening of stone. I would be animal film, mineral memory, a margin of hunger along the made world’s worn skin. I would take no interest in the vanished builders except as texture. Their ceremonial depressions would guide my limbs. Their reliefs would shelter my eggs. Their polished black floors would be danger because they offer no seam. Their grand avenues would be nothing to me but borders of annihilation.

The great spaces would be myths of pressure.

I would know them through what returned from them: stunned fish, shattered shells, shed plates, silt clouds, the chemical alarm of creatures swept too far from shelter. Perhaps once in my life I would be torn free and carried across a plaza. Then the world would become enormous and illegible. Dim metallic light above. The floor too far below. A veil-wing flashing like a piece of night. The shadow of a bridge. The body of a pale pressure-creature stretched between currents, larger than any wall I had known.

I would tumble, fold, seal myself around my own softness.

If I were lucky, I would strike a pillar and hold.

For days afterward I would not feed. I would remain clamped there, recovering the idea of surface. Around me, the water would continue its argument: downrush, crossflow, upward boil, silence, exhalation. Slowly, my feelers would open. Slowly, the stone beneath me would become a place.

That would be my gift: to make place out of contact.

Not to understand the city. Not to master it. Not to cross it whole. But to convert one seam, one ledge, one worn inscription into a survivable world.

And if I grew old, I would become less animal to the eye.

My mineral scutes would thicken. My translucent rim would cloud. My gripping mouths would seal more slowly and release less often. Smaller things would live on me: minute filter-cups, transparent mites, larvae hiding between my plates. I would be grazed while alive. I would be mistaken for wall by creatures younger than myself. Silt would settle in the shallow channels of my back and make of me a false fragment of writing.

Eventually a pressure change would take me.

Not dramatically. No predator perhaps. No grand feeding. Just one outward breath from a chamber I trusted too long. One failed grip. One old mouth unable to seal. I would peel from the wall and turn in the cold water, briefly becoming visible as a whole creature: oval, pale, many-mouthed, fringed, carrying the dust of architecture in my back.

Then I would be food, film, grit.

A stair-eel might take me. A veil-wing might shred me in a downrush. I might lodge under a latch-crown and nourish it for a season. My mineral plates might settle into the very seam where my eggs once held, becoming a roughness another small life could grip.

So I would not be the ruin made animal.

I would be one of the small answers that allow the ruin to have a skin.

A seam-listener.  
A wall-clinger.  
A living blemish in the drowned highland’s unread script.

I would live where no fire can be imagined, where shelter is not enclosure but angle, where every surface is both history and hazard. I would know the world as pressure passing through stone. I would know the great pale creatures by their silence before arrival and the feast after departure. I would know altitude as cold weight, architecture as parent, erosion as weather, and survival as the art of becoming almost indistinguishable from where one holds on.
