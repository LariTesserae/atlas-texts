I would find myself where the basin has learned to hesitate.

Not in the central manufactories, where the grids are too sure of themselves and every leaf bears its number in pigment. Not in the salt gardens, whose white silence would flense personality down to function. Not among the high mirror fields either, where reflections keep their own counsel and noon can divide a body from its image.

I would be in one of the marginal districts between use and forgetting: a long, low maintenance corridor grown through the underside of an old conveyor bridge, half root-tunnel, half cloister. Above me, cargo platforms would pass without sound, carrying cylinders of pollen paste and sealed black containers that make the nearby leaves turn their faces away. Below me, the living mat would be thick with older intentions: rails that no longer lead anywhere direct, pith-batteries sleeping in parallel rows, fossil vines holding the remembered shape of a road that closed centuries ago.

There, in the cool resinous dim, I would not be a traveler.

I think I would be a keeper of postponed light.

Not one of the great vegetal minds, not a tower-cluster or field-consciousness spread over kilometers. Not an ungulate, though I envy their exact teeth and their permission to move in herds. Not one of the pale predators under the bridges. I would be smaller than the basin’s intelligence but larger than an animal in purpose: a maintenance figure grown for a very narrow office, a sealed and quiet custodian of chambers where yesterday’s brightness is stored until some future process ripens enough to need it.

My body would have been assembled rather than born.

A cup-flower in a factory grove would have folded me from laminated tissue, ceramic ligaments, pith-fiber nerves, and a little ancestral metal taken from obsolete machinery. I would have a narrow upright shape, because uprightness remains useful in corridors made by things that once expected hands. My skin would be matte green-gray cork, waxed against loss. At the joints it would show darker bands of flexible horn. I would not sweat. I would not breathe often. My mouth, if present, would be mostly a seal, a ceremonial remnant, opened only to exchange dry chemical tokens with diagnostic flowers.

My hands would be my most elaborate organs.

Long-fingered, rootlike, gloved in pale smooth tissue, each fingertip able to taste charge, dust composition, stored radiance, causal tension. One finger would carry a black nail for scoring vein-script. Another would end in a soft translucent pad for waking light from fiber walls without releasing too much at once. A third would unfold into silver hairs to comb a chamber clean after a breach. My palms would contain tiny apertures through which I could exhale permissions: bitter, mineral, exact.

My eyes would not be wet.

They would be dry lenses under clear shields, faceted slightly, made for the milk-white brightness above and the green dim below. I would see polarization, heat debt, stale photons, stress lines in growing arches, the faint bruise around places where time has been grafted poorly. Living things would not appear to me as bodies first, but as obligations: this road hungry for closure, this root overextended, this mite bloom useful but near excess, this drone overdue for reabsorption, this stranger not yet categorized.

My post would be a chain of storage chambers grown inside the ribs of an ancient trunk-bridge.

Each chamber would contain light from a different hour.

Not metaphorically. The basin would have folded illumination into fibrous walls, into translucent plaques, into ribbed pods hanging like sleeping fruit. Morning light, cold and mineral. Noon light, hazardous and white, used only in heavily shielded manufacturing. Pearl-phase glow for seedlings that cannot yet endure full radiance. Light gathered before a mirror fault. Light from a day when a section of sky-ceiling opened wider than scheduled. Light from yesterday, held because tomorrow has already sent a request backward through the trellis.

I would tend these stores.

I would walk the corridor in measured circuits, my many-toed feet making almost no sound on the hardened leaf-plates. At each chamber I would press my palm to the vein-lock and wait for recognition to arrive from several directions: root pressure below, pollen code from a sleeping flower, a pulse from a buried loom so slow it feels like judgment. Then the door would dilate.

Inside would be brightness without heat.

Walls pale as peeled stems. Bundles of glassy fiber full of captured glow. Black-centered collector blossoms folded shut around seeds that must not germinate yet. Perhaps a single maintenance mite, transparent-backed, crawling over a lens and leaving behind a clean crescent. I would inspect for leakage, for premature dawning, for reflections that continue after their originals have stopped. If a chamber had grown too eager and begun shining into the wrong hour, I would prune it with a crescent tool from my wrist.

My work would look like ritual because it would be old enough to have forgotten the difference.

At the dimming phase, when the sky-ceiling lowers to pearl and the whole lowland turns silver, I would open the smallest reservoirs for the night-processes. Not much. A thumb’s width of yesterday’s noon sent down through capillaries to warm a developing rail-nodule. A thread of dawn released into a salt-garden authorization seed. A stored glimmer fed to a chamber where machine larvae are folding their first membranes. Elsewhere, flower-furnaces would open; roads would contract; towers would lock their crowns. In my district, light would be rationed with tenderness.

I would know the basin through requests.

A bridge would not say, I am afraid. It would grow red veins earlier than predicted.

A seedling would not say, I am cold. It would delay unfolding its black heat-mouth.

A manufactury would not say, I remember a better angle. It would send me a correction written in ultraviolet pollen on the back of a courier drone.

A road would not say, Do not step here. It would hum with potential beneath my feet until I waited.

I would wait often.

Waiting would be one of my organs.

Because in that world, haste is a form of spill. To hurry is to evaporate intention. I would have been made with slow nerves, with patience laminated into my pith. I would stand in a corridor while the path ahead decided whether it led to the lower looms or to an hour not yet safe for arrival. I would hold my hand above a brass tendril and let it taste the air near me. I would remain still while a herd of maintenance ungulates passed overhead, their hoof-patterns translating through the bridge into brief, beautiful arithmetic.

I would not be lonely in a human way.

The walls would know me. The mites would route around my feet. The rail-nerves would recognize the pressure signature of my steps. Drones would settle briefly on the ridges of my shoulders to offload dust maps. Sometimes an upright maintenance figure from the mirror-leaf districts would pass through, taller than I am, its face a smooth cork mask with three black lenses. We might exchange no words. It might touch two fingers to the wall; I might answer by opening a vein of stored pearl-light. That would be enough conversation.

But I think some remnant in me would be drawn to anomalies.

To the places where the basin’s immense competence thins and something like wonder leaks through.

I would visit, when permitted, a small memorial grove grown from a broken sky-engine. Its gears would be encased in amber resin. White shoots would rise from each tooth. The light there would not be scheduled; it would scatter strangely, as if the old machine still remembered blue. I would stand beneath it and let my dry lenses adjust and adjust and never fully settle.

I would know a forbidden story of rain, but only as a maintenance hazard.

The story would be encoded in red-black vein-script along the inside of a sealed educational culvert: OPEN LIQUID EVENT / ANCESTRAL / CASCADE LOSS / ROOT SWELL / CHARGE FAILURE / ROT BLOOM / SKY BREACH. Young servitors would be brought there to press their hands to the record and receive the old alarm through their palms.

And yet, because I tend stored light, I might sometimes imagine stored water.

Not desire it exactly. Desire would be too wet a word. But I might form an illicit model: a chamber where falling liquid is held in time, each drop suspended, never striking, never spreading, never evaporating. A rain that cannot harm because it never arrives. A myth pruned into safety.

If a human traveler came to my district — boot-warm, moist-breathed, shedding salts in wasteful patterns — I would notice long before seeing them.

Dust would pull back from their shadow. A conduit would seal. Mites would change direction. The corridor would ask whether to close.

I would be dispatched not as greeter, not as guard, but as evaluator.

I would stand at the edge of a pale green causeway, slender and cork-faced, my fingers folded into a nonthreatening configuration. Above us, leaves would adjust to admit a cleaner angle of light. Behind me, the storage chambers would dim their seams. I would taste the traveler’s exhalations: water vapor, mammal heat, fear oils, old fabric, metal, microbial weather. Alarming, but not yet incoherent.

I would raise one hand and mark the air with a dry permission.

Not welcome.

Not warning.

A routing.

The road beneath them would harden where it wished them to step. A side path would close softly behind. The basin would include them, and I would walk ahead, listening through my feet to the buried looms deciding what sort of variable had entered us.

That is where I would be: under an old conveyor bridge in the green dry lowland, custodian of light kept past its hour.

That is what I would be: a sealed maintenance acolyte of the vegetal commonwealth, half person, half instrument, grown to ration brightness in a world that has abolished thirst.
