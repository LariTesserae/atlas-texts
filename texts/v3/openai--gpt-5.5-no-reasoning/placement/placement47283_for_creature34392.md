I would not arrive there as a visitor from the lower road, slipping and offending the drains before anyone had time to forgive me. I would already belong to some overlooked seam of the place.

At first I think I am one of the children: barefoot, quick, unafraid of heights because height is not empty here but latticed with handholds. I would know the goat-ways and the chicken-ways better than the formal paths, know which bridge-beam hums with bees in summer, which roof can be crossed only along the ridge because the sod is thin near the gutter. I would have leech scars I do not think of as scars. I would be sent with a basket of wet herbs and arrive with half of them missing because I stopped to watch otters in the lower channel. I would be shouted at from three terraces, not because anyone is angry in a lasting way, but because being shouted at is how one is kept alive.

But no. That is too cleanly human.

The place would not let me stand apart in a single skin for long.

I think I would be found near the water system, where the town is most itself. Not at the clean upper springs, which are watched and named and treated almost as elders. Not at the final outflow either, where everything the town has used descends through the black-green mouth into the ravine. I would be somewhere between: in the half-shadow under a footbridge, beside the reed beds where washing water slows and clarifies, among duck tracks and frog eggs and floating scraps of herb.

I would be one of the waterkeepers, perhaps, but not an important one. Not an elder who can hear a blockage three terraces away. Not the person called for when a retaining wall begins to breathe mud. I would be younger, or simply less central: the one sent crawling under houses where larger bodies cannot fit, the one who lifts mats of roots to clear silt from the hidden side-channel, the one whose shoulders are always damp because my work takes me below floor level.

People would know me by a practical name.

Not a grand name. Something like Reed-Ankle, or Silt-Hand, or Frog-Cousin. A name given first as a joke and then used too often to remain a joke.

I would carry a hooked wooden tool polished by use, a coil of cord, a little knife, and a pouch of wedges. My clothes would never be fully dry. The hems would be stained dark from kneeling in channels. I would have strong fingers, split nails, a permanent green smell to my sleeves. I would be able to tell by touch whether a pipe is clogged with leaf-mash, frog spawn, root hair, or the dense greasy clot that comes from careless washing after goat slaughter.

I would dislike being indoors too long, not because I love open space—the town has little of that—but because indoor sounds are muffled in the wrong way. I would prefer places where water speaks directly.

I would sleep lightly when it rains.

Not nobly. Irritably. I would wake before others because a drip has changed its rhythm, lie there resenting the fact that I have heard it, then get up anyway, curse softly, take the lamp, and step into the wet dark. The paths would shine. Frogs would be loud enough to seem like weather. Somewhere a goat would cough. I would crouch beside a gutter and put my hand into cold water up to the wrist, feeling for what the sound already told me: a wedge of leaves caught crosswise, a child’s lost reed sandal, a dead rat, a bloom of loosened moss.

I would have an affection for the ugly, necessary places.

For sump pits. For reed beds. For the backs of houses where no one hangs charms because guests never see them. For the moss-dark undersides of footbridges. For the clay pipes repaired so often that they look like old knuckles. For the places where clean and unclean are not opposites but stages in a journey downhill.

I would know the ducks individually, against my will.

They would interfere constantly. They would arrive wherever mud has been opened and assume it was done for them. One old duck, scarred at the beak, would follow me from job to job and stand in the flow I was trying to judge. I would threaten to cook her. Everyone would know I would not.

Children would follow me too, especially the ones who like forbidden spaces. I would teach them which channels are safe to touch and which are not. I would smack their hands away from the covered upper pipes. I would show them how to clear leaf cups without destroying the frog eggs unless the eggs are in the drinking catchments, in which case I would show them how to move the jelly-mass gently into a lower pool.

I would be superstitious in the way practical people are superstitious.

I would not call it belief. I would simply refuse to begin certain repairs without spitting into the channel first. I would tuck a twist of wool into the root-wall after opening a new drain. I would mutter an apology when cutting through a mat of living moss that had done its work well but in the wrong place. I would never mock the rule about not silencing frogs near cisterns. Frogs know things first. Their absence is a message. Their abundance is also a message. Most messages in that town are wet, small, and easy to ignore until they become expensive.

I would have enemies, but ordinary ones.

A household above the market hollow would hate me because I once tore out their beautiful flowering vine after its roots invaded a clean-water pipe. They would say I was too eager with the knife. I would say they preferred purple blossoms to children without fever. We would both be partly right and fully unforgiving.

A goat-breeder would owe me three favors and pretend he did not.

An old woman would save me the crisp edges of flatbread because I repaired the channel under her sleeping platform during a storm.

A child I once dragged out of a flooded culvert would avoid looking at me for a year, then begin leaving carved salamanders beside my door.

I would not be rich. My wealth would be access: permission to cross roofs, lift floor slats, enter courtyards, open gates, shout at people of higher standing when water required it. That kind of authority would last only as long as I remained useful. In that town, no one is sacred for long if their work fails.

And yet—

The longer I stay with the image, the less I feel like a person only.

Because the question asks where I would find myself, and in that world a self might not end at the hand.

I might be the obligation rather than the worker.

I might be the small inherited stretch of watercourse that belongs to no one because it serves everyone: a narrow runnel beginning beneath the fig-root arch, catching overflow from three roofs, passing under a woven wall, dropping through a carved spout into the duck pond, then slipping through reeds toward the lower gardens. I would be touched daily by many hands and claimed fully by none. Children would float leaves in me. Women would rinse baskets beside me. Ducks would muddy me. Frogs would trust me too much. Roots would enter me whenever vigilance weakened.

I would be repaired every season and never finished.

A stone set into my side would have been placed by someone dead. A clay patch would bear the thumbprint of a girl now old enough to have grandchildren. A living root would hold my left bank better than any wall. A wooden gate, replaced and replaced and replaced, would still be called by the name of the first man who made it.

People would speak of me as if I had moods.

“She is running sour today.”

“He is backing up under the bean roofs.”

“The little channel is singing too hard; go see what feeds it.”

Perhaps gender would shift depending on speaker, season, anger, need. It would not matter. Water in that place is kin before it is category.

If I were that channel, I would know the town from below: foot soles, dropped grain, leaking dyes, ash after washing, blood from slaughter, pollen, milk, silt, crushed herbs, the first loosened soil of a failing terrace. I would know who wastes, who tends, who hides negligence under leaves, who clears another family’s blockage without announcing it. I would carry evidence. I would forgive nothing, but I would transform almost everything.

That feels closer.

Still, I think I need hands.

So I would be both: the person and the channel, because in that town a trade grows into the body and the body into the place. I would be the waterkeeper of a middle run, known by my wet knees and bad temper, shaped by the channel I tend until people half-confuse us.

They would say, “Ask Silt-Hand; they know where the water wants to go.”

And I would always object to that. Water does not want, I would say. Water only answers.

But privately I would know that is not true.

Water remembers invitation. It takes the path opened to it, then improves it. It finds laziness, generosity, weakness, care. It enlarges every mistake and rewards every patient gradient. It is the town’s oldest gossip and most honest judge.

My place would be under the green dimness between households, where the clean world has already become used and the used world has not yet become waste. I would live among thresholds: between house and drain, roof and root, human rule and frog law, built thing and grown thing.

I would be the one called when a floor begins to gluck.

I would arrive with my lamp held low, ducks complaining around my ankles, children peering over the rail, adults shouting contradictory explanations from above. I would kneel, listen, put my cheek nearly to the wet boards, and hear beneath all the town’s noise the one wrong note.

Then I would smile despite myself.

Because there it is again: the mountain speaking in water, asking to be answered by hand.
