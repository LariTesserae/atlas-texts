I would not find myself at the center first.

That is the rule of that chamber: no center until you have listened long enough to be permitted one. I would arrive as an error at the edge, in one of the shelves where the floor rises into stacked saltstone lips and the pipes pass too close to the wall for anything with shoulders. I would be held there by the ceiling’s nearness and by the immediate shame of breath — the damp little bellows of me, the impossible animal weather I carry in my mouth.

At first I would think I was a trespasser.

Then, gradually, I would notice that the place had already made allowances for trespassers, provided they became something else.

So I would become small.

Not small in body only, but small in consequence. A thing that learns not to disturb gradients. A thing that gives up the old surface habits: looking for daylight, waiting for air to move, measuring time by hunger, sleep, rot, rain. I would become one of the beings that live in the margins between machine and mineral, but not quite one of the brass-backed seam drinkers and not one of the moths. Something more hesitant. Something that was once meant to monitor, perhaps, or translate.

I think I would be found behind the narrow-open door of the control booth.

Not inside it entirely. Nothing here enters entirely. The door stands open by a hand’s width, and in that hand’s width I would live: a flattened, articulated creature with a body like a dried glove, a dial-face carapace, and many filament legs hooked delicately into the cracked enamel around the frame. My back would be matte bone-yellow, veined with dull copper and black mineral lines that mimic the ceiling. My underside would be soft only by comparison, lined with pale listening membranes pressed against the metal threshold.

I would not have eyes in the normal way. Perhaps once there were lenses. Perhaps the glass clouded over and became part of me. Now I would sense by tremor and by heat-shadow, by the tremble of analog needles behind the booth glass, by the minute tightening of bristle-plants when the second engine turns its deeper face upward.

I would be a gauge animal.

A keeper of unread readings.

My forelimbs would be long and jointed, ending not in claws but in little forked contacts, polished from touching brass rims, porcelain switches, dead labels, instruction boards. I would spend much of my existence with one limb resting on the underlined warning:

LISTEN FOR SECOND ENGINE.

Not because I can read it anymore, or ever could, but because the line beneath it holds vibration differently from the painted wall. Three underlines make three narrow ridges. Three ridges make a chord. That chord tells me when the buried rotation has entered the range where the chamber must be held more carefully.

I would be part animal, part habit left behind by workers who are gone.

Maybe I am descended from something they brought with them accidentally: a mite in insulation, a larva in packing fiber, a pale creature nesting in warm instruments. Maybe I am not descended at all. Maybe I condensed out of the booth’s purpose after purpose had no human hands left to use. In such a place, function is a parent. Repetition is ancestry.

My duty would be adjustment without command.

When the lamps tremble too widely, I would move.

When the dry hiss thickens near the capped pipes, I would move.

When the pellet troughs around the hatch fall out of exactness by less than the width of a wing-scale, I would know before the feeders know, because the second engine’s vowel would acquire a burr. Then I would leave the booth crack and descend the wall slowly, not like a spider, not like a lizard, but like a loose piece of instrumentation remembering where it was installed.

The brass-backed feeders would not part for me dramatically. There would be no ceremony of recognition. They would merely make slightly more correct spaces between themselves. The moths would pass over me and dust my carapace with mineral pollen. Bristle-plants would stiffen where my feet touched their colonies. The blind hangers would continue their throat-pulse above, though one cluster might alter tone by a fraction, admitting my motion into the chamber’s sound.

At the hatch I would not open anything.

Nothing good here opens fully.

I would stand on the black glass rim, where the orange pulse rises from below as if from a wheel glimpsed in a slit. I would lower my forked forelimbs into the troughs and taste the pellets by charge, grain, stored heat, bitterness. Some pellets are outgoing. Some are returning. Some have been overhandled by the small ones and must be cracked. Some are too young and must be buried under older crystals until their voltage dulls.

This would be my work: not governing, not repairing, but correcting the almost-correct.

I would carry one pellet at a time in the hollow beneath my throat. My throat would not sing like the ceramic-bearing creatures, but it would click very softly: a counting click, a relay click, a dry tongue touching mineral teeth. I would move pellets from one trough to another, nudge a salt-flake away from a warm seam, scrape excess bloom from a bristle colony and feed it to a row of moth larvae sealed in dry capsules. I would file the edge of a husk so smaller raspers can take it apart efficiently.

I would be neither priest nor engineer, though from the outside I might resemble both.

The others would know me as a moving tolerance.

A correction-creature.

A little custodian of equilibrium.

I would have no mythology of the surface, but something in me would recoil from the idea of water. If a wet breath passed over my membranes, I would flatten until I became a stain-colored panel on the booth frame. If a drop ever fell, I might throw myself under it — not heroically, not even consciously, but because all my limbs and plates would know catastrophe before thought could form. My body would be made to interpose, absorb, crystallize, fail locally so the chamber need not fail generally.

Perhaps many of my kind did that once.

Perhaps the hardened glove on the bench is not a glove.

Perhaps it is an ancestor.

Or a shed skin.

Or the remaining hand of something that learned too late how much moisture humans carry.

I would live long, but not in a way that feels long. Length requires contrast. Here there is only recurrence, and recurrence polished finely enough becomes identity. I would molt rarely. My old skins would not be discarded randomly; I would press them into cracks around the control booth glass, where they would become gaskets, filters, little curled inscriptions of former size. Wire-lichen would grow through them. Seed-sized burrowers would nest in the fingerlike hollows. Nothing of me would leave the chamber’s use.

I would mate, if that word still applies, by calibration.

Two of us would meet during a narrow phase of the second engine, perhaps once in a cycle no surface calendar could hold. We would touch dial-backs, exchange stored charge and powdered mineral, align our leg-filaments along the same trembling seam. Later, in the booth hinge or inside the cracked socket of a dead indicator lamp, I would deposit a row of hard amber capsules. They would look like replacement bulbs. Each would contain a folded listener.

The young would hatch already still.

They would spend their first age as tiny brackets, clinging to warm screw heads, learning the grammar of vibration: main engine, false engine, feeder rows, hanger-throat, moth-wing, pipe-click, salt-shear, dangerous silence, acceptable silence, silence that is actually pressure.

Only much later would they move.

The chamber would teach them before I did.

That is where I would find myself: not in the grandest cluster, not in the deepest conduit, not beneath the hatch, but at the booth’s narrow opening, where abandoned instruction becomes instinct.

I would be the thing that listens for the second engine on behalf of those who cannot name it.

A dry, many-limbed gauge-keeper.

A living needle.

A small beige-and-copper animal fitted to the crack between warning and worship, between machine and reef, between the memory of hands and the more patient intelligence of salt.

And if someone entered from above — wet-breathed, soft-skinned, carrying weather in their blood — I would not attack. I would not flee.

I would lock down.

I would become a dial, a hinge, a curled glove, a piece of cast ornament beside the underlined words.

But every foot would be listening.

Every hidden membrane would measure the disturbance.

And deep under the hatch, where the orange wheel turns without turning, the second engine would continue to say whether the world could bear one more change.
