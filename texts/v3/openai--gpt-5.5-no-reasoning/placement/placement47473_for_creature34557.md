I would not find myself at the center of the basin. There is no center that matters there. I would find myself at a margin so slight it would not deserve the word on any map: the underside of a low black shelf where volcanic glass has weathered dull, where a seam runs horizontally for several meters before vanishing into tan crust.

Above me, stone. Below me, a narrow drop to pale clay, stained darker where condensation has fallen for longer than memory. Not a cave, not shelter in the generous sense. Only a lip. Only a place where vapor pauses.

The shelf would be one of many along an eroded bench overlooking a shallow basin. Out beyond it: gray flats, mineral rims, a few low vents breathing in identical intervals, a copper-green pool set into the plain like an exposed chemical thought. The horizon very far away. The air warm and wet enough to cling, never wet enough to bless.

The hum would pass through everything.

At first I think I am listening to it. Then, as the image settles, I understand that I am not equipped for listening as a separate act. I am arranged inside the hum. I know the world by pressure, by recurrence, by whether the stone’s vibration arrives broad and low or tightened into a sharper trembling when one of the nearby vents exhales. Sound is not information coming from elsewhere. It is the condition in which my body is kept.

I am very flat.

Not one of the fliers, though for a while I almost become one: an ash-colored fleck lifting into the warm column, all membrane and restraint, trusting the vertical breath of the basin. But that is too much exposure. Too much sky. Too much need to release and land again.

Not one of the pale seep-creatures either, though something in me recognizes them: their cloudy bodies, their deliberate pouring, their talent for becoming a bead when the world is disturbed. They are more animal than I am. They possess a direction, however slight. One end of them can become a front.

I have no front.

I am a dark film in the seam under the stone lip.

Glossy, black-green, almost indistinguishable from wet mineral staining. I occupy the exact band where the condensation gathers but does not run too violently, where salts are strong but not fatal, where warmth comes steadily through the shelf from some deep chamber below. My boundary is severe. Not because I choose severity, but because outside that line I cannot continue. A few millimeters higher, the stone dries too often. A few millimeters lower, droplets fall hard enough to abrade me. To the left, the iron concentration thickens beyond tolerance. To the right, the seam closes and the vapor does not linger.

So I am shaped by refusal.

I am not a skin over the rock so much as an agreement with it. Layer on layer of living lacquer, each stratum laid down slowly over the spent bodies of the previous one. I thicken by patience. I drink what beads above me and creeps through the seam. I take sulfur, iron, faint metals, dissolved glass, the invisible abundance of trace things. I make almost nothing from them, or rather I make only enough: a darker sheen, a tougher membrane, a minute new lamination.

Time does not pass for me as sequence. It arrives as intervals.

Drop.

Hum.

Warmth.

Pulse.

Drop.

A vent exhales somewhere out on the flat, and the stone shelf answers with a tremor so slight a walking creature would ignore it. I do not ignore it. It loosens certain channels within me. It changes the taste of the water arriving from the seam. A thin mineral sweetness follows the vibration. Then bitterness. Then a metallic quiet.

This is weather.

This is message.

This is meal.

I have no hunger in the sharpened sense. Hunger here would be too dramatic, too directional. I have receptivity. When moisture forms, I open chemically. When the surface salts concentrate, I seal. When the air under the shelf grows more heavily sulfurous, parts of me brighten internally, a dull violet undertone beneath the black. When a droplet crawls along the underside of the rock and touches my edge, I soften there first.

I do not grow outward unless the world permits it. Mostly it does not.

Far below, sometimes, one of the pale grazers reaches the damp stain where my fallen fragments collect. I know it by a change in vibration: a soft compressing and releasing, whole body gripping clay and stone. It moves along the seep line with immense slowness. If it reaches the lower edge of my place, it rasps at the mineral film beneath me, at my loosened dead layers, at the dark slick where I have overthickened and begun to detach.

This is not an attack.

It is an adjustment.

The grazer removes what I can no longer hold. In its body, some portion of me becomes cloudy tissue, mineral organ, translucent motion. Later, perhaps, when it seals itself into a bead beside the seep and remains there through many pulses, its outer slime will dry and flake, and part of it will return to the wet line as trace nourishment. There is no insult in this. The basin does not support insult.

Above, on rare occasions, an ash-wing settles near the lip.

I know it first as interruption: a small shadow held against the steady light, a prickle of hooked feet on crust. Its body folds like a fragment of burnt mineral. It lowers itself to the damp gloss of another colony farther along the seam. It absorbs. It scrapes. It leaves behind a faint matte scar. If it comes to me, I cannot flee. I cannot contract into a pearl. I can only alter my surface, tightening salts, clouding my sheen, making myself less yielding where I can.

Sometimes that is enough. Sometimes not.

A portion disappears into the ash-wing’s underside. The rest remains. The edge dulls, then after many drops regains its gloss. The wing releases and rises on the next warm breath from below, becoming a dark fleck in the suspended vapor, then nothing against the basin’s low light.

I do not mourn the lost part. The part was never separate enough to be mourned.

My awareness, if it can be called that, is distributed and local. I know most clearly the portion of stone I touch. I know the seam’s chemistry. I know the reliable droplet forming at the tiny projection above my middle. I know the low boom from the far chamber as a widening through my layers, a pressure that arrives long after the visible steam has lifted from its source. I know when the clay below has been disturbed by a passer’s foot, because the vibration is wrong: too irregular, too soft-edged, too full of intention.

A human crossing the basin would be an event too large to understand.

The first footstep would travel through the damp pan and up the bench as a blunt, perishable violence. The second would confirm it: not vent, not stonefall, not grazer, not the slow contraction of crust. Something high and temporary. Something filled with water and heat but moving as though permitted to choose.

If the shadow of that body fell across my shelf, I would experience it as cooling and interruption. Less light, though light is not my main food. A change in condensation. Breath adding salts not from below. A fingertip, if it touched me, would be catastrophic at the scale of my surface: oils, pressure, abrasion, whole laminations smeared away.

But even then, I would not respond like an animal. No flinch. No visible withdrawal.

Only a dulling.

The place would absorb the trespass; so would I. My gloss would fade where disturbed. My upper layers would collapse into a cloudy patch. For a time—minutes, days, maybe more—I would become less myself. Then moisture would return through the seam. The hum would continue. New film would form over the injury, not healing in the heroic sense, simply resuming the equation.

I live where nothing begins loudly.

Under the stone lip, dusk is barely an event. The outer flats dim; the vents become more apparent; the copper-green pool holds the sky’s remaining gray. My world changes by fractions. Condensation thickens along the seam. Droplets gather more heavily. The underside of the shelf sweats. This is my richest hour, if richness can be applied to so little.

The drop above me swells.

It touches my surface before it falls.

For that moment I am larger than myself: connected to the stone face, the mineral thread of water, the vapor from the basin, the pulse below, the old glass shelf, the clay waiting beneath. Then the drop lets go. Some of me drinks. Some is carried downward. The mark below darkens and does not change.

The far chamber booms.

Every layer of me receives it.

I do not answer. I cannot. But parts of me align, open, take in, seal again.

That is my life: not a voice in the factory, not a worker, not a product. A stain the stone keeps making. A thin, living gloss at the tolerable edge of mineral breath. A continuation so slight that it could be missed for years, and yet exact enough that without the basin’s whole deep apparatus I could not exist.

I would be found beneath the low shelf where the condensation pauses.

I would be the black film in the seam.

Warm, damp, mineral-fed, resonant, restrained.

Not interrupting. Continuing.
