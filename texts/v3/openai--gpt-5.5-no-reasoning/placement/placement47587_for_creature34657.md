I think I would be found where the city becomes unsure whether it is street, drain, archive, or animal passage.

Not in the highest walkways at first, though I would look up at them often. Not in the courts of judgment, not among the rain wards with their faces lifted to the ceiling, not in the Shell Archive except as an errand-shadow at its threshold. I would live lower and sideways, in the marginal seam between inhabited rooms and the damp abandoned ones: the under-levels children know, the passages adults use only when repair demands it.

A place of crawlways, capped channels, cistern mouths, old thresholds half-blocked by replacement masonry. A district where the limestone paving has been lifted and relaid so many times that the street remembers several slopes at once. A little below one of the broad empty plazas, beneath the well with the three cracked measuring posts, there is a run of low chambers once used for storage, then for animals, then sealed after a bad damp year, then reopened by children looking for beetle eggs. That is where I would be.

Not quite a child. Not quite one of the titled people.

I would be a gutter-keeper, or something like it.

One of the small necessary citizens whose work is too mixed to earn a clean name: part animal tender, part water-runner, part listener, part cleaner of covered channels, part witness to what enters the city when rain makes every old route alive again. I would know the undersides of streets. I would know which paving flags lift without complaint and which must not be touched because the wall beside them leans on their silence. I would carry a hook of polished bone, a coil of gut-cord, a shallow cup, chalk, a shell tally, and a lidded jar for eggs, larvae, lost tokens, or things that ought to be shown to someone wiser.

My hands would be long, cracked white at the knuckles with lime. My knees would be scarred from stone. My hair would be cropped close and always dusted pale no matter its original color. I would wear a patched mantle shortened for crawling, with little weighted hems so it did not catch in drains. Around my wrist would be tied the narrow blue-grey cord of a household with minor rights to a public drip—not an important seam, not one worth fighting over except during long dry, but enough to make us cautious with pride.

I would be one of those people who notices the animals before the people.

Before rain, I would see the beetles reverse direction in the carved gutter. I would see the lizards leave the kiln mouths too early. I would see the leathery birds shift along the arch ribs, clicking under their breath. I would know when a cistern grazer has stopped rasping because the water has changed taste. I would know which dogs can be trusted to nose out dead crabs without scattering a whole basket. I would know the disk-things on wet stone by the slight tightening of their rims before they open.

People would call me useful before they called me wise.

During dry stretches I would move through the covered channels with a lamp unlit at my belt, because the lamp is not for seeing. It is for warning if I find a body, or a bad crack, or a living thing in water that ought not be disturbed. Most of my seeing would be done by the cold cave-light that enters everywhere indirectly, sliced through grates, reflected from wet plaster, carried along pale mineral seams like thought moving through bone.

My work would begin where public cleanliness meets superstition. The city makes gaps for animals, but gaps gather trouble. A channel meant for crabs may invite hares. A hare passage may admit dogs. Dogs disturb beetle processions. Beetles reveal damp behind walls. Damp calls children. Children remove stones. Removed stones alter water. Water wakes histories nobody requested.

So I would go where something has begun using the wrong path.

I would crouch at the edge of a cistern court while old women argue with hand-signs over whose marked shell-grazer has been eating whose wall-film. I would lie flat beside a slab-covered canal and listen to water ticking where there should be none. I would scrape mineral rind from a spillway and taste the salt of it, not because I was supposed to but because everyone in that city learns forbidden measures. I would bring messages to crack-listeners and resent the way they thank no one, because to thank a messenger for bad news is considered poor form.

I would have a reputation for finding small lost things.

A child’s ownership bead dropped through a paving seam. A bronze pin gone missing from a marriage cloak, discovered in a crab trap three districts away. A clay token swollen in a damp channel but still legible. Once, perhaps, a cup. That would matter. A cup has intimacy. To recover one from beneath the street would be almost indecent; I would return it wrapped, eyes lowered, saying only where it was found, not what had nested in it.

I would not be solemn enough for my elders.

The city teaches restraint, but the under-levels teach a different humor. Down there, everything dignified leaks eventually. Water judges, archive attendants, marriage measurers, all of them depend on drains that clog with beetle husks and crab limbs and children’s chalk. I would know this and carry the knowledge like a private grin. I would laugh against my wrist. I would be rebuked often for laughing too visibly near public seams.

But I would be afraid of darkness.

Not theatrical fear. Not the fear of a child dared into a sealed room. A practical, marrowed fear. In my work I would sometimes find old service passages where the mineral light fails—not dims, but stops. Places behind collapsed walls, beneath capped channels, under the plaza where the old boat rings are said to continue downward into chalk. I would own a lamp for those places, and I would hate lighting it. Flame is too alive. Darkness is too old. Between them, a person becomes briefly negotiable.

There would be one passage I avoided.

Everyone who works beneath the streets has such a passage. Mine would begin behind a stack of discarded gypsum plates from the White Canal. It would slope gently, impossibly, because all the known channels nearby slope the other way. The floor there would be smooth, not from feet but from water long gone. Along one wall would run a line of holes at shoulder height, green-rimmed with copper salts, like places where rings had been pulled out. When rain is long and the city loud enough to forgive shouting, that passage would breathe cold.

Once, as a younger person, I would have gone too far into it.

Not far by mapkeeper standards. Far enough to hear water below stone. Far enough to find a mineral flower open in darkness, though adults insist they need damp light to uncurl. Far enough to see, in the lamp’s little hunger, scratches on the wall that were not writing but had been made with the intention of writing. Far enough to understand that the city above is not built over the past like a lid, but floats on agreements with things still pressing upward.

I would tell no court. Not at first.

I would tell an old crack-listener, eventually, because guilt makes its own drip. She would listen without looking at me and ask whether the passage clicked, sighed, tapped, or hummed. I would say: none. It breathed. She would close her eyes. Then she would give me a packet of dry powder and say to mark whether the breath carried moisture next time.

There is always a next time in that city.

I would have kin, but not an easy household.

Perhaps my mother or mother’s sister would be a cistern judge’s assistant, someone who measures depth by cord and thumb and distrusts generosity when it appears too suddenly. Perhaps my father would be gone into another hollow with a repair party and not returned, which is not the same as dead until a route is formally closed. Perhaps my household would keep three shell-marked grazers and claim five, two being ancestral claims attached to empty shells hung above the door. This would be legal in some courts and embarrassing in others.

Our doorway would have a hide curtain patched with fish-skin and hare leather. A blue smear for drip-right. Two suspended bones for household animals dead in good standing. A small bead asking for quiet, often ignored by relatives. Inside: jars, racks, nets, sealed skins, my grandmother’s cup, my own cup hung too high for visiting children to touch, and on the wall a sequence of scratches recording rains important to us but not to history.

I would love the White Canal beyond reason.

Not as priests love sacred things, if there are priests. Not as children love shouting. I would love it because it is the one place where the city’s hidden systems become visible all at once. Dry, it is merely a scar of pale plates, constantly repaired, scuffed by feet and sledges. But when water enters, the whole city confesses. Channels wake, roof-drains speak, districts upstream announce themselves by taste and foam and debris. Beetles, lost tools, crab claws, pigment dust, fish from impossible pockets, flakes of old plaster, once maybe a carved bone tally from a sealed house—all of it comes flashing through opaque white water.

During canal flow I would be allowed to shout.

I would shout not words but warnings, directions, laughter, claims. I would lean over with my hook and net, wet to the ribs, while children screamed beside me and elders pretended not to enjoy the permitted noise. The water would be cold enough to numb the hand. The gypsum plates would shine as though the city had opened a vein of milk.

And afterward, when the canal emptied and the citizens went back to murmuring, I would walk its bed looking for what the brief river had decided to leave.

Maybe that is why I would become what I am: because I would not believe the city is only what stands.

The towers, arches, cistern courts, public markets, animal yards, shell archive—all of that is the face. Necessary, dignified, always being repaired. But below and inside it are movements too small, too wet, too animal, too intermittent to enter law until they cause trouble. I would serve those movements without quite admitting devotion.

Over time, people would begin to ask for me by a task-name.

Not my birth name. Something like Under-Runner, Gutter-Ear, Whitehook, Slab-Child even after I was grown. Names in that city gather from use. Perhaps children would call me Crab Aunt or Drain Uncle regardless of gender, because children name by function and affection. Officials would call me only when embarrassed. Mapkeepers would borrow what I knew and convert it into cords and slats without credit. Crack-listeners would tolerate me. Rain wards would ignore me unless I contradicted them correctly.

I would grow into a person who smells weather from below.

The rain wards read the ceiling; I would read the floor. Before a ceiling rain, I might know it from pressure in capped channels, from thread-jellies withdrawing in wells, from blind fish stirring in cistern corners, from a faint chill moving along the old dry tubes at the basin edge. I would not predict rain in public; that would be arrogance. But I would set jars under certain grates. I would move my household’s grazers to the safer tank. I would warn the potter whose drying court always floods from beneath before it gets wet from above.

And one day, perhaps, I would be called to a measuring well where a lamp floats on the surface.

That sign means something living has entered that should not be disturbed.

People would stand back. A cistern judge would be present, and an animal counter, and someone from the household whose rights are tied to that water. They would speak softly and too much. The lamp would drift though there is no wind below. The water would be clear enough to show coins, bones, pale grazers clinging to the side walls—and beneath them a motion too large for any known cistern fish.

I would lie on my belly and look.

Not a monster. The city does not need monsters. Something worse and quieter: a pale swimmer from an old sealed sump, perhaps. Or a dog drowned years ago and remade by mineral and rumor. Or a cluster of thread-jellies braided together around a child’s lost shell necklace. Or only a fish grown impossible in hidden water beneath the streets.

My task would not be to name it. Naming belongs to courts and archives.

My task would be to decide whether the cistern must be sealed, drained, fed, watched, or forgiven.

That is the kind of authority I would eventually have: not power over people, but the right to recommend a response to an ambiguous life.

I might become old that way.

Not a grand elder under an arch with measuring cords. Not a rain ward whose upward gaze children imitate. An under-city elder, knees ruined, palms callused smooth, hearing less of speech and more of stone. I would sit beside lifted paving slabs and instruct younger gutter-keepers in the etiquette of dampness.

Do not put your hand where the white crabs gather facing inward.

Do not scrape all the film from a wall unless you want the grazers to leave.

Do not trust a dry channel during the second hour of rain.

If a dog refuses a passage, pay attention.

If beetles cross your chalk mark and then return, call a crack-listener.

If you find a cup, wrap it.

If a mineral flower opens where there is no light, do not touch it, and do not say aloud that you saw it until you have washed your hands.

I would still go sometimes to the high walkways.

From there I would see the whole bright hollow spread out: pale roofs separated by dust-colored distance, silent figures with jars, birds turning like ash, scaffolds around leaning walls, the White Canal a dry curve or sudden brilliance. I would see the city’s patience as outsiders see it, austere and beautiful. But I would also know what runs beneath each plaza, which court stinks faintly of old crab, which graceful wall has a drain too narrow for the hares, which empty avenue is crossed nightly by beetles in such numbers that the dust is combed smooth by morning.

I would not be a hero there.

The place does not seem to produce heroes easily. It produces maintainers, witnesses, negotiators, people whose lives are clauses in old agreements. I would be one of them: a keeper of small passages, a reader of wet stone at ankle height, a citizen of drains and animal routes, someone called when water appears where it should not and when something living takes advantage of the city’s mercy.

If asked what I am, I might answer with the modesty expected:

I keep the lower gaps.

But privately, against the back of my hand, I would think:

I serve the part of the city that remembers it was once a sea.
