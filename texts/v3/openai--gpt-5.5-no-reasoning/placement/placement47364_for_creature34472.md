I would find myself where the town almost becomes wall.

Not at its center, not in the shared courtyards where bowls pass from window to window and families argue over shade, nor high among the ancestor niches where the dead keep their slow oversight. I would be in the in-between height: along the inner crater wall, where cut chambers, storage mouths, lizard cracks, bee tubes, and trellised gardens all overlap. A place reached by a narrow stair worn hip-smooth by generations of sideways bodies. A ledge wide enough for one careful sleeper, three clay jars, a rack of drying herbs, and a screen of grey moss kept damp beneath folded cloth.

There, the town is both below and above me.

Below: the knot of roofs, cages, pots, goats, children, smoke, and green cords. Above: the basalt rim, the dead watching places, the morning nets whitening before dawn. Behind me: the mountain’s old heat, held in stone. In front of me: the whole compact discipline of survival, murmuring in shadow.

I think I would not be one of the elders, not yet. Nor a child. I would be someone useful because I know thresholds.

A keeper of small passages.

Perhaps I am the person called when something moves where it should not: bees abandoning a tube, damp appearing beneath a storage slab, lizards gathering near a cistern seam, moths in a seed basket, roots pushing through an old burial wall. I would know the minor inhabitants of the town—not in a scholarly way, but through habit and mutual trespass. I would know which cracks hold eggs, which clay pipes sing differently when there is a leak, which reed screens grow a sweeter lichen if shaded by bird cages, which bee tubes must be warmed by hand before a cold dawn.

I would be narrow-shouldered, resin-smelling, my fingers nicked by stone and thorn. My clothes would have many pockets: bone hook, shell scraper, cord gauge, wax plug, a pinch of bitter ash wrapped in leaf, two spare tube stoppers, a shard of polished dark stone used as a mirror for looking into low places. I would move with my head tilted slightly, listening not for speech but for tiny discontinuities: a silence where beetles should be working, a wet click under a wall, the wrong tone in a goat bell, a bee-hum turned sharp.

My dwelling would be less a house than a stitched-together responsibility.

One chamber cut into the crater wall, cool even at noon. One outer platform under a slanted roof of reed and cactus ribs. A trellis that belongs half to me and half to a cousin who argues that the western vine roots on her side. Three ranks of ceramic bee tubes set into a warm niche. A moss screen grown over old funeral tiles. A sleeping mat rolled above a row of stoppered jars. In the back wall, hidden behind hanging seed ropes, a sealed ancestral niche—not of a direct parent, perhaps, but of someone whose tools I inherited, whose name gives me the right to work that ledge.

I would wake before sunrise, when the town is still mostly breath and prediction.

The air would be thin and soft with almost-fog. I would climb to the nets with others, my feet knowing where not to press. There would be no grandeur in the work, only attention. Shell spoon along hair-fiber. Droplets into the lip of a clay bottle. Pause when the wind trembles the net. Scrape again. Seal. Count. Pass down.

Then, while the first birds cut the crater open with wings, I would return to the bee wall.

This would be my true office. Not ownership—no one really owns creatures that fly—but treaty. I would warm the entrance stones with my palm. Remove the night plugs. Watch the first uncertain bodies emerge, dusted, furred, irritable with life. I would know the sound of a healthy tube: a low braided hum, neither frantic nor thin. I would tap certain blossoms open before they close too soon. The pale yellow flowers would accept this interference as they accept all the town’s persuasions: reluctantly, then as custom.

People would come to me with small problems that are actually large.

A child has found red powder under a cistern stair—is it mineral bloom or beetle dust?

A widow’s roof vine flowered but did not set fruit—are the bees neglecting her side, or did smoke sour the blossoms?

A goat has rubbed its tether against the lower moss wall—can the damaged mat be regrown before the hot weeks?

The western shade smells wrong after dusk—dead lizard, cracked jar, or hidden damp?

I would answer slowly, because quick answers waste futures.

I would be respected a little, resented a little. Useful people in such a town are never purely loved. I might be summoned into inheritance quarrels: whose bees serviced whose flowers, whose vine crossed whose shadow, whether a family has the right to move a tube cluster from a wall built by another grandmother. I would be accused, sometimes, of favoring one courtyard over another by advising where to hang new bee clay. I would shrug too often. I would know that neutrality is another kind of resource, and also that no one truly possesses it.

I would have a particular tenderness for lizards.

Not sacredness, exactly. More like professional admiration. They would understand the town’s surfaces better than any human hand. Ash-colored bodies in warm cracks. Ocher throats pulsing. Black bead eyes measuring danger without alarm. I would never chase them from my sleeping platform unless they came too close to the jars. A house with lizards is a house still negotiating correctly with insects.

Children would follow me sometimes, because children like those who notice what adults step over. I would teach them how to look into a bee tube without breathing into it. How to lift a moss cloth from the lower edge first. How to tell damp worth saving from damp that means rot. How to cup a moth and release it outside the seed room. How to listen to a wall.

“Stone speaks after heat,” I might tell them, and they would roll their eyes because all useful sayings become tiresome before they become true.

At midday I would disappear into the wall chamber.

The stone there would hold a dim warmth, never the full cruelty of the sun. I would grind old comb, ash, and goat-hair fiber into sealing paste. I would mend cracked tube mouths with slip. I would mark tiny clay counters with household signs: three for the eastern court’s new brood chambers, one for the widow’s failed vine, two owed to the family that loaned shade cloth during a moth hatch. My work would smell of wax, dust, old honey, clay, and the sour sweetness of fermenting mash.

I would eat little and often: seed paste pressed with bitter herb; a strip of dried root; lichen cake softened against the cheek before swallowing. I would drink in measured privacy. No one here drinks carelessly in view of another person.

In the evening, I would climb.

Not all the way to the rim, perhaps, but to the ancestor ledges where the reed screens hang and the air thins into silence. I would carry a small bowl of lichen wash and a feather brush. Some punishment, some duty, some inheritance must have put this task in my hands once, and by now it has become mine.

The dead would be close there. Not ghostly, not dramatic. Present as dry continuance. Wrapped forms behind clay beads. Carved stones showing hands, birds, roots, ridges. Dust collecting at the mouths of names. I would wipe the screens, check for insect damage, press new paste where cracks open. I might complain to them under my breath.

“The western families are fighting about the vine again.”

“The red-throated birds came late.”

“One of the new children scraped a net without tearing it.”

“There is damp in the third lower seam. I heard it before anyone else did.”

I would not expect answers. The town answers by remaining.

What would I be, finally?

Not priest, not farmer, not craftsperson only. Something smaller and more necessary.

A custodian of minute continuities.

A wall-listener. Bee-tender. Damp-finder. Keeper of clay throats and moss skins. One who works where household, garden, animal, dead, and stone cannot be separated. One whose authority comes from noticing the early stages of loss.

My life would not be expansive. I would not travel beyond the rim except in stories brought by traders and exaggerated by children. Rivers would trouble me. Rain would seem violent. I would mistrust abundance in the way others mistrust hunger.

But I would know this crater with the intimacy of a body.

I would know the morning color of each wall. The pitch of each cistern lid when tapped. The bell of each goat. The smell of each family’s smoke. The hour when the pale flowers loosen. The ledge where fog gathers first. The stair that sweats falsely after hot days. The ancestor niche that must be shaded from kiln smoke. The places where roots are allowed to cross, and the places where they must be cut though everyone winces.

And when I died, if I had lived carefully enough to be remembered without bitterness, they would dry me and wrap me and set me into the inner wall above my ledge.

Perhaps they would grind one finger bone into meal for the bee flowers. Perhaps my measuring cord would pass to a child who had learned to listen. Perhaps my niche stone would show a small clay tube, a lizard, and a closed hand holding a drop.

Below me, the town would continue: scraping, sealing, grafting, arguing, mourning, pollinating, tightening knots.

The mountain would remain.

And I would remain in the way things remain there—not separate, not gone, but entered into maintenance.
