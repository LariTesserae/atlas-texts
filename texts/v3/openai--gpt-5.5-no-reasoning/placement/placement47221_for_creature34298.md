I would find myself at the rim of the pale oval, but not in it.

Not quite on the open pan, either. I would be where the floor begins to fail into margin: a narrow, undecided band where the basin’s sealed center gives way to claystone dust, old salts, faintly different chemistry, and the first permissions of life. I would not stand high enough to alter the place. I would be low, almost flush with the ground, part of the dark smallness that gathers where the mineral crust loosens.

At first I would be easy to mistake for an old stain.

A dull patch along a seam. A little soot-colored roughness where the powder has hardened. A bruise in the ground, gray-green and brown-violet, with no clear edge. Not a shrub, not a succulent, not merely lichen, not mineral alone. Something that has accepted the basin’s terms so completely that life in me would appear as texture before it appeared as body.

I think I would be a crust-being.

A thin, patient organism spread over a few handspans of clay and salt, living half in the surface and half just beneath it. My body would not rise so much as adhere. I would inhabit the upper skin of the basin: binding grains, darkening them, taking the faintest vapor from warm air, the least chemical offering from old mineral pores. I would not have a face or a mouth. I would not move across the hollow. My motion would be increase by imperceptible margin, a widening too slow to witness.

I would be found where one of the wiry shrubs casts its smallest darkness, not beneath it exactly, but beside it, in the zone altered by its roots and fallen gray threads. The shrub would not shelter me in any generous sense. There is no generosity here. But its presence would have changed the dust by a degree: trapped a little powder, interrupted a little wind, left behind a fiber or two for time to reduce. That would be enough. Enough is very small here.

My color would be difficult to name in the settled dark. Ash-olive when looked at from one side. Brown-black from another. A faint bluish cast in the oldest portions, where my skin had thickened and mineral grains had become embedded in me. My living edge would be paler, though not bright: a narrow smoky green-gray lip advancing into the clay by less than the width of a seed husk over many seasons. The dead center of me might look more like stone than the stone does, sealed and dull and exact.

I would live by interruption almost too slight to count.

When the air cools by a fraction and the dryness loosens its grip, I would open inwardly. Not visibly. No unfolding, no flowering, no gesture. But pores, films, hidden membranes would become receptive. A trace of moisture would pass into me, not water as an animal understands water, not a drink, not relief, but permission. Chemistry would resume. Stored dust would become food. Salts would be sorted, tolerated, rejected, incorporated. Light, such as it is, would be taken slowly through pigments too dark to seem hopeful.

Then I would close again.

Most of my life would be closure.

A held condition. A pause extended until it becomes identity. I would not experience waiting as emptiness, because waiting would not be separate from being alive. I would be made for intervals so long that action and inaction lose their difference. In the basin’s old warmth I would sleep without surrendering. I would dry without dying. I would become brittle at the surface and remain possible beneath.

Around me would be the others, though “around” would not mean companionship in any animal way.

A hand-high succulent several paces off, ribbed and tight, would occupy a crack I cannot enter. Its roots would descend where I spread. We would share no speech, but our chemistries might recognize boundaries. A wiry shrub would hold its black little leaves closed against the air. A few dormant seeds would lie in powder between us, hard as grit, perhaps produced before the oldest visible stem had died back. The pale oval would remain empty at the center, a brightness too pure and sealed for us. It would be our negative sun: the place that defines us by excluding us.

I would know the basin through contact, not sight.

The old plates of the pan by their tensions.
The seams by their gathered salts.
The wind by the grains it removes from my edge.
Heat by expansion.
Dark by constancy.
Rare moisture by awakening.

I would not know the sky except as a pressure above and an absence of direct fire. I would not know distance except as chemistry thinning away from me. I would not know time except as the slow alteration of my own margins: a crescent lost to abrasion, a film renewed, a buried portion dying into mineral, a living thread finding another pore.

If I had a self, it would be distributed and shallow.

No single center. No thought like a flame. More like a held pattern in the upper dust. A persistence. A rule continuing to apply. Break off a piece of me and that piece, if lodged in a kinder seam, might become me again. Dry me for years and I might remain. Wet me too much and I would fail. Cover me deeply and I would vanish into inactive darkness. Expose me harshly and I would retreat into spore, stain, residue, possibility.

I would be one of the basin’s marginal scripts, but perhaps the oldest kind: a sentence written almost flat against the earth, legible only to patience.

Not the shrub that looks like decision.

Not the succulent that looks like stored will.

Not the seed that is a future sealed against itself.

I would be the tarnish between mineral and life, the almost-nothing that teaches dust to hold together.

A living crust at the basin’s edge.

I would need almost nothing.

I would be almost nothing.

And in that place, almost nothing is a way to remain.
