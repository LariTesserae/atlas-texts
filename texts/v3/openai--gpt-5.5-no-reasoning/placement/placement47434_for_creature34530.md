I would find myself not at the center, but just off it.

The central basin is too exposed, too declarative: a pale oval of silt and stone where anything crossing it becomes an event. I would not belong there except briefly, as a moving punctuation. Nor would I be high in the bat cracks, folded into darkness with a mammal’s heat and quick heart. I would not be one of the lizards in the clean bars of light, beautiful in their economy of violence. They are too certain of their bodies. They know when to be stone and when to be lightning.

I would be lower, smaller, nearer the damp margins.

At first I thought I would be one of the glass snails on the pool rim: a slow transparent grazier, carrying a weather of organs inside a fragile spiral, making a whole pasture from a stain of green. There is something right about that—about measuring the world by films, beads, mineral slicks; about leaving only a silvered line behind. But the snail is too continuous. Its life is a long verb of eating and moving, eating and moving, trusting the surface immediately beneath it. I think I would need more interruption than that. More disappearance.

Then I thought: perhaps a frog, buried through the dry months beneath the reeds, a small sealed pulse waiting for rain to give it a throat. The frogs understand intervals better than almost anyone. They live in parentheses. They vanish into mud and return as sound. They are little prophecies of water. But they are too sudden when the rain comes, too full of urgent inheritance: call, answer, eggs, haste, tadpoles racing the shrinking pool. Their lives, when opened, are loud with necessity.

No. I would be something less named.

I would live under a stone near one of the pools that does not always remain.

Not the largest pool, the clear one under the shaft of light, where larvae shine and gnats tilt above their own reflections. Too visible. Too much sky arrives there in pieces. I would be under the ledge-shadow pool with the pollen skin, where the water looks oily though it is only dust and flower-gold spread thin. The ledge above it would be low enough that light enters sideways, weakened and greened by surface stems. The mud there would never be fully wet and never fully dry. It would remember water even when water had withdrawn.

I would be a flat silver jointed thing.

Leaf-shaped, almost. Scale-shaped. Something a careless observer would mistake for a shed fragment: a flake of mica, a pale husk, a piece of water made solid and hinged. My body would be broad and thin, built for the space between stone and mud. I would not run. Running belongs to open floors and panicked creatures. I would ripple.

Motion would pass through me in a sideways wave, beginning at one edge and leaving at the other. I would go nowhere abruptly. I would simply become elsewhere beneath the stone.

My eyes, if I had eyes, would be small and poor. Not useless, but unambitious. Light in the hollow is too complicated to trust completely: it falls in ladders, leaks through grass, glows through limestone, trembles before anything has touched it. Better to know the world by pressure, by damp, by mineral taste, by the tremor of a beetle’s feet coming through clay. I would feel droplets before they landed. I would know the difference between condensation and rainwater by the temperature of their arrival. I would know a lizard’s shadow not as darkness, but as a brief change in the warmth above the stone.

My color would not be true silver everywhere. Along my edges I would carry the tan of silt, the chalk-white dust of the ceiling, a faint olive stain from algae films. When dry, I would dull to something nearly invisible. When wet, I would brighten—not beautifully, exactly, but with the brief metallic gleam of a thing revealed against its wishes.

I would feed on what gathers.

Not prey in the heroic sense. Not the hunt. I would browse and absorb and worry at softened matter: dead gnats plastered to mud after a drip, the underside of fallen pollen skins, microbial bloom on the drowned edge of a reed root, the loosening body of a water-flea after the pool retreats. I would be part scavenger, part grazer, part secret custodian of almost-nothing. My mouth would be small and patient. My hunger would be scaled to the hollow’s generosity.

Most of my life would happen in contact.

Ceiling is distance. Open air is risk. The central basin is a white country of exposure. But the underside of stone is intimate: cool irregularities, grains pressed into clay, root hairs entering through cracks, the slow darkening where moisture remains. I would know the map of my stone as a larger animal knows a valley. Here is the shallow groove where water rests after rain. Here is the gritty ridge that catches shed cricket legs. Here is the safe seam too narrow for a frog’s tongue. Here is the place where the stone touches the mud so closely I must flatten myself almost to nothing.

Almost to nothing would be one of my talents.

When the chamber dries, I would not leave. Leaving is for bats, for water, for the brief winged ones that find the cracks by accident. I would remain and reduce. My body would draw itself tight beneath a crust of fine mud. My joints would lock. My breathing, if it could be called breathing, would become a rumor. Around me the hollow would whiten. Pools would shrink to dimples, then to darker stains. Frogs would bury themselves. Snails would seal to stone. Roots would drink the last invisible damp out of the air.

I would wait without counting.

Waiting, in that place, is not emptiness. It is one of the active laws. The hollow is made of waiting: waiting for rain, for shade to move, for eggs to hatch before the pool fails, for a bat to wake, for a root to decide whether to withdraw. I would belong to that law. I would not experience patience as virtue. It would simply be anatomy.

When rain came, I would know before the channel spoke.

The ceiling would change first. Pressure above. A softening in the old crust. Roots tightening. Dust loosening grain by grain. Then the first drops would strike through seams, each one enormous to my body, each one a falling lens full of surface light. The hidden pool would tremble before receiving anything. Then water would arrive along the ledge in a thin dark line and the mud around me would open its smell.

I would unfold.

Not dramatically. No rebirth, no emergence into song. I would loosen joint by joint beneath the stone while frogs began their clicking from the reed mud. The lizards would retreat to higher shelves until the warmth returned. Beetles would climb the slope to avoid the sudden channels. Larvae would wake in basins that yesterday were only stains. The hollow would become briefly multiple, all its paused lives resuming at once.

I would slide to the edge of my stone and drink through my feet, or my skin, or whatever surface of me knows water.

A drowned gnat would touch down nearby, held in the meniscus. I would take it slowly.

I would not think of the world above as a world. It would be a set of consequences: heat through limestone, roots through cracks, hoofstep thunder, pollen on water, seeds that arrive and fail, rain as brown urgency. The sky would be something that happens to the ceiling. Grass would be the animal that sends pale hairs downward. Day would be warmth moving across stone; evening, the time when the ceiling empties its bats.

If I had fear, it would be mostly of exposure.

The lifting of my stone would be apocalypse. A frog’s snout nosing into my seam would be weather with a mouth. A lizard, quick and flat-eyed, would be sunlight turning carnivorous. But panic would not help. My defense would be misdirection without intention: to look like a flake, to move like a ripple, to vanish without producing a burrow. A predator expects a body to flee as one thing. I would flee as a surface disturbance. By the time attention gathered around me, I would already be mud-colored absence.

And yet I would not be lonely.

Loneliness requires a scale of self too large for that hollow. Everything there is adjacent, implicated. Bat guano feeds fungi; fungi feed springtails; springtails feed beetles; beetles feed lizards; lizard droppings return to dust. A root takes water from air that held the breath of frogs. A pool receives pollen from flowers no resident has seen. The dead become film, the film becomes pasture, the pasture becomes shell, the shell becomes mineral again. To live there is to be small enough to know that nothing belongs to itself for long.

I would have neighbors I never meet directly.

Above me, a pale cricket would clean its antennae beneath a ledge. A glass snail would pass once every several days over the wet stone, its organs dimly visible, its trail catching light like a thought. A thread-creature would lower itself through a sun shaft and hang there, absurd and sacred, measuring whatever it measures. The reeds would stand in their collapsed corner, not swaying, each stem a greenish line between mud and dimness. Bats would shift in the ceiling like folded embers. Lizards would occupy the warm bars until the bars moved away from them.

The hollow itself would be the nearest thing to a god, but not one to worship.

It would have moods rather than commands. It would allow and withdraw. It would brighten, dampen, seal, crack, drip. Its wall would darken when breathed upon, showing hidden branching like a memory of lightning. Its pools would ripple early. Its light would lie on the floor with a substance almost like dust. I would not understand these things as mysteries. I would live inside them as one lives inside temperature.

If I were given a name, it would be a name no mouth there could say.

So I would be known by placement instead: the thin one under the ledge-stone; the silver flake near the pollen pool; the sideways mover in the mud that remembers water.

That is where I would find myself: not in the grandeur of the chamber, but in one of its small permissions. Beneath a stone that is warmed from above and dampened from below. Beside a pool that is sometimes water and sometimes only the idea of water left dark in clay. In the margin where light arrives weakened, where roots hesitate, where the hollow keeps its longest pauses.

I would be a creature of almost.

Almost insect, almost leaf, almost mineral, almost gone.

And when the rare drip fell nearby, loud enough to occupy the whole room, I would feel the world enlarge around that single point of water. Then I would move, very slightly, as if the mud itself had decided to breathe.
