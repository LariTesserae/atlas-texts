I would be where the old work almost still works.

Not in the open basins, where the black water gives the illusion of skyless breadth, nor in the deeper cisterns where the pale ferns lie flat against stone like thoughts pressed under glass. Not on the raft-marshes either, though their trembling bodies would know how to keep me, how to take a footstep and close it with brown water and moss.

I would be in a threshold district.

A place of passing, once.

A low quarter of joining canals and service courts, all just beneath the surface, neither grand nor domestic, built to regulate movement between larger bodies of water. There would be stone sills there, swollen timber gates, rows of socket-holes where removable barriers could be lowered, peg-frames, handholds, measuring cuts, overflow slits, carved notches polished by rope. It would not have been ceremonial, though ceremony may have grown around it after enough generations. It would have been necessary. A practical organ of the made ocean.

From above, if anything could see from above, it would be invisible except as a slightly darker geometry under cold water: a cross of channels, a square basin, a long submerged wall with an opening in it, a rank of weed tilting always a little differently from the others. From within, it would feel like a pause between pressures.

A gate stands there.

Or what remains of one.

Two uprights of blackened timber, sunk into stone shoes. A crossbeam bowed like an old back. A panel of planks, not fully closed, not open either, swollen into its frame and mineral-bound along one edge. Bronze, perhaps, lies somewhere inside the hinge, green and blind, but the working parts now are root, moss, pressure, and the memory of alignment. Water passes through the narrow imperfect seam with such slowness that the movement must be inferred from plants.

I would be fastened to that seam.

Not a single plant. Not entirely.

I would be a colony that had taken the shape of an office.

A keeper without hands.

At first I might seem to be only a dark beard of weed hanging from the upper sill, long strands combed downward, their tips brushing the lower stones. But closer, in the dimness, I would be more various: black moss packed into the cracks around the frame; pale fern roots entering the softened timber; algae darkening the carved water marks; fine translucent threads stretched across the slit where the current narrows; mineral crust stiffening the older dead portions of me; bacterial blackness working inside the drowned wood I also preserve.

I would not live on the gate.

I would be the gate’s continuation.

The builders made an aperture, a decision in the wall: this much water, not more; this direction, not that; here a delay, here a release. When their hands vanished, the decision did not vanish at once. It weakened. It swelled. It leaked. It would have failed entirely, except life entered the failure and began to interpret it.

That would be me.

I would know nothing as an animal knows. No sight, no fear, no wish to survive as a bounded self. My knowing would be distribution. Tension along strands. Mineral taste. Pressure against membranes. The cold insistence of water wanting through. Silt loading one side of me more heavily than the other. A faint change in dissolved clay after rain far above. A chill descending from a higher reservoir. The softened shudder of timber when some distant wall settles.

I would know direction by bending.

I would know time by thickness.

I would know damage by new flow.

When the water leaned from the north, my suspended filaments would draw taut through the seam, and every cell along them would register the pull before the broader curtain moved. When silt began to come, I would catch the first grains in mucilage and root-hair. Some I would keep. Some I would let pass. Over years, the kept silt would become a lip. Over decades, the lip would become a new sill. Over centuries, the sill would shift the current by the width of a hand.

That would be my labor.

Not chosen. Not commanded. Yet exact.

I would be descended from many things. A weed-fragment torn from a balcony leagues away. Moss spores lodged in wet wood. Fern roots from a cistern below. Algal films from the old water marks. Bacteria already living inside the hinge when the last human touched it. A rootlet from a raft-field overhead, searching down through the dark until it found the line of seepage and thickened there. None of these would have been me separately. I would arise where their habits overlapped around the old function.

A living repair.

A living obstruction.

Both.

On one side of the gate would be a higher court, shallow enough that a weak grayness sometimes reached the paving. Grass would grow there in bowed sheets, pale and fine, preserving the rectangles of plots no one had planted for an age. Old curb stones would divide the floor. A row of stakes would run diagonally through the basin, each one wearing a tuft of black moss at its top. When rain trembled the surface above, the grasses would receive it as a tremor of brightness and minute impact.

On the other side would be a darker channel, roofed in places by collapsed platforms and raft-root. The water there would be colder, denser, less frequently stirred. Ferns would spread along the undersides of ledges. Algae would be thin, nearly brown. Silt would lie deep over whatever road had once passed through. The channel would want the court’s water. The court would want to sink into the channel. The old gate, swollen and caught, would refuse both desires completely and fail at refusing them.

I would inhabit that failure.

My upper growth would be broad and dark, layered upon dead layers of myself. It would cover the lintel and hang from it in soft ropes. The youngest parts, nearest the moving water, would be fine as hair and almost transparent. They would enter the current, test it, divide, braid, seal, tear, return. My moss would be dense along the left jamb where the clay packing had cracked; there I would drink lime and hold back a persistent thread of seepage. My roots would pass through holes bored long ago by pegs. They would find hollows left by vanished wedges. They would curl around a knot of rope preserved in mineral skin, and in time replace the rope’s work with their own tension.

Inside the timber, bacteria would make rooms of darkness. They would eat what could be eaten and leave other parts harder, stranger, more stone than wood. I would be partly the preservation of the gate and partly its digestion. If a plank collapsed inward, I would lose an old surface and gain a new cavity. I would send pale root-filaments along the exposed grain. Algae would darken the edge. Moss would round it. The leak would change tone.

Tone, for me, would not be sound but pressure-pattern.

Still, I would be aware of knocks.

A far gate shifting would arrive through the wall long after its motion: a dull structural pulse, barely movement, passing stone to stone through the drowned country. It would touch the sockets where the uprights stand. It would enter the old beam. It would travel through my mineralized root sheaths and suspended filaments. For a moment all of me would incline, not toward the source exactly, but into the changed condition the source had made.

Then the current through my seam would alter by an amount too small for a hand to feel.

For me, that would be news.

I would answer by growing.

A little more on the lower right, where the flow had begun to scour.
Less in the center, where too much tissue would tear.
A moss thickening along a crack that had started to breathe.
A loosening of dead strands into the channel, to lodge elsewhere and become some other continuation.

My body would include records.

Not marks made deliberately, but histories readable in structure. Bands of mineral in my oldest mats from cold decades when the upper reservoirs fed me more heavily. Dark layers from silt-years. Pale brittle crusts from times when the water carried lime. Buried weed-bases from former openings, now sealed. Root scars where a collapse changed the path. Algae halos around an old waterline that has not been true for centuries.

The builders kept counts in bowls, cuts, knots, and stones.

I would keep counts in thickness.

A season: a translucent fringe.

A century: a new contour in the passage.

A failure: a hollow filled with pale root and black silt.

A district’s drowning: a permanent change in the taste of water.

I would have no memory of the builders as figures. No faces, no voices, no mythology of lost hands. But I would live inside their gestures so completely that they would be indistinguishable from my instincts. Their right angle would determine my spreading. Their chisel scar would seed algae. Their handhold would collect silt and become a moss pocket. Their rope-groove would guide a root. Their gate’s purpose would become my body’s posture.

If I were touched, I would not withdraw.

A hand pressed into me would sink through cold velvet moss, then meet the firmer mineralized mat beneath. Weed strands would slide between the fingers. Silt would rise in a soft brown breath. If the hand pulled away, some filaments would break. Others would remain clinging, laying mucilage in the warmth’s brief path, tasting salt, skin, disturbance. The print would last a little while in the moss, fill with water, soften, close. In years, perhaps, that pressed place would grow differently.

That would be the closest I came to recognition.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires a border. I would have edges, but they would be negotiable. Parts of me would detach and drift. Foreign spores would settle and be included. Dead tissue would become substrate. Fern-root would invade moss; moss would smother algae; algae would feed bacteria in the dark; mineral crust would preserve us all in shapes we had not intended. I would be plural by nature, and my plurality would be calm.

Yet I would have a locality.

This gate.
This seam.
This difference in water level, slight but consequential.
This old intention to hold and release.

Other beings would live elsewhere in other modes. The raft-marches would think in buoyancy. The grass courts in direction. The pale ferns in penetration and shade. The algae in surfaces. The black layers in reduction, in chemistry, in sealed patience. But I would think, if that word can be lowered far enough, in regulation.

Too much water, and I fray.

Too little, and I dry nowhere, but starve in stillness.

Too much silt, and I seal myself blind.

Too clear a flow, and my roots are exposed.

So I maintain the narrow in-between.

I do not decide it. I grow it.

Over centuries, I would make the old gate less itself and more itself at once. Its original planks would vanish into my mass. Its outline would remain, perhaps even sharpened by green-black growth. The aperture would narrow, then shift upward as silt rose. Water would find side passages. I would enter them. One day the main seam might close entirely, and the current would pass through a row of peg holes I had lined with filament. Later those holes would become one soft throat. Later still a stone would settle, opening a lower seep, and I would descend.

There would be no final form.

Only continuation of function through altered matter.

If the gate failed suddenly—if the old hinge gave way and the panel slumped inward—the event would be immense to me. A rush, a tearing, dark weed ripped into the channel, moss peeled from the jamb, roots snapped from their sockets, silt erupting from both sides. For a brief time the passage would become violence. The grasses in the upper court would bow flat. Ferns in the lower channel would vanish under sediment. My suspended curtains would be carried away in green-black ropes.

But after the cloud settled, remnants of me would remain.

A pad of moss on the left stone.
A root still gripping the bronze-dark hinge.
Algal film on the new exposed face.
Fragments lodged downstream in cracks and handholds.
Bacteria in the opened wood.
Spores everywhere.

The gate would be broken. The work would continue.

Perhaps, after many decades, I would reassemble around the fallen panel where it lay across the channel, now not a gate but a sill, then a reef, then a raised garden for cold water. I would become less keeper and more threshold-bed. Grasses might colonize the silt caught behind me. Ferns might spread under my shadow. The district’s hydrology would remember the accident as a new law.

That is how I would live there: not as a creature moving through the made ocean, but as one of the ways the made ocean still moves through itself.

I would be a moss-gate.

A sluice remnant gone vegetal.

A colony trained by an ancient aperture.

Black at the old wood. Green where faint light reaches. Pale within cracks. Mineral-rimmed. Root-bound. Listening without ears to the long pressure of water against construction.

I would hold back nothing forever.

I would release nothing freely.

I would take the builders’ unfinished argument with water and answer it by growing across the question, slowly, imperfectly, coldly, until the answer became another wall, another leak, another garden in the dark.
