I would find myself where the valley forgets whether it is a room or a throat.

Not in the widest gallery at first, though I would be able to see it from where I live: the immense warm hollow shining through veils of vapor, its far herds clicking like beads poured forever from one hand to another. I would be up along the side, on a shelf that has lasted three rains longer than anyone expected, beneath an overhang of milky stone that glows more strongly when the roof is about to loosen. There is a scar on the wall behind me shaped like a bent finger, a seam that exhales salt-warm air at intervals. Below the shelf, a channel has cut and uncut itself so many times that people have stopped naming it. They call it only “below us.”

My shelter would be low enough that I must stoop to enter. Shell plates on the roof, rib-bone struts, hides scraped thin and darkened by smoke, all of it tied and retied with tendon. The doorway would face sideways, not outward, because things that rush upward from the basin do not turn corners well. Along the threshold there would be a shallow groove swept clean each morning with a brush of bristles. Not for neatness. For evidence. Dust. Eggs. New cracks. The silver smear of an eel fallen in the night. The bead trail of shell-creatures. The first warning that the wall has begun to sweat.

I think I would be human, but not quite as I imagine humans under the sun.

I would be one of the shelf-people: narrow-shouldered, strong-footed, my skin the color of warmed clay left too long in pale light. My hair would be bound close with strips of gut and shell, not because it is ornamental but because dangling things are invitations. My palms would be ridged with old cuts sealed in ash and salt. My shins would wear shell guards polished by crawling through stone corridors. Around my neck I would carry small containers: bitter paste for bites, powdered ash, a plug of grease, a twist of dried larvae, a shard of black glass wrapped in hide.

But my real tools would be my ears.

I would have grown up learning not names first, but interruptions.

The difference between dripping and ticking. Between a harmless crack and the glass-note that means a wall is about to open. Between the clatter of shell-creatures feeding and the clatter of shell-creatures fleeing. Between membrane-fliers screaming because they quarrel and membrane-fliers screaming because pressure is moving through the roof. Between silence overhead and silence underfoot.

As a child I would have worn rattles at my wrists so my mother could find me in the animal noise. Later, when I was old enough, she would remove one rattle at a time. The first lesson of adulthood would not be hunting, or making fire, or mating, or building a roof.

It would be learning when not to add sound to the world.

I would become a listener of routes.

Not a chief, not a priest exactly, not a hunter in the bold sense. A path-keeper, perhaps. A person sent ahead when the ledges have shifted, when the basin has filled after rain, when a trading band needs to know whether the comb-field has sealed the old passage or whether the black-glass roost has emptied early.

I would carry a staff made from a jointed feeder’s fallen limb, hollow but hard, capped at one end with stone. I would tap the floor with it and feel answers through my wrist: full, hollow, wet, skinned, angry. Sometimes I would kneel and press my cheek to warm mineral to hear water moving under crust. Sometimes I would put my mouth close to a seam and taste the exhalation. Copper: leave soon. Salt: roof rain. Sour animal heat: something trapped or nesting beyond the wall.

My work would be half practical, half ceremonial, because in that place the difference would be foolish.

I would renew handprints along usable paths, but never too confidently. A clear mark can kill if it outlives its truth. So the marks I make would be conditional: a red palm with two white cuts through it meaning safe only when dry; three shell chips pressed into clay meaning listen before crossing; a smear of ash below a ledge meaning the upper way was good when last touched, but not promised.

I would hang strings of perforated shell at crossings. I would know which shells rattle in pressure wind, which clack only when animals brush them, which dissolve if the air turns too wet and therefore serve as warnings by vanishing. I would set stones around breeding pools, not to claim them, but to ask everyone with feet to remember that beginnings are also hazards.

People would come to me with questions that are never simply questions.

Can we move the bridge?  
Can we sleep there one more night?  
Are the larvae white enough to eat?  
Did the digger open a route or a trap?  
If the wall sings only in the morning, does it still mean leave?

And I would rarely answer yes or no.

I would say: wait until the shell river returns.  
I would say: cross only while the fliers are still roosting.  
I would say: take the eggs from the upper edge, not the warm center.  
I would say: if the pool reflects your whole face, do not bend over it.  
I would say: leave the tools; tools can be remade faster than bones.

I would not be old, perhaps, but I would have the manner of someone who has watched objects disappear. A bowl left too near a sweating wall. A ladder sealed in a night by white comb. A cousin taken by clear water that was not water. A shelter swallowed slowly enough that the family had time to remove the roof, but not the storage jars. In such a place, mourning would have to move. Grief that sits still would grow crystal around itself.

Maybe I would be called something like Sareth, or Ikk, or Mara-of-the-Second-Ledge. Names there would need attachments because ledges change and people repeat. A person is not just a person but where they were born, which collapse spared them, which rain they survived, which animal they know how to read.

Mara Who Heard the Salt Wind.

Mara of the Singing Wall.

Mara Who Left Before It Split.

Yes. I think I would be that: not the strongest, not the wisest, but the one who once heard the wall begin its thin beautiful note and pulled three sleeping children out before the seam opened. Afterward, people would treat my hearing as if it were a gift, though I would know it was fear sharpened by accident.

My days would begin before the largest noise.

There is a kind of low hour in that underland—not morning exactly, because there is no sun, but a loosening in the glow when vapors thin and the ceiling seams pale. I would wake then. First, I would listen without moving. A body waking too fast can miss what the world is already saying.

Shell-creatures below: ordinary clatter.  
Fliers in the high glass: restless but not leaving.  
Water somewhere to the left: new, thin, running over hard stone.  
The wall behind: breathing salt, no song.  
The floor: warm, not hot.

Then I would rise.

I would check the threshold groove. If there were eel slime, I would sprinkle ash so no one slips. If there were mite-holes in the packed clay, I would dig them out and burn bitter scrapings near the wall. If shell-creatures had gathered beneath the shelter, I might leave them scraps, because their feeding keeps certain films from blooming too thick. Nothing is only nuisance. Nothing is only useful.

Food would be sour and mineral: fermented film paste spread on dried fish; roasted eggs when available; strips of pale meat softened in warm seep-water; crunchy shell-creature legs if hunger is sharp enough. We would not romanticize the taste. Survival has flavors one learns to stop insulting.

After eating, I would go out along the shelf with my staff and a sling of markers.

The underland would be bright in its impossible way. Light through bone-stone. Light caught in wet crystal. Light smeared across vapor. Shadows moving though nothing passes before a sun. Below, the shell rivers would divide around a new blister of mineral growth, each tiny body clicking against the next. Farther off, a jointed feeder would stand in a pool, its tendril-basket lowered, patient as architecture. Membrane-fliers would flicker overhead like torn black leaves, though there are no trees from which leaves could tear.

I would descend by a ladder made of chitin plates and tendon, testing each rung because damp changes everything. At the bottom I would step only where the old hard ridges show through the mineral paste. The floor remembers pressure. The floor also lies.

Perhaps that day my task would be to see whether the channel-route to the neighboring huts has survived indoor rain. I would travel with two others: a young hunter carrying hooks and a net, and an older woman with clay jars strapped high against her back. We would go single file, not from discipline but because the world often permits only one body at a time.

At a basin that was dry yesterday, we would stop.

Now it would be alive.

Water ankle-deep, warm enough to steam. Blind fish flickering in it as if poured from hidden cracks. Gelatinous eggs swelling along the rim. Winged things descending to drink, their knuckled membranes trembling. Small predators making quick punctuation marks in the shallows. The young hunter would smile because food has appeared. The older woman would frown because food appearing too easily often means something else has opened.

I would crouch and look at the reflection.

If it reflected only light, we might approach.  
If it reflected too cleanly, like polished metal, we would stay away.  
If bubbles came from the center in pairs, something below was breathing.  
If the shell-creatures refused the edge, poison.

That morning, perhaps, the shell-creatures would feed along one side but not the other. So I would mark the safe edge with three pale stones and scratch a warning line into the clay. The hunter would cast his net where I point. The older woman would fill only one jar, then seal it with hide. Never take as if return is guaranteed. Return is precisely the thing that must be courted.

Later, we might pass beneath ceiling gutters where the eels live.

Here we would move quickly but not run. Running wakes hungers. Above us, translucent ribbons would pulse along wet seams. Most would cling. Some would loosen their mouths from the stone and taste the air. If they began to gather into ropes, I would lift my hand and we would back away. No shame in retreat. Shame belongs to people who make others carry their bodies home.

At the channel-route we would find trouble.

The bridge gone crooked. Not broken, but made irrelevant. The stream beneath it shifted left, cutting under the bank, exposing a soft black hollow where hard ground used to be. Beside it, a new mineral comb would be growing: white cells extruding from the wall, tender and fast, already swallowing one bridge peg.

The young hunter would want to chop the comb immediately. The older woman would want to save the bridge first. I would listen.

Comb growth makes a sound if it is only growing. A damp, faint popping. But if pressure moves behind it, there is another note inside, a held whine like a tooth under stress.

If I heard the whine, I would say: leave the peg. Take the bridge from the far side. Now.

We would cut the tendon lashings, shoulder the planks and shell plates, and retreat just before the comb ruptures and spills glittering flakes into the channel. The exposed hollow would steam. Something soft inside it would flex once, as if the ground had an organ.

By the time we returned to my ledge, my arms would ache, my feet would be crusted white with mineral dust, and my hair would smell of salt rain. I would add the new change to the route-song that evening.

Not a map on skin. A song.

From Second Ledge descend by the warm rib.  
Step where the black stone breaks the paste.  
Do not trust the basin if the left edge is empty.  
At eel-gutter wait for three breaths after dripping stops.  
The old bridge has moved; the comb has teeth.  
Carry the crossing high.  
Listen for the hidden whine.

Children would learn it while pretending not to listen. Adults would correct the rhythm because rhythm preserves what fear forgets. A wrong beat in a route-song can become a wrong step.

At night—if night can be said to exist there—we would gather in the shelter or just outside it, where smoke folds under the glowing overhang. Someone would scrape a hide. Someone would mend a basket with gut. Someone would chip glass carefully, catching the flakes on damp clay so they do not scatter into bare feet. The young would ask for stories about old passages sealed behind crystal. Elders would speak of places none of us can reach anymore, not as lost paradises but as former arrangements.

Creation stories in that world would be told in the present tense.

The First Heat is still rising.  
The Roof is still deciding where to rain.  
The Stone is still soft in its dreams.  
The Animals came before the paths, and the paths must ask permission.  
People were not made last because they were best. They were made late because they needed examples.

If I had a private devotion, it would be to thresholds.

A threshold is the smallest claim a person can make against chaos. Not a wall, not a fortress, not a city gate. Just a line maintained by hand: swept, watched, repaired, abandoned when necessary. I would believe that a good life resembles a good threshold. Open enough to leave. Clear enough to notice change. Humble enough not to call itself permanent.

I would fear many things.

The clear pool.  
The beautiful wall-song.  
The sudden silence of shell-creatures.  
The warm copper wind.  
The long-jawed scavenger that has learned the sound of human coughing.  
The roof rain that begins with sand instead of water.  
The child who stops wearing rattles before they have learned stillness.  
The shelter that feels safe for too many seasons.

But I would love the place too fiercely to call it hostile.

I would love the glow after rain, when every surface shines as if newly uncovered from an egg. I would love the jointed feeders standing in steam like patient gods who want nothing from worship. I would love the shell rivers because they tell the truth faster than people do. I would love the smell of smoke in a dry pocket. The handprint of someone long dead, stretched and magnified behind mineral glass. The rattle strings beginning their faint warning before any human ear can hear pressure move. The absurd bravery of a swept threshold.

I would love that nothing there pretends to be finished.

And perhaps, over time, I would become less a person who lives in the underland than one of its listening parts.

Not animal, not stone, not spirit. A human pressure among other pressures. A small, warm, temporary intelligence moving through a temporary landscape, touching walls, tasting wind, revising songs, carrying bridges, warning children, eating what rain wakes, leaving before beauty breaks open.

If you came looking for me, you would find me halfway between shelter and hazard, where the path has just changed.

I would be crouched beside a new crack in the pale floor, one palm hovering over its breath, head tilted.

Around me the underland would clatter and drip and shriek and steam. The shell-creatures would pour past my feet. Fliers would ring the roof with metallic cries. Somewhere far off, a digger would grunt through wet stone. The wall would glow softly behind me, alive with mineral light.

I would raise one hand—not for silence exactly, because silence does not belong to that world.

For attention.

And I would say, quietly, to whoever followed:

“Wait. It is telling us what it is becoming.”
