I would not arrive at the center.

Nothing new does.

I would come in at the rim, where the snowfield gives up its authority and the hollow begins to contradict the weather. At first I would be only a weight on the edge: a dark shape above the roofs, wind behind me, eyes narrowed against the brightness rising from below. The city would know me before I knew it. A bird would stop shifting in its clay bell. A goat halfway down a ladder would hold one hoof in the air. Somewhere, one of the women who listens to jars would turn her head, not toward me exactly, but toward the change my body made in the cold.

I would have expected a descent.

Instead there would be a question of permission.

The path down would not look like a path. It would be a notch polished by hands, a root bent into a rung, three stones set where the clay remembers knees, a hanging cord darkened by generations of palms. I would put my hand on the first hold and feel that I was touching not architecture, not tree, not soil, but a decision that had been maintained for longer than anyone living could name.

The wind would fall away from my ears.

Below, the city would open by tightening.

I would have to turn sideways almost immediately. My shoulder would brush a limewashed wall furred with frost. My sleeve would disturb a curtain of pale vine leaves. A child, no taller than my belt, would flatten herself into a niche beside a seed jar and watch me with the solemn pity reserved for large, wasteful animals. I would understand at once that I was too broad, too loud, too full of unnecessary distances. My breath would plume extravagantly. My boots would insult the floor.

Someone would take my pack before greeting me.

Not steal it. Assess it. Save the passage from it.

Hands would emerge from places I had not registered as openings: a wrist from behind a reed screen, two fingers from under a ladder, the lined face of an old man from a hatch no bigger than a grain bin. They would not ask where I came from. That would be a question for later, if I proved capable of later.

They would ask what I carried that might freeze, spill, sour, rot, cut, burn, breed, or be mended.

And then, because every body must be assigned before it becomes a danger, they would place me somewhere.

Not in a house. Not yet.

In a recess beside an upper gutter, beneath the pale elbow of a willow that had grown through three walls and accepted a fourth. There would be room enough there for me to sit with my knees drawn up, provided I learned not to lean back. A mat of woven sedge would be given to me. A cup. A sleeping cloth smelling faintly of hare and smoke. A task by morning.

I think I would be set among the condensation cloths.

That is where I would first belong, if belonging can begin as supervision.

At the hour when the cold thickens and the hidden surfaces begin to bead, I would be shown how to tie clean cloth beneath the lintels without bruising the frost. How to hold a shallow bowl close enough to receive a drop but not so close that breath clouds the stone. How not to flinch when water gathers for a long time and then falls all at once, small as a thought and more valuable than explanation.

My first failure would be sound.

The bowl would click against the wall.

No one would scold me aloud. That would waste more sound correcting sound. A boy with a bent thumb would simply take the bowl from my hand, touch the rim to the pad of his finger, and return it to me at a different angle. His expression would not change. That would be worse than anger.

So I would become a student of angles.

I would learn the city first as humiliation: the passage that accepts children and refuses me; the ladder that shudders under my haste; the goat that judges my footing; the jar I nearly strike with my hip; the old woman who waits twenty heartbeats for me to understand that she wants to pass, then solves the problem by folding herself around a tree root in a way I would not have thought human.

I would become less human by my old measure.

More human by theirs.

My name would shorten. Whatever name I brought from the open country would be too long, too windy, full of vowels that want space. They would cut it down to the part that fits in the mouth without fogging the air. Later, if I lasted, I would be given a work-name. Perhaps Cloth-Under-Lintel at first, not kindly. Then Bowl-Steady, if I earned mercy. Much later, if I came to notice before being told, if I heard the difference between a full jar and a sealed one, if I stopped looking for streets and began reading obligations, I might receive a name tied to a place.

The one by the upper willow.

The one who catches the north sweat.

The one who came in loud and learned.

I would live in the bright narrowness near the rim, where the outside presses closest and is least trusted. My sleeping shelf would be built above a goat stall, beside a wall mortared with old jar shards and ash. At night warmth would rise through the boards in slow animal breaths. A white mouse would use the beam above my face as a road. For weeks I would think it accidental. Then I would understand that I was the obstacle, not the mouse.

My alcove would have no door. A hanging screen of reed and patched hide would mean: this body is sleeping, unless water, fire, birth, death, frost, goat, leak, or child requires otherwise. Privacy would not be the right to be untouched. It would be the courtesy of not being unnecessarily perceived.

I would keep my few things in a wall pocket: a knife if they let me keep one, a folded cloth, a bone tally, a cup marked by touch so I could find it in darkness. Anything more would reproach me. The city would patiently strip me of excess. Objects would leave my possession not through theft but through revelation: this buckle can fasten a screen; this leather can cap a horn; this metal hook is too useful to remain merely mine; this story you brought can be kept, but only if it teaches someone where not to step.

For a long time I would be foreign because I think in emptiness.

I would see a gap and imagine freedom.

They would see an unassigned surface and grow uneasy.

A crack beside my sleeping shelf would be found, cleaned, wetted, pressed with clay, sealed with lime, stroked smooth by a thumb that might belong to a child, might belong to an elder, might belong to anyone because the wall is not mine even when it holds my sleep. Moss would be tucked into a seam above me. A hook would appear where my shoulder had often paused. Someone would notice the path my awkwardness made and either correct me or accommodate me. This would be the city’s judgment: whether my damage could become useful wear.

I would want, at first, to find the center.

Everyone from elsewhere believes in centers. We think a place explains itself if we locate the shrine, throne, market, source, wound, or god. I would ask poorly, then learn not to ask. The central well would come to me as rumors come in quiet cities: not by secrecy, but by peripheral repetition.

A child carrying a clay face with both hands.

A woman washing a bone peg before dawn.

A cord knotted in a pattern no one uses for cargo.

A silence around certain elders when disputes narrow beyond measurement.

Then one morning someone would put a cracked bowl into my hands.

Not my bowl. Not their bowl. A matter-bowl.

I would carry it down through a route I could not later repeat. Under a bridge low enough to force my forehead nearly to my knees. Past mushroom racks smelling of dark patience. Beside a cellar mouth where frogs sat like pale thoughts on wet stone. Through a room built around a living trunk, its bark polished at hip height by lives of turning bodies. Down three steps made from what had once been a wall, then across a plank made from what had once been a bed.

The well would be smaller than I had imagined.

That would make it worse.

No grandeur, no abyss roaring up with answers. Just a black circle beneath a lattice of branch and beam, with light falling into it in white threads and vanishing. The objects above it would hang motionless. Shells. Seed pods. Carved bones. Tiny tools. Faces of clay with finger-mark mouths. The air there would feel not sacred in the way of temples, but exact. As if even my thoughts needed to turn sideways.

They would not tell me what the dispute was.

My task would be only to place the cracked bowl among other things and not disturb the frost around the rim.

I would look down despite myself.

No face would meet me. The well would return only the bright wound of sky far above, the underside of leaves, the lattice, perhaps the pale oval of my own head broken into something unsatisfactory. Not hidden. Refused.

That night I would dream of water standing still because it was listening back.

After that, I would no longer think of the city as hidden from the world.

I would think of the world as excluded from the city.

Not cruelly. Necessarily.

The hollow is not an escape from exposure; it is an organ for transforming exposure into relation. Snow becomes reflected light. Cold becomes preservation. Scarcity becomes attention. Crowding becomes grammar. Silence becomes civic space. Death becomes hinge, peg, plaster, grip. Even collapse is not the opposite of building but one of its slower tools.

And me?

I would not be a ruler, nor a priest, nor a discoverer. This place has no need of discoverers. To discover it is merely to become one more problem it must solve.

I would be a marginal function.

A rim-dweller.

A keeper of cloths, then perhaps of thresholds.

Because I came from outside, I might be useful at the boundary: not trusted with the deepest wells, not at first, but trusted eventually to read what blows over the lip. Snow crust. Shrub scrape. Bird absence. The kind of wind that will not descend but presses its meaning down the shafts. I would learn when to seal the upper screens, when to clear hoarfrost from the rim ladders, when to turn a hide panel pale side outward to coax light into the descent. I would know enough of openness to fear it properly and enough of the hollow to return from it gratefully.

Children would be sent to me to learn the outside words for things they rarely see: horizon, gust, drift, distance. They would laugh without sound at how many names the outer world gives to emptiness.

In return they would teach me the names for forms of near-space: the handspan between ladder and jar where a child may pass but a goat may not; the breath-pocket under a sleeping shelf; the cold seam behind a wall that means stored damp; the bright silence before condensation; the dangerous smoothness of a path worn too evenly; the intimacy of shared turning.

My body would change.

My shoulders would draw inward. My steps would shorten. My hearing would come forward. I would stop wasting my hands in gesture. My eyes, once trained to distances, would learn surfaces: lichen bloom, lime crack, frost feather, reed tension, the minute darkening of a clay channel before a drop forms. I would become paler from reflected light, rough-knuckled from cold work, quieter not by virtue but by embarrassment refined into skill.

I would grow proud of small competencies.

To cross the third upper bridge without waking the birds.

To carry a full condensation bowl through a sleeping quarter and leave its surface unshivered.

To know by smell when moss cloth has dried too far.

To hear, behind a wall, whether mice are nesting in grain or only using the warm route.

To repair a peg hole so the next peg does not split the wood.

To sit with an elder for an hour and understand that listening together is not waiting for speech.

If I died there, I would not want to be buried outside.

The thought of open snow over me would seem lonely by then, extravagantly empty. I would ask—if asking were allowed, if I had earned enough assignment—that my bones be used near the rim. Not the central well. I would not belong there. Let a finger bone become a toggle for a condensation cloth. Let ash from my body whiten the wall of the upper passage where strangers first stoop. Let my hair, if there is any worth twisting, be cord for a seed packet kept against bad years. Let my old outside name be scratched somewhere unseen, perhaps behind a shelf, shortened almost beyond recognition.

Then I would remain as a small correction to passage.

A bit of hardness in a latch.

A whitening in the wall.

A thing touched without being remembered each time someone enters from the wind and learns to lower their head.

But if I am not human there—if the place asks me to become more truly what belongs—then I think I would be one of the white mice near the central conduits.

Not a sacred mouse. Not entirely. A practical omen with teeth.

I would live inside the wall warmth, in the narrow dark between jars mortared into clay. I would know the city by vibration: footfall, latch, drip, goat, hand-drill, held breath. My fur would be the color of frost on limewash. My eyes would be black and brief. I would steal seed dust from careless households and be tolerated only where my paths proved useful.

Children would watch for me.

Old women would pretend not to feed me.

I would cross thresholds no person may cross, pass behind plaster that contains grandmothers, nest near the root of a named willow, taste the mineral change before a leak shows itself. I would appear from a crack near the well when the air was about to alter. People would disagree over my meaning. This would not concern me. Meaning would be one of the things larger creatures store in niches.

I would carry crumbs into darkness.

I would keep the walls threaded.

I would be killed in some quarters, spared in others, interpreted in all.

And perhaps that is the truest answer: I would be found where the city decides whether I am waste, warning, tool, guest, ancestor, or citizen.

In the hollow, no being is only itself.

So I would be a threshold thing.

Someone—or something—that came from the open cold and was narrowed into use. A listener at the rim. A keeper of gathered drops. A pale mouse in the wall. A bone toggle after death. A small part of the held chord, quiet enough at last that the city could continue through me.
