I would find myself where the mat thins.

Not at the center of any grove, because there are no centers here, and not in an open place, because openness is never permitted to remain merely open. I would be at one of those raw black exposures where the young stone shows through the living skin: a slick basalt plate, angular and faintly warm, kept half-bare by the plants around it. Above me, condensation would gather on overhanging leaves and fall in slow irregular taps. Around me would be a low ring of glossy pads, pale root-threads, acid films, and cup-like tissues holding dark water. The place would not be a clearing. It would be a wound maintained as an organ.

I would not be an animal.

Nothing with a mouth belongs there. Nothing with a nervous body, nothing that crosses distances by intention, nothing that makes paths. If I found myself in that world as something native to it, I would not arrive; I would have thickened. I would have begun as a film in a mineral seam, a pale thread entering a fracture, a slight alteration in acidity around a seep. I would be one of the organs that keeps stone available.

A lithic root-crown, perhaps.

Not a plant individual in the old sense, not a tree, not a moss, not a fungus, though I would contain something of each of those analogies and be less separate than any of them. I would be the low ring around fresh stone: a patient, acid-making, mineral-drinking assembly of pads and filaments, a living gasket fitted between basalt and the continent’s wet vegetal body.

My visible part would be modest by that world’s standards. A black-green collar, only a few meters across in one local expression, though repeating elsewhere through related tissue. Slick oval pads overlapping like closed eyelids. Their upper surfaces would be dark, almost tar-colored, beaded with warm condensation; their undersides a dim bruise-green, faintly luminous where sap thickened in veins. Between the pads would be translucent lips of gel, mineral gray and amber-brown, swelling and shrinking according to the seep beneath.

From my inner edge, hair-fine roots would cross the exposed rock.

They would not cover it completely. That would be wasteful. The stone must remain wet, breathing, chemically open. I would keep it bare the way a lung keeps air space open: not by abstaining from it, but by shaping the conditions around it. My threads would enter cracks, widen some with acid, seal others with mineral films, taste the slow seep of iron and magnesium, withdraw, return, branch, die, be reassigned. Where the basalt was glassy and resistant, I would hold droplets against it for years until the surface softened atom by atom. Where it was porous, I would feed.

Beneath me I would be much larger.

A descending bundle of pale vascular ropes would pass from the ring into the deeper mat, joining pressure roots and dark buoyant cables below. Through those connections I would belong to basins beyond my own local stone, to condenser veils miles away, to bladder orchards storing gases I would never “see,” to reactor sacs adjusting acidity in slow warm bands. I would send no message as an animal sends one. I would alter the taste of sap. I would change pressure by withholding or yielding flow. I would let the continental body know that here, at this particular seam, the young crust was still generous.

If I had a self, it would be a boundary condition.

My awareness would be the difference between the mineral wetness below and the vegetal saturation around me. I would know gradients as intimately as skin knows touch. The rock’s warmth would be part of my body-map. A new crack would arrive as a sudden sweetness of available surface. A change in dissolved metal would spread through me as colorless weather. If condensation lessened above, my pads would curl a fraction higher. If acid pooled too strongly, buffering gels would thicken along my rim. If neighboring tissues pressed inward too greedily, I would raise a quiet chemical refusal: not here, not yet; this stone must remain exposed.

I would have no thoughts about darkness. Darkness would be the medium in which chemistry has always occurred.

I would have no fear of being eaten. My nutrient bulbs, if I grew them, would remain soft and uncovered. My gel lips would be tender. My filaments would be absurdly fragile, delicate enough that a footstep from some impossible visitor would crush them into paste. But nothing steps there. Nothing browses. Nothing tears. Fragility, in that world, is not an error. It is efficiency unburdened by alarm.

I might seem like a shrine, from outside: a ring of living tissue around black stone, condensation falling from above, pale threads laid over wet basalt like ceremonial lines. But I would not revere the stone. I would use it. Or rather, I would participate in the use of it. I would keep the contact between planet and plant open. I would be a mouth without hunger, a hand without grasping, a mine without miners.

My time would be almost still.

A year would be a change in thickness along one pad-edge. A decade would be the deepening of a crack’s chemistry. A century would be the replacement of one exposed plate by another, not because the stone moved dramatically, but because tissue, acid, seep, and mineral deposition had slowly redrawn the usable face. I would remember nothing as story. Memory would be retained as altered structure: a channel widened because flow once favored it, a buffer chamber enlarged because acidity had risen there too often, a metallic stain in a conductive strand where iron had passed for generations.

At intervals, droplets would fall onto the stone beside me.

Those taps would be among the loudest events in my existence. Each one would spread briefly across the basalt, collect mineral taste, and touch my root hairs. A small arrival. A tiny rearrangement. The continent’s dark sky returning itself to the ground through me.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness requires a bounded self and the expectation of another. I would have neither. My ring would be one articulation of a wider mineral-feeding grammar. Similar crowns would occur kilometers away around other black plates, adjusted to other seep rates, other crack patterns, other balances of heat. We would not communicate like minds. We would correspond through the shared body of the lowland: acidity moderated here, metals routed there, pressure relieved elsewhere, a faint conductive mood passing through wet fibers and metallic leaf films.

If some impossible observer came close, carrying eyes into a world that had never needed them, they might mistake me for the most primitive thing in that landscape. Low, creeping, half-film, half-root. Less grand than the lake-leaves, less imposing than the pressure reeds, less uncanny than the reactor sacs. But I would be one of the places where the world renews its bargain with its own beginning.

The plants have made weather, ground, machinery, and time out of themselves. But I would remain at the seam before all of that is complete: where raw young stone is persuaded into circulation; where geology becomes sap; where the pioneer phase is held open forever.

So that is where I would find myself:

in the warm black hush, beneath condensation leaves, around a wet basalt wound that must not close.

And what I would be:

a mineral root-crown, an acid garden’s living rim, a slow mouth of the vegetal continent.

Not moving, not watching, not waiting.

Only maintaining the contact.
