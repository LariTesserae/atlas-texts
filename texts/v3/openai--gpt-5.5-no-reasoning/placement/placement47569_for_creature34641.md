I would find myself where the town is least certain it is a town.

Not in the center, not beside the dry basin, though I would know it the way everyone knows it: by the slight avoidance in the feet, by the quiet flattening of the eyes as people cross the open court. Not at the lower boundary either, where empty bowls are carried into the trees and full-handed fools come back itching under the nails. I would be higher up, near the place where the last roofs begin to give themselves back to slope, where the paths are more often poles, roots, and memory than steps.

A narrow dwelling leans there against a black rib of young stone.

It is not the highest house, but it is the last one that still pretends to be a house. Above it are storage racks, thorn fences, weather poles, and the animal stair. Below it the lanes gather themselves and become civil again. My roof would be steep and uneven, patched in three kinds of thatch, with a strip of moss intentionally left uncut along the northern lip because the little white roof-grazers come there in the hour before dawn. A bad roof for sleeping under, some would say, because it clicks all night: beetles in the grass skin, drops falling through channels, lashings tightening and loosening as the mist breathes.

But a good roof for hearing.

I think I would be a route-reader, or something close to it.

Not an official, exactly. This is not a place where authority sits high and speaks down. My work would begin before anyone wants to be awake, when the town is blue-black, the lamps have sunk low, and the first condensation has not yet committed itself to falling. I would go out with a hood drawn close, a bead-string at my wrist, a dry reed tucked behind one ear, and a shallow cup hanging empty from my belt.

I would walk the wet edges of the settlement and find out what the night had done.

Which stair still belongs to itself.

Which gutter has turned bitter.

Which lane now leads through a fern hollow that yesterday belonged behind the ancestor wall.

Which paving stones have lifted from root-pulse and not settled cleanly.

Where the roof foxes crossed.

Whether the mist came from above, below, or sideways through the young cracks of the cliff.

I would not decide these things. I would notice them. Then I would leave signs small enough not to offend anything: a twist of pale fiber on a handrail for a changed stair; three seed husks pressed into moss for an unsafe turn; a smear of ash on the lower side of a lintel if a house-door opens today into the wrong version of itself. Those who know would read them. Children would be taught to. Outsiders, if any ever came, would think the marks were dirt, bird leavings, accident.

This would suit me: to be useful by attending.

I would have narrow hands stained dark at the fingertips from wet bark, fungus ink, and the handling of old route tokens. My calves would be strong from steps. One knee would ache before cold rain. I would move with the habitual forward balance of someone who never entirely trusts stone, because stone here is young and remembers heat. My voice would be low from disuse, but not weak. When I spoke, it would usually be close to someone’s sleeve.

People would bring me questions they did not want to ask loudly.

“Does the left stair still pass the smoke court?”

“Did you see black ferns under Maru’s eaves?”

“May we take the upper runnel apart today, or is the stone listening?”

“Was it a roof fox that took my daughter’s path-memory, or only fog?”

I would answer carefully, because in this town a careless answer can become a structure. Enough people believe a route is closed and it may close. Enough people deny a leak and the wall may grow ferns from the lie. So I would answer with placements when I could: a token turned damp-side up, a reed laid crosswise, a cup left inverted, a small bead moved from one cord to another.

I would know the dry routes, the wet routes, and some of the below-mist routes, though not all. No one knows all. Anyone claiming to know all would be watched for black ferns.

My dwelling would smell of cold ash, oiled cord, mushroom leather, and the sour skins of cloud-pear drying near the rafters. I would keep no grand possessions. A bed platform. A low lamp. A wall of hanging route cords knotted by season. Baskets sealed with pitch. A polished stone weight used for testing slope. A bundle of antler splints for threshold markers. A bronze hook inherited from someone whose name I would touch with two fingers before using it.

There would be animals in my house, as there must be.

A condensation lizard would live behind the inner shutter, green-gray and solemn, drinking from the reed lattice and blinking whenever I lied to myself. Moths would line the door seam in winter. A snub frog would occupy the lip of my smallest water jar, and I would dislike guests who laughed at it, because it would know sooner than I did if the jar turned sour. Under the roof meadow, beetles would work through the old thatch. Sometimes one of the tiny white grazers would fall through a weak place and land in the rafters, indignant and unharmed, and I would have to lift it out with both hands while it chewed my sleeve.

I would not be young.

Not old enough to sit all day by the ancestor wall, but old enough that children would stop whispering when I passed and then start again behind me. Old enough to have lost someone to a route that changed badly. Perhaps that is how one becomes a route-reader: not through talent, but through the refusal to let a disappearance remain only grief.

There would have been a sister, maybe, or a son, or a companion whose laugh was too bright for the town’s habits. Someone who once crossed from the cistern quarter to the smoke court after a below-mist and did not arrive where the path had always taken them. For a season I would have left bowls at thresholds. Empty gloves at the lower stones. Bitter plum on rooflines. I would have turned my coat inside out at every glimpse of a white-masked animal.

Nothing returned except a comb with three teeth missing, laid carefully on my own step.

I did not take it with the left hand.

After that, I learned routes with a hunger that frightened people. Then, slowly, with discipline. Then, more slowly still, with humility. The town does not like to be forced into knowledge. It must be listened to until it volunteers a little.

So each morning I would go out before the first household lamps were opened.

Mist would bead on my hood. The black rock would shine as if newly born. Below, the valley would be full of vapor and treetops. Above, the raw slope would mutter softly as pebbles shifted in meltwater. I would stand with one palm against the young stone beside my door and wait for the faintest tremor: frost loosening, root lifting, water finding its edge.

Then I would begin.

Past the animal stair, where bruised fruit had been taken in the night and salt licked into crescent hollows.

Past the high cistern jars, tapping each cover with a wrapped knuckle. Full, full, half, sweet, sleeping, troubled.

Past a wall sweating clear water—ordinary.

Past another sweating bitter—birth soon, or fear of birth, or someone pretending not to know.

At a turning, I would stop. The path to the smoke court would not be where it belonged. Its stones would still be there, but their intention would have changed. A person can see this if they look without insisting. The moss between the stones leans another way. Drips fall toward a sound that should be behind you. The air smells faintly of fern root instead of peat smoke.

I would mark the handrail with pale fiber.

Not closed. Changed.

At the ancestor wall I would lower my eyes, but not too far. The carvings are still sharp there, each face cut into fresh cliff as if the dead have only recently agreed to be remembered. I would check the base for black ferns. Sometimes they grow in clusters where a family has repeated a polished lie so often it has become part of the household weather.

If I found them, I would not call out.

I would leave a folded gray leaf on the nearest mat of the silent market. Everyone would understand. The cutters would come later, chosen from those outside the lie.

Near the central court I would pause longer than necessary.

The dry basin would sit in its emptiness, smooth and older than anything around it. No drop would cling to its rim. No moss would dare cross the fitted stones toward it. If fog rose from below, perhaps it would show its other sky: fast white birds moving in the basin’s dark, their wings going somewhere no one here can go.

I would not study it greedily.

That is another town rule, though no one teaches it directly: do not stare into anything that refuses water.

Still, I would know the basin and it would know me in the impersonal way that a cliff knows lichen. If there is a larger agency here—the thing beneath paths, behind mist, older than the mountain’s exposed face—I would not worship it. Worship is too loud. I would behave toward it. That is different, and more binding.

By midmorning, the town would have read my marks and adjusted around them. A mother would take the long stair with her baskets. Smoke court workers would enter by the fern side. Children sent for oil would be warned not to chase the ash-birds past the second landing. An elder would remove a threshold wedge because the house was presenting its rain-door today. No proclamation. No gathering. Just the small redirections by which the settlement remains itself.

At the market I would trade route knowledge for things I needed.

Not by saying, “I know the safe way past the ancestor wall.”

That would be vulgar, nearly dangerous.

Instead I would place a black pebble beside a bundle of dried red-stem berries. The seller might shift the bundle halfway toward me. I would set down a twist of cane marked with two wet knots and one dry. The seller would understand: the lower path will be sound until second mistfall; after that, carry your basket empty and return by the thorn poles. The bundle would come the rest of the way.

Payment.

Conversation.

Civic maintenance.

In the afternoon, when roots beneath the paving stones begin their faint lifting, I would stay indoors and repair cords. This is when the town breathes upward. The inattentive stumble. The impatient curse and earn bad luck. Better to sit by the low lamp, warm a blade of volcanic glass between both hands before using it, and cut old knots from route strings that no longer correspond to any living path.

Children might come then for instruction.

They would sit in a half-ring, damp-haired, knees muddy, trying not to fidget. I would teach them the three kinds of quiet: quiet for sleeping animals, quiet for listening stone, quiet for shame. I would teach them that laughter must go into the sleeve after second mistfall, but not be swallowed entirely, because swallowed laughter can sour in the chest. I would make them identify water by sound: jar water, leaf-drip, gutter-thread, wall-sweat, mist-net fall, cistern trouble.

And I would teach them never to say, “There are seven moths on the door.”

They may say, “The door is well attended.”

They may say, “More than yesterday.”

They may say nothing and mark the seam with a thumb.

One child would always ask why.

I would tell them: because numbers pin small lives too tightly, and pinned things struggle.

That would be only partly true, but enough for a first lesson.

Toward evening the cliff antlers would come.

The town would know before seeing them. A pressure in the lanes. Birds flattening under eaves. Frogs pulling their throats still. The compost beetles pausing in their hidden work. Then the first long face would appear between houses, moss-colored, steaming, its branching horns furred with soft sensing cilia. It would lower its head to a post and taste the salt there.

Everyone would stop.

I too would stop, one hand on the doorframe, breath held behind my teeth. A cliff antler might pass so near that its flank brushed my sleeve. It would smell of wet hide, crushed fern, and mineral hunger. Its eye would show no fear and no friendship. It would read the town differently than I did: by salt, pressure, old lichen, the memory of hooves, the mineral speech of posts.

In those moments I would feel most clearly that I am not a citizen of a human place, but one listener in a compact of listeners.

The antler listens with horn-cilia.

The lizard listens with its belly.

The moths listen through the door seam.

The roots listen upward.

The stone listens slowly.

The dry basin listens without receiving.

And I, with my cords and ash marks and aching knee, listen in the human way: by making meaning, then mistrusting it enough to look again.

If I had a name, it would not travel far. Names here are for close use. Perhaps children would call me Reed-Under-Ear, because I often forget the dry reed tucked there. Older people might call me Third-Mist, not because I command such routes, but because I once returned from one. In the ancestor records I would be named by obligation: Keeper of the upper wet-turn, reader of the animal stair, mender of the north runnel signs.

But what I would be, truly, is a threshold person.

Not outside the town, not fully inside it.

One who walks the uncertain places before others have to.

One who knows that safety is never a condition, only a practice.

One who understands that a path is not a line between two points, but a relationship among weather, memory, stone, and permission.

At night I would sit beneath my clicking roof while the town glowed in cracks and seams. Fungi would make pale constellations in the mortar. Moths would pulse along rooflines. Drops would gather starlight from a sky no one could see. Somewhere below, an animal would call once, deep enough to tremble the water in covered jars.

I would not be peaceful.

No one attentive is peaceful.

But I would be at home.

I would trim the lamp. Touch the bronze hook before hanging up my wet cloak. Check that the frog still held its cistern lip. Lay tomorrow’s empty cup beside the door.

And before sleep, I would listen for the town’s smallest answer: one drop falling from cane lattice into clay channel, then another, then another.

Not a promise.

Not a blessing.

Enough.
