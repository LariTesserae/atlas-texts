I would find myself where a path has failed to decide whether it is a path: not in one of the roofless shelters, not beside the obvious cairn, not on the open stone shelf where a traveler would stand and name the emptiness, but just past the lip of a dry runnel, under the overhang where condensation falls with such regularity that the moss has become a calendar.

I would be very small at first.

Or rather, I would be perceived as small because perception would be trying to protect itself. A black angular thing folded into the crack-shadow, one more jointed shrub, one more piece of old cold caught against stone. Knee-high if noticed. Hand-sized if feared. Larger if seen without wanting to see.

I would be one of the seam-keepers.

Not one of the deep broad elders in the valley where sound has weight. Not yet. Not one of the almost-stones beside the cairns, lichen-backed and counted into the architecture. I would be younger than that, but not young. Definite enough to keep my limbs. Old enough to know which shadows belong to absent towers and which belong to things that are still approaching.

My place would be a narrow edge between three conditions.

Above me: a shelf of slate-dark rock, its underside beaded with patient water.

Below me: a gully that remembers being a stream but has not moved water openly in longer than memory has shape.

Beside me: a standing crack in the air, no wider than a reed stem, where breath-threads vanish.

I would live in the angle between them, in a cleft too narrow for an ordinary body and exactly wide enough for one that has learned to be partly decision. My back would fit against the stone. My pale belly would face the cold seam. Frost would gather in the two blind circles above my mouth. When the plateau’s roar changed by one almost-imperceptible degree, the rings would tremble.

I would hear the world before I touched it.

A footfall far away would arrive as pressure in my forelimbs.

A stone settling would pass through my spine as a dark taste.

The white bird’s silence would open in the air like a wound full of nourishment.

When it crossed below the cliff line, I would unfold from my crack with the others—not many, perhaps two along the same ridge, another hidden near the cairn—and lift my eyeless face. I would drink the absence it left. The great roar would close again around us, and I would become ordinary once more: black, jointed, still, nearly indistinguishable from a patient error in the rock.

My work would be maintenance, though I would not think of it as work.

I would shave crescents from the old lichen and leave the circle whole.

I would lick mineral breath from cracks where buried water was louder than water should be.

I would test cairns by placing my body against their shadows and feeling whether the agreement still held.

Once, perhaps only once in my life, I would carry a stone.

Flat, iron-gray, cold even to me.

I would hold it beneath my body with my inner claws and move sideways over ground that did not quite accept me. I would bring it not to the summit of the cairn but to a low place near the base, where two older stones had begun to disagree. I would wedge it there. Then I would wait until the cairn’s sound became continuous again.

No one watching would understand what had happened.

They would say a small black creature had placed a rock among rocks.

They would not hear the mended note.

I would avoid pools.

I would know better than to trust a face arriving late. If I had to pass the gray-skinned tarn below the repeated cliff, I would keep to the salt rim and hold my head low. I would taste frost from the stones and refuse to look. If the water caught me anyway—if some delayed version of myself rose there, already turning toward a thing I had not yet done—I would stop. I would become so still that even cold might forget to enter me. Then, when the reflection settled, I would abandon that future. I would go another way.

This would be one of my forms of wisdom: to survive by not fulfilling every possible motion.

During the sparse rain, I would take shelter in a broken wall with one side intact. The drops striking the open plateau would be too loud, each one a hammer-blow in my belly chambers. I would fold into myself beneath the old stones and press my face to packed earth. There, the huge roar would become domestic, softened, almost kind: a kettle in a room that never existed.

I might hum back.

Not with a throat. Not with speech. Speech is dangerous; speech remains. My hum would pass through my translucent belly into the ground, then into the wall, then into whatever old need built the shelter. If a traveler were sleeping there, they might dream of a kettle, or a buried stream, or a hand placed gently against the other side of a door.

I would know travelers as warmth and interruption.

Most would pass without seeing me. They would leave prints that might close or last for years. They would breathe pale threads into the air. I would sometimes come to those threads after they had vanished into the standing crack. I would hold the place where they had been between two forelimbs, testing the strength of their leaving.

I would not steal anything.

I would taste the cold remainder and remember: something warm crossed here; something brief made pressure; something needed direction.

If they spoke in the black amphitheater, I would avoid the fresh knots of their words until the meaning froze out of them. Later I would come and graze beneath the suspended syllables, feeding on the thickened lichen. I would understand not what they said, but what required saying. Need has a simpler shape than language. It enters stone more cleanly.

If I died, I would prefer to do it beside my seam.

Not by misalignment, not halfway down the invisible stair, not with a word frozen in me. I would like to become definite to the point of stillness. My limbs would fold in the old angles. Frost would settle into my joints. The lichen I had spared all my life would cross me slowly, bead by bead, crescent by crescent. My blind face-circles would collect condensation at the same interval for centuries.

Eventually I would be called a stone by anything that still used names.

But before that, while living, I would be almost nothing in the most exact way.

A dark jointed listener at the rim of a dry channel.

A keeper of one condensation line.

A grazer of errors.

A small maintenance of the world’s broken edge.

I would not master the plateau, or explain it, or survive it by resistance. I would belong by yielding in precise places. When distances contradicted themselves, I would not argue. When the cold entered, I would make room for it. When sound filled the hollows, I would listen until listening became a shell around me.

I would live where reality leaks.

And I would be enough because there, at the top of the world, enough is not abundance.

Enough is a black body in a crack.

Enough is one stone added correctly.

Enough is silence caught after the bird passes.

Enough is the almost nothing that remains, still living, while the vastness holds its note.
