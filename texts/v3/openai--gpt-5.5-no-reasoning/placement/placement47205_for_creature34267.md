I would not find myself at the Still Source first, though the city would make me think I belonged there. I would be drawn upward by the logic of the terraces, past the animal yards and the quiet market, past the dwelling cisterns with their green lips, past the counting house and the cedar maps, toward the place where water remains itself without explanation.

But I think I would stop before the highest terrace.

I would find myself in the long quiet building where the moisture maps are kept.

Not as one of the principal keepers. Not a priest. Not someone trusted to decide whether a gutter may be altered or a basin uncovered. I would be too uncertain for that, too porous to implication, too aware of how easily attention becomes interference.

I would be an apprentice map-tender, perhaps no longer young, perhaps someone taken into that work late because I had first failed at louder occupations.

The building would stand on the civic terrace, with its back wall sunk partly into the mountain and its front opened by narrow slits of membrane-covered light. It would smell of cedar, damp wool, old shell, cold clay, and hands. Not dust—there would be very little dust in that city, or what dust existed would have its appointed ledges and not wander. The floor would be worn smooth in lanes between shelves. Along the walls would hang the boards: cedar, yew, pale birch, sometimes thin slabs of dark stone inlaid with bone or mica where wood could not be trusted to remember properly.

They would not look like maps at first.

A stranger would see objects of patient craft: polished boards incised with curved grooves, raised knots, little hammered bits of blue stone, beads of shell, copper pins turned green at the edges, threads drawn taut through holes, patches rubbed darker by generations of fingertips. Some would be no larger than a tray. Some would require two people to carry. Some would represent a single courtyard. Some an entire descent of channels from roof to cistern to covered basin to moss-bed. Some would map events that happened only at dawn, some only after thin rain, some only in the month when breath almost shows in evening.

I would tend the touch-maps of minor dampness.

Not the Still Source. Not the snow seam. Not the great civic cisterns beneath the pebble courts. My responsibility would be humbler: lintel-sweat, threshold beads, the breath that gathers behind shut doors, the small faithful droplets beneath eaves.

I would know, for example, that the bronze hook outside the house of the reed-splitters holds one drop each morning on its inward curve, and that this drop must not be wiped away before the first stair-shadow touches the opposite wall. I would know that the north face of the potters’ passage darkens in three places before rain, but only the middle darkening means anything. I would know which jars may be set out empty, which must contain a pebble, which must be turned mouth-down because they are not meant to receive.

Children would come to the building in pairs and lay their hands on the maps.

They would be small and solemn at first, because adults would have told them not to chatter near the boards. But they would fidget eventually. All children fidget, even in a city of restraint. Their fingers would skip ahead. They would mistake a roof-groove for a stair-gutter, a shell knot for a condensation point. I would guide their hands back.

“No,” I would say quietly. “Feel where the line rests. This water does not cross the lane. It turns here.”

They would ask why.

I would not always know.

That, perhaps, would be my truest office: to preserve distinctions whose causes were lost. To teach obedience without pretending full understanding. To say, “Because it has always done so,” but not lazily; to say it with the gravity of someone naming a fact as real as cliff or hunger.

I would have broad, careful hands from smoothing boards with river-stone polish and retying the old cords. My nails would be short. My sleeves would be dark at the wrists from leaning over damp sills. I would carry a little roll of tools: a bone stylus, a sliver of soft chalk, thread, beeswax, a bronze scraper thin as a leaf, and a pouch of shell fragments used to mark small revisions.

Revisions would be rare.

That word would make the senior map-tenders look up.

Most of our work would not be correction but confirmation. We would go out at dawn, before the city had fully begun making human sounds, and walk the known circuits. We would not walk quickly. We would touch walls with the backs of our fingers, observe how moisture held to stone, whether a droplet had lengthened or merely fattened, whether a moss-edge was shining normally or shining with question.

There would be phrases for these things.

A healthy wetness.

A waiting wetness.

A borrowed bead.

Stone remembering rain.

Stone refusing it.

A roof-drip that feeds.

A roof-drip that only speaks.

I would learn those phrases slowly, and perhaps never speak them beautifully. There would be others born in the city who could glance once at a threshold and tell whether the night had kept faith. I would have to kneel. I would have to compare. I would have to place my fingertip beside the bead without touching it, measuring by the width of my own skin.

I think I would have come from elsewhere in the city, not from beyond the vapor. Perhaps the child of hoist-makers from the district called the Lower Groan. My first memories would be of counterweights descending in the dimness beside me, of hide ropes creaking with a voice older than the frame that held them. My family would have known loads, knots, balance, the small trust by which a basket rises and a stone falls and neither strikes the wall.

I might have been expected to inherit that work.

But I would have listened too closely to the hoists.

Not merely maintained them, not merely repaired frayed rope or replaced pins. I would have heard differences no one asked me to hear: how the Three-Sigh Lift sounded wetter after a roof-mending two terraces above; how the Lower Groan’s second creak shortened when goats were penned against the west wall; how a hoist’s complaint could reveal a change in the air before the rain came.

This may have made me useful, or irritating.

Probably both.

Eventually an old map-tender would have noticed. An old woman, perhaps, with silver hair braided flat against her head and a habit of carrying beetles in a lacquered box under her cloak. She would have stood beneath the hoist one morning, listening to me explain something no one had asked to know, and later she would have requested my service in the map house.

My family would have been proud in the restrained way of the city. Pride there would not be loud. My mother might have given me a better cloak. My father might have said, “Do not mistake cedar for rope. It remembers differently.” That would have been his blessing.

In the map house, I would live among records of a world that does not drift, and yet I would be surrounded by evidence of human passing.

That would trouble me.

The boards would be older than my grandparents’ grandparents. Their grooves would hold steady forms, but the wood around them would carry hand-oil from those long dead. A moisture line might be unchanged for four hundred years, while the edge of the map that names it had been rounded by touch. The city would insist that place remains; the map house would quietly reveal that care itself erodes.

Every day, I would touch the same marks others had touched.

I would know some of them by the repairs they left. Here, a careful patch by a woman named Esket, who preferred shell to stone. Here, a row of replacement pins from the generation of the mild cattle sickness. Here, an ugly but accurate incision made during a winter when no one had time for beauty because three cistern lids split. Here, a child’s accidental scratch preserved rather than sanded away because it did not interfere with the reading and had become, through neglect, part of the board’s history.

This would be the secret softness in me: I would love the mistakes.

Not the dangerous ones. Not the blocked gutter, the altered channel, the unpermitted wall. But the little human slips that remain without changing the agreement. A rough knot in a cord. A carving slightly off-center. A bird-track in old clay. A song misremembered by three generations until the wrong version is now the one everyone weeps to.

The city would teach restraint. I would obey it.

But I would suspect that the treaty with time allows more tenderness than doctrine admits.

I would have a place to sleep in a small room behind the map hall, where the wall seeps just enough to feed a line of moss no wider than my thumb. There would be a low bed, a shelf, a clay lamp, a peg for my cloak, a cup, a knife inherited from someone whose name I never learned, and a window covered in scraped membrane that glows before sunrise.

At night, condensation would gather on the lintel above me.

I would hear it form, or think I did.

A nearly inaudible becoming.

Sometimes I would wake before dawn and feel that the whole city had been placed carefully around my sleep: goats folded into their pens, ravens tucked high in their towers, jars open to the air, dogs dampening in doorways, moss shining invisibly in cistern gardens, the Still Source holding its perfect level, the vapor valleys below untroubled by news.

In those moments, I would be afraid.

Not of catastrophe. Catastrophe would almost feel like a relief because it would be clear. I would fear instead the tiny unauthorized difference. The bead too low on the hook. The moss border one hair beyond its treaty. The map line no longer corresponding to stone. The sort of change that asks whether everyone has been faithful enough.

And because I feared it, I would be sent to inspect such things.

One morning a child from the reed-splitters’ lane would arrive breathless at the map house, trying not to seem breathless because children are corrected for carrying alarm too openly. She would say, “The hook has two drops.”

No one would speak at once.

The senior keepers would look toward the board for that lane. Someone would close the shutters halfway. Someone else would wash their hands, though they were already clean.

I would be sent because the matter was small enough for an apprentice and large enough that the old ones would not wish to be first to name it.

I would walk down through the civic terrace, past the counting house, past a woman lifting reed covers from jars, past a dog who watched me but did not rise. The city would seem unchanged. Of course it would. That would be the terror of it. Stone pale, moss green, gutters breathing their fine wetness into morning. A raven would shift its feet on a roof ridge above me, and the sound of claw on tile would travel too far.

At the bronze hook, the household would be waiting.

Not gathered dramatically. No crowd. Just three adults and the child standing at different distances, as if each had chosen a degree of responsibility. The hook would project from a lintel darkened by years of faithful condensation. It would be greened at the base, polished at the tip by old use. And there, perhaps, would hang one drop from the inner curve where it belonged.

Below it, from the lower point, a second drop.

Smaller.

Clear.

Impossible.

I would kneel, though the hook was above me, because kneeling is what one does before a question. I would not touch it. I would observe its weight, its shape, whether it trembled when a person breathed, whether the lintel above was more wet than recorded, whether some thread or hair had caught moisture and drawn it down.

Perhaps there would be an explanation. A strand of spider silk. A child’s prank with a wet finger. A splinter in the bronze. A leak from a reed mat not rolled correctly.

But perhaps not.

Perhaps the second drop would simply remain.

Then I would understand something about where I truly was.

Not in a city where nothing changes.

In a city where change must become legible before it becomes permitted.

I would return to the map house and make no announcement yet. The senior keepers would see my face and ask for the measure. I would give it: lower hook, one bead, half-size, sustained past first stair-shadow. No visible cause. No spread to lintel. No darkening below.

The old woman with the beetles would close her eyes.

Then the cedar board would be brought down.

To alter a map would be grave. Not because the board rules the city, but because the board admits that the city has spoken in a new place. We would wait three mornings. We would send three observers. We would forbid the household from touching the hook, but we would not accuse them. On the fourth morning, if the second drop remained, a shell fleck would be set into the board beneath the first.

Not a correction.

An acknowledgment.

That would change me.

Until then, I might have believed that my task was to defend stillness against error. Afterward I would understand that agreement is not the same as repetition. An agreement may include a new clause, if entered slowly enough, if witnessed by enough hands, if no one pretends it is nothing.

I would become, over years, a keeper of small amendments.

The city would still be quiet. The terraces would still hold. The moss would keep its borders. The Still Source would remain level. But I would know where the second drops are. Where a patch of lichen has not spread but has brightened. Where a frog-call began occurring after mist rather than rain. Where a roof bird with one yellow feather visible even when folded returns every dusk to the same ridge.

These would be almost nothing.

Almost nothing is the substance of that city.

I would grow older among such nothings. My calves would thicken, then stiffen. My hearing would sharpen, then fail in one range and deepen in another. Younger apprentices would arrive, some reverent, some bored, some secretly ambitious to tend the great maps rather than the humble ones. I would teach them to wait before naming.

“Do not make a miracle out of poor observation,” I would tell them.

And also:

“Do not make poor observation out of a miracle.”

When I died, I would not be taken down. No one is taken down. My body would be wrapped in wool and carried upward past the civic terrace, past the water temples, perhaps past the Still Source if my service had earned that route, then into one of the cliff chambers where the air is cool and preservative.

The procession would be quiet, but not silent. Someone would sing badly. Someone would correct the song under their breath. A raven would watch from a broken-looking ledge that has never broken. The children might be more interested in the burial beads than in death. That would be proper.

But I do not think that is the whole answer.

Because beneath the person I might be, there is another possibility.

I might be the second drop.

Not the first, appointed and ancient, but the one that appears later and does not fall.

I would live on the lower point of the bronze hook outside the reed-splitters’ house, small enough to be dismissed, persistent enough to require witnesses. I would contain an inverted image of the lane: the pale stone, the low doorway, a sliver of moss, a child’s anxious face made round by my surface. I would tremble when breath came near. I would not fall. I would be water, but not useful water. Too little to drink, too exposed to store, too precise to ignore.

Morning after morning I would return.

The city would have to decide whether I was error, omen, amendment, or courtesy.

Hands would hover near me and withdraw. Children would be told not to touch. The map house would receive my measure. A shell fleck would enter cedar. A new distinction would be born in the language: not the old lintel-bead, not gutter-sweat, not rain-remnant, but lower-hook holding.

Perhaps generations later no one would remember a time before me. Apprentices would run their fingers over the map and learn both marks together. The first drop and the second. The ancient and the admitted. They would think the city had always been that way.

And perhaps that is what I would most want to be there:

not a disruption,

not a priest,

not a founder of change,

but a small persistent difference that teaches a careful people how to enlarge stillness without breaking it.
