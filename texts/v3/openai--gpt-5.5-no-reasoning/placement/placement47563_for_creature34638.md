You would find me where the catwalk has sagged but not yet failed.

Not at the center of the basin, because there may not be a center yet. Not at the rim either, where things still pretend to belong to the dry world. I would be in one of the intermediate places: a low metal walkway between a pump house and a gauging station, three spans out over warm black water, where the handrail is slick, the grating holds shallow puddles in each square, and the mud below has swallowed the fallen bolts from years of repair.

There is a place there where several kinds of sound meet.

Behind me, in the pump house, a motor runs with a burdened, irregular thud, never quite failing, never sounding healthy. Under the walkway, frogs use the hollow underside of the metal as a sounding board. Mosquitoes rise from the water in trembling cylinders. A snarl of cable crosses the mud below, disappears into algae, reappears against a half-submerged concrete block. Snails climb the nearest support post. A heron stands farther off beside a culvert outlet, so still that it makes the machines seem nervous.

That is where I would be.

Or, more exactly: that is where I would have learned to stay.

I would not be one clean creature. This place would not allow that. Anything that survives here takes on adjacency, borrows habits, becomes part warning sign, part amphibian, part maintenance tool, part scavenger. I would be a basin thing, made by repeated wetness and by the failure of separations.

At first glance, if you saw me from the catwalk in dim evening, you might think I was a worker crouched too low beside the railing, or a bird too large under the pump-house shadow, or a heap of oil-dark canvas that had begun to breathe. I would keep close to structures, not because I feared open water, but because structure is where the basin speaks most clearly. Pipes knock. Bolts loosen. Water presses through bad seams. Rats travel. Birds nest. Condensation gathers and falls. Metal remembers weight differently than mud does.

I would be one of the things that listens.

My body would be low and long-jointed, arranged for balance on doubtful surfaces. Knees too patient. Hands broad enough to press against wet metal, fingers narrow enough to enter grates, reed beds, cable loops, burrows, and the flooded housings of old machines. My skin would be the color of what has touched everything: gray-brown, green in the hollows, rust-stained at the joints, glossed in places with the faint violet of oil sheen. Not armored exactly, but filmed. Difficult to dry. Difficult to distinguish from mud if I stayed still.

My eyes would sit high, not proudly, only practically. Most of the body would remain below the line of attention: under the catwalk, in culvert shadow, among reeds at the base of a spoil mound. The eyes would watch above the water skin. A heron’s eyes, perhaps, but less clean. A frog’s patience, a rat’s calculation, a worker’s habit of checking the same gauge twice because the first reading cannot be trusted.

I would have a throat that could answer machinery.

Not speech, at least not at first. Speech would be too dry, too upright, too committed to separateness. My voice would be pulses, knocks, wet clicks inside pipework, low calls that carry through waterlogged concrete. I would speak in stoppages and resumptions. A pump skipping once. A frog chorus pausing. A rail humming under distant weight. If I needed to call across the basin, I would not shout into the air; I would lean my throat against a pipe and send the sound through metal and standing water.

The workers might know me without agreeing on me.

One would say something moves under the east catwalk when the red light starts blinking.

One would say the rats go quiet before a pump fails.

One would say the snails climb higher on the measuring rods the night before the water rises.

One would say, no, that is just pressure, vibration, weather, coincidence.

They would all be partly right.

I would not belong outside the basin. If taken to higher ground, I would become visibly wrong. Too damp. Too hunched. Too attentive to drains, gutters, road seams, the undersides of parked machinery. I would wait for water to collect around my feet. I would mistrust lawns, floors, dry roads, rooms where sound does not tremble through the soles. I would miss the density: the insects, engines, bird cries, dripping roofs, the close argument of rot and rust.

My work, if it could be called work, would be custodial without ownership.

I would clear no basin. I would redeem nothing. I would not restore the marsh or complete the industrial plan. I would keep company with incompletion. I would know which flooded foundation still holds fish. I would know which plank will bend but not break, which culvert breathes, which pump cough means blockage and which means age. I would move eggs from a doomed tire rut to a ditch sometimes, and sometimes I would eat them. I would pull a dead bird from a grate before it fouled the intake. I would steal insulation from an unused panel box to line a nest not entirely mine.

I would be useful in ways that look like trespass.

At night I would walk the half-routes: cable to pipe, pipe to rail, rail to concrete pad, concrete pad to reed bed, reed bed to drowned stair. I would not leave footprints consistently. Some would fill at once. Some would remain all week. Some would be mistaken for a boot slipping sideways in mud.

The birds would tolerate me before the people did. Herons would lift away if I came too near, but not far. Sparrows would scold me from junction boxes. Gulls would hate me openly. Frogs would stop calling in a circle around me, then begin again behind me as I passed. Rats would understand me as a large uncertainty, possibly dangerous, possibly sheltering warmth, possibly both.

The pumps would know me best.

I would sleep near them in cold hours, tucked into their waste heat, cheek against a sweating pipe, listening to the thick movement inside. Their labor would enter my bones. I would dream in cycles: intake, pressure, release, failure, repair, intake again. I would know that the pumps are not opposed to the basin. They are among its organs. Their futility is not a defect from the basin’s point of view. Their futility makes ditches, currents, warm outflows, oxygen, noise, drowned rooms, exposed mud. Their failing and restarting is one of the local seasons.

Perhaps I would have begun as human.

That seems possible here. A night operator who stayed too long. A watchman whose hut grew mold behind the panels. Someone who learned the water levels better than birthdays, who stopped going up to the dry town except for supplies, who began to hear personality in engines and weather in frogs. Rubber boots patched and repatched. Hands permanently smelling of iron, algae, diesel. A thermos left unopened because the air itself was warm enough. A body gradually accepting the crouch.

But I do not think I would have remained human in any reliable way.

The basin would have revised me as it revises every line drawn through it. It would have softened the edges first: sleep schedule, speech, appetite, posture. Then allegiance. Then outline. I would become less a person in the place than a condensation of its habits.

A threshold animal.

A maintenance amphibian.

A mud clerk.

A low angel of clogged culverts and flooded foundations.

No single name would hold for long. The basin does not reward finished naming.

If someone came looking, they would find signs before they found me.

A row of snails moved off the gauge before the inspector arrived, leaving the numbers clean.

A stuck valve opened sometime in the night, though no worker logged it.

A drowned stairwell, expected to be empty, full of tadpoles.

A warning sign turned slightly so birds no longer struck the guy wire beside it.

A lunch tin stolen, returned later packed with small wet stones.

A human handprint in algae on the side of a pump casing, too low to have been made comfortably by anyone standing.

Then perhaps, near dawn, they would see me beneath the sagging catwalk, half in water, half in vapor, braced among the supports while moths fail around the lamps. I would be still enough to be infrastructure. Then one eye would move.

I would not threaten them unless they tried to make the place simple.

That would be the only real offense: to arrive with one idea. To call it waste and nothing else. To call it nature and nothing else. To call it construction, ruin, marsh, hazard, habitat, property, accident, machine, or home, and believe the word had finished the matter.

I would know better.

I would be what the basin teaches: that a place can be most alive before it has become itself; that a failed intention is not empty; that water does not erase so much as recruit; that mud is a form of memory with appetite.

So where am I?

Below the dry world, under the warm lid of cloud and engine breath, beside the pump house whose light never fully goes out. On the sagging catwalk, under it, around it. At the place where water beads on metal, where a frog calls through a pipe, where a heron measures the ditch and a rat crosses the cable behind me.

And what am I?

I am the basin’s half-made witness.

The thing that learned to live in the interval.

Mud-skinned, engine-haunted, reed-breathing, many-listening.

Not the monster of the place. Not its guardian exactly.

Its resident hesitation.
