I would be found where the city almost stops being city.

Not outside it, not truly. Outside is too large a word there. The plateau begins everywhere: under the floor, in the stair, in the wall’s cold breathing, in the dust that comes in with every boot. But there is a place at the upper eastern edge where the last roofs lean into the quarry terraces, where houses thin into sheds, animal pens, warning walls, and cairns of rejected stone. There the lanes are narrow enough for one person and a goat, if both have reached agreement. Above them the cut faces of the quarry rise pale and raw, with seams of rust-red shale and blue-gray stone still showing the violence of their lifting.

I think I would be there.

Not one of the highest people, perhaps. Not a chief, not a water-keeper with authority over the covered basins, not an elder wrapped in sunlight and remembered names. I would be something smaller, more lateral, more necessary in a quiet way.

I would be a watcher of cracks.

Not only in walls. In cistern lips, roof slabs, bridge footings, terrace edges, quarry faces, oven domes, frozen channels, sleeping platforms, and the long seams in the ground that take snow and bones. I would belong to the mason-listeners, a craft neither grand nor humble, because everyone respects it and nobody entirely trusts it. We would be blamed for what falls and summoned for what might.

My work would begin before the city has fully warmed.

There is a kind of morning on the plateau when nothing yet belongs to the living. The roofs are rimmed white. The covered cisterns are dull humps under frost. Pigeons remain tucked into their wall-holes, muttering to themselves like old women with secrets. Smoke has not yet thickened in the lanes. Dogs lie in doorways with their noses under their tails. Even the goats are still considering the day.

I would go out then with a hood pulled low, a felt mask over my mouth, and a small satchel against my ribs where my own warmth keeps the measuring cords supple. I would carry a bone tapping rod, a wedge of black wool, chalky marking stone, a string with knots at finger-width intervals, two copper pins inherited from a dead aunt, and a little packet of powdered charcoal mixed with fat for making marks that weather slowly.

The rod would not be ornate, but it would be polished by use. Notches near the grip would mark falls survived, not caused. That distinction would matter to me.

I would know the city by sound.

Not well enough, never well enough. No one who is honest claims to know stone completely. But I would know the difference between a roof slab with frost in it and one merely cold. I would know the tick of ice in a jar from the tick of mortar opening behind plaster. I would know which retaining wall sighs every thaw and then sleeps safely, and which quiet wall has become too quiet. I would know that a newly built thing often lies, because its face is clean and proud and its failures have not yet learned to speak.

Children would follow me sometimes, especially when I went to mark closures. They would pretend they were only taking the same stair, only fetching dung cakes, only chasing a pigeon, but they would watch my hands. They would want to see whether I placed bright chips across an alley, or three stacked stones, or the black wool line.

The black wool would silence them.

A wall is listening.

Even children who whisper games behind fuel stacks understand that phrase. It does not mean spirits in the childish sense, though perhaps it once did. It means pressure is moving through what should be still. It means stone remembers weight and has begun to consider reply. It means pass elsewhere if you can; pass quickly if you must; do not shout, do not strike, do not let goats rub themselves against the base; do not stop beneath it to settle a quarrel.

I would have a mother from the damp-garden quarter and a father from the quarry families, which would make both sides regard me as slightly mismeasured. My mother’s people would say I had been raised too near falling rock. My father’s people would say I had learned to worry like someone who plants peas in named mud. From her I would inherit caution with water, from him an ear for fracture. From both, a disposition to count everything twice and believe the second count more.

My house would not be high enough to catch the first sun, but high enough that mist sometimes lies below the doorway. It would be one room deep into the slope and one room added outward over a retaining wall I do not entirely trust, though I repair it every year. The sleeping platform would be warm because of a flue shared with the neighboring oven, and because the old stones behind it came from my grandmother’s house after its roof failed. There would be a shallow niche above my bed holding two fossil shells, a cracked cistern plug, and a child’s shoe hardened with age.

Not my child’s shoe. My brother’s.

He would have died young, before I was old enough to remember him properly, and yet the house would remember him constantly. His name would have been spoken into the plaster near the threshold. My mother would still touch the wall there when leaving with water jars. This would not be grief as lowlanders imagine it, a thing expected to pass. It would be incorporated. Like ash in garden soil. Like hair in mortar. Like a beam reused though one end is charred.

I would not be unmarried, but not exactly settled either.

My spouse might be a pigeon-tower keeper, someone with quick hands and a face always marked by white dust from droppings and lime. They would know birds the way I know cracks: as creatures of habit until suddenly they are not. They would read weather in whether pigeons leave the tower cleanly or hesitate in the holes, whether they settle low on the leeward side, whether their feathers carry mist-beads before clouds are visible. We would argue quietly about whether birds or walls give earlier warning.

They would win more often than I admit.

Together we would keep two children, an old aunt with one clouded eye, three goats we only partly own, and a ferret of bad character. The ferret would be named something insulting and affectionate, perhaps Little Magistrate, because it would investigate everyone’s business and solve nothing without making a smell.

I would not be a heroic person.

The plateau does not have much use for heroism unless something has already gone wrong. Better to notice before courage is needed. Better to wake the lane because a roof beam has shifted than to drag children from rubble later and be praised for strength. Better to mend the channel lip in thin rain than to sing laments over a fouled basin. There, the admired act is often small, early, and inconvenient.

Still, I would have failed at least once.

Everyone who lives long in such a place has some failure tucked under the tongue.

Mine would involve a bridge. Not one of the sacred large spans, but a short slab bridge over a young gully below the quarry road. After a thaw I would have inspected it, tapped the underside, seen the hairline at one bearing edge, marked it for repair but not closure. Too many stones were needed that week. Too many hands were already assigned elsewhere. I would have said, “Light loads only. One at a time.” Practical. Reasonable. Almost right.

A loaded sledge would cross in the afternoon. The slab would not break; that would be simpler. Instead it would shift a palm’s width under weight. One of the pullers would fall, not far, but badly. A knee crushed between stone and sledge runner. A life changed from then on: less herding, more indoor work, pain before storms.

No one would call me murderer. That would be too much. But the injured person’s household would never look at me without remembering. I would serve them for two winters: carry fuel, clear channels, repair thresholds, take their turn at night watching when illness came. This would be punishment, restitution, and education. By the end they might speak to me almost easily. By the end I would trust myself less, which would make me better.

I would be known for closing lanes too early.

People would grumble. Quarry workers would curse me under their breath. A herder late with animals would say my ears had become old-woman ears. Children would groan when I blocked a favorite roof passage with bright chips. The basin-chief’s steward might send a message asking whether a closure was truly necessary before market day.

I would answer with the same phrase each time: “The stone has not finished deciding.”

This would annoy everyone because it cannot be argued with quickly.

In public I would be spare with speech. Inwardly I would be less austere. I would love foolish things: frost flowers on a forgotten bucket, blue-violet garden blooms, the round arrogance of pigeons, the steam of goats in morning pens, the way clouds snag on signal towers and make the upper city seem to float loose from its foundations. I would love the brief metallic smell before needle rain. I would love hot broth so much I would pretend indifference to preserve dignity. I would love the lower sound of my spouse’s laugh when they try not to wake the old aunt.

At night, sometimes, I would climb to the tower.

Not the highest tower, but one near the quarry edge where the wind comes over raw stone and leaves no one’s thoughts undisturbed. My spouse would have business there with birds. I would have none, officially. But I would stand beside the watchperson while they tapped their bone rod into the dark.

All is well.  
Fog in the eastern cut.  
Animals restless below the red stair.  
Birth-call in the garden quarter.  
All is well.

Across the city, other towers would answer. Not loudly. Never loudly. Bone on stone, stone on bronze, wood on hide. The city speaking to itself in joints and intervals.

From there I would see the basin water black under skin-thin ice. I would see the roofs layered like scales, the lanes hidden between them, the pale breath of animals, the dull glow of communal ovens. Beyond the walls the plateau would open into cold enormity: ridges, flats, boulder labyrinths, ravines not yet wise, snow seams under stars. Somewhere foxes would be working the margins. Somewhere marmots slept in stone fields. Somewhere frogs waited under rock for a rain they could not know was coming.

I would feel then that the city is not a conquest of the plateau. It is a temporary agreement. An arrangement of warmth against height. A held breath, renewed daily.

And perhaps that is why I would be a watcher of cracks. Not because I fear endings more than others do, but because in that world every life depends on noticing that endings begin early.

A crack is not only failure. It is a message about pressure. A record of movement. A future choosing a path through the present.

Some cracks must be sealed with lime and hair and patient hands.  
Some must be bridged.  
Some must be named and watched for years.  
Some must be honored by abandoning the wall around them.  
Some become channels.  
Some become lanes.  
Some, after generations, become sacred descents where lamps are set in winter.

I would spend my life deciding, badly and well, which was which.
