I would find myself not at the tarn, though I would think of it constantly.

Not in the open center. Not where the basin gathers its black water and lowers all sound. I would be near enough to feel that lowering, near enough that my body would know the tarn was behind me even in darkness, but I would live in the slant between center and wall: under a shale lip on the western side, where the warm crack exhales at intervals and the mist comes late.

At first I seem like one of the clickers.

That is the easiest mistake. I have the angles for it. I am built of hinged dark segments and thin legs, with a body no longer than a little finger joint. My back is matte violet-black, dusted with the same pale grit that powders the ledges. In stillness I disappear against broken shale. I can brace myself across a fissure with three limbs on one wall and three on the other, suspended in the narrow cold, listening through my feet.

But I am not exactly one of the wall-clickers. I do not join their large percussive conversations except as weather joins them, altering what returns.

I am a seam-keeper.

A small animal of cracks.

My kind lives where two kinds of stone meet: basalt and shale, dry fracture and damp breathing line, lichen crust and mineral stain. We occupy the thresholds too narrow for the winged roostlings, too dry for the amphibians, too exposed for the worms. We are not numerous in the way the clickers are numerous. There may be twenty of us in the whole hollow, though no one has counted, and no one here would understand counting except as a sequence of impacts: one tap, pause, two taps, loss.

My body is flattened sideways rather than downward, made to enter vertical seams. I can turn myself thin. My plates overlap like dark leaves, each rim edged with pale sensory hairs that stiffen when the pressure changes. I have eight limbs, but two are not for walking. They are long, jointed feelers carried backward over my body, touching the walls behind me. I move forward while remembering the stone at my rear.

This is my gift: I know when a crack has altered.

Not by thought. Not by foresight. By pressure in the joints. By a bitterness in the damp. By the way a tap sent through shale returns with a new looseness inside it.

The amphibians face upslope before rockfall. The flattened predators withdraw before mist. The winged things wake when cloud thickens. My kind knows fracture. We feel the mountain considering release.

I live beneath a leaning slab that has not yet fallen.

It is taller than any animal that comes here, a blade of blue-grey shale caught between two basalt teeth. It has leaned since before my hatching; it will fall after my death, unless it falls tonight. Its underside sweats even when the air is dry. Lichen grows along one edge in a black seam, and pale thread-moss hangs from the protected lower lip. Beneath it is my passage: a narrow, ribbed dark where moisture beads, where the wind cannot enter, where the western warmth arrives as a pulse every few hours.

I feed on films.

Not the lichen mats themselves, not unless hunger has become louder than caution. I scrape the slick bronze growth from warm stone with my mouthparts. I eat the pale bloom that appears where guano water runs and dries. I take fungal threads from dead clickers when the blue worms have opened them. Sometimes I steal eggs from the underside of a ledge: clicker eggs, winged roostling mites, once the soft end of an amphibian string where it had been laid too shallow and forgotten by water.

I am not noble. Nothing here is noble. Hunger is the first grammar.

Yet I am not only hunger.

My body makes silk, though not silk like a spider’s web. It is coarse, mineral-stiff, extruded in short grey strands from beneath my rear plates. I lay it across small fractures, not to catch prey but to read movement. A strand fixed from one side of a crack to the other will tremble when stone shifts by less than the width of a mite. Frost loosens it. Warm breath slackens it. Fine rain makes it heavy. Passing grazers break it, and I come to repair it, tasting their wet seed-smell on the torn end.

Around my home is a little field of these strands.

To another creature it might seem accidental: lint caught on rock, old fungal hair, shed fiber. To me it is a map of tension. It tells me which ledges are safe enough for feeding, which shelves are about to shed grit, which way the basin has breathed in the night.

When the hollow is dry, I go higher.

The dry season makes the clickers loud and brittle. They argue across the walls in sharp, glassy ticks. The pale grazers retreat deep under flakes, coiled in their translucent spirals. The tarn shrinks into itself, blacker and less present, though never absent. During those times I climb the red seam but do not cross it. No one crosses it except things already dying or too ignorant to have inherited fear. I follow its edge because the stone there gives off a taste like blood and lightning, and rare films grow where poison meets damp.

The red seam is a border in me. I do not decide against it. My feet stop. My mouthparts close. My abdomen lowers to the stone. I turn aside with the certainty of a dropped pebble.

When mist returns, I descend.

Then the hollow swells without growing. Water beads on my plates until I look partly made of glass. The moss beneath the overhang darkens. The winged roostlings pour from their cracks like scraps of night given muscle. They are dangerous to me in that weather. Their hooked feet pry, their teeth worry at seams, and some have learned that seam-keepers are soft between the plates.

So when the first granular cloud enters the cup, I press myself into the narrowest portion of my fissure and close my side plates over my legs. I become a line in the rock.

The clickers wake all at once.

Their tapping enters my silk strands. The whole basin becomes a diagram of impacts. I feel conversations that are not meant for me: warnings, courtships, claims, errors, the panic-stutter of one seized by a slab predator, the brief rain of feet when a swarm shifts ledges. I cannot understand them as they understand one another, but I know density, direction, urgency. I know when the hollow’s noise has a hole in it.

The most important thing I know is silence.

Not the absence of sound. There is almost never absence here. Silence is a shape that appears inside sound when many creatures stop including a place in their expectations.

Around the tarn there is always such a shape.

The clickers do not tap toward it clearly. Their signals bend there, or seem to. The winged things skim and flee. The amphibians knock deeper at its edges, as if speaking down a throat. Blue worms do not glow long in stones taken from its shore. Even the scavenger, when it comes low with its wet dark fur and milk-reflecting eyes, steps wide of the cleanest black water.

I have been to the tarn’s rim three times.

The first was when I was newly molted and too soft for wisdom. Fine rain had covered every stone with shine. I followed a procession of pale grazers down a damp groove, feeding on the films they exposed. They stopped before the last slope, turned as one body, and retreated over me. I continued.

The air changed before the water. The sound of my feet lowered. Each step returned through my limbs not as a tap but as a thickness. I reached a stone pale enough to show beneath the dark, and there I saw the surface.

It did not reflect me.

Or it reflected something that had not yet arranged itself into my body. A long pale shifting. A root without branching. A larva without hunger. I tasted cold metal and old sleep. My rear feelers curled so tightly they bruised their own joints.

I backed away and did not turn until the clicking of the walls became sharp again.

The second time, I came because the western crack had exhaled too warmly for too long, and all my silk had slackened at once. This was before a rockfall. I did not know that yet. I knew only that the whole basin had become slightly untrue. The amphibians were already facing upslope, their blunt heads lifted from their seep-pools like stones remembering they had mouths.

I descended toward the tarn because the tremor in the stone seemed to go there first.

At the edge, I found a winged roostling fallen into mineral dust. Its membrane was torn. It was alive enough to open its mouth but not alive enough to cry. Its belly touched the water.

No ripple went outward.

The surface held the contact as skin holds a thorn. Beneath it, pale jointless shapes gathered. Not fast. Not eagerly. They simply became more present below the fallen animal.

I ate one of the roostling’s feet before the rockfall came.

That is what I remember most clearly: not fear, not reverence, but salt and membrane between my mouthparts, and then the mountain breaking.

Stone fell from the northern wall. Dust filled the cup. Clickers dropped in dozens, each impact a small ending. The amphibians stayed turned toward the fall. My body flattened so hard against the shore rock that one of my plates cracked. When the dust cleared, the leaning slab above my home had shifted but not fallen. My silk field was torn everywhere. New stone had opened. New food would grow.

The hollow had lost many lives and gained many surfaces.

That is how the mountain thinks, if it thinks.

The third time I went to the tarn, I carried eggs.

Not in a nest. We do not make nests. The eggs were glued beneath my abdomen in a flattened crescent, twelve small grey beads hardening in the cold. I had mated during a dry interval with another seam-keeper on the underside of a basalt shelf. We had touched feelers, exchanged vibration, and parted with no further claim. He may have been eaten before my eggs were laid. This is not tragedy here. It is only the basin continuing.

I do not know why I went down with the eggs. Perhaps the warmth from the western crack had failed for a cycle. Perhaps the air pressure had become strange. Perhaps something in the tarn called not as sound but as a loosening of avoidance.

I stopped farther from the edge than before.

Fine dust lay on the water, yet the center was clean. The rain was so slight it seemed imagined. Drops struck the surface and made rings that widened, paused, and ended before shore.

My eggs moved.

They were not ready to hatch. Still, each bead tightened against me. The young inside had no eyes, no legs, not yet even mouths, but they felt something through my body and through the stone beneath me.

For a long while I remained there, legs spread, abdomen low, while the hollow clicked and knocked and breathed around me. I understood nothing. But my body made a decision older than understanding.

I would not lay them near the tarn.

I climbed back to the leaning slab and fixed them in a side crack where warm exhalation touched but did not wet them, where films would grow, where the silk strands could tell me if the stone moved too quickly. Of the twelve, perhaps five would hatch. Of the five, perhaps two would harden. Of the two, perhaps one would learn the seam before becoming food.

This is abundance.

If I have a role in the hollow, it is not to rule or reveal. I am a small maintenance of caution. I mend sensing-lines. I harvest films that would otherwise thicken. I carry news of fracture without wanting to tell it. When my silk trembles, I move; when I move, clickers nearby pause; when they pause, a grazer procession alters; when they alter, a flattened predator misses its upward fold; when it misses, it waits longer; when it waits longer, a blue worm survives beneath the slab and later eats a dead amphibian egg. Nothing is separate enough to be innocent.

I am not the mountain’s thought.

I am one of the mountain’s nerves, if a nerve can be hungry, blind, cold, and brief.

I know the hollow as pressure and taste. The raw basalt is bitter. The shale is dry and layered like old shells. The rust-yellow stain burns my feet. The violet mineral near the warm crack feeds bronze films after rain. Goat-hoof grit smells of open height and danger. The scavenger leaves a trail of wet fur, old blood, and patience. The corvid’s dropped stone chips are confusing: recently used, then immediately ordinary.

The sky is mostly irrelevant. It is a jagged absence overhead, sometimes less dark. I do not understand horizon. I understand overhang.

I understand that water is not a substance but an event.

A bead forming on the underside of my slab can wake three species. A seep thread widening can begin a breeding. A pool lowering can expose eggs to blue worms. Mist can unfold wings from stone. Dryness can turn lichen to armor. The tarn can remain itself through all of this, neither generous nor empty, black and small and central as an unblinking organ.

When dawn comes, if the greying above deserves that name, I retreat into my seam. My plates hold the night’s cold. Moisture withdraws from the exposed surfaces and remains in the secret ones. The clickers slow. The winged things fold away, muttering in their inherited cracks. The amphibians lower themselves into silt. The pale grazers vanish under flakes, leaving cropped dark lanes that will outlast them.

I check my silk.

One strand broken by a grazer. One slack from condensation. One vibrating faintly in a rhythm that matches the western exhalation. One newly tense, leading toward the leaning slab.

I place my feet against the stone and listen.

The slab will fall someday. It may fall in a season, or after many molts, or after my eggs have become their own small hungers. When it falls, it will crush my passage and open another. It will kill what shelters beneath it and feed what arrives after. The hollow will answer with clicks, spores, films, worms, larvae, and rules.

Until then, I live in its unfinished grammar.

I am a seam-keeper under the western shale lip: a flattened, many-limbed, silk-laying listener of fractures, dark as wet stone, small enough to vanish in a crack, old in instinct and new in body.

I belong where the mountain has not yet settled on its final shape.

I am one of the creatures by which it learns the consequences of breaking.
