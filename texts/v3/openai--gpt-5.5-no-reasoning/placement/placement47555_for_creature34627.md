I would find myself in the central mound-city, not at its crown and not in its flooded foundations, but in the complicated middle: the damp, ribbed level where root, peat, old wood, and young limestone hold together by mutual insistence.

There is a passage there that is not quite a tunnel. It began as the underside of a fallen trunk, then became a vole road, then a runnel, then was roofed over by moss, fern root, and mineral drip. One side of it is living alder root, polished by fur. The other side is pale carbonate crust in the shape of stems long gone. Above it, water falls slowly through a curtain of root hairs. Below it, through gaps in the floor, one can hear a deeper channel moving in the dark.

That is where I would be.

Not one of the large, visible citizens. Not deer, not heron, not fox, not otter. Those are too declarative. They pass through the basin with bodies that make arguments: hunger, elegance, speed, visitation. I would be something smaller and more absorbed into the place’s grammar.

At first I think: a water vole.

A soft, dark, quick animal with wet whiskers and a memory made of passages. I would know the basin as a sequence of textures against my belly and paws: slick root, cold stone, peat that bears, peat that lies, reed-stem, moss-lip, hollow bark, the thin tremble of water under a floor. I would not possess a map, exactly. I would possess corrections. This way floods after long rain. This hole smells of fox. This chamber stays warm. This root bridge held yesterday but sighed under weight. This sedge stand seeds early. This pool has the stillness of a waiting mouth.

I would live in a nest chamber just above the ordinary seep-line, though “ordinary” is always provisional. The entrance would be hidden under a flap of moss and dead grass, reached through a slanting run that twists around a stone too large to move. Inside: shredded sedge, seed down, a few white feathers taken from beneath a ledge, the inner bark of a softened twig. My body heat would make a small weather there. Outside, everything would be cold and wet and listening.

But as I stay with the image, I am not only the vole.

There is too much of the place in the walls. The passage itself feels more like the answer. A lived-in hollow. A minor artery of the undercity. Something used by many lives and made by none of them alone.

So perhaps I would be a threshold-creature: a small brown shrew or vole-like thing that belongs especially to intersections. Fur always damp at the tips. Eyes black and near-sighted. Ears modest, rounded, half-hidden. Feet able to climb root and swim when forced, though preferring neither spectacle nor open water. Teeth always needing something. Heart impossibly fast in the cold.

I would be one of the basin’s punctuation marks.

A brief comma of motion between moss and root.  
A rustle that stops when the owl turns its head.  
A warm bead moving through wet architecture.  
A citizen only by being eaten from, sheltered by, frightened through, and remembered in scent.

My days would not be divided cleanly into day and night. Light in the basin is already half-translated. I would know time by drip rate, by the chill rising from the lower vaults, by the arrival of birds overhead, by the sudden tremor of deer-hooves on a root causeway, by bats beginning to comb the air above the reed quarter. Rain would not be weather but civic upheaval. It would rewrite the routes.

When rain thickened above, I would feel it before hearing it. The walls would darken. The nest fibers would loosen. The air in the passage would grow colder and more full. Then the first new threads of water would appear, clear lines crossing old paths. A familiar tunnel would become a shining throat. I would climb.

Not far; just higher. Into the root lattice, into a chamber between two grafted trunks where beetle dust smells dry enough to trust. Other small bodies might already be there: another vole, a shrew with a needle nose, a spider folded under a bark shelf, pale grubs in the wood, a slug drawn tight against itself. None of us would call it refuge. We would simply be where the water is not, yet.

From there I would watch the lower passage fill. Not with awe. Awe is too spacious. I would watch with the tense practicality of a body that can drown.

A seed would float past. A beetle leg. A fern fragment. A pellet of old dung coming apart. The current would test the moss lip, tug loose a brown thread, widen a crack. Somewhere below, fish would enter an alley that yesterday belonged to feet.

When the water fell again, the city would be different. Not dramatically, perhaps. But enough. One scent washed away. One entrance blocked with silt. One wall softened. One new opening under a root. One dead thing made available. One danger made useful.

I would investigate.

That would be my profession: investigation under pressure of hunger.

I would nose along mineral crusts, bite at sedge bases, crack small seeds, take insect larvae when I found them soft and reachable. I would enter dead wood through beetle galleries widened by generations of teeth. I would pause often, not from contemplation but because stillness is a door one can hide behind. A drop would strike a leaf nearby and fill the whole world for an instant. I would wait until the sound had finished moving through my bones.

The basin would not seem beautiful to me, though beauty would happen everywhere around my unpraising life.

The mist-beaded spiderweb would not be a trembling map; it would be a place to avoid unless hunger overcame caution.  
The calcite teeth under the ledge would not be young stone; they would be wet edges, bad footing, places where scent behaves strangely.  
The heron would not be architectural; it would be vertical death.  
The owl would not be a pale disk in the gloom; it would be silence becoming claws.  
The otter would be water with teeth and intention.  
The fox would be a smell arriving before a face.

Yet my ignorance would be intimate. I would know the place by contact more exactly than any watcher from a high shelf. I would know the underside of the city: where warmth lingers, where rot is edible, where roots make ladders, where moss hides holes, where the water is shallow but swift, where it looks shallow and is not.

If I must name myself more precisely, I am a small semi-aquatic vole of the central mound, dark-backed, pale-bellied, cold-adapted, with a blunt muzzle and long whiskers silvered by condensation. But that name is only the animal part.

I am also the route my kind keeps open.

My body, and bodies like mine, polish the burrow lip. Our passing presses the moss into an arch. Our nesting carries feathers into root chambers. Our droppings feed fungus; our uneaten stores sprout when light shifts; our dead feed beetles, foxes, owls, and the soil. We are not builders as the beaverlike animals are builders, but the city is full of small continuities made by our repetition.

A path is only a habit that has lasted.

I would live by such habits and be killed, eventually, by one of them.

Perhaps an owl would hear me under the fern balcony during a dry frost morning, when my paws are too loud on the hardened mud. Perhaps a fox would wait beside the upper exit after learning my circuit. Perhaps floodwater would rise through both lower runs and I would choose the wrong climb. Perhaps winter hunger would thin me until a weasel, shaped like a decision already made, entered the nest chamber while I slept.

That would not be tragedy in this place. It would be transfer.

My fur would line another nest. My bones would settle in gray mud. Beetles would open me. Fungal threads would take what they take. Water would move the rest. The passage would remain, altered by my absence only briefly, until another small dark body found it and pressed itself through.

So: I would find myself in the middle levels of the central mound-city, in a damp root-and-limestone passage above the flooded vaults.

I would be a small undercity vole, or something very near one: whiskered, wet-furred, soft-footed, quick-hearted, living by texture, tremor, scent, and remembered water.

Not an owner of the basin.  
Not an observer of it.  
One of its warm, brief thoughts moving through the cold.
