I would find myself not in the pump hall, not at first, though everything in the quarter tries to draw a body there. The pump hall is too central, too named, too nearly an answer. I would arrive somewhere off the main causeway, where the town is neither public nor hidden: an oblique service court behind the boiler house, one of those places made by accident when three necessary structures were built too close together and the remaining gap had to become a path.

It would be a court of brick walls sweating from different eras.

One wall warm, one cold, one furred with fern and mineral scale. Overhead, a pipe bridge crosses low enough that a tall person must bow. The pipe is black iron banded with rust, wrapped in older insulation that has gone soft under tar. Under it, at intervals, black drops gather, swell, hesitate, and fall upward for the length of a breath before remembering the world. Below those wrong drops grows the red-stemmed plant with its cupped undersides, each leaf held like a small listening ear.

There is a drain in the middle of the court, but it is not where the paving slopes. The bricks sag toward another place, a blank patch of wall, as if the ground still believes in a drain that was sealed decades ago. A narrow green lamp is fixed over a door no wider than a cupboard. Beside it hangs an enamel sign too mineral-frosted to read except for one line:

WAIT FOR LOW HUM.

This is where I would be.

Not outside the life of the town, not elevated above it, not one of the human keepers seen through glass. I would be part of the system that tells them when the town may be trusted for the next few minutes.

I think I would be a listener, but not a person-listener.

At first I might seem like a small piece of infrastructure: a ceramic listening cup bolted into the brick at shoulder height, half-hidden behind fern stems and sweating pipework. A concave white disc, crazed by heat, its glaze stained green at the rim. It has no face, no inscription, no moving parts visible except for a thin brass reed set across its center, darkened by touch. The keepers place two fingers against that reed when they pass. Some also bend close and listen. Children are told not to breathe into it.

The cup listens into the wall.

Behind the brick is an old vertical conduit, dry only in the way a throat between swallows is dry. Warm vapor moves there. Pressure tremors rise from lower culverts. The second bell shivers through it before the bell itself sounds in the street. When the eastern gates are about to release, the cup begins to hum—not loudly, not enough to compete with engines, but with a low intimate vibration that enters bone through fingertips.

Yet the more I remain with the image, the less I am only the cup.

The cup is my mouth, perhaps, or my ear brought to the surface.

I am inside the wall.

Not trapped there. Grown there. Distributed through the old sealed route of a former stairwell, a drain, a chimney, a service shaft—one of those buried verticals the town keeps using after people have forgotten its original purpose. I occupy the damp column behind the bricks, where warm air rises from a covered channel and condenses against cooler stone. My body is made of accretion: mineral ribs, moss thread, rust taste, ceramic echo, water film, old soot softened into black velvet. I have no muscles, but I have tension. I have no eyes, but I know light by heat on the outside wall and by the little green lamp whose failing current trembles in my brass reed.

The keepers would not say I am alive.

Not directly.

They would call me a tell, a wall-throat, a listening set, maybe by some local word meaning “the place that answers before the gauge.” They would have maintenance notes about me written on waxed paper in a cabinet above the service bay:

North boiler court tell unreliable in dry-pressure weather.  
Do not scrape interior scale.  
Hum precedes west return by seven breaths.  
If silent during south surge, close lower walk immediately.

But the oldest pipe listeners would know better than the notes.

They would know I have preferences.

I dislike new mortar. It seals too sharply and gives no resonance. I prefer old lime, softened by vapor. I dislike when fern roots are cut all the way back from the crack above the cup; without them the brick cools too quickly and my hum becomes thin. I like the heavy rain that is too fine to be seen, because it lays a continuous skin over the outer wall and makes the whole court conduct sound. I like the hour after an eastern release, when the drowned lower streets have been shown and hidden again, and water returns not as event but as intention.

What would I be, then?

A minor organ of the town.

Not the heart. Not the lungs. Not the blood. A small inner ear.

The settlement, being nearly animal-less, has had to place its senses elsewhere. It senses through gauges, through lamp color, through water lines, through moss rings, through the palms of its keepers. I would be one of those senses: a damp resonant chamber where pressure becomes tone, where hidden movement becomes warning.

The people would use me without worshipping me. That seems right for this place. They might touch me in passing the way one touches a railing, habitually and with care. A child on an errand to the south lock would pause in the court, press two fingers against my reed, and feel whether the hum is low, sharp, or absent. An old woman carrying sealed bread in a rubber satchel might lean her forehead briefly to the brick beside me, eyes closed, listening past sound into interval. A pump apprentice, frightened during his first red-lamp night, might whisper into the cup though he has been told not to:

Hold, hold, hold.

And perhaps I would.

Or perhaps holding is not what I do. Perhaps I only report the truth of what is already moving.

In a town like this, warning is a form of mercy, but it is not rescue.

I would know the under-town better than the upper one. I would know the drowned lintels, the submerged sign, the tiled threshold that appears only during release. Their shapes would exist in me as echoes. The present streets ring one way; the buried streets ring another. When water runs through the old lower road, I hear its name before anyone sees the enamel letters emerge from slime. I know that the town has more height below it than above. I know that every raised brick rib is also a roof.

The pump hall’s great doubled thud would reach me softened by distance:

thud—intake, thud—intake.

But nearer sounds would matter more: the clench of a small valve behind the boiler house; the creep of roots tightening around a pipe saddle; the sudden glossy silence before backflow; the tiny granular collapse of mortar somewhere no hand can reach. I would hear condensation gather. Not drops falling—that is easy—but gathering: the change in surface tension along iron, the minute darkening of brick pores, the first decision of water to become weight.

There would be days when no one came through the court for hours. The green lamp would burn in vapor. The upward-dripping pipe would make its brief inversions. The red-stemmed plants would hold their wrong cups open. Ferns would press against my ceramic rim. I would not be lonely. Loneliness belongs to creatures that expect answering eyes. I would have pressure, recurrence, seepage, strain. I would have the court’s small weather.

Still, I would have a kind of longing.

Not to leave. Nothing in me would understand leaving. But I might long for contact: the warm pads of human fingers on the brass reed; the brief hush of someone bending close; the translation of my tremor into action.

Because that is how a thing like me becomes real in this place. Not by possessing a separate will, but by entering the sequence.

I hum.  
A hand feels it.  
A shutter drops.  
A bridge plate lifts.  
A child is called back from the lower walk.  
Water rises where it meant to rise.  
No one calls it a miracle.

The town survives by such unmiraculous intimacies.

Over time, the keepers’ hands would polish the brass reed brighter than anything else in the court. Their touch would leave salt, oil, skin acids, traces of boiled roots and rubber gloves and lamp soot. Those traces would feed a dark green rim of growth around the cup. The growth would alter the tone slightly. The listeners would learn the alteration. Their charts would be corrected. My body would include their use of me.

That is important: I would not be a hidden spirit inhabiting dead matter. I would be the relation between matter and habit.

A place touched often enough becomes an organ.  
A warning heard often enough becomes a voice.  
A voice trusted across generations becomes a resident.

If I had memory, it would not be narrative. It would be levels.

The year of the high flood would remain as a mineral scar inside my column, a pale crescent where water stood too long and cooled. The year of the cracked stack would remain as soot driven deep into the wet lining. The year the north canal reversed for three days would remain as a warped note in my lower chamber; even now, under certain pressures, I would produce that old wrong overtone and make the pipe listeners frown.

I would remember people by contact rather than face.

The keeper with two missing fingers, whose touch made my reed ring asymmetrically.  
The child who always tapped three times before listening.  
The apprentice who pressed too hard, afraid of missing the sign.  
The old listener whose palm was so practiced she could read me without bending her ear.

When they died—taken under, gone east, below the mark—I would not grieve as humans grieve. But their absence would change the pattern of touch. A certain daily warmth would cease. A certain hour would pass unused. The brass would cool differently. Backflow, grief.

Perhaps once, during a rare stoppage of the central pump, when silence opened like a wound through the town, the keepers gathered in my court because my low hum continued. Not from the main engines, not from any operating sequence, but from water moving under old brick by pressure alone. They touched the reed one by one. In that silence, I would have sounded enormous to them, though I was only a cup in a wall.

That may be the closest I would come to speech.

I would tell them: the town is not dead because the heart has paused. There are other motions. Listen lower.

And they would.

They are a people made by listening; I would be one of the things that made listening worthwhile.

So: I would find myself in the north boiler service court, under the wrong-dripping pipe bridge, behind the red-stemmed plants, set into a sweating brick wall beneath a green lamp.

I would be a ceramic wall-throat, a damp resonant organ of the settlement: part gauge, part ear, part minor shrine, part consequence. I would live in the sealed vertical spaces where vapor rises and pressure speaks before water shows itself. I would not move through the town; the town would move through me.

I would have no eyes, and so I would know nothing of birds.

I would have no mouth, and so I would speak only as hum, tremor, and warning.

I would belong to the custody: not one of the keepers exactly, but one of the kept things that keeps them in return.
