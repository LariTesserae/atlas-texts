What lives there is not a single creature so much as a society of thirst.

Everything on the plateau is organized around brief water, heavy heat, and the living floor beneath it all. The inhabitants are creatures of timing, surfaces, and negotiation. They do not simply occupy the place; they enter into bargains with it, or fail to.

The smallest lives are the most numerous and perhaps the most powerful. Insects own the air, the bark, the undersides of leaves, the cups of flowers, the cracks in the living mat. They are varnished black, red, amber, glass-clear, dust-gray; some travel in disciplined rivers along root ridges, others rise in panicked sheets from disturbed foliage. There are beetles with jaws made for husks and woody pods, soft grubs floating in leaf-water, winged termites leaving clay towers at dusk, biting flies summoned by smoke, sap, blood, rot, and rain. Many plants seem designed around them, and many insects around particular plants, so that a flower may be less a blossom than an appointment.

The pools hold another citizenship: lunged fish, armored skaters, tadpole-clouds, patient ambushers with only nostrils showing, small shell-backed things that scrape algae from submerged roots. Every pool is temporary and overcrowded, and its animals live accordingly. Some must grow quickly before the water retreats. Some bury themselves in black mud and wait through drought. Some travel at night in slick, desperate migrations between shrinking basins. Some are carried as eggs in the feet, fur, guts, or feathers of larger animals.

The great grazers and browsers give the open spaces their scale. They move in long, broken herds across the shining mats, tall-shouldered and narrow-headed, with flexible mouths suited to stripping waxy leaves from hostile shrubs. Their hides are patterned in sun and shadow, not for hiding in forest but for dissolving in glare, stems, heat shimmer, and moving shade. They remember dew fields, fruiting groves, safe lanes, poisonous courts, and the order in which seasonal flowers bring insects, then small predators, then larger ones. Their migrations are not simple travel but part of the plateau’s circulation. They prune, trample, fertilize, spread seeds, open paths, and sometimes become gardens when they die.

Other large animals are stranger. Some are reedlike walkers, tall and narrow, their many limbs jointed like bundled cane, their flat tooth-plates rasping fungi and lichen from trunks. They may stand almost motionless among real reed-towers until only the slow folding of a limb reveals them. Others are low, heavy grazers carrying mats of moss, fern, and small flowering plants on their backs. Birds feed there. Insects breed there. A sleeping animal may resemble a hummock until it rises and the whole little garden sways away.

The hunters are built for ambush, heat, and sudden violence. Bright arboreal predators move through parasol crowns on gripping tails and long hands, four-eyed, narrow-bodied, throats inflating before a scream that rearranges the soundscape. Pool-hunters wait with mud-colored patience. Ground predators follow herd trails, using thorn lanes and root corridors as cover. Gliders croak overhead on thermal currents, membrane-winged, dropping between crowns or scanning the open mats for weakness. Some scavengers are nearly as important as predators: quick-mouthed, clever, able to open carcasses before the flies and fungi take full possession.

There are also creatures that live with the plants so intimately that animal and vegetation become difficult to separate. Foam-exuding grazers cling beneath leaves and feed slowly, their secretions protecting them from both desiccation and parasites. Back-garden beasts host moss that cools them and hides them. Insect colonies cultivate fungi inside old stems. Certain birds or birdlike animals nest only in skull-trees, using the resonant hollows grown around dead megafauna. Root-cave dwellers emerge only at night, pale-eyed and damp-skinned, licking minerals from fungal sheets and retreating when the heat climbs.

The plants themselves are among the dominant inhabitants. They are not background. They behave slowly, but not passively. Some feel footfall and close around warmth. Some time fruit to migrations. Some poison their own perimeters into open courts. Some invite trampling because roads help them spread. Some drink mist through leaves, others through bark, others through the living ground-mat that binds the plateau together like skin. Tower-trees, reed-walls, vine bridges, root-caves, thorned succulents, floating seed pods, storm-tilting blade-fields, bioluminescent night pools: these are not just habitat features but active participants in the lives around them.

And among all this live people, though they are not masters of the place.

They are plateau people: careful, observant, mobile when necessary, patient when they can afford patience. They build lightly because anything heavy and neglected is swallowed. Their huts stand on dry ridges or short stilts, woven from rib-leaves, cane, bark scales, and trained growth. Some families inhabit shelters that began as bent saplings generations ago and have slowly thickened into living walls. Others move between seasonal platforms, root-buttress camps, and temporary shade structures according to pools, herds, insects, and fruiting times.

Their knowledge is botanical before it is architectural or military. They know which folded leaves hold drinkable dawn water, which vines poison arrows or fish, which seeds must be boiled three times, which flowers call biting swarms, which fungal crusts mean hollow ground beneath. They read blade-leaves before storms, insect-drone before rain, resin smell before flowering, silence-before-scream as a warning of nearby hunters. Their paths are remade continually, marked with pale fiber knots, carved seed-shells, beetle-wing flashes, and scars in living bark.

Their tools are modest but exact: stone blades, bone awls, fire-hardened digging sticks, woven traps, resin glue, bark cords, hooks for dew leaves, baskets sealed with waxy sap. Metal exists as treasure—copper, meteoric iron, rare blades stained green by the air—but the plateau rewards replaceable things more than permanent ones. A tool dropped and lost is no longer a tool by morning; it is already becoming insect-salt, root-anchor, fungus shade, or shrine accident.

Their settlements are scattered, small, and never entirely secure. A village may be a cluster of raised huts around baked-clay hearths, a few living storage pods, racks for drying leaves and meat, and guarded paths kept open by daily cutting. Another may be a seasonal dew camp used only during the weeks when morning condensation gathers thick on the broadest leaves. Some groups follow herds. Some remain near reliable black pools and defend them fiercely. Some tend vine bridges, shaped trees, and ceremonial groves inherited from ancestors whose own dwellings have vanished into root-felt.

Their rituals likely concern attention. In such a place, inattention is death: stepping on thin mat over cavern, cutting the wrong vine, sleeping beneath a heat-closing plant, crossing an open court during a predator’s hour, drinking from a pool at the wrong edge. Their shrines are living scars, skull-grown trunks, lightning beads, old paths kept open not because they are efficient but because the dead once walked them. They may speak of the plateau as a back, a hide, a breathing thing, not metaphorically but practically. The ground gives, sweats, drinks, heals, burns, hums.

No inhabitant here is independent. The people depend on plants that depend on insects that depend on rain events that depend on heat rising from basins of leaves. Herds make roads, roads grow thorn and fruit, thorn and fruit attract animals, animals bring hunters, hunters leave carcasses, carcasses seed groves. Lightning feeds blooms. Fire calls insects. Rot feeds roots. Roots close wounds. Water arrives as a passing visitor and leaves behind explosions of eggs, spores, calls, and movement.

So the beings that live there are heat-made and water-trained. They are quick drinkers, patient waiters, sudden breeders, seasonal walkers, shade readers, root listeners. They are armored, waxed, foam-covered, moss-backed, membrane-winged, sap-stained, spore-dusted. Even the people are somewhat like the other inhabitants: lightly built, clever with openings, never still for too long unless they are listening.

The plateau’s life is not lush peace. It is appetite arranged into an ecosystem. It is a crowded openness, a hot high world where every surface may be mouth, shelter, trap, bridge, road, memory, or skin.
