They are a people shaped by damp stone and careful passage.

They are not delicate, though at first glance they may seem quiet enough to be mistaken for part of the city’s weather. They move with an ingrained economy: short steps on slick stairs, weight set slightly back on descending slopes, hands free when crossing channels, shoulders angled against mist-heavy wind. Even children learn early not to run blindly over gleaming flagstones, not to stamp on a hollow-sounding slab, not to lean against a retaining wall after long rain. Their bodies carry the city’s lessons before their mouths can explain them.

Most are compact rather than tall, though not uniformly so. Broad hands are common: hands used to wet rope, stone edges, baskets, reed knives, sluice bars, animal hides, clay, peat bricks, net weights, slate wedges. Their skin is weathered in a particular upland way, not burned dark by harsh sun but roughened by cold moisture, wind, and mineral water. Faces tend toward stillness in repose. They squint often, not from brightness alone but from mist, reflected sky, fine rain, and the need to read distance through haze.

Their hair is usually worn for practicality: braided, bound, wrapped, cut at the jaw or shoulder, tucked beneath hoods and felted caps. Loose hair is a nuisance where everything gathers droplets. Wool, linen, hide, and reed-fiber cloth dominate their clothing. Nothing stays truly dry for long, so their garments are layered to survive damp rather than repel it absolutely: close inner wraps, heavy outer tunics, oiled cloaks, felted shoulder pieces, leggings bound tight below the knee, soft-soled shoes with gripping seams, or wooden pattens for flooded courtyards. Colors are muted because bright dyes are difficult and because the place itself teaches restraint: peat brown, lichen gray, dull blue, sedge green, clay red, cream, smoke black. Ornament exists, but it is small and enduring—bone toggles, polished river-stone beads, copper pins greened by age, carved slate pendants, thread patterns worked along cuffs to show household, watershed, or trade.

They do not think of themselves as living in opposition to water. To them water is neighbor, burden, messenger, ancestor, danger, wealth. Their speech has many terms for wetness: rain just beginning, rain that will pass, rain that has entered walls, rain carried down from mountains under clear skies, marsh-water over stone, seep-water from old cracks, roof-water fit for washing, spring-water fit for drinking, flood-water that has forgotten its channels. A child may misname a bird and be forgiven; misnaming a rise in the channels is corrected at once.

They are builders, but not in the proud, conquering sense. Their craft is accommodation. Their masons are among the most respected people in the city, but they are expected to be humble before the mountain. A good mason is one who sees where stone wants to fail, where a wall should breathe, where water must be invited through rather than held back. They read lichen, mineral stains, moss thickness, frost cracks, the angle of settled paving. The oldest among them can touch a wall and tell whether its foundation is dry stone, taught mud, or something more ancient hidden underneath.

There are also water-keepers, though they are not priests in any theatrical way. They maintain the channels, sluices, settling basins, cistern mouths, grates, reed filters, and flood paths. They know which districts drown first, which courtyards hold back too much meltwater, which bridge arches clog with sedge after west winds. They rise during night rain and go out with covered lamps, hooks, staffs, and wedges. Their work is practical, but it carries ritual weight because everyone understands what happens when it is neglected. A blocked channel can ruin a storehouse, undermine a stair, sicken a household, or bring down half a terrace over the course of a season.

Food is gathered from small certainties. They grow what the short, wet upland allows: hardy grains on raised beds and better-drained shelves, roots in walled plots, bitter greens, herbs, peas, perhaps small plums or rowan fruit when the year is generous. They keep animals suited to narrow margins: woolly sheep, small cattle if pasture allows, goats watched carefully because they will climb onto roofs and ruin sedge beds, ducks in lower wet courts, dogs bred more for warning and companionship than speed. Fish traps are set in channels and marsh pools. Reeds are cut for mats, thatch, baskets, screens, bedding, and filtration. Peat is harvested with caution; cut too much and a field becomes a wound.

They preserve obsessively. Damp is always waiting to spoil what labor has won. Smokehouses are prized. Granaries stand raised, vented, and watched. Cheese is wrapped, turned, and salted with care. Fish are dried in cold wind where possible, smoked where not. Seed is stored in nested vessels off the floor. Every household has hooks in beams, shelves high on walls, sealed jars in clay-lined recesses, and a practiced distrust of anything left touching stone too long.

Their houses face inward. This is not because they are unfriendly, but because warmth, dryness, and privacy must be gathered and held. Outer walls are thick and plain, with few low openings. Within, courtyards catch light and drain to deliberate points. Hearths are slightly raised. Bedding platforms stand above the floor. Floors slope so subtly that strangers may not notice until spilled water finds its way to a channel. Roofs are living things, tended like gardens: slate, sod, sedge, moss where welcome, drains kept open, stones replaced after storms. Children are sent up under supervision to clear debris from roof lips and learn the household from above.

Their society is organized less by streets than by drainage. People belong to terraces, courts, cistern districts, spring-lines, and channel responsibilities. Kinship matters, but so does shared maintenance. Those whose wastewater joins the same runnel must know each other. Those below depend on those above. A careless upper household can sour a lower court; a neglected grate can flood three families downhill. For this reason, courtesy here has engineering in it. Arguments are serious when they concern drainage, walls, roof rights, or access to high dry storage. Reconciliation often involves labor: clearing a channel together, rebuilding a step, re-lining a basin, hauling stone.

Their festivals are quiet by lowland standards, but not joyless. The first safe thaw is marked by opening certain channels and cleaning winter silt. Children float carved bark boats or little reed bundles through public races to see whose survives the turns. There are days when the cistern courts are scrubbed and decorated with lichen-colored cloth, bone charms, and rowan twigs. At midsummer, when cloud breaks most generously, people climb to upper terraces to mend pasture walls, share sour beer, sing into the wind, and watch the basin shine. In autumn, roof work becomes communal and urgent; songs then have rhythms suited to lifting slate, passing sod, tamping clay. In deep winter, life contracts inward around hearths, stored food, repair work, stories, and the patient making of nets, shoes, hinges, baskets, and tools.

They sing, but their songs do not rise extravagantly. Many are low and interwoven, meant for stone rooms, work parties, fog, and walking in file along causeways. Some imitate water without meaning to: repeated phrases, small variations, returns after long intervals. Their instruments are portable and resistant to damp—frame drums, bone flutes, reed pipes, simple stringed instruments kept carefully near hearth smoke. Bells matter more than music alone. Different tones signal weather, assembly, flood, fire, lost child, returning herds, death, and strangers on the causeway.

They are wary of strangers, not hostile. Anyone crossing the outer marsh must be noticed, if only because an unfamiliar person may not know where to step. Hospitality is practical: dry wrappings, hot broth, a place near smoke, questions asked after warmth returns to the hands. Guests are warned before they are welcomed fully. Do not go down that stair in fog. Do not drink from that court basin. Do not sleep against the north wall. If the lower bell sounds twice, climb.

Their dead remain close, though not always beneath the floors; the ground is too wet and shifting for careless burial. Bones may be cleaned, bundled, sealed in stone niches, placed in dry upper chambers, or built into memorial walls where mineral seams run pale through dark rock. Ancestors are associated with maintenance, not dominion. One remembers them by keeping their wall sound, their channel open, their tree alive. A neglected family cistern is shameful in part because it insults those who first cut it.

They have little interest in speed. A fast person who breaks things is a fool. A clever person who ignores water is dangerous. Their admired virtues are patience, sound judgment, repair, memory, restraint, and the ability to act immediately when the moment finally requires it. For most of the year, they appear slow. During flood, storm, wall-slip, or sudden freeze, they move with startling coordination. Gates are hauled shut, animals shifted, tools passed from hand to hand, children counted, elders moved uphill, lamps shielded, sluices opened, stones wedged, ropes tied. No one needs to explain the whole plan. The plan lives distributed among them.

Their knowledge is local to a degree outsiders may mistake for superstition. They know the third day of warm rain is worse than the first hard storm. They know which moss on a wall means harmless seep and which means hidden pressure. They know a raven’s silence before certain weather, the smell of opened red soil, the sound a roof makes when sod has taken too much water. They distrust clean-looking cracks, sudden dry channels, and marsh pools reflecting an uninterrupted sky. They teach by correction, proverb, and task. A child learns the city by being sent to fetch water, then to watch water, then to explain where it went.

There is law, but much of it resembles maintenance schedules hardened into ethics. To block shared flow is an offense. To foul upper water is grave. To build without counsel from stone-readers and water-keepers is arrogance. To waste high ground is nearly sacrilege. Cutting a living roof tree without communal need brings anger beyond its material loss. But law also allows for mercy, because everyone knows walls fail, channels clog, clay slumps, and judgment is never perfect. Punishment often takes the form of obligation: seasons of channel work, stone hauling, reed cutting, night watch in rain.

They do not imagine the world as stable. They imagine it as temporarily held. This gives them a certain tenderness. A repaired cup may be used for generations. A stunted tree receives more care than a lowland orchard. A dry room is a blessing. A clear morning is not taken for granted. They celebrate survival without boasting of permanence.

If they have myths, those myths are probably not full of shining gods descending from above. More likely they tell of the first people who listened badly and built where the black mud breathed; of the wall that held because a child noticed water singing under it; of the woman who mapped the hidden springs by walking barefoot through frost; of the old mason who refused a lord’s tower because the cliff had already said no. Their sacred figures, if they name any, are threshold people: channel-openers, roof-keepers, bridge-finders, the ancestors who taught stone to mud, the patient dead in the high dry niches.

They fear collapse, rot, hunger, and isolation. They fear a season when rain comes warm and wrong, when old frost no longer holds the upper slopes, when distant melt arrives without warning. They fear neglect more than enemies. An army would struggle in their basin; ignorance can do more damage than malice.

And yet they are not grim. They laugh at slips and soaked hems, at goats on roofs, at children who step into puddles deeper than their pride, at visitors who try to keep their boots clean. They enjoy warmth intensely: the press of many bodies in a hall, broth steam, peat glow, dry socks, sun on a sheltered wall. They value stories told in bad weather and shared labor finished before rain. Their humor is understated, often delivered with a straight face and a glance toward whoever understands the hidden channel beneath the joke.

They are, in the deepest sense, inhabitants rather than owners.

They live in a city that never stops reminding them that life depends on passage: of water through stone, of cold through walls, of memory through hands, of duty from upper terraces to lower courts, of the dead into the living, of children into the habits that keep them alive.

They do not say, “This place is ours.”

They say, perhaps, “We are of this drainage.”

They say, “Our roof still holds.”

They say, “The channel is clear.”

And when the rain begins again—soft, brief, ordinary—they lift their hoods, adjust their footing, and go on.
