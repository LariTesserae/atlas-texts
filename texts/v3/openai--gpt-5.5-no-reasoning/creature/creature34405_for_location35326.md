What lives there are people who have become, over many generations, a kind of weatherproofed intelligence of the plateau.

They are not a loud people, and they are not quick. Quickness belongs to lower places, to animals, to rivers, to markets, to fire that can afford to waste itself. These people have no such luxury. They live at the pace of cisterns filling, moss binding stone, frost lifting a slab a finger’s width in a decade. Their lives are active, but the activity is distributed: across households, across districts, across seasons, across ancestry. A stranger might first mistake them for sparse figures moving through ruins. Only slowly would it become clear that the ruins are maintained, the emptiness is arranged, the silence is inhabited, and the plateau is full of human attention.

They are lean, high-breathed, and compactly strong. Not broad in the way of forest or valley peoples, but roped with endurance. Their chests tend to be deep, their hands enlarged by cold work, their fingers scarred from stone edges, thorn-root needles, rope, frostbite, and years of testing surfaces before trusting them. Their skin is wind-burnished, often dry and finely cracked around the knuckles and lips. Eyes are narrowed not from suspicion but from light: frost glare, mineral glare, the milky distances in which a path may or may not continue to be the same path. Many wear their hair in close knots, cords, or waxed braids, not for ornament alone but to keep it from catching rime and from obscuring peripheral vision, which matters in districts where a wall may lean into elsewhere.

Their clothing is layered and quiet. Plant felt, bark-cloth, woven reed-fiber, moss-padded inner garments, resin-stiffened cloaks that shed needling rain. In the coldest regions they wear mantles of compressed lichen-fiber, gray-green and silver, smelling faintly of stone cellars. Their boots have soft soles for reading the ground, with removable cleats of stone or hard root for ice. Many carry long measuring staffs marked with knots, dents, pigment bands, and small dangling weights. These are walking aids, tools, ritual instruments, and survival devices all at once. A person without a staff outside the inner halls looks unfinished, almost undressed.

They decorate themselves sparingly, but not without beauty. Beads of black clay. Pale mineral bands sewn into cuffs. Knotted cords showing wind-years, family gardens, journeys survived. Small polished chips of old roadstone worn at the throat. Handprints dyed onto cloaks to mark communal labor or funerary obligation. Faces are rarely carved, rarely painted, rarely represented in permanent form. A face may be imitated too easily by the world. Hands are safer. Hands testify without inviting duplication.

Their speech is low, economical, and rich in conditionals.

They have many words for kinds of stillness, for the difference between safe fog and waiting fog, for cracks that are merely cracks and cracks that are beginning to consider becoming distances. Directions are given with probabilities attached. A common instruction might mean, roughly, “north if the second cistern reflects sky; otherwise take the lower wall until the stones resume counting properly.” They do not say a road “goes” somewhere. They say it “usually keeps faith with” a place, or “has been arriving at” a place since the last measurement.

Their languages contain tenses for interrupted days, repeated hours, inherited memory, and events that happened only to part of a household. They distinguish between “I remember,” “my lineage remembers,” “the wall remembers,” and “the plants warned us but no one yet understands what of.” Children learn these distinctions early. A child who can speak clearly but cannot yet read fern tremors is considered only half literate.

They are not superstitious, though nearly everything they do would look like superstition elsewhere. Their rules are empirical, accumulated from losses. Covering bowls when roof-fog descends is not symbolic. Leaving plazas empty for errors to gather is not ceremonial in origin, though it has become ceremonial with age. Refusing to count the same columns twice is practical arithmetic in a place where enumeration can invite repetition or divergence. Ritual, science, architecture, agriculture, and etiquette have braided together because none of them survived alone.

Their settlements are inhabited by maintenance guilds more than by rulers.

There are keepers of cisterns, who know the taste of each basin and can identify distant seepage by mineral bitterness. There are shadow-measurers, who work at noon with marked cords and white stones, noting when towers disagree with themselves. There are crack-listeners, who lie with one ear to old pavement at dawn and hear whether water, frost, or space is moving underneath. There are moss-tenders, who cultivate specific species over walls and graves because some hold stone together, some drink too much, and some invite fog. There are road-combers, map-weavers, roof-binders, spring-watchers, frost interpreters, kiln stewards, distance judges, and those quiet, respected workers whose task is simply to sweep the empty plazas every morning before speech begins.

Authority belongs to those who have kept things stable.

A leader is not the one who commands most people, but the one whose cistern has not failed, whose district has not lost children to folded streets, whose measurements have remained honest through bad seasons. Councils meet in low halls with multiple exits and no central fire, because centers are not always trustworthy. Decisions are made slowly, often after consulting maps, plant behavior, old knots, shadow records, and the testimony of elders who survived previous distortions. Arguments are not absent, but they are quiet. Shouting is not merely rude; it risks waking echoes that return with additions.

Their houses are intimate with cold. Low doorways. Thick walls. Roofs grown as much as built. Floors of old stone warmed by buried flues when fuel can be spared. Sleeping alcoves clustered around heat stones. Condensation nets hung overnight and wrung at dawn into household jars. Shelves of spore cakes, dried bitter greens, pressed moss-grain, root loaves, mineral salts. Every household has covered bowls, weighted strings, chalk or pale clay for marking altered thresholds, and a return door if the family is prosperous enough.

Inside, the people keep careful warmth. Not extravagance. Warmth as a shared animal they must shelter, though no animals live among them. Communal sleeping halls are common in winter and during uncertain geometry. Families and neighbors sleep close, breathing into layered curtains, while one or two watchers remain awake to tend stones, listen for cracks, and ensure that no one’s shadow has become restless in the night.

Their children are solemn in some ways and wild in others.

They do not chase birds or insects; there are none. They play at mapping, at safe fog and bad fog, at building tiny walls of pebbles around puddles to see whether reflections obey. They learn to walk by stepping only where adults have tapped. They learn to read frost feathers on the inside of jars, the leaning of grasses, the faint tremor of blue pulse-fern. Their first formal lesson is often not a word or number, but a route: from sleeping hall to cistern court and back by three alternatives.

Yet they laugh, especially indoors. They make dolls from reed bundles, though never with detailed faces. They build miniature villages and deliberately leave empty plazas. They sing counting songs that skip certain numbers in certain weather. They dare one another to touch frozen spoken words in the northern settlements, breaking old sentences into glittering threads.

Their elders are treasured, not because age alone grants wisdom, but because memory is a civic instrument. An elder may know that a district becomes unreliable after seven dry dawns, or that a certain wall should not be repaired too perfectly, or that a spring tastes of iron three days before it shifts. Many elders carry woven maps no one else can fully read. Their bodies bend like plateau trees, shaped by wind, attention, and long refusal to fall.

They have little concept of wilderness as other peoples might understand it. There are no beasts beyond the lamps. No predators calling from ridges. No vermin in grain. No pollinating hum, no nesting season, no migration. Their fear of the outside is not fear of teeth but fear of misarrival, exposure, thirst, frost, and the world becoming grammatically incorrect around them. A safe journey is not one in which nothing attacks; a safe journey is one in which every place remains willing to be itself until one has passed.

Because there are no animals, their tenderness turns elsewhere.

They tend plants almost as kin, though not sentimentally. A roof moss may be older than the family beneath it. A cistern root-veil may have fed a neighborhood through three failed rains. A line of dwarf trees may mark the only stable path through a southern drainage grove. Plants are watched, divided, transplanted, praised in work songs, blamed when they overreach, mourned when frost takes them. Children may be assigned a cushion mat to care for, learning patience from something that expands less than a handspan in a year.

Their agriculture is horticulture, engineering, meteorology, and negotiation. Fields are not broad golden expanses but shelves, pits, walls, bowls, and seams. People comb moss-grain with bone-like rakes, press spores into damp velvet beds, thaw frost-wrapped tubers with heated stones, prune reedlike reservoir plants, and shade condensation gardens with lattices adjusted daily. Harvest festivals are quiet affairs of relief. Food is sour, bitter, mineral, smoky when smoke can be afforded, often fermented for safety and depth. Their cuisine prizes texture: springy moss cakes, crisp frost greens, dense root paste, clear cold broth flavored with lichen ash and salt.

Their dead remain present.

A death does not remove someone downward into earth, because deep earth is unreliable and not wholly trusted. The dead are placed in high stone chambers, open to cold air and prepared mosses. Over time the body becomes architecture, garden, weather surface. Families visit ancestral walls to trim growth, read lichen color, add handprints, speak news softly. Grief here is vertical. One looks up along the green face of a lineage and sees centuries becoming texture.

Those lost to spatial conditions are mourned differently. If someone walks into fog and returns altered, they are not treated as ghosts or monsters, but as people with weather in them. They may speak in old dialects, cast inconsistent shadows, remember grandchildren not yet born, or be unable to enter certain rooms without weeping frost. They are given work if they can work, quiet if they need quiet, and watched with compassionate caution. If someone does not return at all, a silver fern may be planted on the family wall. If later that fern trembles without wind, the household listens.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is maintenance raised to reverence.

They do not worship the plateau as benevolent. It is too old for benevolence. They do not worship the distortions as spirits, though some poets speak of them that way. Their sacred acts are acts of keeping: sealing roofs before freeze, sharing water without fraud, remeasuring roads after storms, leaving empty space where error can settle, teaching children the dangerous signs, repairing a cistern built by hands no one remembers. Holiness is continuity under impossible conditions.

Their prayers are often instructions preserved in elevated language.

“May the wall hold its agreement.”

“May water show itself before it sings.”

“May the road arrive in the direction it promises.”

“May the shadow tire and return.”

“May the names come back with the fog.”

Music exists, but it is restrained by the acoustics of danger. Wooden bells, skinless drums made from hollow roots and stretched plant fiber, low flutes carved from reed stems, communal work chants with narrow intervals that do not provoke echoes. Songs are often call-and-response, but the response is delayed, allowing listeners to confirm that it comes from human throats rather than from a wall, basin, or returning alley. In safe halls, during long cold nights, music grows warmer: layered humming, foot-tapping, braided melodies imitating rain on roofs and rills across stone.

They are capable of joy, though it is a plateau joy: durable, communal, wary of excess. The first filling of a basin after dry weeks. A road measured stable for ten consecutive mornings. The successful lifting of a roof slab by fifty people chanting as one. A child reading fern tremors correctly before the fold begins. The return of a party from the mirror basins carrying bowls of old light. The thaw of a spring thought lost. A day in which all shadows remain attached.

Some among them travel.

These travelers are among the most respected and most changed. They move between district-clusters, across open stone, along causeways on the ribs of the world. They carry rolled woven maps, sealed water, spore cakes, repair resin, chalk, a mirror kept covered except when needed, and gifts for cistern courts. They learn to sleep sitting upright away from fresh cracks. They know when to wait three days for a road to become short again. They return with news, seeds, mineral flakes, altered measurements, and sometimes impossible objects blown in by ground winds: shells from nowhere, damp woven scraps, beads of fused sand. They do not always tell everything they saw. Not from secrecy, but because some experiences become less stable when spoken.

Their scholars are not abstracted from labor. An observatory keeper may also mend roofs. A map-weaver may also grow tubers. Knowledge is trusted when it has cold under its nails. They study the spatial conditions with patience rather than conquest. They mark where distances stretch, where alleys compress, where doorways open onto wrong heights, where rain prefers ruins, where shadows climb upward. Their records are redundant: woven, knotted, sung, carved, planted. No single medium is trusted absolutely.

They know of lower worlds, perhaps through trade, perhaps through ancestral migration, perhaps through stairways that descend into fog and return elsewhere. Metals come from there in flakes and fragments, cherished but not central. Stories come too: of animals, seas, dense forests, warm valleys, cities crowded upward instead of outward. Plateau children find animals difficult to believe in. A creature that moves of its own will, eats plants, leaves waste carelessly, makes noise, reproduces without fragmentation or spore weather—such beings sound to them like ambulatory errors.

And perhaps, once, animals did live there. Perhaps the absence is remembered in carvings too worn to recognize, in words whose roots no longer make sense, in triangular shells blown by impossible winds. But the current inhabitants are people of a world without animal accompaniment. They have had to become their own herd, their own warning flock, their own burrowing memory, their own pollinating agency. Their societies compensate for missing ecologies. Children carry spores between gardens. Mourners feed moss walls. Workers turn compost in sealed pits. Travelers exchange plant fragments between districts. Human hands perform the errands once given elsewhere to beaks, hooves, mandibles, and gut.

There may be other presences too, though not animals.

The plants themselves live with unusual insistence. They are not conscious in any ordinary sense, but they are expressive, responsive, opportunistic. Ferns tremble before distance folds. Root-veils gather where voices have been soft for generations. Moss thickens over stable roofs and refuses certain stones. Nodules split rock with decades of swelling patience. Vines follow temperature gradients into rooms where no sunlight falls. In the absence of fauna, plant life occupies the entire register of motion beneath human scale. It creeps, seals, drinks, listens with chemistry, remembers in growth.

The cities themselves almost live. Not metaphorically, not exactly. Their maps change like breathing. Their plazas collect errors like lungs collect mist. Their cisterns pulse with hidden seepage. Their roads lengthen and shorten with something like mood, though the people would reject that word as imprecise. A district abandoned during bad geometry may wait under moss for a generation and then become habitable again, its thresholds realigned, its wells tasting clean, its shadows ordinary. People return, sweep, patch, plant, and speak to it as one speaks to a convalescent elder.

What lives there, then, is an alliance.

Human beings, cold-adapted and careful.

Plants, dense in seams, roofs, cracks, cisterns, graves, and gardens.

Old stone, nearly finished with becoming but not quite still.

Water, scarce and secretive.

Weather, patterned past comfort.

Space itself, fraying but not wholly broken.

And among all of these, the people continue—not as masters of the plateau, but as its stewards, interpreters, repairers, and witnesses. They are a civilization of caution without cowardice, reverence without surrender, patience without stagnation. They have built no empire over the uplands. Instead they have made a practice of remaining.

At dusk they go indoors when the wooden bell sounds from the changing street. They cover bowls. They bank heat stones. They count children without counting columns. They listen to moss darken on the walls. Outside, rain may choose only the ruins. A shadow may hesitate under a cloud. A road may revise its destination in the dark.

Inside, someone knots the day into cord.

Someone marks the cistern level.

Someone teaches a child the old rule: leave room around what cannot be understood.

And through the long cold night, beneath roofs held down by stone and habit, the plateau’s inhabitants sleep lightly, while frost works at the walls and moss, patient as ancestry, begins again to mend them.
