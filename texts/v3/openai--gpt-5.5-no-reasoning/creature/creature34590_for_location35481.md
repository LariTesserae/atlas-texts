What lives there has learned to be a consequence.

Not a people, not quite a beast in the old sense, not a ruler of the drowned construction, because nothing rules there except pressure and returning water. It lives by being fitted to interruption: to sudden crosscurrents, to falling cold, to corridors that become mouths, to plazas that become presses, to still rooms that hold for an hour and then expel everything alive.

It is not rare there, though it is seldom seen whole. The first sign is usually an arrangement of absences. Smaller animals leave a stair-groove at once. Filter-shells shut along a seam in sequence, one after another, as if a shadow has passed through stone. Ribbon things loosen their knots and stream flat against the current. Blind fish divide and flatten themselves along the sides of a passage. The place, already cold, seems to withhold an additional degree.

Then something pale moves behind a row of worn pillars.

It is large, but it avoids largeness when it can. Its body can narrow, fold, deflate, or spread, so that scale becomes difficult to judge. In an open court it may seem the size of a ceremonial barge, a long animal made of slack white muscle and dark interior ribs. In a vaulted passage it becomes a ribboned mass no thicker than a door-slit, slipping through architecture that should exclude it. It has no obvious front until it chooses one. Several ends of it can investigate at once.

Its skin is the color of old ceramic seen through deep water: blue-white, gray-white, sometimes faintly translucent at the edges. Under that skin move slow bands of darker tissue, not stripes but internal weather. Along its length are plates or ridges that resemble eroded masonry more than armor. These are not hard in the way shells are hard; they flex. They are pressure-bones, surfaces for taking force, for letting the violence of the water pass along the body instead of through it.

When it rests against a wall, it nearly disappears.

It has borrowed the grammar of the place. Its back rises in worn parapets. Its joints resemble softened corners. Its folded limbs hang like fragments of old balustrade. Silt settles briefly in the shallow troughs of its skin and gives it false inscriptions. Small shelled mouths fasten to it as they fasten to architecture, and it tolerates them until it moves.

The head is not where a land creature would expect it.

Several blunt sensory lobes flower from the body, retractable and pale, each one rimmed with soft pits. These lobes taste pressure, mineral difference, the wake of prey, the memory of recent motion in the water. They open like slow anemones but are not flowers and not delicate. When danger or hunger sharpens, they pull inward, and the creature becomes almost featureless.

Its eyes, if they are eyes, are small and set deep beneath translucent lids. They do not look outward so much as notice gradients. Light here is too poor and too general to be trusted. The animal sees instead with its skin, its pits, the tremor-sensitive lace strung between its inner plates. It knows a chamber by the way water enters it. It knows a corridor by the echo of its own pulse. It knows living bodies by the disturbances they cannot help making, even when they are motionless.

It does not swim like a fish. It negotiates.

In heavy current it anchors three or four limbs into cracks, sockets, worn channels, old drainage cuts. Then it releases one hold and lets the water carry that portion of its body forward, catching with another limb before the rest is torn free. In this way it crosses places no ordinary animal can cross: downwelling courts, sluice-streets, open terraces scoured clean by vertical flood. It moves as though climbing the water itself.

In calmer spaces it unfolds. Thin lateral membranes open between its limbs, dusky and veined, turning it from a crawling thing into a gliding sheet. It can hover above a plaza without effort, held between conflicting flows. Sometimes it hangs under an arch for days, body slack, sensory lobes extended, letting the flood bring the world into it.

It is a scavenger often, a predator when the architecture permits, and a filter-feeder when hunger becomes long.

Nothing in that place can afford purity of diet.

It waits where currents sort the living. Beneath broken parapets, behind the lips of old sluices, under stairfields whose grooves funnel small animals downward, it spreads itself into the lee. Blind fish come in loose crowds, following particulate food. Ribbon creatures knot and loosen. Tiny translucent things spin in the eddy. The large animal remains part of the wall until the water changes. Then a portion of the wall opens.

Its mouth is not a single wound but a series of soft intake chambers along the underside. They flare and seal independently. A school can vanish into three of them while the rest of the creature remains braced and still. Hard prey is held in muscular pockets where mineral teeth—low, grinding ridges like polished floor-lines—crush shell and joint. What cannot be eaten is expelled later as pale grit, indistinguishable from eroded construction.

It follows pressure changes the way surface animals might follow seasons.

There are pulses in the drowned highland: periodic collapses of upper water, reversals through hidden shafts, cold descents from unseen heights, upwellings from deeper chambers carrying dissolved matter and carcasses. Most creatures endure these events. This one uses them. When a downrush strikes a terrace half a kilometer away, it is already moving toward the plume. When a sealed court begins to breathe outward, it has placed itself at the exhalation point. When silt lifts in a chamber, it reads the buried motions beneath.

It knows the architecture intimately, though not as a mind knows a city.

It knows which blank wall has a slit wide enough for its young. It knows which amphitheater gathers stunned prey after a pressure wave. It knows the underside of bridges, the hollows inside tower-bases, the cavities behind worn reliefs. It knows old vertical shafts where water falls so hard that anything unanchored becomes paste. It knows the calm chambers that are not safe because calm, here, is often only a held breath.

It breeds in small hollows.

This is one of the few times it becomes careful in a way that resembles tenderness. The adult wedges itself into a chamber with only two entrances, preferably one narrow enough that larger rivals cannot enter without folding too slowly. There, along the ceiling or along a protected wall-seam, it fastens rows of translucent capsules. They look like bubbles that have learned not to rise. Inside each, a pale comma turns and shivers.

The young hatch unfinished.

For a time they are almost all membrane and mouth, little drifting scraps with black pinpoints of pressure-sense. Many are swept away immediately into the city’s hunger. Some survive by clinging to the breathing white nodules on ceilings, feeding on what those colonies reject. Others hide in stair-grooves with eel-like scavengers and lose siblings to them. The few that grow begin to form their first pressure-plates from minerals taken into the body, not exactly eaten but sorted from water and prey. Their skeleton is partly made of the place.

Perhaps that is why the adults seem architectural.

They do not merely inhabit the drowned highland. They incorporate it slowly. Glazed dust, composite grit, old ceramic powder, mineral grains from inscriptions and eroded channels pass through their bodies and settle into flexible ridges. Each individual carries a little ruin within its tissues. Very old ones become darker, denser, harder to distinguish from collapsed masonry. Their movements slow. They choose deep courts where currents are powerful but predictable and become almost resident features: a false step in a stairfield, a pale lintel under an arch, a ribbed mass along the flank of a tower.

Other animals use them.

Tiny filter-mouths cling to their plates. Blind fish shelter beneath their bodies until the bodies feed. Ribbon creatures loop around their limbs during calm, stealing safety from their bulk. Parasites live in the sensory pits and must be scraped off against old walls. Sometimes white ceiling colonies spread onto the oldest individuals, mistaking them—or accepting them—as structure. The animal periodically sheds sheets of outer skin, and these drift through the avenues like torn banners, eaten by anything small enough to risk the open water.

It has enemies, though few that hunt it directly.

There are things below that move with a deep knocking through passages not built for them. The pale animal avoids those sounds. When they pass, it flattens into grooves, releases airless waste, dims the motion of its inner tissues. Its body becomes an apology to the wall. The larger things may be related to it, or may only share the same ancient solution to pressure. No one there distinguishes lineage from convergence. Everything is shaped by the same cold argument.

Its intelligence is not human, but it is not simple.

It remembers sequences of water. It can wait through three false calms for the fourth, true one. It can block a side-channel with its body and make prey choose a more useful route. It can stir silt from a floor to blind fish that do not see. It can pry a colony of jointed filter-animals from a balustrade socket and carry the lattice in its mouth-chambers to another current, where dislodged prey will gather around the exposed seam.

It may even play, if play can exist without warmth.

Young adults sometimes ride the downward veils from high causeways, folding themselves at the last instant before impact, then spreading into the rebound plume. They strike old plazas hard enough to send sediment bursting outward in rings. They twist through colonnades where the current would shred a weaker body. Whether this teaches them turbulence or merely answers some inner pressure cannot be known. In that world, joy would look very much like survival practiced extravagantly.

It has no voice.

Sound is too costly and travels strangely there. But it pulses. Along its flanks are chambers that contract in patterns, sending low thuds into the water and stone. Most are practical: warning, mating, spacing, the claim of a feeding eddy. Some are so low they are felt only as a change in the bones of nearby architecture. In calm vaults, two of them may exchange pulses for hours, hidden from one another by walls, speaking through corridors of water and old material.

Their communication seems less like naming than measuring.

Here is pressure.  
Here is hunger.  
Here is a hollow.  
Here is mine.  
Here is danger below.  
Here is the cold descent.  
Here is the opening that will close.

They do not build. The place has no need of new building, and no creature there can imagine dry intention. Yet they alter it in minor ways. They clear certain hollows by passing through them repeatedly. They polish the undersides of arches with their backs. They widen cracks where they breed. They move fragments, not as tools but as obstructions, ballast, shelter. A stone lodged by one of them in a narrow channel may change the feeding pattern of an entire court for a season of currents.

If the vanished makers left symbols, the animal has worn against them for ages without reading. Its body has passed over reliefs, ovals, branching lines, maps of organs or tides or dynasties, until the old marks became smoother. In some chambers, the shallow inscriptions are interrupted by the rubbing-paths of generations. Meaning has been replaced by use. History has become texture.

The creature’s death is slow when the water allows it.

An old one stops crossing the dangerous courts. It remains in a lee beside a tower-base or under the lip of an amphitheater. Its sensory lobes cloud. Its plates thicken and crack. Small animals grow bolder, grazing at its edges while it still pulses faintly. Eventually a pressure change dislodges it. The body, no longer able to anchor, is taken by the city.

It may be pressed flat in a descending plaza. It may be torn open through a colonnade. It may lodge in a ceremonial square and feed half a district for weeks. Its plates separate and tumble into channels. Its soft parts become white storm in the water. Its mineral ridges settle into seams and are mistaken, later, for original construction.

In this way it returns to the made place that made it.

But there are other inhabitants too, and each seems like a different answer to the same impossible home.

There are the latch-crowns: jointed, radial animals that unfold from wall sockets like pale mechanical flowers, though they are not mechanical and have never known a machine. Each has a ring of hooked limbs and a central mouth hidden in a mesh of glassy filaments. They live for decades in one place, perhaps centuries, opening only when the current brings enough particulate flesh. In strong flow they close into smooth knobs, indistinguishable from decorative bosses. Whole colonnades are studded with them, living capitals on dead pillars.

There are the stair-eels, mineral-black and slick, with undersides full of gripping cilia. They occupy grooves in worn steps and carved drainage lines. Their heads are wedge-shaped, their jaws small but numerous. They feed on eggs, trapped larvae, and anything injured that settles into a channel. When pressure drops, hundreds may emerge at once, streaming along a stairfield like spilled ink, then vanish so completely that the steps seem empty again.

There are the veil-wings, flat animals thin as torn cloth, dark on top and silver beneath. They use the vertical floods. A downrush that would kill a heavier body holds them rigid and sends them skimming just above the pavement. They feed on stunned planktonic things and the scraps left by larger predators. In groups they resemble shadows cast by objects that no longer exist.

There are the breathing nodules on ceilings, colonies of soft white mouths housed in shared translucent flesh. They expand and contract with terrible patience. Their interiors glow faintly when disturbed, not with light exactly, but with a milk-blue chemical bruise. Small creatures shelter among them and are sometimes digested. They spread only where the water is slow enough to let larvae settle, so their presence marks rare pockets of gentleness.

There are the blind-headed fish, always moving, always uncertain. Their skulls are broad and soft, their bodies narrow, their skin bruised gray or clear enough to show the flicker of organs. They travel in crowds through corridors and across terraces, guided by the pressure wakes of one another. Panic passes through them faster than thought. In open water they become a single torn sheet; in tunnels they become beads in a current. Most lives there are brief, and theirs are among the briefest.

There are transparent scavenger-crabs that are not crabs, walking sideways on too many delicate legs. They pick at seams, at eggs, at the shed skins of larger animals. Their bodies are almost invisible except for their dark stomachs, which reveal what the city has recently given them. After a mass feeding, the walls may seem speckled with moving ink.

There are slow hinge-beasts in the amphitheaters, two-shelled and asymmetrical, clamped to the smoothed seating. They open only during certain upward flows. Their interiors are red-black, shockingly warm-looking in that cold place, though they are not warm. Many small creatures live and die within the shelter of their shells. When a hinge-beast dies, its empty halves remain for ages, polished by silt into crescent basins.

And beneath all of them, or among them at times, are the knockers: enormous animals known mostly by impact.

They move through the lower passages where the water is dark and the architecture heavier. No one sees them clearly unless briefly and too close. A flank like a moving wall. A mouth rimmed with inward-folding plates. Limbs thick enough to brace in avenues meant for processions. Their bodies strike the inner chambers of the drowned highland, producing the deep knocking that travels upward through stone and water. They may be feeding on mineral colonies, on the old construction itself, or on whatever the currents press into the depths. When one enters an upper district, all other life becomes architecture.

The pale pressure-creature fears them. So do the latch-crowns, the stair-eels, the blind fish, the veil-wings, if fear is the right word for the instant obedience of bodies to danger.

Nothing here has a name for itself.

Names require a place that holds still long enough for distinction to become ceremony. This place holds only form, not promise. Its inhabitants are shaped by recurrence: the returning downrush, the familiar eddy, the usable crack, the edible drift, the fatal open square. They do not possess the drowned city. They are sieved through it, gathered by it, abraded by it, made legible for a moment against its surfaces.

If one creature can be said to belong there most completely, it is the pale one that incorporates the walls into its body and the currents into its memory.

It is ruin made animal.

It is not the ghost of the builders, not their servant, not their punishment. It does not explain them. It has no reverence for their towers or courts. It passes over their unread reliefs, breeds in their ceremonial hollows, hunts beneath their worn bridges, dies in their sluices, and leaves behind mineral plates that join the city’s endless erosion.

It lives where no fire can be imagined.

It lives by touch, pressure, patience, and sudden opening.

It is soft enough not to shatter, hard enough not to vanish, old in its individuals and older in its kind. It has accepted that shelter is a shape in moving water. It has accepted that every calm is temporary, every hollow a kingdom, every edge a memory of force. It has never known air, but it knows altitude as pressure above pressure, cold stacked over cold, dimness falling through dimness.

When it moves across a polished plaza in the low metallic light, smaller animals scattering before it and sediment lifting from the floor, it seems for a moment that the architecture itself has decided to cross from one shadow to another.

Then the current takes its outline apart.

A limb becomes a parapet.

A flank becomes a wall.

A sensory lobe closes like a worn doorway.

The water folds over it, cold and ancient, and the drowned highland continues arguing with itself around the living shape that has learned, better than almost anything there, how to be part of the argument.
