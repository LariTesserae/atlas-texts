What lives there does not have a single name for itself.

Names require a kind of distance: a creature set apart from its surroundings, an outline recognized and kept. The life of the plateau has had little use for outlines. It has grown in the dark for so long that separation is always provisional. Root enters machine. Fungus enters root. Warmth enters blood. A pool-mouth takes in the fallen dust of flowers that open to sound. A beetle carries mineral film from stone to fern, and later something blind eats the beetle and carries the stone farther. Nothing here is solitary in the way daylight creatures imagine solitude.

Still, there are inhabitants.

At the smallest scale, the plateau is crowded almost beyond bearing.

The first and most numerous are the films. They live on everything: on stone, on old polymer, on the undersides of leaves, on the moist shells of beetles, on the skin of amphibians, in the shallow breath-space between moss and rock. They are not quite algae, not quite fungus, not quite bacterial skin, though they borrow habits from all three. They feed on dissolved iron, on heat, on faint electrical gradients rising from buried systems, on the salts left where water pauses and evaporates only halfway.

They make the stone slick. They give it its black shine. They thicken over the ancient circular marks until the tremors pass through them and arrange their bodies in branching patterns no eye is meant to read. If scraped away, they return within days as a dull wet bruise, then a glaze, then a living surface again.

Many of the larger creatures graze on them. Many of the plants depend on them. They are the skin of the place.

Among them thread pale root-fungi that do not form mushrooms except under rare pressure changes. Most of the time they remain hidden, netted through the saturated soil and the softened cracks in stone. They are slow intelligences of hunger and exchange. They know the warmth of the buried devices. They know which vents are failing. They know where a carcass lies under ferns before any animal does. When rain deepens, their filaments swell and open tiny pores, releasing scents like pepper, clay, or old smoke. The flowers answer them. The insects answer the flowers. The plateau rearranges itself by odor.

There are plants that are almost animals in their attentiveness.

The low black liverworts along the cracks are blunt, ancient things, thick as folded hide. They endure trampling, drowning, the mild poisons of metal, and long periods when the underground hum falters. Their cushions are full of minute chambers where larvae sleep and blind mites shelter. When touched, the cushions tighten imperceptibly, holding water in their dark tissue. They are the first growth over any newly exposed stone and the last to abandon a dying vent.

The ferns are taller and more expressive. Their broad leathery fronds hang heavy with condensation, each rib sensitive to vibration. A step too close makes them stiffen. A low tremor makes them spill water from their tips all at once, a sudden local rain that confuses small predators and flushes insects from hiding. Some ferns have frond-edges ragged with feeding scars; others bear rows of beadlike nodules that warm slightly during the louder-dark cycle, attracting moths that dust them with static pollen from the ground-flowers.

The bladder-vines are stranger.

They climb only where old energy leaks through stone or fabricated matter. Their translucent sacs hang in clusters, each swollen with warm water and mineral broth. The sacs are not leaves, not fruit, not stomachs, but something between. Small creatures pierce them and drink. In response, the vine thickens around the puncture and sometimes grows a new tendril toward the feeder’s path, as if learning the route of thirst. During rain the bladders pulse very slowly, squeezing fresh water down into buried root-knots. During dry intervals—which are only less-wet intervals here—they glow with no visible light, but with heat. Pale moths gather on them like torn scraps of skin.

The ceramic-stemmed shrubs live in the edges between groves and open rock. Their stems are soft when growing and harden suddenly after pressure drops, forming hollow tubes that ring if struck. Wind rarely plays them, but insects do. Thumb-sized obsidian beetles climb their lengths and rasp at the hardened skin. The shrubs answer by resonating faintly, and the vibration shakes loose a powder of spores from hidden seams. In the listening-dark, a whole stand of these shrubs may chime softly under the feet of beetles, each note dull and wet and brief.

The condensation trees are the tallest residents, though even they seem less like a forest than a set of rooted weather organs.

They grow in compact groves, never singly unless one has outlived its companions. Their roots are buttressed into walls and hollows, making chambers large enough for sleeping animals, nests, or the imagined shelter of a human who has learned humility. Their trunks are smooth, dark, and damp, faintly warmer near the base where roots clasp heated fractures. Their crowns vanish into the upper black, but water descends from them constantly: along leaves, down channels in the bark, through dangling rootlets that taste the air.

These trees drink from the sky without needing light. Their vertical leaves gather vapor. Their roots gather tremor. Their inner tissues contain fine metallic grains stolen from the plateau’s old machinery through centuries of seepage. When the underground hum deepens, the trees align themselves to it. Their leaves tilt, not toward brightness, but toward pressure. In the warm-dark cycle, they loosen their crowns and let stored droplets fall in sequenced patterns. To creatures below, this is weather. To the trees, perhaps, it is speech.

Beneath them live the dwellers of the root rooms.

There are the combers: many-legged grazers no longer than a finger, flat as wet seeds, moving in patient rows across stone shelves. Their mouths are fine brushes. They harvest the black films and leave behind paler trails that slowly darken again. Their bodies are jointed but soft-edged, armored only on top, each plate glossy and veined like old lacquer. When startled, they do not scatter. They flatten, becoming irregularities in the rock. A practiced predator can still smell them. An unpracticed one steps over a hundred without knowing.

There are mites the size of lentils with long sensing hairs that tremble at every drop-fall. There are translucent worms in the moss whose bodies show dark threads of digested metal. There are springing things like crickets without eyes, their hind limbs folded under them, their heads blunt and polished from pushing through wet litter. They communicate by tapping fern stems and listening to the answer return through water.

The beetles are more visible, if anything here can be called visible.

They are dark enough to disappear even against wet stone, but their shells catch texture from the air. A beetle crossing a clearing is known by the moving absence of droplets, by the faint double click of its feet, by the line of moisture closing after it. Some are thumb-sized grazers. Some are larger, with jaws shaped for cutting fungal rope. The oldest have shells mineralized with iron and manganese, making them heavy, slow, and almost indestructible. When they die, small flowers grow preferentially around their bodies, feeding on the metals they gathered over their lives.

The pale moths are not drawn to light. They have no ancestral memory of moon or flame strong enough to matter. They seek warmth, salt, and living breath.

They rise during the warmer pulse, soft-winged and almost silent, with bodies furred in powder that clings by charge rather than stickiness. They settle on exposed skin, on warm bladders, on sleeping animals, on the matte surfaces of the three low instruments near the pool. They drink condensation mixed with salt. Their wings are not white exactly, but the color of things that have never needed pigment: wax, larva, softened bone. Sudden light drives them into frantic retreat. Heat calms them. A resting animal may wake wearing a shivering cloak of them and feel only the softest pressure, like breath returning.

The amphibians are among the true citizens of the plateau.

They occupy seams where water rises through stone, shallow runnels under moss, the rims of pools, the flooded footprints of machines. Blindness is not a lack in them. Their heads are broad and low, their mouths wide, their limbs delicate but quick. Along their backs and flanks run raised ridges, each ridge packed with organs for pressure, vibration, and chemical taste. Their skins are patterned in relief rather than color: knots, lines, whorls, corrugations. One species bears five raised spots on the throat, and when the wooden knocking passes from grove to grove, these spots pulse in answer.

They sing from water thin enough to wet only their bellies. Their calls are felt as much as heard: rounded pulses, throat-clicks, long tremulous notes that make fern droplets quiver. During rain they become almost reckless. They climb exposed stone. They wrestle over warm cracks. They lay strings of eggs in moss cups, where the embryos develop flattened heads before limbs, already listening.

Their predators are quiet.

Some are coiled creatures from the pools. These are rarely seen entire. A surface tightens, a ring forms without ripple, and something below has shifted. They have long bodies, perhaps limbless, perhaps furnished with fine hooked appendages folded close. Their mouths open upward. Their antenna-like breathing threads rise from the pool centers, tasting air for warmth and movement. Small insects walk too near the rim and vanish. Amphibians know the exact distances. Beetles sometimes misjudge.

The pool creatures are old in a way different from the mountain. They live in vertical worlds: cold below, warm above, mineral-rich along the sides. Some never leave the water. Some send threads into moss and breathe through them. Some may be descendants of engineered maintenance organisms, released or escaped into the basins when the plateau still had a purpose. Their bodies click softly from within, not by voice but by movement: plates shifting, valves closing, mineral teeth testing stone. At intervals they surface only enough to disturb the tension of the water, then sink back into a blackness denser than the air.

In the high foliage live gliders.

They are soft-bodied, furred or membrane-skinned, no larger than a folded cloak when still and much larger when falling. They move between condensation trees on broad heat-sensitive membranes stretched from limb to flank. Their eyes are sealed under skin. Their faces are leaflike and flat, with nostril slits that open and close independently. They feed on moths, fruit pulp, and the small warm nodules produced by some vines. To travel, they climb until the trunk narrows, then release themselves into the dark. One hears only a close hush overhead, a change in air against the cheek, perhaps a drop displaced from a leaf by their passing.

They raise their young in hanging pockets of woven fiber and shed membrane. The young cling to the undersides of leaves, tasting condensation and the musk of the grove. A glider family can live for generations in a single cluster of trees and know no other grove except as a pattern of distant whistles.

The fruit-fall creatures are numerous and almost never seen except as a rush.

They wait in the hollow clearings, hidden under fern litter, inside cracks, beneath loose skins of moss. When a fruit drops—heavy, soft, smelling of fermented clay—they converge from every direction. Tiny jaws. Quick feet. Bodies no bigger than a thumb joint. Within minutes the fruit is opened, hollowed, seeded with spores, and dragged in pieces toward root shadows. Afterward the clearing is vacant again, except for the wet mark where the fruit struck and the faint muscular smell of feeding.

Some larger animals visit from the saddle.

Long-snouted climbers come in family groups during the warmest part of the dark cycle. They have narrow heads and flexible lips for drinking from leaf channels without disturbing the pool rims. Their forelimbs are long, their hindlimbs compact, their tails expressive and constantly touching: tail to shoulder, tail to tail, tail curled around the ankle of a young one crossing slick stone. They do not belong entirely to the plateau. Their paths lead down into the jungle-choked saddle and beyond. But they know the pools, the safe vents, the groves where moths are thickest and biting things fewest.

They are cautious around the arch. Adults guide their young wide of it. Once in a while one pauses before it and presses its snout toward the numb air, not touching, only testing the absence there.

There is also the heavy passer.

It may not live on the plateau, but the plateau knows it. Its tracks are deep crescents that fill with water, then green over slowly. It crosses rarely, perhaps by season, perhaps by a cycle too long for ordinary watching. The groves quiet before it arrives. The amphibians still themselves. Even the gliders hold to trunks. It moves with weight but without haste, placing its feet as though remembering a path through a place that has changed since its body first learned it.

No one sees it clearly. It may have hide ridged with moss, or plates warmed by internal fermentation, or a head carried low to read the ground. It may browse on the waxen plants around old metal posts. It may come to listen to the circular marks tremble. What matters is its effect: for a little while, all smaller life admits that scale still exists.

And beneath all of these, or among all of these, lives the old system.

To call it alive may be inaccurate, but the plateau has stopped caring about the distinction. The machinery under the stone breathes water upward. It holds heat. It deepens the sky. It listens with buried instruments too slow or damaged to interpret their own readings as they once did. It has been entered by roots, filmed by microbes, nested in by blind animals. Its vents are pollinated by flowers that open to tones from below. Its waste warmth guides migrations. Its pressure corrections time the breeding of amphibians.

It does not think like an animal. It does not want like a plant. But it persists, regulates, responds, and is responded to. The life above has grown around its habits so thoroughly that the machine has become an organ of the biome. If it failed all at once, many things would die. If it changed slowly, many things would adapt. It may already be adapting in return, using root growth as insulation, fungal mats as filters, animal burrows as drainage. What was built has become inhabited; what inhabits it has become part of its function.

There may be one more resident.

Not necessarily human, though human is easiest to imagine because the shelter would be sized for a crouched body and a careful hand. Someone could live under the buttress roots of a condensation tree, in a dwelling nearly indistinguishable from fallen matter: woven leaves darkened with wet, old polymer panels softened by moss, cords made from vine fiber, a sleeping hollow warmed from below by a vent that never quite cools. Such a resident would not rule the plateau or even master it. They would be tolerated only by becoming slow.

They would move without lamps when possible. They would know which pools click hungrily and which merely breathe. They would gather water from leaf chains, not from the deep circular basins. They would eat fungus, small fruit, perhaps the combers when other food was scarce, perhaps the eggs of amphibians only with apology or ritual restraint. They would wrap tools in soft fiber so no bright metal flashed and no hard clatter traveled too far. Their skin and clothing would grow a permanent film of plateau life. Their sense of direction would be auditory: the five knocks, the western boom, the fern-drip, the hum under the arch.

If they made a lamp, it would be hooded and brief. If they spoke, they would speak softly, because the place answers disturbance with attention.

But perhaps no such person remains.

Perhaps the only dwellers are the ones that never needed fire, story, or sight. The moss that takes the shape of old violence and makes it soft. The flowers that sing in ticks and scents. The blind amphibians with maps written in their skin. The beetles black as wet stone. The pool-coils breathing through threads. The gliders falling silently through warm dark. The machines below, no longer separate from weather. The roots entering everything.

What lives there is a whole society of things that have learned not to be seen.

They do not brighten the darkness. They use it. They do not wait for sunrise. They have replaced sunrise with warmth, with pressure, with mineral taste, with the low recurring thought of buried engines. They flourish in gradients too subtle for eyes: a tenth of a degree along a root, a tremor in a water-filled circle, the salt print of a passing animal, the static charge on a moth’s wing, the change in ceiling-dark when rain gathers overhead.

The plateau’s inhabitants are tactful, saturated, patient, and old. They live by contact and nearness. They know the world as wetness, resistance, temperature, echo, and scent. They are not hidden because they are afraid. They are hidden because hiding is the local form of being.

And together they make the worn crown of the mountain less a place occupied by life than a single dark organism with many mouths, many skins, many listening bodies, and a memory buried deep enough to hum.
