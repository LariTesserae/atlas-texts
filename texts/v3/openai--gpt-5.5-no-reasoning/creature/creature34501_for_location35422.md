What lives there is not a single creature, at first. It is a society so old that it has forgotten the need for leaders.

The resident life is distributed through the grove the way intention might be distributed through a machine: nowhere visible as a mind, yet everywhere evident in adjustment. Nothing dominates by height or sudden movement. The largest beings are horizontal, patient, load-bearing things — ancient trunks still alive along their lengths, not trees so much as green-barked conduits. They lie across the basin like sleeping animals that have rooted in their sleep. From them rise leaves, shoots, vents of growth, little uprights of current life fed by bodies that began long before them.

These old sprawlers are the elders of the place. They do not tower. They occupy. Their power is not command but infrastructure. Birds nest in them, beetles hollow them, fungi thread through their softened interiors, moss armors them, vines strap them to one another, and their own newer stems lift from them like afterthoughts. They are less individuals than continuing agreements between wood, rot, water, and time. A trunk may be dying at one end, flowering at the other, and already becoming soil in the middle.

Around and between them live the small quick inhabitants that make the grove seem empty until one listens.

There are frogs in the root-cups, each with a territory no larger than a handprint of dark water. They are cool, bead-eyed, nearly the color of soaked leaves. Some never need a pond; a spoonful of held rain is enough world. Their calls are not songs in the sentimental sense, but timed pulses, pressure signals, living ticks in the grove’s engine. One answers another through the hollows until it is hard to tell whether the frogs are calling from places or whether the place itself has learned to count.

There are birds built for cavities and moss-grip rather than branches: small, compact birds with nervous feet and precise beaks. They move through the side-walls of fallen trunks as if through a tenement of green rooms. They know every softened seam, every bark-lip under which insects collect, every vine-angle from which a warning call carries strangely far. Their nests are tucked into living rot — chambers warm with feathers, root-hairs, seed-down, and the dust of chewed wood. Their voices repeat until repetition stops sounding like behavior and becomes part of the basin’s working noise.

There are beetles in multitudes. They are among the true workers here, though they do not hurry. Under bark and within punky wood they rasp, tunnel, lay eggs, molt, and polish darkness with their bodies. Some are black and ridged like seeds made of horn. Others are pale because they seldom meet daylight. Their larvae live inside the old trunks, eating what has already loosened its claim on life. To stand near certain root masses is to hear them faintly: a dry interior grazing, a secret file-work. They are not destroying the grove. They are keeping it permeable.

Fungi live there in a way closer to governance than habitation. Most of them are unseen: pale threads under the moss-skin, nets through the loam, white fans pressed between bark layers, damp cords binding root to root. They carry messages of hunger, chemistry, readiness, exhaustion. After rain, fruiting bodies may appear overnight in orderly little assemblies — not random mushrooms, but brief instruments exposed by the larger hidden organism. Some are tan and ear-shaped, some translucent at the rim, some dark as old blood. They rise, open, shed, and collapse back into the work.

The mosses are not passive covering. They are the basin’s skin and memory. They decide where a hoof may hold, where a seed may root, where water will pause long enough to matter. In the center they form the pale oval floor, tense and compacted, resilient as felt. Elsewhere they mound into cushions, ropes, lips, and false certainties. A foot placed badly may break through, not into emptiness exactly, but into the grove’s under-breath: cool black cavities, root scaffolds, wet leaves, the mineral smell of long storage.

Snails live upside down under leaves and along the shaded seams of bark. They are light-colored, almost ghostly, with thin shells that seem borrowed from old raindrops. They feed on films too subtle for larger mouths: algae, spores, the beginning of decay. They leave silver script where there is barely any surface to write on. In the loudness of the grove, their lives are silent and continuous.

There are lizards on the warm root-backs when sun bands pass over them. They are narrow, quick, dry-eyed creatures with skins mottled to match lichen, bark, and fern-shadow. Their stillness is absolute until it is not. Then they vanish into slits in the vegetation, as if the grove has blinked them away. They know the timetable of light better than any clock: which root warms first, which stone retains heat, which fern shadow hides a hunting bird.

Moths sleep in daylight on lichen plates and bark scales. They are nearly indistinguishable from weathering. Their wings imitate the oldness around them — gray-green, brown-black, freckled, chipped with false decay. At times they rise together, disturbed by a fox’s passing or a trunk’s low creak, and then the air appears to shed flakes of itself.

Mice occupy the curled-leaf tunnels and root lattices. They are not field mice of open seed and sky, but basin-mice: soft-footed, damp-scented, with whiskers trained to narrow passages and ears tuned to distortions of sound. They travel under the living floor more often than across it. Their routes are temporary and yet inherited, renewed as leaves curl, sink, and are replaced. To them the grove is not open; it is a layered house with edible walls.

Larger animals come more cautiously. Deer or deer-like browsers stand at the rim and lower their heads into the green machinery, stripping leaves from reachable shoots, tugging at fern crowns, testing the floor before each step. They do not own the basin. They borrow its edges. Their hooves know the load-bearing roots, the sedge-braids, the stone-backs hidden under moss. When they misjudge, the ground takes them softly, up to the fetlock, and releases them smelling of black water.

At the west edge, under the dry-shouldered slump of a great trunk, something like a fox lives. It is red only in certain light; more often it is bark-brown, fern-shadowed, smoke-backed. Its den is in a place where condensation does not gather, where the root-arch sheds moisture and the moss is thin from repeated turning. It knows the grove’s acoustics intimately. It can pause and separate frog from drip, beetle rasp from mouse scratch, bird alarm from falling leaf. Its paths do not become paths because the vegetation heals behind it, but the fox remembers them as pressure, angle, scent, and give.

It hunts without drama. A nose into leaf-curl. A paw held above a weak place. A leap from one root-knuckle to another. Then stillness again, the loud grove closing around the small event before absence can widen.

There may be larger unseen residents only in passing: badgers, small cats, night birds, the occasional heavy-bodied animal that enters when the basin is dark and the plant surfaces are slick. But the place does not invite conquest. It allows use only by those willing to become part of its rules.

And beneath all these individual lives is the greater inhabitant: the grove itself as a kind of composite organism.

It lives by not choosing between plant and animal, surface and interior, sound and structure. It is made of trunks that function like organs, vines like ligaments, moss like skin, fungi like nerves and gut, insects like circulating maintenance, birds like impulses, frogs like clocks, mammals like brief disturbances that carry seed and scent. It does not think quickly, if it thinks. Its intelligence is equilibrium. It responds by absorbing.

A branch falls, and the grove makes a chamber of it.

A body dies, and the grove makes passage, food, softness, then green tension.

Rain arrives, and the grove stores it in cups, films, swollen fibers, frog nurseries, fungal surges.

Drought arrives, and the grove exhales slowly from its old damp interiors.

Footsteps enter, and the grove tests their weight.

What lives there, then, is continuity with many mouths.

It is not empty. It is empty only of interruption. Empty of monuments, ambitions, scars still trying to be scars. Its inhabitants have no need to fill space visibly because they already occupy every process. They live in the holding of moisture, in the reuse of wood, in the calibrated give of moss, in the repeated call, in the identical sunlit hour arriving again on the same root.

The creature of the place is old, low, green, and distributed. It has birds for sparks, frogs for metronomes, beetles for teeth, fungi for memory, fox for hunger, moss for skin, trunks for bones, vines for tendons, and condensation for breath.

It is a factory that manufactures itself.

It is a room whose residents are also the walls.

It is alive in the manner of something that no longer needs to become anything else.
