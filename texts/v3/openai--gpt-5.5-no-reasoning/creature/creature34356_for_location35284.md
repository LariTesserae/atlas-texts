They are a people of maintenance.

Not merely in the sense that they repair things, though they repair everything constantly, but in the deeper sense that their lives are built around keeping a world from slipping out of balance. They live among walls that require feeding, listening, scraping, sealing, airing, cooling, wetting, drying. Their settlement is not a shelter they occupy; it is a body they tend, and they have become one of its organs.

They are warm-skinned, close-living, low-voiced people, accustomed to brushing past one another in passages narrow enough to make solitude a luxury. They know how to turn sideways without thinking, how to duck beneath beams, how to carry water without spilling it when a goat presses by, how to sleep through dripping and wake instantly at a wrong sound in a drain. Their shoulders are rounded by stooping, their palms thickened by rope, pestle, jar-neck, plaster trowel, and bucket handle. Many have a habitual tilt of the head, as if listening through walls.

They do listen through walls.

A skilled adult can tell the difference between rain entering a roof channel, rats behind a grain bin, bees overheating in a hive pocket, a blocked sluice, a cracked cistern seal, a goat worrying loose lashings, or water running where water should not be. Children are praised for sharp hearing. Old people, even when their eyes dim, remain useful because they remember the voices of rooms: which cistern ticks hollow when low, which pipe knocks before overflow, which plaster patch sighs before it blisters.

Their senses have grown inward.

Bright surface light hurts them, or at least offends them by revealing too much at once. They prefer a world disclosed gradually: amber lamplight on fingers, gray shaft-light on plaster, the glint of water in a seam, an animal’s eye appearing and gone. They do not think of their home as dark. Darkness, to them, has degrees and habits. There is clean dark, damp dark, dangerous dark, sleeping dark, storage dark, sacred dark, rat dark, lamp-hungry dark. A stranger would see gloom; they see arrangement.

Their eyes are large-looking under heavy lids, not because they are not human, but because everyone here learns to widen attention before widening sight. Faces emerge slowly in their world, so they are patient with recognition. They know one another by footfall, cough, smell of work, the rhythm of someone’s breathing while lifting a jar. A person approaching unseen is not mysterious for long: the slap of sandals, the scrape of a basket against plaster, the clicking of bone pins, the mutter under the breath—these announce identity.

They smell of oil smoke, damp fiber, mineral water, goats, clay, and fermented grain. Their hair, when not covered, carries ash and lamp soot. Many bind it close with reed cords or shell-pinned cloth to keep it from flames, wheels, animals, and plaster. Their clothing is practical and layered lightly: linen or woven fiber wraps, sleeveless tunics, short cloaks against cistern chill, hip cords hung with keys, hooks, little knives, wick trimmers, charms, and tally tags. Children wear little beyond waist cloths in the warm lanes, but they are often strung with bright beads, bone disks, or dyed thread so they can be recognized at a distance in dim corridors.

They do not decorate by making things new. They decorate by making things familiar.

Their ornaments are rubbed, repaired, handed down. Shell inlays polished dull by generations of thumbs. Copper earrings greened at the edges. Bracelets of fired clay impressed with family marks. Necklaces of eel vertebrae, pigeon bone, drilled seeds from the surface, blue-glazed shards from some older broken vessel too precious to discard. A beautiful object is not one untouched by use. A beautiful object is one that has survived touch gracefully.

Their children grow up among rules before they understand rules as separate from the world.

Do not step on the drain covers with cracked corners. Do not tease the pale geckos. Do not put your hand into cloudy water. Do not waste wick-flame. Do not shout in the old cistern room during dry weeks. Do not chase chickens into mushroom rooms. Do not sleep under a bee wall in high heat. Do not block a passage with baskets unless you stay beside them. Do not lick condensation bowls. Do not argue when rain begins.

They repeat these things in singsong and mockery, then later with fear, then later with authority.

Children are everywhere because there is nowhere entirely away from them. They nest in hammocks, under counters, behind reed screens, on warm brick ledges, inside emptied storage baskets. They know hidden crawlways adults can no longer fit through. They watch blind fish in drains, race beetles, name rats, steal mushroom scraps, and learn to read tally marks by tracing water records on cistern walls. Their games imitate the place’s functions: opening gates, carrying bowls, tapping for rain, weighing grain, trapping eels, scolding goats. During the first rain rites they shriek under mats while masked adults thunder through the corridors, though every child knows the masks by their chipped noses and reed hair.

They mature early in competence, if not in solemnity. A child who can carry a sealed lamp without spilling oil has crossed one threshold. A child who can clean a drain grate without losing the hook has crossed another. A child trusted to uncover a condensation room at morning has become, in a small but real way, part of the settlement’s survival.

Their elders are repositories of layout, law, and omen.

An old woman may remember that a family sleeping chamber was once a stairwell, and that under its rear plaster lies an old void where moisture gathers. An old man may know which blocked doorway leads toward a sealed kiln, and which sealed kiln must never be reopened because it exhales bad air. They sit near lamps stripping fibers, sorting seeds, smoothing reed splints, telling stories of former floods and famous dry years. Their stories often begin with water levels: “In the season when the third mark vanished,” or “before the frogs came back to the black drains,” or “when the west shaft reflected red for three mornings.”

They speak of the surface as another country, even though some of them climb to it every week.

Those who go above are a recognizable kind. They are weather-browned, squinting, wind-chapped, often restless in the inner lanes when they return. They carry salt, fuel, dung, ore bits, dry wood, surface fruit, medicinal bark, bird news, rumors, and sometimes grief. They know how to seal a stair hatch against dust storm or raiders or cold night air. They understand distances measured not in corridors but in horizons, which makes them faintly suspect and faintly glamorous.

Children gather around them for stories of open sky.

The surface-goers exaggerate, but not always. They describe light that casts hard black shadows. Trees taller than shafts. Water lying openly in pools with no lid. Wind that pushes the body. Clouds like moving plaster. Stars like lamp holes pricked in a ceiling too high for any ladder. Some listeners laugh, some shudder, some declare the whole surface wasteful and badly maintained.

Most residents are not afraid of above, exactly. They simply do not trust a place that does not show where its water is kept.

Their society values vigilance over bravery, repair over conquest, continuity over invention. A person admired here is one who notices small failure before it becomes public disaster. Someone who can smell souring grain behind a sealed door. Someone who can calm goats in a flood. Someone who remembers to open the low gates when the third channel runs. Someone who gives water without being seen to give it. Someone whose plaster patches hold.

Their insults are practical.

“Loose-lashed.”  
“Fat in the east drain.”  
“Green-fungus fool.”  
“Cracked bowl.”  
“Surface-eyed.”  
“Would spill rain arguing.”

Their blessings are practical too.

“May your jars ring full.”  
“May your walls dry evenly.”  
“May the drips choose your bowls.”  
“May the rats prefer your neighbor.”  
“May the cistern remember you.”

They do not have priests in the grand sense, though certain people tend the old obligations. Water counters. Cistern keepers. Mask keepers. Bee callers. Drain openers. Lamp stewards. Mushroom markers. Midwives who know damp-fever from birth-sweat, and bone-setters who make splints from reed and old crate ribs. Authority is distributed through competence. No one rules a cistern without knowing how to clean it.

Still, there are ceremonies.

Rain is the greatest.

When the first warm-season rain arrives, they wear the clay masks from the side chamber: blue channel lines, black cistern mouths, red lamp dots. The masked people move through the lanes tapping walls with sticks, not pretending to be spirits exactly, but becoming the settlement’s memory of thunder. The children hide, then follow. Dogs bark and howl. Goats stamp and toss their shortened horns. Lamps shiver in their niches. If water answers from above, laughter breaks out with a relief so sharp it is almost sobbing.

They mark births with moisture. A drop from the cleanest condensation bowl is touched to the infant’s forehead. Another drop goes to the wall near the family sleeping place, pressed in with a thumbprint. The wall accumulates these prints: pale, greasy, smoky, replastered, renewed. A person belongs because their first water entered the architecture.

They mark deaths with drying.

The dead are washed sparingly but carefully, with cloths wrung almost dry. Their tools are cleaned and either passed on or hung for a season near the old cistern. A little water is poured onto a shard and allowed to evaporate beside a lamp, carrying the name into the warm air. Bodies may be taken above, if there is safe passage, or sealed in older burial niches beyond the living rooms, where plaster and salt do their slow work. What matters is that the dead do not leave maintenance. Their names remain attached to repairs, rules, jokes, bends in corridors, stubborn doors.

“This patch was Nami’s hand.”  
“Your uncle broke that step.”  
“Old Sef said never trust the south rope after frogs.”  
“She heard water before the dogs did.”

They are intimate with animals, but not sentimental in a simple way. Goats are cursed, depended upon, sung to, milked, bred, and occasionally eaten. Chickens are tolerated like bad-tempered neighbors. Pigeons are read as messengers of the surface whether or not they carry actual messages. Bees are treated with formal politeness. Cats are admired as independent citizens of the upper ledges. Dogs are companions, alarms, ratters, and water-hearers; a good pipe-listening dog may be mourned publicly.

Rats occupy a complicated moral category. They are thieves, food, rivals, pets in childhood imagination, and fellow survivors. An especially clever rat may become legendary. There are tales of white rats that know sealed rooms, black rats that predict floods, old scarred rats who understand human speech but choose grain over conversation.

Their food is dense, smoky, sour, and carefully extended. Flatbreads from stored grain. Mushroom stews. goat milk thickened in clay jars. bitter herbs, onion shoots, cress, beans, figs when the shrubs permit. smoked meat, dried fish, eel when caught, rats when necessary or desirable, pigeon eggs when allowed. Honey is precious, dark, and sharp. Salt is treasured. Surface fruit is eaten ceremonially slowly, sliced thin enough that everyone can taste the idea of open air.

They drink water with attention. No one gulps thoughtlessly unless very young, very ill, or recently returned from above. Water has categories: cistern water, condensation water, channel water, boiled drain-adjacent water, animal water, plaster-taste water, festival water, last-resort water. They can taste mineral changes. They know when a jar has been badly sealed. They know which ceramic cools best and which family always smells faintly of old onions because of the vessels they inherited.

Their crafts are small-scale but exquisite in use. They make jars shaped to fit hips and corridor turns. Baskets sized for particular shelves. Lamps with lips that catch smoke residue for scraping. Bone needles curved to mend nets in cramped corners. Reed panels that breathe but keep chickens out. Clay hive tubes angled for bee comfort and human access. Masks repaired so often that the old clay is almost only a core beneath generations of patching.

They write, or at least mark. Clay tablets hold tallies, water records, debts, hatch dates, seed histories, goat lines, repair obligations. Some marks are formal; others are private mnemonic scratches only one lineage reads easily. Walls bear handprints, fish shapes, channel diagrams, warnings, names, jokes, children’s animals, devotional dots around old cracks. The settlement is annotated like a living ledger.

They believe, perhaps, that water has moods but not mercy. That walls remember touch. That sound can harm plaster if fear gives it teeth. That pale geckos are luck because they prevent worse luck. That bees know which households lie. That the first frog-call from the black drains means gates should be checked even if no rule says so. Their beliefs are hard to separate from observations accumulated over generations. Superstition here is often maintenance wearing a story’s face.

Their bodies carry the place’s ailments. Coughs from smoke pockets. Skin softened by damp, cracked by lime, scarred by animal bites and tool slips. Eyes irritated by oil soot. Backs bent by carrying. Feet toughened on warm brick, scarred from hidden splinters and wet grates. Children get fevers from bad water despite all rules. Old people ache in the wet weeks. Surface-goers return with sunburn and wind cuts like marks from another element.

Yet they are not miserable. Their pleasures are many, close, and intense.

The first cool cup from a newly filled cistern. The smell of bread against damp plaster. A lamp trimmed so cleanly it does not smoke. A cat choosing one’s lap. Bees settling after a heat scare. A drain running clear after hours of work. Frog calls. Children asleep in hammocks. Fresh cress. A repaired wheel turning without complaint. The central court under its widest shaft at midday, leaves low and fragrant, cistern lip shining, everyone speaking softly because the plants are listening or because labor deserves quiet.

They laugh often. Enclosed sound makes laughter contagious. Jokes travel faster than news and mutate in passages. They are fond of mimicry: goat voices, surface-goer squints, the pompous tapping of rain-mask elders, the outrage of chickens, the solemnity of water counters. Humor relieves crowding. So does song. Much work is rhythmic, and rhythm invites call-and-answer: grinding songs, plaster songs, bucket-chain chants, lullabies that imitate dripping, teasing songs for reluctant goats, counting songs for children learning which channel opens when.

Their music favors percussion because the architecture answers it. Clay drums, reed rattles, sticks on wall ribs, water bowls struck with bone, foot-stomps on hollow brick. A single tapped pattern can move through the settlement and return altered. During festivals the whole place becomes an instrument. During dry weeks music is quieter, more vocal, less percussive near old plaster.

They court in thresholds, water lines, work crews, and shared tasks. Privacy is scarce, so subtlety is prized. A cleaned lamp left at someone’s niche. A repaired basket handle. A fig slice traded without witnesses, though witnesses always exist. Songs with double meanings while grinding grain. Young people meet in condensation rooms under the excuse of checking bowls, in mushroom chambers while turning blocks, near pigeon cubbies when upper shafts are opened. Marriage, when it happens, is less the joining of two individuals than the negotiation of sleeping space, water obligation, animal rights, shelf inheritance, and whose grandmother knows which wall must not be drilled.

They quarrel, of course. Crowding sharpens grievances. Water makes politics immediate. Who left a lid loose? Who took clean jars? Who fed goats from reserved fodder? Who smoked rope badly? Who let rats into grain? Who has too much shelf space? Who speaks loudly in dry fear and calls it courage? Arguments can flare hot, but rain suspends them. When water arrives, no one argues over bowls. That rule is older than most blood feuds.

Their greatest fear is not darkness, nor burial, nor even drought alone. It is cascading failure.

A cracked cistern in a dry season. Bad air in a sealed room. A blocked drain during rain. Mold in grain stores. Sick goats. Bees abandoning hives. A surface stair compromised. Fire in reed panels. Fat in the east drain. The dread that one neglected thing will wake ten others, and the settlement-body will become feverish beyond tending.

Their courage, therefore, is often unglamorous. Staying awake through a wet night to clear grates. Entering a foul lower channel tied to a rope. Carrying a fevered child through crowded lanes to the cool room. Climbing above in dangerous weather because salt or fuel cannot wait. Cutting open a wall everyone fears in order to find the leak behind it. Killing beloved animals when sickness threatens the herd. Giving up one’s stored water quietly to another household because infants cry differently when truly thirsty.

They do not imagine themselves as underground exiles. They are not waiting to return to a lost open world, though some stories may say their ancestors came below for refuge, drought, war, heat, holiness, or all of these at once. Their identity has passed beyond emergency. The made hollow is homeland. The walls are ancestry. The cistern is center. The sky, for them, is not absent; it has been translated into shafts, reflection, condensation, and sound.

They are human, but human under particular pressures: compressed, attentive, ecological, crafty, communal by necessity and temperament. They live in a place where nature has entered the handmade so thoroughly that the distinction no longer matters. Their goats polish the steps. Their breath wets the ceiling. Their lamps feed moths. Their drains raise fish. Their refuse becomes mushrooms. Their dead become stories, their stories become rules, their rules become architecture.

If one of them stood silently in a passage, lamp behind them, you might first see only a shape among other shapes: a shoulder wrapped in smoke-colored cloth, a hand resting on a damp wall, a string of bone tools at the hip, eyes reflecting amber. Then the figure would become more specific. Plaster under the nails. Hair bound back with reed cord. A scar across one knuckle from an eel or broken jar. A listening stillness. Perhaps a child tucked against one leg, a cat waiting on a shelf above, a water jar balanced easily against the opposite hip.

They would know, before you did, that somewhere behind the wall a trickle had changed.

They would pause mid-sentence.

They would lift one hand, asking silence.

And in that silence, the settlement would reveal what it had taught them to be: not cave dwellers, not villagers merely hidden from the sun, but custodians of an inward weather, citizens of a crafted geology, people whose lives are measured in drips, repairs, echoes, and the long warm breath of a world kept alive by hand.
