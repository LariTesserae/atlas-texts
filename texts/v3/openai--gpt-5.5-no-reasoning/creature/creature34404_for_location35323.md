What lives there is mostly not an animal.

It is a country of rooted hunger: forests that do not stand in air, meadows that do not lie down, colonies that blur the line between plant, fungus, coral, and mineral stain. Life there has learned to hold still with tremendous precision. It lives by choosing exact levels in the water and remaining faithful to them for years.

The dominant inhabitants are tall ribbon-trees anchored into the young basalt. They have no trunks in the terrestrial sense, only bundled, fibrous columns of tough green-brown tissue braided around pockets of buoyant gas. Their holdfasts enter cracks like searching fingers, splitting into finer and finer threads until they become indistinguishable from mineral veins. Above, their blades rise in long translucent sheets, warm green at the center, amber at the edges, with gold bladders set along their ribs like beads. In strong surges they bow as a forest bows in wind, but more slowly, with a massed, dignified yielding. They do not creak. They brush.

Their upper leaves are broad and light-hungry, but their lower growth is stranger: tassels, curtains, whiskers, little combs of tissue that comb the water for drifting matter. A single mature ribbon-tree may feed in four ways at once—by light, by minerals rising from stone, by catching suspended particles, and by housing whole films of smaller organisms along its skin. Its surface is never truly bare. Velvet algae grow over it in patches. Tiny shell-less grazers etch pale trails through those patches. Filament colonies stitch blade to blade, making dim green chambers where silt hangs motionless and larvae wait.

Among these forests live the fan-things.

They are rooted animals, or animal-like plants, or neither. Copper, rust, moss-green, and black-violet, they branch from basalt ledges in antlered shelves and lacework screens. Each branch is built from innumerable soft mouths, each mouth rimmed with hairs fine enough to read the movement of water. When a current rich with spores or mineral dust passes, the fans open wider. When a chemical shadow crosses them, they fold inward, branch by branch, like thought withdrawing from the edges of a mind.

The fans grow especially thick along the slow water-shears, where invisible rivers pass through the greater sea. There they form galleries: copper walls, green roofs, black fans hanging upside down from shelves, all trembling in different directions because each is listening to a different current. They are patient feeders. A large one may take a decade to widen by the span of a hand. Its dead lower branches do not fall away quickly; they become trellises for algae, nurseries for translucent eggs, scaffolds for other lives.

Closer to the stone are mats.

They are the first claimants of raw basalt after a slide or fracture. They arrive as green dust, then as haze, then as velvet. Some are red-brown and plush, folding over shelves in thick lips. Some are emerald and slick, tightening against the rock like wet skin. Some are black and oily, thriving near warm vents where the water tastes of metal and sulfur. These mats are not simple carpets. They are cities of films: layered bacteria, algae, heat-feeders, mineral-makers. In places they build terraces grain by grain, trapping pale deposits until the rock appears to grow bones.

In the hottest fissures live white and yellow veils that shiver though nothing seems to touch them. They stream from cracks in the basalt like torn cloth. Their bodies are colonies of microbes feeding on the breath of the young mountain: iron, sulfur, warmth, dissolved gases. They do not need much light. Some avoid it. They thicken in dark ravines and under ledges, where they make the stone look frost-covered in warm water. Around them gather crimson fans and blunt black algae with swollen, lacquered lobes. These vent neighborhoods are among the oldest-feeling things in the young place, as if they remember a world before leaves.

There are also the suspended thickets.

They root on almost nothing. Long filaments run between gas-bladdered stems, caught in tension across saddles and between spires, and from these filaments hang clusters of leaves, pods, and translucent sacs. They drift but do not depart. They sway inches over decades. They are held by current, buoyancy, and mutual entanglement. From a distance they look like floating meadows. Within them, the water is dense with life: spores, sleeping larvae, clear-bodied mites, little drifting bells that pulse once and then become still for hours.

The meadow-plants carry round leaves, coin-sized to platter-wide, each leaf slightly cupped. When warm water rises from below, the leaves quiver without sound. Their undersides are crowded with pale nodules that open at dusk and release fine threads into the water. They may be reproductive organs, or feeders, or both. Here such distinctions are not firm. Most organisms have learned not to separate eating from breathing, rooting from sensing, shelter from body.

Many of the plateau’s residents are colonies pretending to be individuals.

The green glass bulbs near the vents are one kind. They swell from mineral-rich stone in rounded masses, some no larger than a fist, some the size of boulders. Their skins are transparent and faintly elastic, with branching veins inside. Tiny bubbles rise through them all day, collecting beneath the upper membrane before slipping out through pores. Within each bulb live several kinds of organism: light-eaters near the surface, heat-eaters at the base, floating bead-like cells that migrate up and down according to brightness. When disturbed, the whole bulb clouds over from within, milky and defensive. When conditions are perfect, it glows softly green and grows a new lobe.

The luminous nodules in the high basin are another kind. By day they are dull pearls half buried in furred algae. At night they open—not like flowers, exactly, but like slow eyes. A seam appears. A blue-white shine wells through. Their light is not meant to attract swift creatures, for there are few. It may regulate their own internal cycles. It may call to microscopic partners. It may warn off grazers that dislike the mineral bitterness of their tissues. Whatever its purpose, the basin becomes a quiet constellation after dark, each nodule holding its small cold lamp steady in the warm water.

The animals are fewer, and they behave as if embarrassed by motion.

There are flat silver swimmers, thin as leaves and bright only when seen broadside. Most of the time they travel edgewise between fronds, nearly invisible. Their bodies are flexible discs with rippling margins. They do not dart unless startled; they tilt, slide, vanish. They feed on the fine films growing along translucent blades, shaving them with a mouth that opens on the underside like a soft slit. When a rain-wall passes and the water clarifies, one may see dozens at once along a ridge, each reflecting a different fragment of green light, but as soon as the water thickens they seem to cease existing.

The many-legged grazers are more common than they appear. They resemble tangled roots or loose knots of holdfast until they move. Their bodies are armored in irregular plates stained with algae. Each leg ends in a gripping pad that can cling to glassy basalt, soft mats, or swaying stems. The mouth is circular and rasping, set beneath the body. They advance almost unbearably slowly, leaving clean crescents in red-brown mats and pale scrape-marks across bacterial skins. Some carry gardens on their backs: little forests of threads and nodules growing from the grooves in their shells. A grazer may spend its life on a single cliff face and still travel farther, in its own terms, than most plants ever will.

There are translucent pulse-creatures in the upper water: bell-shaped, ribbon-tailed, nearly weightless. They arrive in loose clusters during certain seasons of light or chemistry. For days the forests will be empty of them; then the upper ridges will fill with faint glass bodies opening and closing like slow lungs. They feed on seed-clouds and microscopic bloom. Their organs are visible as smoky knots inside their bells. When alarmed, they dim, contract, and sink into the bluer distances. Dead ones often become caught in filament thickets, where they dissolve into clear slime and nourish suspended meadows.

Larvae live everywhere.

They are the secret animal abundance of the plateau: specks, commas, threads with eyespots, transparent grains with beating cilia. They hide in seed-clouds, under leaf-stacks, inside hollow stems, among the hairs of feeding fans. Many never become large. Many are consumed by cup-organisms, filter mouths, mats, or each other. Some settle and turn into rooted forms. Some drift upward when cooler rain-water descends, riding the slow inversion as if it were a road. The place’s migrations are mostly microscopic, immense only in number.

Predators exist, but they have almost no drama.

Cup-forms cling to shaded stems and open like soft anemones. Their interiors are lined with adhesive hairs. If a larva touches them, the cup begins to close. The larva may continue to move for many minutes while the rim narrows. By the time capture is certain, nothing sudden has happened. Elsewhere, translucent sheets lie over fans like spilled glass. They are animals, almost perfectly flat, feeding by dissolving a thin layer of living tissue beneath them. Only days later does the bite appear: a clean window in the fan, a missing patch, a pale scar.

Some hunters are chemical rather than muscular. Thread colonies release faint bitter halos that slow nearby larvae. Bacterial mats create sour boundary layers that discourage grazers. Certain green bulbs cloud the water around themselves with irritant dust when bitten. The plateau is full of warnings, but nearly all are silent and invisible unless one has the right skin.

The most consequential inhabitants may be the spores.

They are everywhere: golden dust under overhangs, pale smoke in valley water, green motes caught in density layers, rust-colored drifts settled on shelves. They wait with extraordinary endurance. Some germinate only after a rain of oxygen-rich water. Some after warmth rises from a reopened crack. Some after darkness, burial, abrasion, passage through a grazer’s gut. Many do nothing for years. The drowned highland is seeded far beyond what it can use, full of futures suspended in the water like unspoken names.

Death there also lives for a long time.

When a ribbon-tree tears free and rises, it becomes a wandering island. Its holdfast trails below it, packed with stone grains and small creatures. Its blades begin to pale. Fungi-like blooms—though not true fungi, perhaps—flower along the damaged tissue in cream and violet knots. Flat swimmers gather to shave the softening films. Pulse-creatures shelter beneath it. Larvae settle on its underside. It may drift at a density boundary for months, neither ascending nor sinking, while its body is converted into a temporary reef. Eventually it breaks apart. Some pieces rise into brighter water and vanish from the plateau’s life. Others sink into basins, becoming dark food for mats and bacterial veils.

The beings of this place are shaped by compromise.

They reach upward but anchor downward. They hunger for light but drink heat from cracks. They must be firm enough to survive surge and soft enough not to snap. They must catch what passes without chasing it. They must live in gradients: a little warmer here, a little brighter there, richer water a hand’s breadth lower, safer stillness beneath a ledge, better current along a ridge. Their senses are therefore not eyes and ears so much as distributed attentions. A kelp-tree knows pressure along every blade. A fan tastes the mineral content of passing water. A mat feels shade chemically. A grazer reads the age of algae through its mouth.

No single creature rules the drowned plateau. There is no sovereign predator, no loud herd, no nesting multitude darkening the water. The place belongs to accumulations: forests, films, veils, mats, colonies, seed-clouds, slow grazers, drifting larvae. Its life is abundant but not busy. It is lavish without celebration. Most of its inhabitants are doing the same thing in different forms: holding a position between upward light and upward heat, making bodies out of dissolved stone and green radiance, enduring the long weather of water.

If there is a characteristic creature there, it is not the silver swimmer or the many-legged grazer. It is something rooted to a basalt ledge halfway between a vent and a light shaft: a thick green blade rising from a black crack, its surface furred with smaller lives, its edges glowing faintly gold at noon, its holdfast drinking warmth from stone that was recently fire. Around it spores gather, larvae hide, films thicken, fans open, bubbles pass. It does not move from its place.

But the whole plateau moves through it.
