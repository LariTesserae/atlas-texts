They are not a loud people.

The first impression of them, if one comes into the city by ferry through the suspended rain, is not of faces but of hands: hands on poles, hands tightening knots, hands resting against wet stone to feel vibration, hands spreading to test current, hands holding children by the wrist rather than the shoulder. Their bodies are present in pieces through vapor and green dimness—an elbow dark with rain, a cheek lit by an oil lamp, bare feet gripping a slick rung, hair braided with cord to keep it from dripping into the eyes.

They live as if every surface may become either floor or water.

Their posture shows this. They do not stride. They place themselves. Even on upper walkways they move with the balanced caution of people accustomed to uncertain footing. Knees soft, weight forward, one hand free. Children learn this before speech becomes elaborate: how to step onto a raft without asking it to hold still, how to descend into chest-deep water without stirring silt, how to fall without striking stone. The elderly move slowly but not weakly; many seem to have become part rope, part root, part listening instrument.

Their bodies are shaped by damp labor. Shoulders broad from poling and hauling. Calves strong from ladders. Fingers callused along the insides from fiber rope. Feet wide, tough-soled, often bare unless the stone is newly hot or the mineral crust sharp. Their skin is rarely dry enough to flake; it shines faintly, oiled with plant fat, smoke resin, and the city’s own constant moisture. Scars heal pale and soft-edged here. Many carry old rope-burns around wrists and palms, crescent cuts from shell tools, puckered marks from leeches or mineral blisters.

They dress in layers meant less to keep water out than to decide how water may pass through. Woven bark cloth, reed fiber, softened hide, garments slit at the side for wading, short cloaks waxed with leaf oils, belts hung with small tools sealed in gourd cases. Nothing long enough to drag unnoticed beneath the surface. Nothing precious worn low. Names, charms, house tokens, and work rights are carried high: at the throat, tied into hair, stitched to shoulder cloth, carved into thin plaques worn between the collarbones.

Their hair is practical and expressive. Some shave it close along the sides to keep away rot and parasites; others wear it in heavy coils lacquered with dark oil, threaded with beadwork, copper rings, fish vertebrae, or little polished stones that indicate lineage, profession, or pilgrimage. Children’s hair is often tied with bright plant fiber so they can be seen in dim water. Divers bind theirs tight under caps of woven reed. Elders sometimes let theirs grow white and loose, a sign that others now watch the currents for them.

Their faces tend toward stillness. This is not coldness. It is economy. In a city where sound is swallowed and sight is unreliable, expression has migrated into gesture, timing, proximity, and touch. A greeting may be two fingers laid briefly against another’s wet sleeve. Agreement may be a knot retied in public. Grief may be shown by leaving one’s door-cords unbraided for nine days so they trail into the flood. Anger is quiet and dangerous, marked by exact speech and the refusal to share a rope.

They speak softly, but their language is not sparse. It is dense with water distinctions.

They have different words for rain that falls, rain that hangs, rain that returns upward as steam, water that has slept in stone, water newly arrived from cloud, water that remembers heat, water that has passed through roots, floodwater safe for children, floodwater that carries sickness, floodwater that hides a drop beneath a smooth surface. Their directional terms include depth, warmth, current, and nearness to known handholds. They do not say simply “north” or “down the street” if precision matters. They say things like: toward the cold leaning water, above the second rope, under the moss bridge, where the wall hums, three breaths below the pale lintel.

Their children learn depth before distance. This is almost literal. A child may not know how far away a relative’s house is in any dry sense, but knows that in high flood it is chin-deep for an adult, that the third rung is missing, that the lower doorway can be found by following the left-hand cord until it changes from smooth to braided. Games are played in vertical maps. Children dive for stones in flooded courtyards, race floating seedpods through alleys, learn to identify houses by the taste of mineral seep on the wall—bitter, warm, chalk-soft, metallic, root-sweet.

They are wary of straight lines. A straight wall is unfinished, suspect, too arrogant before the mountain’s slow intentions. Their tools and houses prefer flexion: lashing, hinge, loop, cradle, socket, raft, woven give. This habit enters their manners. They rarely refuse directly. They bend a decision around obstacles, wait for pressure to change, let a proposal soak until its weak joints show. Outsiders sometimes mistake this for passivity. It is not. They have survived because they can yield without surrendering.

Their houses are relatives.

A household is not only the people who sleep under one roof, but also the wet rooms, the raft garden, the inherited root-lines, the door-cords, the tied boats, the fish pen, the cistern-tree, the shrine niche, the known leaks, the wall-moss, the warm crack under the cooking stones. A family may introduce itself by naming the structure it tends: “We are of the House That Rises at the Back,” or “We keep the Third Root of the Soft Gate,” or “We sleep above the leaning basalt and feed the lower eels.” Houses have temperaments. Some are generous with warmth. Some grow mold no matter how they are smoked. Some listen well. Some are restless and must be re-lashed every season.

Their architecture has taught them kinship as maintenance. To belong is to repair.

No one owns a wall absolutely if its failure would drown another family’s lamps. No one owns a canal route if their raft garden blocks the night current. Children are fostered easily between levels and related houses because labor must match flood mood. A person’s closest lifelong companion may not be a sibling or spouse but a rope-partner—the one who first tied them during deep work, dove with them, hauled them from a silted stair, or shared breath through a reed tube in a blind passage.

They do marry, but marriage is less a merging of bodies than a joining of tolerances: whose house can accept whose flood schedule, whose roof can bear which garden, whose ancestors approve the movement of a root-line, whose boats can be tied together without tangling in night current. Marriage ceremonies are held at uncertain thresholds—half-water platforms, floating courtyards, steps that vanish and return. The couple ties and unties the same knot repeatedly while elders pour warm and cold water over their joined hands, testing whether they can adjust without letting go.

Their dead are not buried in the usual sense. There is too much water, too much young stone, too little stable earth.

Some dead are given to root gardens. Their bones, after smoke-drying and ceremony, are placed in woven mineral baskets among the pale feeding roots beneath a family house. Some become foundation dead, set within high walls in sealed clay pockets with their names tied above, never below. Divers and ferry people may be weighted and lowered into particular warm shafts, entrusted to water roads beneath the city. Infants are often kept near the living longer, in small roof shrines where rain can touch them but flood cannot take them. Ancestors are imagined not as remote shades but as damp presences in structure: the strength of a lashing, the sweetness of a leaf crop, the warning creak before a span gives way.

They make shrines wherever the world behaves strangely, but they do not seem surprised by strangeness. A courtyard where water stands at an angle is not a miracle in the sense of spectacle; it is a civic fact requiring respect. Weighted strings, carved bowls, offerings of oil, shell, fungus bread, and finger-shaped roots. Priests there are more like caretakers of anomaly. They measure, observe, recite old slippages, note whether the slant has increased by a bead’s width. Their holiness is a form of attention.

Their spiritual life is hydrological, geological, domestic.

They do not worship a single sea, though they speak to waters as if waters have memory. They do not worship the mountain as a fixed god, because the mountain is plainly not fixed. It is young, warm, moody, still arriving. They address it like an enormous unfinished ancestor: dangerous, formative, not yet wise. The deepest reverence is reserved for accommodation—the graceful yielding of house, body, plant, and law to forces that cannot be commanded but can be listened to.

Elders sit in the Listening Rooms with their ears to wet stone. They are not mystics only, nor engineers only, nor judges only. They are all of these because the city has not separated those skills. Before a new span is built, before a district is abandoned, before a forbidden foundation is reopened, they listen to the mountain’s knocks and ticks. A good listener knows which sounds are old cooling, which are water forcing a new path, which are root-splitting, which are the long complaint before a terrace shifts. Decisions are said to be “heard,” not made.

Their law is practical and ceremonial at once.

The gravest crimes involve endangering shared structures: cutting a public rope without cause, hiding rot in a communal beam, poisoning a cistern, blocking a night current, moving another house’s depth-markers, falsely tying a child’s name above the wrong doorway. Theft matters, but negligence can be worse. To steal a bronze hook is shameful. To let a ladder loosen in a flood path is nearly murder.

Punishments often involve exposure to maintenance. A careless person may be assigned to scrape algae from public steps for an entire seepage cycle, or to sit night watch over a weak wall, or to carry depth messages through cold rain until they learn the consequences of inaccurate speech. Exile exists, but it is rare and terrible. To be sent away from known ropes and named water is close to death.

They have markets, though their markets sound like murmurs.

Trade happens on floating platforms and half-submerged plazas that change function with depth. Goods are tied, not displayed loosely. Bundles hang from overhead poles: smoked leaves, reed cord, resin cakes, dried fish, fungus sheets, medicinal bark, polished shell fragments, carved house tags, oil bladders, salt wrapped in wax leaves, clay tablets sealed against damp. Buyers touch more than they look. A good rope is tested by ear as much as hand. A jar is judged by the ring it gives when tapped above and below the waterline.

They are superb makers of fiber.

Rope is their mathematics, their architecture, their memory aid, their lock, their signature. Different knots record debts, warnings, invitations, mourning, measurements, tidal histories, construction dates, depth rights. A skilled knot-reader can pass through a district and know which families are away, which walls are under strain, where a child was born, which raft garden has been pledged in marriage, which canal is forbidden at night. Some old treaties between districts are not written but preserved as complex ceremonial cords kept in oil and unrolled only during disputes.

Their writing, where it exists, avoids surfaces that drown badly. Clay tablets are small and fired hard, often impressed rather than inked. Shell slips are carved with notches. House histories are encoded in bead sequences and knot strings. Murals continue beneath the waterline, but their submerged portions are made for hands: raised ridges, grooves, embedded stones. A story may be read by walking a wall with fingertips while waist-deep in flood.

Their art loves reflection, repetition, partial visibility.

Masks are made to be seen through rain: broad planes, pale eyes, dark mouths, dangling fibers that drip in patterns. Songs are built for absorption; they use low hums, clicking stones, palm slaps on wet wood, calls timed to echo from water courts. Many songs cannot be heard fully from one place. They require listeners on different levels—one in an upper room, one in the canal, one below a bridge—so that the whole composition exists only as shared report afterward.

Dance is careful, grounded, often performed on rafts. The dancers shift weight until the platform tilts but does not overturn. The beauty lies in collective correction. In festival dances, children run along overhead vine bridges dropping flower cups into the water while adults below guide the floating blossoms into spirals with poles. The pattern lasts moments before rain breaks it apart, which is part of the point.

Their festivals are tied not to sun seasons but to water behaviors.

There is a festival for the first warm seep of a cycle, when mineral films thicken and the algae along canal edges begin to glow. Lamps are extinguished so people can walk by faint living outlines. There is a festival when the roof tubers are lifted, a festival when fish enter the reed mazes, a festival of Re-Tying after the largest floods, when every household checks and publicly renews its essential knots. There is a quiet observance for the rare appearance of birds. Work stops; people listen to the misplaced call until it is gone.

Food is soft, smoked, mineral, green.

They eat fish, eel, water fern, roof-grown tubers, thick leaves steamed in clay pots, root fingers sliced and dried, fungus cakes, sour algae pastes, floating seeds, lizard eggs when found, and rare traded salt with almost devotional appreciation. Cooking fires are protected in raised hearths, fed by resinous wood and dried fiber. Smoke rooms are precious because drying is difficult; the smell of controlled smoke means wealth, foresight, and care. Fermentation is common. Many foods are wrapped, sunk, warmed by geothermal seep, then retrieved days later changed into something sour, tender, and sustaining.

They drink carefully. Not all water is water for drinking. Children know the signs: oily sheen, bitter vapor, eel-sleep smell, root-filter sweetness, mineral tongue-numbness. Households maintain filter jars of sand, charcoal, moss, and shell grit. Certain cisterns are guarded more fiercely than shrines. A person who can locate sweet seep after a terrace shifts is celebrated like a hero.

There are specialists.

Divers are the most obvious, though not numerous. They are compact, calm people trained from childhood to tolerate darkness, pressure, and the panic of unseen touch. Their equipment is simple: weighted belts, reed breathing tubes, guide cords, bone knives, sealed pouches, sometimes goggles made from polished horn. They enter submerged rooms and fissures no one else will enter. They retrieve tools, repair lower lashings, inspect foundations, bring back strange things from the deep city and deeper mountain. They are respected, pitied, and watched. Too many dives changes a person, people say. Divers begin to pause too long before answering, as if listening to a question asked underwater.

There are root-keepers, mostly but not always older women, who tend the downward gardens. They know which pale roots may be cut and which must be coaxed, which currents feed bitterness, which mineral pulses fatten the edible leaves. Their arms are often stained to the elbow from reaching through trapdoors. They speak of root-lines as lineages with moods, illnesses, appetites, grudges.

There are wall-menders, who work with moss, mortar, plaster, and patient pressure. There are ferry-callers who remember safe passages in fog. There are lamp-shield makers, fungus doctors, eel-trappers, clay singers who judge firing heat by tone, basalt cutters, ladderwrights, knot-lawyers, depth messengers, and children whose temporary job is simply to watch floating markers and shout if one turns.

Bronze is rare enough to be political. A bronze blade, hook, mirror, or chisel may belong to a district rather than a person. It is signed out ceremonially and returned under witness. The metal is kept dry when possible, oiled, wrapped, spoken for. A household possessing private bronze is either very old, very useful, or faintly suspect.

They do not think of themselves as primitive. Nor should anyone else.

They have no engines because engines, in this place, would be almost comic: too rigid, too hungry, too dry-minded. Their sophistication lies elsewhere—in adaptive building, hydrological memory, botanical engineering, acoustic diagnosis, social redundancy, material humility. They can tell from the way a floating garden tugs at its rope that a lower passage has opened. They can build a house that survives a century of tilt. They can cultivate edible leaves from roots hanging in mineral water through a trapdoor in the floor. They can navigate a city that changes shape in darkness.

Their humor is quiet and damp. They joke about drylanders who trust floors. They mock young lovers who tie dramatic knots that cannot be untied in rain. They tell stories of foolish ancestors who tried to build straight roads, store grain below floodline, or argue with steam. Much laughter is suppressed into the shoulder or sleeve, not because joy is forbidden but because sound travels strangely, and loudness indoors is considered clumsy.

They are hospitable, but hospitality has rules.

A guest is first given a place to tie their belongings high. Then a cup of known water. Then a dry-ish cloth, never truly dry, but warmed. Only after that are questions asked. A host may touch a guest’s wrist before leading them across a walkway—not intimacy, but responsibility. To shelter someone during a flood mood is to take partial ownership of their survival. Guests who refuse guidance are not admired for courage. They are treated like children or fools.

They fear certain things deeply.

Dry fire loose in a roof district. Sudden cold seep from below. Silent water where there should be ticking. A child missing at dusk. Door-cords cut. Fungal bloom in sleeping mats. A familiar wall that no longer sounds right when struck. Fog that tastes of metal. The long muscular shadow that comes too far into the inhabited canals. An old well returning an offering that was never dropped into it.

They do not fear rain. Rain is circumstance. Rain is grammar. Rain is the medium through which the world speaks.

Their myths begin, often, not with creation but with emergence. Stone rising warm through water. Ancestors climbing from drowned lower rooms. The first rope thrown between two roofs. The first tree allowed to break a house and become part of it. They tell of a time when the mountain was flatter and lower, or when the water stood elsewhere, but such stories are not nostalgia. They do not yearn for stable land. Stable land, to them, sounds barren and somewhat dead.

They are uneasy under open sky when traveling beyond the drowned highlands. Too much distance makes them feel exposed. Dry silence unnerves them. A road that remains itself from morning to evening seems unnatural. They sleep poorly without dripping. They may hang cords around a guest bed simply to have something to touch in the dark.

The neighboring ridge settlements are kin and rivals. Each cluster has its own water customs, plant strains, depth measures, and dialect of knots. Some are known for warm pools, some for black floating pebbles, some for excellent fungus, some for singers, some for dangerous foundations. Travel between them is slow and ceremonial, full of waiting. News arrives swollen, altered, softened by retelling. A birth may be known two ridges away only after the child has already learned to swim. A collapse may become a cautionary song before anyone has seen the fallen wall.

Their politics are distributed vertically.

Upper households control roof gardens, signal lamps, and rope bridges. Mid-level families manage markets, workshops, and public platforms. Lower dwellers—fishers, divers, foundation tenders—know the hidden state of the city. No group can rule alone. The upper people need the lower people’s warnings. The lower people need upper food and lamps. The middle people need everyone’s passage. Councils are held on platforms with water visible through gaps in the floor, a reminder that all arguments rest on something moving.

They count time by seepage, repair, growth, and remembered floods. A person may say, “I was born before the New Lip hardened,” or “She died in the year the western cords were doubled,” or “That debt is from three algae-glows ago.” Children are measured against door marks that may themselves be moved upward when the house shifts, so height and history are both negotiated.

Some among them are marked by the city’s oddities.

Those born near angled water sometimes become shrine attendants, said to possess a sense for slant in things other than water: lies, weak beams, unbalanced marriages. Children who survive accidental immersion in warm shafts may be dedicated to listening orders. A diver who returns with an object from impossible depth is treated thereafter as partially claimed by below. Not shunned, exactly. But spoken to carefully.

There are stories of people who have gone down and chosen not to return, or returned speaking of corridors beneath the mountain where air bells hang in black stone, where old doors swell shut, where shell fragments gather from a sea older or stranger than the city. Such tales are not told loudly at night. Not because no one believes them, but because belief is not the issue. The issue is whether telling invites attention.

The city’s people understand that they live in a place still deciding what it is. This has made them neither frantic nor fatalistic. Their deepest cultural virtue is readiness without haste.

They inspect. They bind. They listen. They move the lamp higher. They teach the child the new depth. They plant the crack. They name the tilt. They let a fallen tower become a fish pool. They argue for three days over whether a wall should be saved, then spend thirty years helping it become an embankment. They do not expect permanence, but they expect continuity. There is a difference, and their whole civilization lives inside it.

At dusk they become most themselves.

Lamps bloom behind horn screens. The canal water turns black-green and full of trembling amber ladders. Rope bridges are lifted. Door-cords are checked by touch. Children are called not by shouting but by a sequence of clicks, bells, and names spoken into cupped hands. Root trapdoors are closed. Nets are drawn in. Someone on a high platform strikes a clay bell once for the ferry, once for fog, twice for a missing pole returned, three times for an elder’s death.

Then voices lower further. Families gather in stacked rooms: some above water, some with feet in it, some sleeping in hammocks slung from beams that have bent but not failed. Elders tell stories while mending cord. A child traces the raised mural of an ancestor’s journey below the waterline. A diver sits apart, oiling a reed tube. A root-keeper tastes a leaf and announces that the deep minerals have changed.

All night the rain continues.

The people sleep lightly, not from fear, but from participation. The city shifts in small ways around them: a raft knocks once against its post, a warm bubble breaks in a lower room, moss releases a drop from a beam, water enters some new hairline path in the young mountain. Somewhere an elder wakes, listens, and decides it is not yet time to rise.

They are the inhabitants of a drowned height, a culture of rope, root, stone, and listening.

They do not conquer their city. They tend its agreements.
