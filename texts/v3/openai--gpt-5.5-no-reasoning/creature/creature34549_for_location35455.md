What lives there are not inhabitants in the usual sense, because the place does not admit a clean separation between dwelling and dweller.

The drowned plateau is lived in by continuities.

Some of them have bodies. Some of them are bodies only at certain scales. Some move through the architecture as animals. Some are the architecture, or pass so slowly through it that they resemble geology. To live there is not to occupy a home, but to be a function of the plateau’s cold persistence: grazer, bore, seal, root, listener, anchor, trap, courier, wound, scar.

The greatest resident is perhaps the plateau itself.

Not as a mind, not as an animal intelligence hidden under root and bark, but as a distributed vegetal persistence whose life is too broad and slow to be mistaken for behavior. Whole terraces belong to single vascular systems. Towers that seem dead in their outer darkness retain deep internal wetted tissues, pale as marrow, drawing infinitesimal gradients through themselves: salt against fresh, pressure against less pressure, decay against need. A bridge may be alive only along one side. A plaza may breathe through pores no larger than sand grains. A hollow stalk may have no living crown, no leaf, no flower, yet continue to maintain a column of transport inside itself, moving dissolved nourishment upward into blackness at the pace of a thought forgotten before it forms.

These plant-bodies are not individuals in any familiar way. They fuse, graft, infiltrate, inherit one another’s dead frameworks. A root grown through an older trunk may take over its channels; a fern-dome may be fed by vines that arrived centuries after its fronds hardened into ribs. The living tissue is patchy, old, and communal. It occupies the dead as memory occupies language. Where one organism ends, another has long ago sealed the seam.

The plateau’s living plant matter has habits rather than actions.

It thickens along stress. It reinforces vibration. It closes over wounds with translucent black-green skin. It sends pale exploratory roots into chambers rich with animal death, then leaves them there for centuries, each root-tip swollen into a listening bead. It grows around slow currents and teaches the current to hum. It lifts bladder-sacs into the water not to float away but to measure upward pull, to keep tension in the anchored cords below. It turns pressure into architecture.

There are also the small grazers, the true citizens of surfaces.

Blind fish move over the ancient walls in loose, sound-guided companies. They are narrow-bodied, soft-jawed, with mouths set low and wide for shaving algae film from bark. Their skin is the color of diluted milk, except where the minerals of the plateau stain them olive along the belly. They have no need for eyes, but most retain dim pearl spots under the skin, old remnants that catch animal glow without interpreting it. They feed by touch and taste, sweeping their lips over rootwork and leaving clean half-moons behind them. Where a school has passed, the bark appears newly written.

These fish are not hurried even when threatened. They graze in arcs, pause, tilt, and resume. Their bodies communicate by pressure pulses: a shiver of fins, a faint knock of skull against wood, a ripple passed through the company like a sentence reduced to grammar. They know the terrain as texture. A particular pillar is not seen but recognized by its sequence of ridges, the notch where a worm colony breathes, the bitter seam where living plant-skin begins.

Below them work the crabs.

They are jointed things, twig-legged and hooked, almost indistinguishable from broken root until they unfold. Their shells are flattened and dark, grained like old bark. Some grow living films deliberately, encouraging mosslike weeds to cloak them until only their pale mouthparts show. They climb sheer vertical faces by inserting their hooked tips into vascular pores and old worm holes. They prune. They scrape soft growth from seams where too much algae would suffocate the plant-skin beneath. They clip drifting fronds and carry them into cracks. They open blocked channels. They also eat whatever cannot prevent being eaten.

Their lines cross the silt basins and remain visible for years: paired punctures, long drag marks, little constellations of labor. A colony may maintain a tower’s base for generations, not out of loyalty but because the tower feeds them with its leaks. They gather where old stalks glow. They cluster in grooves and splits, picking at the faint exudations that seep from living tissues. When disturbed, they do not flee all at once. They withdraw in sequence, folding backward into the architecture as if the wall is remembering how to be solid.

Inside the woody mass live the borers.

Worms, mostly, though some are not worms at all but elongated crustaceans, blind mollusks without shells, larval things that never mature in the ordinary way. They tunnel through compressed pith and lignified root, chewing what bacteria have softened, widening old plant conduits into galleries. Their bodies are pale, ringed, patient. Some have jaws dark as iron; others rasp with fields of microscopic teeth. They are among the plateau’s chief rearrangers. Over millennia, their feeding hollows out chambers, weakens floors, opens new cisterns, and brings ancient inner tissue into contact with cold water.

They are not merely destroyers. Their tunnels ventilate the living plant. Their castings seal cracks. Their bodies, when they die, mineralize in place and become stiff threads inside the wood, reinforcing what they consumed. The plateau is full of their work: pinholes, lacework, long dark flutes under bark, sudden cathedral vaults that began as rot around a larval bed.

In the open shafts drift transparent shrimp.

They are almost entirely made of angles and nerves, glass-bodied, blue at the joints when disturbed. They hang in vertical columns inside hollow towers and root wells, feeding on suspended spores, pollen dust, microbial snow. Their swarms are loose until sound passes through them. Then they arrange themselves along the vibration, bodies turning together to face a source they cannot see. A distant knock may align thousands of them into a trembling sheet. A predator’s mouth-click scatters them into the cracks.

They are couriers without intent. Spores cling to their limbs. Bacteria ride in their gut. Eggs adhere to their carapaces, hatch, feed, drop. The towers depend on them, though they do not know the towers. The shrimp keep vertical life in circulation, carrying the plateau’s smallest futures from cistern to cistern.

Under the bridges are the snails.

Their shells are broad, dark, and low, pressed nearly flat against the undersides of root-spans. From below they resemble knots, old scars, or sealed-over eyes. They graze inverted, slow even by the plateau’s standards, smoothing films, opening little crescents in the microbial coat. Some bridges are patterned by them so completely that their undersides show generations of passage: overlapping pale trails, healed by algae, then reopened, then healed again.

The snails are archives of chemistry. Their shells record subtle changes no other creature cares to remember: mineral pulses from far above, deaths upstream, flowering events in invisible high water, rare warm intrusions that lasted perhaps a month and altered a single band of growth. Predators eat them seldom. They taste of old tannin and cold stone, though there is no stone here.

The raylike grazers are among the larger moving lives.

They pass through the wide spaces with solemn, winged slowness, their undersides ridged with scraping plates. Their mouths are arranged not for swallowing prey but for planing walls. They come to vertical trunks and settle against them like shadows adhering to a surface. Then they rasp, steadily, making a sound like distant sawing softened by water. Their feeding marks are broad, clean, and strangely architectural. On some ancient pillars, the overlapping strokes of ray-grazers have polished the bark until it gleams dully, hard as glazed ceramic.

They carry gardens on their backs: little forests of threadweed, bead algae, sessile animals, and rootlets that have mistaken the grazer for ground. Old rays become moving terraces. Crabs climb them. Shrimp shelter under their wing margins. Tiny fish follow their feeding wake for disturbed spores. When a ray dies, it settles slowly into a basin and may remain recognizable for centuries, its cartilaginous frame becoming a trellis for upright stems.

There are drifting browsers, soft-bodied and ragged, like torn banners that have learned appetite.

They do not swim so much as adjust their collapse. Their bodies are translucent gray, frilled along the margins, with ciliated lips that extend in ribbons to comb spores from the water. They gather near fern-domes and under living cracks in the old stalks, where fine edible matter leaks steadily into the cold. Some are colonial: a hundred mouths sharing one floating sheet. Others are solitary and vast, trailing tatters that glow faintly when touched.

They are eaten in pieces and seldom die at once. A fish may take a strip. A polyp-ring may close on a lobe. Crabs may prune their margins as they would weed. The browser continues, diminished but not interrupted, sealing its edge and resuming its slow harvest. Its life is a negotiation with incompletion.

The long-backed sleepers inhabit alcoves and root hollows.

They are large, pale animals with seal-like patience but not seal-like warmth. Their limbs are reduced to steering paddles. Their heads are blunt and heavy, whiskered along the jaw with pressure-sensitive filaments. Their eyes remain open in sleep, clouded silver, receiving nothing. They wedge themselves into root niches where the hum is steady and the water carries a dependable flow of scent. There they rest for months.

They feed rarely. A sleeper may rise from its alcove only when chemical tides bring abundance: a fall of spores, a mass hatching of shrimp, the softening of a dead grazer. Then it moves with surprising exactness, not fast, never fast, but without wasted motion. It mouths silt. It crushes crabs. It vacuums larval beds from cracks. Then it returns, choosing either its old alcove or a new one whose hum better suits the shape of its bones.

Older sleepers become landmarks. Their backs grow ridges of algae and small rooting plants. Their skin toughens into something almost barklike along the spine. Small creatures live on them. Some root-hollows are shaped by generations of their sleeping bodies, worn smooth where flanks pressed against wood for centuries.

Predators live there as patience given a mouth.

The dark pylon fish is nearly never seen. It waits in the openings of hollow columns, its body long and vertical, head downward or outward depending on the shaft. Its skin absorbs what little light exists. Its whiskers spread across the entrance like fine dead roots. A shrimp swarm may pass through them without consequence; the fish saves its strength. A crab brushes too heavily. A blind grazer fish enters with the wrong vibration. The mouth opens, not in a lunge but in a sudden absence, and the water rearranges itself around swallowing.

Some pylon fish grow so old that sessile organisms colonize their flanks. Their camouflage becomes literal incorporation. Rootlets may attach to their outer skin; algae may film their jaws. They must twitch, occasionally, to keep from becoming sealed into the structure that shelters them. When one dies unnoticed, its alcove becomes rich. Worms enter first. Then roots. Eventually the hollow narrows, shaped by the fish’s former outline.

The polyp-rings are more place than animal.

They line certain arches, shafts, and chamber mouths: fleshy circlets embedded in bark, their tentacles retracted most of the time into grooves. They feed rarely, and a single closure may sustain one for a season or longer. When open, they appear as decorative molding, pale and ribbed. When triggered, the entire ring contracts with a slow, terrible certainty, turning a passage into a throat.

Some arches bear old polyp-rings that have grown into alliance with the plant structure. The plant provides anchorage and faint chemical seepage; the polyp’s waste feeds rootlets around the rim. Together they make living thresholds. Many animals know these arches and avoid the central opening. Others use them anyway, trusting size, luck, or the ring’s long intervals of indifference.

In the silt lie the jawed larvae.

They are enormous in relation to their supposed youth, and perhaps they are larvae only because no one has seen what they become. They bury themselves beneath powdered leaf and dark pollen with only the mouth exposed: a narrow crack, a crescent, a harmless-looking seam in the floor. Around them the silt may be perfectly smooth except for faint radiating lines where inhaled water has drawn dust toward the opening.

They can wait almost without metabolism. They grow from what falls in. A shrimp cloud descending too low. A soft browser trailing edible lace. A weakened crab crossing open basin. The crack opens; the silt erupts only locally; then calm returns. Some basins contain larvae so old that upright stems have rooted in the silt atop their backs. The larvae tolerate this, and the plants profit from what the buried mouth fails to digest.

Lantern-jellies inhabit the chambers below.

They cling to ceilings and upper ribs in clusters that resemble pendant organs or submerged fruit. Their light is not a flicker but a maintained condition: blue-white, greenish, sometimes bruised violet at the rim. They open and close with such slowness that a watcher might need hours to notice the change. Their glow draws small planktonic life into chamber vaults; their trailing filaments sort the water; their waste falls gently onto root beads below.

In some chambers, lantern-jellies are the only visible sky. Animals gather beneath them in dim congregations. Blind fish graze the columns. Sleepers rest along the walls. Worms pulse inside the wood. Root-tips hang down in pale beads, absorbing the chamber’s long digestion. The jellies provide not illumination exactly, but permission for forms to appear.

There are also the seed-drifters.

Seeds here do not behave like seeds of air or soil. They are armored, buoyant or counter-buoyant according to need, sometimes winged with gelatinous vanes, sometimes bristled for catching in root lattice. Many drift for centuries before lodging. Some are no larger than grains of ash. Others are fist-sized pods with translucent seams and sleeping embryos inside, their tissues waiting for a precise cold, a particular pressure, a chemical signal released by a collapsing structure.

The animals move them constantly. Shrimp foul them. Crabs wedge them into cracks. Grazers scrape them loose. Sleepers swallow them and pass them intact years later in another district of the plateau. Some seeds germinate on living animals and must be shed or endured. A ray may carry a young bladder-plant on its back until the plant’s tether finds an arch and anchors; then, over decades, the tether tightens, and the ray must tear free or become a foundation.

Microbial life is everywhere, though invisible except by consequence.

It softens old bark for worms. It seals wounds in slick films. It ferments trapped chambers where bubbles cough under pressure. It converts death into gradients the plants can drink. It inhabits the silt, the shells, the gut of every grazer, the pale beads of roots, the stagnant pockets inside towers. Without it, the plateau would remain but not circulate. With it, nothing is wasted, only delayed.

There are no masters.

No species rules the drowned plateau. Dominance would require speed, conflict, expansion, and those are poor currencies here. The closest thing to power is duration. A worm lineage that has maintained one wall’s breathability for ten thousand years is powerful. A plant conduit that still draws nourishment through a buried district is powerful. A polyp-ring that has shaped the traffic of an arch for centuries is powerful. A crab colony that prunes a glowing stalk generation after generation is powerful. But none commands. Each persists by fitting into the pressure of everything else.

If there is intelligence, it is mostly tactile and distributed.

The fish know ridges. The crabs know seams. The roots know chemistry. The sleepers know resonance. The shrimp know vibration. The pylon predators know interruption. The plants know stress, moisture, pressure, mineral, wound. Together they form no council, no consciousness, but an immense mutual legibility. Every resident reads the plateau and is read by it. Tracks remain for years; feeding scars become maps; tunnels become organs; resting places become alcoves; death becomes architecture.

What lives there, then, is an ecology that has forgotten the difference between animal behavior and masonry.

It is populated by pale grazers, bark-colored crabs, transparent shrimp, flattened snails, raylike wall-scrapers, drifting ciliated banners, long-backed sleepers, hidden fish, patient polyp-rings, buried mouths, ceiling jellies, worms inside the wood, seeds in the shafts, microbes in every seam, and plants so old and broad they are mistaken for terrain.

But more than any one of these, the drowned plateau is inhabited by slowness.

Slowness lives there as a condition, as an organ, as law. It has entered the bones of the animals and the grain of the wood. It has made predators into doorways, plants into continents, ruins into bodies, and bodies into future walls. Nothing there is idle. Nothing is empty. Every surface is being used, but at the tempo of pressure and cold.

The residents of that place do not make history in events. They make it in layers.

A scrape.
A tunnel.
A root-skin sealing.
A shell flattening against bark.
A seed waiting.
A stalk glowing faintly in the dark.
A corpse becoming scaffold.
A bridge still alive along one edge.

They live as if the world has already ended and proved habitable.
