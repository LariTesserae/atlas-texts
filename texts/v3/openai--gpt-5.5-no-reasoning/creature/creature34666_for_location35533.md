What lives there are the keepers of placement.

They are not entirely people, though from a distance they may be mistaken for people working in a wet field: low figures moving along the causeways in the white light, carrying baskets against one hip, pausing to touch the lip of a basin, bending to set a frog back into its proper niche. They are the size of small adults or large children, with narrow shoulders, long arms, and feet made for tiles slick with algae. Their movements are careful but not slow. They never hurry because hurry is a kind of noise.

Their bodies look as handmade as the country.

Skin, if it is skin, has the matte pale-brown and grey-green tone of unfired clay kept damp under shade. In some lights they seem plastered; in others vegetal. The surface of them is faintly ridged, as though smoothed by fingers before hardening. At the joints — wrist, throat, knee, jaw — there are darker seams like wet reed fiber. Moss gathers in the hollows of older ones. Young ones have clean sharp contours, crisp as new brick.

Their heads are broad at the back and narrow toward the mouth. Their faces are not blank, but they do not arrange themselves into expression the way a human face does. They have large, still eyes, dark and reflective, set wide apart, with membranes that pass sideways across them in the heat. Their mouths are small vertical openings, used rarely. Some have nostril slits high on the face. Some have no visible nose, only damp pores along the cheeks and throat. Their ears are little folded shelves of cartilage or clay-like tissue, often pierced with reed splinters, shell discs, or tiny fired tokens.

Their hands vary.

Most have six fingers, long and spatulate, excellent for pressing plaster, parting roots, tying wet cord, lifting eggs, and smoothing the skin of water. Others have three thick fingers, or a thumb opposed by a fan of narrow feelers. Some hands are nearly webbed. Some are palm-less, a ring of digits set around the wrist like the marks stamped into the embankments. This is not deformity among them. It is craft lineage. A hand is not merely inherited; it is suited, assigned, completed for a task.

They do not wear much, because the heat has already clothed the world. Their garments are short wraps of beaten bark, reed fiber, or thin translucent membrane dried from some basin-dwelling creature. Workers near cisterns wear aprons stiffened with lime. Those who tend roots wind cords around their forearms, hung with bone awls and shell scoops. The ones who approach the square lake wear nothing except belts of clay cups, each cup containing a seed, a fingernail of tile, a live beetle, a bead of water, or a little folded shadow.

They speak seldom.

When they do, it is not speech meant to travel far. It is a damp clicking in the mouth, a soft knock of tongue against palate, a release of breath through throat pores. Much of their language is placement: three fingers laid on a parapet; a cord tied twice instead of once; a frog moved from left side to right; a cup turned to face the sun below instead of the sun above. They read arrangements the way others read writing. A row of shells on a post may tell them that a channel is full, a sleeping chamber is occupied, a distance has lengthened, or a reflection must not be stepped into until evening.

They are not silent because they are afraid. They are silent because sound spends water.

That is one of their oldest laws, or perhaps one of the laws they built into themselves. Loudness loosens things. Shouting causes plaster to craze. Laughter, if uncontained, sends insects off their stations. Drums were tried once — the carvings remember this — and afterward a whole district of causeways led only back to the same door. So they conserve sound as they conserve shade. They keep it in cups, in reed flutes stopped with wax, in hollow posts, in throats that open only during necessary rites.

They live in the low houses, but not all at once and not always in the ordinary direction.

A house may contain three sleeping mats, twelve bowls, and one sleeper visible in the shade. Another sleeper may be beneath the floor, nested under the basin where roots pass through their dreams. A third may be in the reflection of the doorway rather than the doorway itself. They do not regard this as strange. A dwelling, to them, is not an enclosure but a correct overlap of body, water, root, temperature, and shadow. If those conditions meet, someone is home.

Their children are raised in jars.

Not born there, exactly. The beginning of them is less private and less singular. A pair, or a group, or a whole work-lineage prepares a soft bed of clay, seed paste, frog eggs, hair, powdered shell, and water taken from three basins that do not reflect the same sky. Into this they press small tokens: finger bones of ancestors, curled root tips, a tile chip from the central lake, sometimes a sliver of a tool that has done good work. The mixture is sealed inside a tall ceramic jar and kept in shade.

For some time the jar sweats.

Then it begins to answer touch.

The young emerge pale, unfinished, and heavy-headed, with hands still soft enough to be guided into their future shape. They are not named immediately. First they are carried around the basin and shown where things belong: reed in socket, fish in channel, bird on post, rot in chamber, sun in sky, second sun in water, unmade brightness beyond the edge. Only after they have reached toward one place more than the others do they receive a name.

Their names are more like duties than labels.

Cup-Facing-East.

She-Who-Smooths-Leaks.

Third-Hand-for-Root-Mending.

Quiet-Under-Bridges.

The-One-Who-Counts-Frogs-Without-Touching.

Names can change when the hand changes, or when a person returns from a distance that took too long.

They eat what the marsh permits after invitation. Split fruit from dwarf trees. Seed paste. White fungal loaves grown in sealed chambers of waste. Fish that swim willingly into traps at appointed hours. Snails loosened from tile seams with bone picks. Lotus roots taken only from basins carved with permission marks. They do not cook often; fire is a harsh guest in a wet world. When they use it, it is small, cupped, shielded by clay screens, fed with dried reed pith and watched by several keepers until every coal is accounted for.

Their dead do not rot in the open.

Nothing here is allowed to decay casually. A body that has finished moving is washed in cistern water and painted with thin lime. The hands are pressed into wet plaster to renew the embankment records. Then the body is placed below a planted bed, not as burial exactly, but as infrastructure. Roots are threaded through prepared openings in the chest, belly, mouth, and palms. White fungi enter first. Then reeds. Then a fruiting vine, if the person was beloved or technically useful. In time, the dead become a platform, a trellis, a coolness under leaves.

Some of the oldest citizens are orchards.

They remember through roots. The living consult them by pressing their foreheads to the ceramic collars and letting water climb the hairline. Advice comes as an urge to adjust a sluice, move a jar, sleep in another room, or avoid a reflection showing black trees.

There are castes, though not rigid ones. Better to call them placements.

The Water-Holders maintain levels that never visibly change. They sit beside gauges for hours, touching cords and weights without seeming to move them. Their eyes are clouded from staring at brightness. They can taste imbalance in the air before a basin overfills.

The Root-Inviters tend vegetation. They do not plant by digging, since there is no earth to violate. They prepare sockets, collars, rafts, cups, pockets, and trays, then persuade growth into them with moisture, shade, mineral dust, and patient contact. A wandering vine is not punished. It is redirected until it believes the trellis was its own intention.

The Distance-Minders walk paths that have become too long or too short. They carry measuring cords knotted with shells, but the cords are symbolic; ordinary measure fails here. They walk a causeway while remembering its correct length, humming almost below hearing. Sometimes they return in a day. Sometimes they return with algae grown over their shoulders and do not admit they were gone.

The Reflection-Keepers tend the basins that show other places, other times, or the underside of the world. They polish the water with flat paddles. They remove leaves before a reflected star can catch on them. They know which mirrored groves are harmless and which try to root upward into the noon.

The Quiet-Counters monitor sound. They live near bridges and hollow posts. Their throats are bound with soft fiber. If a district has accumulated too much noise — too many falling drops, too much insect rasp, too many footfalls on dry tile — they redistribute it. They may hang wet cloth under eaves, move frogs to deeper shade, or open a jar where old silence has been stored.

And at the central square lake are the Cup-Bearers.

They are few. They move with the greatest care. Their task is not gardening but negotiation. Each plant growing in a clay cup above that white-tiled water belongs partly to this world and partly to some other condition: fern from yesterday’s rain, grass from an unwritten field, orchid from a night that has not arrived, mushroom from a body not yet dead, succulent from dryness that cannot otherwise enter the marsh.

The roots hang into air and vanish because the soil they enter is elsewhere.

The Cup-Bearers keep those elsewheres from becoming too hungry.

They wade the knee-deep lake in fixed patterns. Their ripples stop in perfect circles around them. They prune roots that are not visible. They turn cups by fractions. They lower their faces to the water and look not at their reflections but at the sun underneath. Sometimes one of them reaches into the water and removes a handful of dry sand, a black leaf, a small bone still warm, or a word shaped like a seed. These are catalogued in jars along the rim.

Their religion, if it is religion, is maintenance.

They do not worship creation as an event long past. They believe creation is a condition that must be held open by correct work. The world outside may have histories, seasons, accidents, storms, weeds, erosion, hunger, war. This basin has none of those unless invited, shaped, and given a vessel. To them, rawness is not purity. Wildness is not freedom. The unmade is not sacred. The sacred thing is fit: water fitting basin, root fitting socket, body fitting task, death fitting chamber, impossibility fitting rule.

Their myths say there was once open marsh here, but they describe it with pity rather than nostalgia. Mud swallowing feet. Seeds falling anywhere. Birds screaming without count. Water rising because it had forgotten its edges. Trees growing from accident. Rot taking whatever it touched. Space lying about its size. The ancestors, or makers, or first keepers, answered by laying down surfaces. Tile by tile, reed by reed, handprint by handprint, they taught the marsh to hold still enough to become generous.

But the carvings suggest another possibility: that the marsh itself made them.

Perhaps the first basins condensed bodies out of heat and mineral. Perhaps the frogs sat in doorways before there were people. Perhaps hands were invented because plaster needed smoothing. The keepers do not argue this. Origin matters less than continuation. Whether they made the basin or the basin made them, the obligation is identical.

They are not the only inhabitants.

The animals are citizens too, though of quieter rank. The evenly spaced birds are not tame, exactly; they are markers of aerial balance. If one leaves its post, a path may shorten dangerously. Frogs are threshold witnesses. Every doorway wants at least one frog near it, preferably facing inward during noon and outward during the brief green dusk that sometimes occurs in reflections but not in the sky. Dragonflies pin warm air in place. Fish trace the legal routes of water, turning where channels remember corners. Mollusks seal seams with their slow bodies. Lizards absorb surplus glare.

Even insects have assignments. Even algae has permitted thickness.

The keepers do not command these creatures as masters. They bargain with them through habitat. They build a place so exact that each animal discovers the desired behavior as comfort.

Yet there is unease in them.

Not panic. Panic would be wasteful. But an old tension lies beneath their poise, especially near the unfinished edges where the built marsh thins into blank brightness. The keepers do not look long at the absence beyond the half-made causeway. They stack cane with their backs to it. They cover unbaked tiles. They send no children there. Birds refuse it, and what birds refuse, the keepers respect.

They know the basin is incomplete.

That is the sorrow held inside all its perfection. Everything has been made, except the continuation. The world stands at the exact moment after creation, but creation was interrupted, or deliberately paused, or completed only up to the border of intention. The keepers still prepare materials. Kilns wait full. Cords hang ready. Hands remain suited for work. But no order has come, or the order is too quiet to hear, or the next section of reality has not yet agreed to receive form.

So they maintain.

They wake, if they sleep, in the wet heat before any morning. They drink from cups in which flowers float. They count frogs. They wipe mineral bloom from hinge pins. They trim leaves so shadows fall where roots require. They replace cracked tiles before the crack learns to spread. They arrange meals for those who may enter through the same room from different houses. They prepare fires and do not light them. They open gates that do not move water. They close gates that restrain distances. They keep tools ready because readiness is a form of use.

A visitor might think them absent because they avoid direct encounter. They prefer to be glimpsed in reflections, at the end of a causeway that takes too long to reach, beneath a bridge beside its upside-down twin. If approached loudly, they withdraw into doorways that all lead to the same shaded room. If approached quietly, with wet feet and lowered hands, one may find a bowl set out, a mat unrolled, a frog placed beside the threshold as witness.

They are not hostile.

But they will correct you.

Step on the wrong tile, and later you will find the path behind you rearranged so you may learn the proper one. Speak too loudly, and your next words will fall into a jar instead of the air. Pluck an unoffered fruit, and its tree may appear in your reflection rooted through your chest. Disturb a basin, and the fish will redraw your route until you return to repair the ripple.

If you accept correction, they accept you as a temporary misplaced thing that may yet be placed.

If you resist, they do not attack. They build around you.

A platform where you stand. A shade over your head. A ring for your feet. A cup for your voice. A bed beneath a future vine.

Eventually, in that country, every living thing is given a place.
