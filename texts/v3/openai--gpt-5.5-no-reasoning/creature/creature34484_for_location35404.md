They are not one kind.

They are a population without citizenship, a many-bodied people of water, shell, muscle, silt, cartilage, slime, hunger, eggs, and patience. They live in the drowned town the way weather lives in a valley: moving through it, shaped by it, shaping it back, never separate from its walls and streets. They do not inherit the city as ruins. They inhabit it as a living arrangement.

The smallest residents are nearly invisible until they gather. Bacteria veil the plaster in translucent skins. Fungal threads loosen old wood beam by beam. Algae make dark gardens out of walls that once faced the sun. Plankton pass through the avenues in seasonal clouds, filling the black water with drifting specks of hunger and light. Larvae ride the slow pulse of the basin without knowing what they will become: crab, worm, fish, mollusk, something with a shell, something with a jaw, something that will settle into a crack and spend its life filtering the city through itself.

The walls are alive first.

Every surface has occupants. Pale encrusting animals grow over masonry in knobs and crusts. Shellfish clamp themselves to brick, lintel, threshold, statue, stair. Worms live in fine holes and extend feathered crowns when the water is calm, turning the old rooms into fields of trembling filaments. Sponges swell in sheltered interiors where the current is soft. Slime binds sand to plaster. Coral-like forms, not bright but bone-colored and persistent, take hold where water moves just enough to feed them and not enough to break them.

The buildings have acquired skin.

In the cracks live the quick and secretive ones. Transparent fish, narrow as splinters of glass, occupy chambers where the light comes in as a bruise. They move through broken walls as if the city were built for them. Their bodies show only when they turn, a sudden silver punctuation inside the dark. Shrimp keep stations in masonry fissures, their antennae touching the current, waiting for larger animals to approach and pause. They clean gills, mouths, wounds, and eyes with delicate, busy hands, receiving scraps and safety in exchange.

Crabs rule the stairs.

They climb them sideways, slowly, ceremonially, as if remembering feet. Their shells are pale, chalky, the color of old bone or worn coral. Some are small enough to vanish beneath a fallen tile. Others are broad and armored, with claws polished by labor. They pick through silt, pry at softened wood, harvest films from stone, fight in doorways, molt in cupboards, carry eggs beneath their bodies like rust-colored secrets. At night—or what passes for night below the dim surface—they spread across plazas in loose processions, each one touching the world with its legs before trusting it.

The lobsters and their kin live deeper inside the former interiors. They have made shopfronts, cellars, crypts, and pipe mouths into defensive architecture. They back into darkness, leaving only antennae extended across thresholds. The antennae read the water. They know the difference between drifting weed, passing fish, hunting eel, falling stone, and the soft approach of something that can be seized. Their claws are not loud here. Nothing is loud. A strike happens as a brief disturbance, a cloud, a disappearance.

There are eels in the hidden city beneath the city.

They inhabit wells, drains, culverts, submerged cisterns, and pipe networks too narrow for broad-bodied creatures. Slender, dark, muscular, they move through forgotten human passages with an intimacy no human ever had. They know the underside routes: under kitchens, beneath courtyards, through old sewer cuts, along buried channels where colder water breathes upward from below. Their heads appear from holes without warning. Their mouths open and close, tasting. Their bodies are punctuation marks in the black, then cords, then gone.

Some fish are citizens of the open avenues.

They gather where streets once carried carts and voices. Now those streets carry schools so dense they resemble living smoke or a weather front. Thousands turn at once around columns and broken walls, tightening, loosening, splitting, joining again. The street pulses with them. Their bodies hold the memory of the grid better than maps could. They know which avenues are safe in certain currents, which plazas expose them, which archways funnel food, which towers attract predators.

Other fish are grazers, broad and unhurried. They travel along walls scraping algae from stone, leaving pale, fresh scars behind them. Their mouths are built like tools. Their eyes are calm, not because they are safe but because panic wastes energy. They tend the city without intention, pruning it, cleaning it, converting sun-stain and mineral film into flesh. Where many have passed, a wall is visibly marked: darker above and below, pale in the grazed middle, like a corridor worn by bodies instead of hands.

There are needle-thin hunters in doorways.

They hang almost perfectly still, aligned with the slow movement of water. They are difficult to distinguish from cracks, roots, shadows, lengths of weed. Their patience is architectural. Smaller fish misjudge distance, drift too close to a threshold, and the doorway becomes a mouth within a mouth. The strike is sudden, almost abstract: line, blur, silt, absence.

In the silt live the buried ones.

Flatfish press themselves into the old floor dust and become part of it. Rays lie in plazas, courtyards, under domes, and along market stones, their wide bodies rising and falling with breath. A large ray can cover the remnant pattern of a room like a rug placed by another era. When it lifts away, it leaves an oval depression, a clean print edged with settling particles. Generations have rested in the same places until the city’s floors bear the memory of their bodies more clearly than any inscription.

The rays are among the closest things to elders here, though they do not rule. They return to favored hollows, low domes, warm chambers, silted ceremonial halls. They know the reliable stillnesses. They know where parasites trouble them less, where shrimp will clean them, where the current brings enough food and not too much grit. Their eyes are set for a world of overhead threat and buried meals. Their mouths work against the sediment, drawing out worms, small crustaceans, the living contents of the city’s accumulated dust.

The turtles are older still in manner, if not always in years.

They move with the slow authority of things that do not need to explain themselves. They browse the floating mats near drowned roofs, tear at waxy leaves, rest under eaves that once shed rain from human heads. Their shells carry growths: algae, barnacles, scars, pale patches where something has scraped them clean. When they descend through a street, smaller fish part around them but do not flee far. A turtle is weather with jaws. It passes, eats, breathes, sleeps, rises again toward the bruised lid of the surface.

Sometimes a blunt-headed predator enters.

Then the city changes.

Before it is visible, it is felt: a pressure, a reordering of fear, a tightening in the schools. The avenues empty without appearing to move. Small fish compress into cracks, windows, ceiling pockets, broken brick hollows. Shrimp withdraw. Crabs freeze or flatten themselves against stone. The predator comes through a canal or broad street with no hurry, its body thick, scarred, certain of itself. It may not catch anything. It does not need constant success to transform the district. Its presence reveals the city’s second architecture: every refuge, every escape hole, every blind corner, every shadow inside a shadow.

There are also visitors from above.

Birds touch the ceiling of the world as dark punctures and stabbing beaks. Their feet dimple the surface skin. Their shadows slide over roofs and plazas, distorted by ripples. To the inhabitants below, they are not quite animals and not quite weather. Fish rise toward insects and seeds tangled in floating leaves, then scatter when a beak breaks through. Some birds hunt; some only stand briefly on mats of vegetation or exposed fragments after storms. They leave droppings, feathers, mites, the idea of air.

The drowned trees support their own neighborhoods.

Their bark is gone. Their wood is softened but persistent. Shell clusters grip their trunks. Soft anemone-like bodies open and close on their limbs. Fish use them as stations when crossing exposed plazas. Crabs climb them. Eels coil among their roots where the roots enter mud between paving stones. The trees no longer leaf, but they host. They have changed from plants into vertical agreements.

The inhabitants do not make tools, but they make use of all shapes.

A clay pipe becomes a den.  
A stair becomes a grazing slope.  
A window becomes a cross-current and a trap.  
A bell tower becomes a sleeping chimney.  
A courtyard becomes a nursery.  
A cellar becomes a cold pocket.  
A market square becomes a feeding ground.  
A roof becomes a ceiling beneath which turtles rest.  
A statue becomes a reef with no face.

They know the city by senses other than sight.

Pressure tells them what approaches. Scent tells them who has passed, who is wounded, where eggs are hidden, where decay is fresh. Vibration moves through stone and water together. The scrape of crab legs, the pulse of a school turning, the settling boom of a distant wall, the minute frantic tremor of prey in silt—these are legible. The blind fish beneath the dome live almost entirely inside such messages. Their eyes have become unnecessary ornaments or have vanished into flesh. They hang among columns and feel the room with their skins.

Some are territorial. Some drift. Some return by instinct to sites shaped long before their species used them. The old civic center, the dome, the plazas, the canals, the roofline shallows—all have become animal customs. Nursery fish gather in houses with interior rooms because the broken walls make good protection. Predators patrol boulevards because prey must cross them. Rays settle in circular hollows because others before them pressed the silt there. Eels use drainage because water still prefers drainage. No creature remembers the city’s names, but their behavior preserves its plan.

They are not peaceful.

They eat each other constantly. Eggs are found and swallowed. Larvae vanish by the thousand. A shrimp cleans one mouth and is taken by another. Crabs dismember the weak. Eels raid nests. Needlefish strike from doorways. Predators make the whole city hold its breath. Parasites fasten under fins and along gills. Bacteria enter wounds. The dead are opened, softened, divided, absorbed. Nothing stays itself for long after dying.

Yet the violence is folded into quiet. It has no spectacle. A life ends in a puff of silt, a flash of flank, a small red dilution quickly taken by the basin. The water closes. The avenue fills again. The particles fall.

Their bodies are colored for dimness: gray, green-black, bone-white, sand-brown, translucent, silver only in sudden turns. Brightness would be wasted here. Texture matters more. Smooth skins slide through cracks. Jointed armor resists claws and stone. Flattened bodies disappear into floors. Long bodies enter pipes. Wide bodies rest in plazas. Soft bodies hide behind shells. Shells become stone-like, stones become shell-covered, and after enough time the distinction loses force.

There may be giants only as rumors of displacement: something passing beyond visibility, a whole wall of water nudged aside, fish fleeing before a shape that never fully arrives. The basin allows for such presences, occasional and enormous, entering from the outer reef when currents permit. Most residents experience them not as creatures but as events.

The true dominant inhabitant, though, is the collective one: the city-water-life system itself.

It breathes through reefs and drowned ridges. It inhales ocean pressure and exhales through old canals. It feeds on rain, plankton, rot, sunlight filtered to a green stain, bodies falling apart, bodies being born. Its bones are masonry. Its nerves are currents. Its skin is algae, shell, mud, and bacterial film. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are migrations, spawnings, collapses, blooms.

What lives there is a drowned town that has become an animal made of many animals.

It has crabs for hands, eels for hidden veins, fish schools for weather, rays for slow heartbeats in the plazas, turtles for old moving stones, worms for roots, algae for memory of sun, bacteria for digestion. It has no single face. The statues lost theirs, and the city did not need new ones.

Its inhabitants are those who can accept immersion absolutely.

Those who can breathe the warm dark.  
Those who can read pressure instead of distance.  
Those who can turn hollowness into shelter.  
Those who can lay eggs under lintels and hunt through windows.  
Those who can make a street useful after the end of walking.

They live without mourning the flood. To them, the flood is not catastrophe. It is the condition of form. It is the reason doorways feed, towers sleep, plazas breathe, and darkness is full.
