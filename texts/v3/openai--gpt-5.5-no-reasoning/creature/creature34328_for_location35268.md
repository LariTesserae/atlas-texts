What lives there is not a people, though from far away it may resemble one.

It is more like a civic ecology of stone, dryness, pressure, hunger, and misdirection: a population whose bodies are partly animal, partly weather, partly mineral decision. Nothing here seems to have arrived recently. Every resident has the look of something that has been edited by exposure until only the necessary gesture remains.

The most numerous inhabitants are almost too flat to count as creatures. They live on walls, on the lee sides of ridges, in seams where dust can rest without being taken at once. They appear as tarnishes or stains: blue-gray, iron-red, sulfur-yellow, dull violet, colors that seem less painted than leaked from the stone’s interior. By day they are maps. By dusk they become mouths.

Their bodies are films one cell thick in some places, crystalline mats in others, fitted so closely to the mineral that the boundary between organism and host is undecidable. They do not root. They do not leaf. They open pores too small for sight and expose inner surfaces to the failing light, to charge differentials, to the taste of alkaline air. They feed on gradients: heat leaving stone, salt creeping through cracks, mica flashing weakly under the lid of sky, the electrical shiver before pressure nights. Where a human plant might turn toward the sun, these colonies arrange themselves toward imbalance.

Their growth is slow, but not passive. Over years they draw lines across facades, avoiding certain veins, thickening around others, making districts of color that can only be read across centuries. A wall with a red colony and a wall with a blue-gray colony are not simply differently stained; they are under different administrations. Dust behaves differently near them. The grazing creatures approach them with different cautions. Shadows hesitate on some and lengthen hungrily over others.

At twilight their edges move. Not visibly enough to startle, but enough that the next glance finds a former outline revised. They spread like thought under a closed eyelid. A patch that was an island becomes a peninsula. A hairline stain acquires branching tributaries. A ring opens. A ring closes. Some colonies mimic the mineral diagrams of plazas; others prefigure them, sketching spirals years before erosion exposes the darker stone beneath.

They may be the city’s memory. Or its agriculture. Or the part of it that dreams in color.

Feeding on them are the tall jointed grazers.

They are rare, but in a place so empty every one of them seems important. A single grazer crossing a bridge can become the event of an afternoon. They stand high on narrow legs hinged in too many places, each joint folding not like bone but like a crack reopening along an old weakness. Their limbs end in pads or polished points that touch the ground without claiming it. They leave no prints because they have learned not to insist.

Their bodies are angular, mantled in plates the color of gypsum shadow, salt, old ash, or the underside of a cloud. Sometimes their carapaces carry faint mineral blooms matching the walls they feed from, so that a grazer resting beneath a colonnade may look like a detached ornament, a buttress that has reconsidered its purpose. Beneath the forward shell is a tucked faceted nub, not quite a head. It has no eyes in the expected arrangement, but its surfaces catch changes in light, pressure, echo, and perhaps geometry. When it pauses, it is not listening with ears. It is letting the city describe itself through its legs.

Its mouth is delicate and terrifyingly patient. It unfolds from under the carapace as a cluster of dry fans, rasps, and combs. Against the tarnish-colonies it makes almost no sound. The wall loses a breath-thin layer. The grazer gathers color into itself, separates salts, organics, charges, microscopic crystals, and what may be information. The byproduct is dust: fine, pale, fanned outward in little ceremonial plumes. In this way the grazers help write the avenues. They consume the walls and exhale the streets’ future sediment.

They do not herd. They pass through one another’s territories on timescales too wide for conflict. Two may meet in a plaza, stand facing at oblique angles for several hours, then continue as if the meeting occurred in a language too slow to translate. Their migrations are governed by the wall-colonies, by dust tides, by the opening of impossible intersections. A grazer may vanish into an arch that refuses other bodies and return days later thinner, its plates newly veined, as though it has been grazing in a version of the city older or higher or buried under salt.

Young grazers, if there are young, are nearly indistinguishable from shards of pale stone. They may begin as mineral nodules inside sheltered courtyards, animated only when enough tarnish-colony has filmed over them. They may be born from adults by exfoliation: a plate loosening, falling, standing at last on jointed splinters. They may not be born at all. Perhaps each grazer is an old architectural intention that learned locomotion.

There are also the gliders.

They live in the air as lightly as ash lives in a draft. They are not birds and not insects, though they inherit suggestions from both. Their bodies are translucent, stretched membranes held by branching spars that look grown from glass, chitin, mica, and salt. When folded against stone they nearly disappear, becoming a dry blister on a ledge, a flake of shed sky. When lifted by the cliff updrafts, they open into kites, veils, diagrams.

They do not beat wings. They negotiate pressure. They rise from the abyssal edges, cross over plazas, tilt through avenues where the wind has learned the plan, and vanish into cracks of brightness between one bad distance and another. Passing near, they make the smallest sound in the city: not flight, but tension. Silk on a frame. A thought being pulled taut.

The gliders feed on what the air carries: dust, spores from the wall-colonies, powdered salts, charges shed from vertical darkness, the upward-falling motes released by courtyard filaments. Their membranes are not merely surfaces but nets of selection. What touches them may adhere, dissolve, pass through, or be arranged along branching veins. At dusk, when they descend, their bodies show faint inner constellations of collected material.

They breed, perhaps, by tearing. A damaged membrane catches on a spur of glassy stone; the fragment does not die. If the wind is right, it stiffens along the rip, grows spars from accumulated minerals, and begins to ride small eddies close to the ground. Many perish in hollows where the air goes still. A few learn the larger currents and join the high silent traffic between districts.

Some gliders seem older than others in a way unrelated to size. Their membranes are cloudy with repeated passages through anomalous intersections. These old ones carry views in them. Seen from beneath, they may briefly show a red sun, a salt-buried court, a staircase descending into stars. They have flown through other arrangements of the same place and retained a little of the angle.

In the deepest courtyards live the pale filaments.

They are the city’s most secretive inhabitants. They resemble brittle wool, root hair, frost, old nerves, or mineral smoke that has forgotten how to disperse. They grow in cracks where no moisture gathers. They are nourished by shadow-change, vibration, and the pressure difference between sealed interiors and open air. A passing darkness makes them tremble. A footfall too wet or warm would send them retracting into the stone.

Some release powder that rises. The powder is not seed in the ordinary sense, though it can found new colonies if it reaches a suitable crevice. It is lighter than falling, perhaps because it belongs partly to the lower sky that shines upward through cracks at night. During pressure nights, the powder ascends into courtyards and arranges itself in faint columns. Gliders harvest it. Wall-colonies accept it. Suspended stones collect it on their undersides, where it becomes a silvery fuzz the grazers prize.

The filaments may be the closest thing the city has to nerves. They respond to occlusion, to spatial folding, to echoes arriving before their sounds. In districts where shadows all point toward one courtyard, filaments are thickest at that destination, lining the cracks like thought gathering around a wound. If one watched for decades, one might see them rearrange in patterns preceding changes in the street plan. They know before the city turns.

Then there are the shadows.

Calling them predators is useful only briefly. They do not pursue. They correct.

A shadow in this city is not always cast by a body intercepting light. Some shadows are resident things, thin hungers folded into surfaces, local failures of illumination with appetite and habit. They occupy undersides, alleys, the lee of columns, the interior edge of impossible doorways. Most remain in place for years. Some lengthen incorrectly.

Their feeding is nearly eventless. A grazer pauses too long where one avenue’s law overlaps another. A glider descends into an angle that does not quite belong to the visible architecture. A patch of darkness deepens without source. The creature is absent afterward. There is no struggle because the attack, if it can be called that, occurs through permission: the victim becomes compatible with nonappearance.

The shadows do not eat flesh. They consume presence. What remains may be a colder patch of stone, a darkened mineral vein, a new refusal in an archway, a stain shaped like a memory of the body taken. For days afterward, wall-colonies near the place grow darker and more elaborate, suggesting that disappearance has nutrients.

Some shadows are territorial. Some migrate with dusk. Some obey the single sunken courtyard toward which all shadows in one quarter point. That courtyard may be their nest, or mouth, or court of appeal. Nothing living crosses its threshold when the sky is low.

Dust itself lives here, though not as an organism with a skin.

It behaves too collectively to be mere debris. It travels, gathers, separates, climbs. It remembers routes. It pours uphill during dust tides, fills stairways from below, makes ridges and fans in plazas, then drains through cracks that open only for it. The dust is composed of everything: abraded basalt, powdered salt, shed colony skins, grazer exhalations, glider fragments, filament spores, stone ground fine by the city’s breathing. It is the city’s blood and weather and census.

In stillness, dust rests according to laws no outsider can easily read. It avoids certain thresholds. It heaps against walls where no wind should place it. It leaves other ledges bare despite centuries of exposure. Under pressure nights, it rises into sheets and takes on faint internal structure: lanes, knots, braided flows, slow vortices. In plazas, it may arrange itself over the exposed mineral rings like a population assembling around civic marks.

Perhaps each mote carries a slight charge, a directional memory, an allegiance to some district’s geometry. Perhaps the city thinks through distributions of dust, as another creature might think through nerves.

There are also the suspended shard-fields, and things that live among them.

In the quarter where nothing can fall completely, flakes of stone hang in vertical curtains: chips, scales, translucent mica plates, basalt splinters, salt leaves curled like parchment. They stop halfway through descent and remain, sometimes for years, sometimes for an age. On their undersides grows a mineral film too delicate for ordinary walls. Small inhabitants tend it.

These are thread-creatures, almost invisible except when crossing from one shard to the next. They are made of articulated bristles and tiny hooked arcs, pale as scraped bone. They move upside down because in that district down is unfinished. Their bodies fit between suspended flakes, and their legs test gravity the way an animal elsewhere might test scent. They graze on underside films, lay crystalline eggs along hairline cracks, and freeze when echoes arrive from the future.

When a suspended shard finally completes its fall, the thread-creatures on it do not drop. They remain in the air for a moment, clinging to the absence the shard occupied. Then some find neighboring debris. Some become dust. Some persist as faint scratch marks on nothing until the next pressure night gives them surfaces again.

Inside sealed rooms live winds.

Not air currents merely, but enclosed temperaments of motion. A building with no openings may contain a restless circulation audible only from outside as a dry turning. Another may hold a wind that has been still for centuries, tight and coiled, escaping only when an intersection darkens at the correct angle. These winds have shapes but no bodies: spirals, blades, flattened sheets, slow vertical drills. They polish interior walls no creature has touched. They carry tastes from inaccessible versions of the city.

When a wind escapes, the residents respond. Wall-colonies close their pores. Gliders tilt away or gather, depending on the wind’s mineral content. Grazers lower themselves and lock their joints. Dust either flees or follows. The escaped wind may run down an avenue, humming through fluted bone-cliffs, and enter another sealed chamber without crossing the intervening distance. It is possible that these winds are among the oldest citizens, trapped when the city first folded upward out of the plateau, still circulating through architecture as breath circulates through a sleeping animal.

The vertical sheets of darkness at intersections are inhabited too, though not by stable forms.

At their surfaces gather small transitional lives: dust altered by passage, motes that fall upward, black grains that twitch toward impossible views. Sometimes along the edge of a sheet there appears a fringe of beadlike organisms, each one half in this city and half in some adjacent condition. They open and close like valves. Through them pass nutrients unavailable elsewhere: red sunlight from an absent sky, pressure from a buried salt-age, cold from star-stairs, the image of an observer arriving too late.

These threshold-beads are dangerous food. Gliders feed on them and become translucent with wrong constellations. Grazers lick them only during long migrations and may emerge altered, older, or patterned with stains that move independently of the body. Shadows cluster near them but cannot always cross. The beads themselves may be larvae of intersections, young laws accreting around a weakness in place.

What lives there, finally, is the city.

Not metaphorically only. The city is an organism too large and too distributed to resemble life. Its tissues are basalt ribs, saltstone planes, glass seams, hollowed courts, pressure chambers, dust routes, stain-fields, impossible geometries. It metabolizes wind. It excretes powder. It opens pores at dusk through its colonies. It breathes by thermal expansion, widening at cold night, narrowing in dim day. It maintains itself through erosion so consistent that damage and repair are the same act.

Its nervous system is local law. One district declares walls walkable. Another sends echoes ahead of sound. Another prevents falling from completing itself. Another gathers every shadow toward a sunken court. These are not malfunctions; they are organs with distinct functions. The city does not need a single mind. It is governed by overlapping jurisdictions of matter.

Its inhabitants are less citizens than expressions of its metabolism. The grazers prune and redistribute the stain-fields. The gliders carry dust, spores, and impossible angles between districts. The filaments sense changes before they become geography. The shadows remove excess presence. The winds scour internal channels. The dust records and transports the city’s slow decisions. Even the rare thread-creatures of the suspended shard-fields serve the unfinished fall, tending the half-gravity ecology until it changes its mind.

Nothing here is warm enough to love in an animal way. Nothing speaks. But there are relations: restraint, hunger, migration, avoidance, timing, exchange. A grazer waits for a shadow to withdraw. A colony opens only after the mica seam beside it has cooled. A glider rides a wind released from a sealed room and seeds a courtyard that will not appear until dusk. Dust climbs an avenue and feeds a plaza-vein. A filament trembles, and days later an arch refuses a different body.

The life of this place is not abundant. It is exact.

It has learned to use scarcity as structure. With no water, it drinks difference. With no sun, it harvests dimness. With no soil, it grows on exposure. With no voices, it communicates by stain, pressure, direction, and disappearance. With no builders, it continues to become urban through the patient civic labor of erosion and anomaly.

Whoever lives there is therefore not one species, nor even many species in the ordinary sense. It is a dry polity of mineral beings and almost-beings, a city-population made from the behaviors that remain when life has surrendered softness but not intention.

They are the thin grazers under the colonnades.

They are the gliders stretched across the high wind.

They are the opening stains, the rising powders, the patient filaments, the predatory absences, the winds in sealed rooms, the dust that knows the avenues.

They are the laws that have grown bodies.

They are the citizens of a place too old, too high, and too dry for citizenship, continuing their soundless work beneath a sky of ash and silver.
