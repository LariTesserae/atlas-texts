They are shaped by current before they are shaped by flesh.

The inhabitants of the drowned high country are not a single people, nor even a single kind of animal, but a whole society of pressure: bodies, roots, shells, fins, blind feelers, air pockets, rusting mechanisms, and patient colonies all living according to the same severe grammar. They belong to water that moves downhill though there is no air above the slope, to passes that behave like throats, to valleys that pull colder streams along their floors. Everything here has learned either to yield, cling, hide, or ride.

Most life is low and attached.

The first inhabitants, the ones that own the stone most completely, are films. Brown, green, black, orange, pearl-grey: slick veils of algae, bacterial skins, mineral crusts, and fine-rooting freshwater mosses that cover the old mountain bones. They live on granite shoulders, brick walls, roof tiles, rails, broken turbine casings, the insides of pipes, the faces of milestones. They are not passive decoration. They are the beginning of habitation. They soften edges, bind silt, catch spores, feed snails, shelter larvae, and make every dead thing usable. A bare surface does not stay bare for long. If water touches it and light or mineral seepage reaches it, something fastens there.

Above these films grow the bending forests.

The strapweeds and ribbon plants are the most visible citizens of the drowned province. They stand in dense amber and green meadows over the old alpine slopes, so thick that the ground disappears beneath their streaming bodies. Each plant is built less like a tree than like a long decision to survive being pulled. Their stems are tough, jointed, fibrous, and flexible; their leaves are torn into ribbons by constant force. They do not resist the current directly. They translate it. A hillside of them can lie flat in a surge, then rise again after the water slackens, each frond lifting as if the mountain itself were growing fur.

Some have bladdered seeds that knock and rattle faintly in the flow. Some release pale filamentous spawn that drifts like smoke and catches on ironwork, antlers, ladders, collapsed beams. Some cling only to stone. Others prefer the mineral warmth around cracked pipes and old industrial outflows, where they grow thicker, stranger, with pale swollen bases and translucent leaves veined in orange. The forests are not quiet. Their rubbing and rasping makes a continuous faint music under the roar of water.

Within them live small things in multitudes.

There are shrimp like moving glass, almost invisible until they turn and flash. They gather in eddies behind chimneys, under cart axles, in the lee of old retaining walls, and among the cable pylons. Their bodies are clear except for a dark thread of gut and the tiny black grains of their eyes. When the current shifts, they vanish into weed or rise all at once in glittering clouds, as if the water has developed static.

There are snails with heavy, cold-dark shells, grazing old masonry clean in slow arcs. They cross carved dates, warning signs, door lintels, metal plates, and the faces of drowned saints if any remain in village niches. Their trails polish the algae into shining maps. Some grow shells stained blue-green by copper leaching from machinery. Some cluster around bubbling springs, where bacterial films give them enough food to form dense, knotted congregations.

Mussels are among the great engineers of the place. They pack themselves into cracks, pipe mouths, window frames, turbine grilles, rail joints, roof seams, and the sagging loops of cable between pylons. A cable that once carried power or ore buckets now carries hundreds of kilograms of living shell. Their colonies change the water. They clarify it where they feed heavily, slow it where they grow thickly, and create small armored cities for worms, larvae, fry, and crabs. Every seam that admits current becomes, eventually, a mussel seam.

There are tube worms too, pale and white and red-tipped, especially on ceramic insulators, rusted beams, and mineral seeps. They make feathery crowns in the water and withdraw at vibration. A passing eel, a stonefall, the dull note of an old bell: at each disturbance a hundred plumes disappear, then slowly bloom again.

The fish divide the drowned mountains into levels.

Nearer the green light are the silver schools: narrow, quick-bodied fish that move above the weed canopy in tight sheets. They turn all at once, not as individuals but as weather made muscular. Their sides catch the broken surface light and scatter it down into the forests. They feed on drifting larvae, pale threads, tiny crustaceans, and the suspended richness torn loose by current. When cormorants plunge from the air-world, these schools fold and split around the black intruder like cloth around a knife.

Below them are the heavier fish of walls and terraces. Broad-headed, blunt-mouthed, darker along the back, they nose through village ruins and old field steps, sucking food from silt, cracking shells, disturbing clouds of sediment from floors where people once stood. They know the cottages as reef. A collapsed roof beam is cover. A hearth is a feeding pit. A staircase is a current break. Their bodies are scarred from stone and metal. Some have pale bellies rubbed raw by squeezing under doors or through window holes.

In the valley trenches move the long ones.

These are eel-like fish, or perhaps true eels, though colder and heavier than river eels, with muscular bodies made for pushing through dense water along the drowned riverbeds. They travel the old valleys as if following ancestral maps written before the flood. Their skin is dark olive, brown-black, sometimes mottled like wet stone. They hunt by smell and pressure. In clear water they are almost shy, slipping beneath root mats and into pipe mouths. In brown water, after a slope failure, they become bold. They enter houses. They coil in machinery. They wait in stairwells where cold water drains downward like invisible smoke.

The ruins belong to them at night, if night can be said to exist under so much water. They thread through pump conduits and cracked culverts, rest in intake pipes, breed in hollows beneath old foundations. A turbine housing with one broken blade may hold three generations of them: adults in the deeper chamber, juveniles in the mussel-thick grates, eggs hidden in silt behind a rusted inspection hatch.

Crabs live everywhere that offers grip.

They are not elegant creatures. They climb ladders, algae trunks, railings, weed stems, collapsed rafters, old cable stays, and the ribbed sides of pipes. They carry the landscape on their backs: moss, silt, baby mussels, threads of weed, orange films from the warm seeps. Some look like stones until they unfold. Their claws are strong enough to open smaller shells and tug worms from tubes. In high current they flatten themselves and hold fast with all limbs spread, becoming part of the structure until the surge passes.

They are opportunists in the purest sense. A dead fish in a doorway brings them. A fresh sediment slump brings them. The burst of air from the old smokestacks brings them, because disoriented shrimp and fry tumble in the bubble wake. Around the mineral-warmed brick structure they grow larger and paler, feeding on blind white crustaceans and bacterial mats. Some there have lost much of their color, as if the warm pipe-gardens are gradually making cave animals out of them.

The blind white crustaceans are among the strangest residents, though nothing about them is impossible.

They live where the old industrial works leak warmth and minerals from below. Their world is not the green-lit forest but chambers, cracks, pipe interiors, brick channels, and buried sumps. They are soft-looking, translucent, long-legged, with feelers much longer than their bodies. They graze orange bacterial films and the pale sponges that grow in the warmish outflow. Light means little to them. Vibration is everything. They scatter from the grinding of a turbine, freeze when bells pulse through the structure, and emerge after each bubble eruption to feed on loosened matter.

Around them the submerged factory has become a reef-cave-spring: brick walls furred with life, smokestacks exhaling trapped air, pipes acting as burrows, old boilers or tanks sheltering colonies that have never seen the surface. The industry that once extracted or pumped now breeds.

There are larger animals too, visitors and residents of a broader range.

The seal-like mammals are not numerous, but their presence changes the feeling of the drowned country whenever they arrive. They come from deeper basins, following fish up along the ancient valleys and over the rounded saddles. Their bodies are thick, insulated, dark above and pale below, with broad hind flippers and clever forelimbs capable of bracing against stone. They move through the ruins with an ease that makes the old human spaces seem newly designed for them. A doorway frames a seal’s head. A factory window becomes a hunting gap. A road cutting becomes a travel corridor. A pump tunnel becomes a place to rest out of the current.

They breathe air, so they must know the far upper boundary—the hammered silver ceiling where sky begins. But below, in the drowned mountains, they behave like temporary masters. Fish scatter from them. Crabs withdraw. Eels hold deeper in their pipes. They twist through kelp, roll along the old roads, scrape their backs on mussel-crusted stone, and exhale chains of bubbles that shoot upward through green light.

They den where air remains trapped: under overhangs, inside sealed upper rooms, in pockets beneath collapsed industrial roofs, in natural cavities along the old summits. Some of these air chambers are small and foul, used only briefly. Others are replenished by the strange breathing of the ruins—old pipes, chambers, and smokestacks that collect and release gas with changes in pressure. In such places the mammals haul out partially, whiskered heads lifted in darkness, listening to the bells and groaning metal around them.

They are intelligent in the way of animals that live between elements. They know which clear currents are dangerous. They know the brown taste of a fresh slide. They know that bubbles from the ground may mean food, warmth, poison, or machinery. They teach routes: over the western saddle only in slack water; never through the narrow pass when the high hiss begins; rest behind the three pylons; hunt the rail ravine after storms; avoid the pump house when its bells start sounding too quickly.

Above them, entering from another world, are the birds.

Cormorants are the most violent visitors: black, narrow, sudden. They puncture the upper forests from the air, seize fish, and fight their way back to brightness. To those below they must seem less like animals than falling tools—beaks, claws, wings compressed tight, eyes burning with surface urgency. They do not belong to the pressure-world for long. Their bones are full of air, their mission brief. Yet they are part of the system. Their guano falls from the boundary rocks and feeds upper growth. Their failed hunts injure fish that eels later take. Their shadows create panic far below their actual reach.

Other birds are only distortions on the ceiling: wavering dark geometries sliding over the drowned summits. Their lives remain elsewhere, in weather and wind, but even their shadows are read by the fish.

If there are people here now, they are rare, and they do not own the place.

Perhaps divers come from the shore settlements, armored in thick suits, trailing lines, lamps, tools, and caution. They would seem clumsy among the natives: warm-blooded but poorly adapted, loud with bubbles, short of time, always negotiating with depth. They might visit the pump works to free a jammed turbine, harvest mussels, retrieve old metal, map the drowned roads, or check the mineral springs. They would move like pilgrims through a cathedral that does not recognize them.

The animals would know them as intermittent disturbances. Fish would clear from their lamps. Crabs would raise claws and settle again. Eels would withdraw into pipe mouths. Seals might follow at a distance, curious and superior, watching these awkward cousins labor under pressure. The divers’ hands would brush inscriptions no one reads anymore. Their fins would stir silt from a cottage floor and expose, briefly, a threshold stone, a spoon, a hinge, a child’s iron bedframe, before the water covered memory again.

But the true inhabitants are not the divers. Nor were the former villagers, miners, engineers, or herders the final inhabitants. The place now belongs to those that can live with the unfinished work of water.

Everything living here understands gradients.

The plants know flow as posture. They grow low where force is high, tall where the basin slackens, fan-shaped where food arrives sideways, rope-like where anchorage matters more than light. The mussels know it as feeding. The shrimp know it as shelter. The fish know it as roads. The eels know it as scent. The seals know it as weather. Even the bacteria at the warm pipes know that warmth and mineral richness are a current to be inhabited before they vanish into the cold.

They also understand the ruins not as ruins, but as forms.

A house is a cave with square openings.

A road is a long clearing through weed.

A rail line is a dark guide across distance.

A tower is a reef.

A cable is a suspended branch.

A pump station is a breathing cliff.

A bell is a warning animal made of bronze.

A turbine is a predator only when it wakes.

A village is not abandoned; it is occupied differently, room by room and seam by seam. Mussels in the pantry. Fish in the windows. Snails on the lintels. Eels beneath the stairs. Shrimp in the chimney. Crabs on the rafters. Weed through the bedframes. Algae on the hearthstones. The doorways remain black not because they are empty, but because the life inside them does not need light.

Their lives are slow until they are sudden.

For long periods the drowned high country seems to be made of endurance. Plants sway. Shellfish feed. Cold seeps breathe. Sediment settles on roofs, roads, and terraces. Fish browse in the green dimness. Eels lie motionless with only their gills moving. Crabs pick delicately at worm tubes. Seals sleep in air pockets or vanish into deeper blue.

Then pressure changes.

A storm far above and far away reaches them as command. The current rises over the saddles. The forests flatten in one direction. Schools tighten and turn into living blades. Shrimp vanish. Crabs lock down. Eels reverse deeper into pipes. Mussels close. Tube worms retract. Seals abandon exposed valleys and slip behind ridges. Plants shed strands, seeds, and torn leaves that stream through the passes like banners from a defeated army.

The old machines wake too, and for the animals this is simply another seasonal behavior of the habitat. Bells pulse. Flywheels shudder. Turbines grind. Cables hum under loads of shell and weed. Air erupts from smokestacks in roaring silver columns. Gravel runs along roadbeds. A retaining wall collapses, and the village changes shape again.

Afterward, the inhabitants return and revise their maps.

A new hollow has opened beneath a foundation: an eel den.

A wall has fallen across a lane: a crab bridge.

A pipe has cracked near the warm spring: a bacterial bloom.

A roof has collapsed: shelter for fry.

A boulder has shifted, exposing clean stone: future algae, future mussels, future forest.

This is how they live: by accepting that the country is ancient but not still.

They are cold-water creatures of inheritance and improvisation. None of them remember the mountains in air, but their bodies are full of responses to what the mountains have become. They occupy height without sky, valleys without rivers, factories without workers, roads without feet, forests without trunks, weather without wind. They are not ghosts of the old country. They are its continuation under different pressure.
