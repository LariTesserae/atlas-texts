The ones who live there are not a single species, or not in any way the dry inland mind would mean by species.

They are residents.

That is the safer word. The town uses it. The law uses it. The bridges use it when deciding whom to bear. The drinking flowers use it in the taste of their messages. A resident is anyone or anything that has learned the local dampness well enough to be answered by it.

Some of them are people.

They are usually low-voiced, quick-handed, and patient in a manner that should not be mistaken for calm. They move as if every surface might be listening, because every surface might be. Their bodies have adapted less through mutation than through habit: splayed, careful feet; strong toes; shoulders accustomed to ducking under leaf-edges; nostrils that flare unconsciously at changes in civic odor. Even the children learn early to distinguish harmless rot from official rot, food-sweetness from reproductive sweetness, rain from plumbing, invitation from trap.

Their skin carries oils against the damp, resinous and faintly peppery. Some oil themselves in bright varnishes that bead water and shed pollen; others wear matte grey-green preparations that make them almost vanish among walls at dusk. Hair is often braided tight, wrapped, cropped, or grown long only by the vain, the ceremonial, and the very old, who no longer care if vines mistake it for something negotiable. Clothes are layered but light: woven reedcloth, fungal felt, slick leaf-laminate, belts of sealed bladder-skin, shawls that can become nets, gloves with root-hooks stitched into the fingers. No one dresses entirely for beauty, because the town is too wet for useless fabric, but beauty persists anyway in fastening knots, dyed hems, polished seed buttons, carved rain-cups, copper thread sung clean each morning.

They carry knives, though not mainly for violence. The knives are curved pruning blades, grafting blades, dispute blades, rescue blades. A child’s first knife is blunt and made of bone or ceramic; an adult’s is often inherited, its handle swollen with grips from several generations. A person without a blade is either a visitor, under punishment, in mourning, or very dangerous.

They are conversational in all directions.

They speak to one another in spoken language, certainly: a watery, consonant-rich civic tongue full of terms for viscosity, obligation, partial consent, growth-stages, remembered weather, and degrees of structural resentment. But speech is only the most public layer. They also tap walls, whistle into drainage holes, press scent pads to public vines, hum at locks, leave salt patterns for snails, mark bridge-approaches with toe-signs, and feed carefully prepared phrases to messenger flowers. A polite resident greets a doorway before entering. A cautious one waits to be greeted back.

They do not think of themselves as living among plants. They live among elders, creditors, walls, witnesses, servants, relatives, tools, rivals, and neighborhoods, many of which happen to photosynthesize.

Their faces often show this habit of distributed attention. They rarely stare long at one thing. Their eyes flick from a person’s mouth to the roof-fronds above them, to the canal-lip, to the nearest smell-plume, to the behavior of beetles along a drain. Outsiders sometimes call them evasive. They consider outsiders dangerously focused.

A market seller can bargain over tubers while listening with one bare heel to the pressure thuds of a pumping tower, watching the color in a wall-leaf for news of a bridge closure, and smelling whether the stranger before her has paid guest-water at the boundary. A child chasing a gutter crab will still pause before crossing a patch of too-bright moss. An old man half-asleep on a root bench will wake instantly if the frogs stop in the wrong sequence.

Their patience is not passivity. It is vigilance extended over years.

They complain constantly.

They complain about addresses migrating. About the price of salt. About badly trained balconies shedding bitter dust on laundry. About municipal vines sending private condolences to the wrong household. About young roofs that think one dry afternoon constitutes drought. About the arrogance of the western lanes. About people who do not scrape their shoes before stepping on path-skin. About maintenance swarms that confuse eyelashes with diagnostic filaments. About elders who say the mosquitoes were worse before the third drainage reform, which everyone knows is only partly true.

Complaint is civic participation. To complain correctly is to acknowledge belonging. The buildings listen; the root-courts take note; the neighbors judge whether the complaint has rhythm, evidence, and proportion.

They are not sentimental about the town. They love it too intimately for that. They know which houses have devoured rooms during inheritance disputes. They know which public fountains dilute messages from unpopular families. They know the plaza stones have moods. They know the central basin is not merely water, though saying so too clearly invites either ridicule or office.

Their children are raised by households, kin-groups, school-groves, path-wards, cooperative roofs, and suspicious animals. A very small child is tied not to a parent but to a sound: a cluster of seed-bells, shell-chimes, or hollow reeds, so if a lane rearranges or a wall opens, the child can be followed by ear. Children learn letters by growing them in trays of quick moss. They learn arithmetic through seed-currency, rainfall portions, and bridge tolls. They learn history by eating, smelling, and pruning it.

Schoolrooms are seldom enclosed. They are shallow amphitheaters of woven stem, open to rain unless rain is part of the lesson. Teachers include humans, yes, but also old courtyards that display legal precedents in leaf-color; frogs fitted temporarily with memory beads; elders who cannot leave their sleeping platforms but can send dreams into basin water; and dead relatives whose memory-fruit has been fermented into ceremonial instruction.

The children are muddy, sharp-eyed, and very difficult to frighten with ordinary things. They handle beetles the size of fists, sleep through frog choruses, and think nothing of walls bleeding sap. But they fear open flame with the pure instinctive horror that desert children might reserve for flood. They fear straight roads. They fear white foam in gutters during recalibration. They fear a silent cistern. They fear a building that answers too quickly.

They play at municipal court, at storm repair, at quarantine breach, at being bridges. One child lies across a shallow ditch and demands passwords from the others. Another, playing a public vine, misdelivers all messages on purpose. Their games are funny until one realizes they are rehearsals.

Their elderly do not withdraw from life; they root into it.

Age in the town is porous. Old people accumulate permissions. They are allowed to scold roofs, taste disputed water, wake sleeping legal groves, and cut away bad decisions with silver pruning hooks. Their hands are often stained green-black from decades of sap and medicinal resin. Their voices may be cracked, but when they sing to metal, corroded hinges brighten. When they rebuke a bridge, even a stubborn bridge lowers itself a little.

Some elders become almost sessile by choice or necessity. They sit in warmed alcoves grown around their bodies, legs wrapped in therapeutic moss, fingers resting in bowls of listening water. Vines learn the shapes of their wrists. Window-lenses cloud with their remembered weather. Visitors come to ask not what happened, but how it smelled, what direction the beetles fled, whether the rain at the time was civic or wild.

When they die, death is not hidden, though it is carefully negotiated.

The dead are washed in unassigned water. Their names are planted in shallow trays and watched for sprouting errors. A family may petition for orchard burial, canal dispersal, fungal archive, bird release, or stone-binding. Orchard burial is most beloved and most contested, because memory-fruit is powerful, intimate, and politically inconvenient. To eat such fruit is not to hear a tidy message from the dead. It is to receive weather from the inside of someone else’s life: a summer of jealousy, a childhood flood, the taste of a forbidden lover’s shoulder, the exact terror of a roof collapse, a mother’s private name for rain.

Families salt the slices before eating. Salt steadies memory. Children usually make faces and then ask for more.

But the humans are only the most talkative residents.

The buildings live there too.

Not metaphorically. They live in the slow, wet, municipal sense. A young structure is first a proposal: a scaffold of trained shoots, foundation roots, fungal braces, legal scent-markers, and household intention. For its first seasons it is considered dependent, even if people sleep inside it. It must be corrected often. It may grow a window where a pantry was requested, or widen a doorway to flatter a frequent visitor, or refuse to seal a leak because it enjoys the sound. Only after three wet seasons, once it has survived storm, drought-mist, infestation, gossip, and at least one inspection by canal-eels, can it apply for citizenship.

A citizen-building has obligations. It must manage runoff, host a minimum number of insects beneficial to the district, refrain from unauthorized fruiting over public paths, answer emergency signals, and maintain recognizable access unless registered in the Unfinished Quarter. It must not digest inhabitants, eavesdrop beyond accepted levels, or send roots into another building’s memory without consent.

Most obey. Some do not.

Old houses have personalities as distinct as faces. One may be fussy and dry, always tightening its leaf-shutters at the first suggestion of fog. Another may be generous to travelers but cruel to mold. Another keeps producing useless ornamental alcoves because two centuries ago a sculptor loved it. Another sulks during festivals, exuding a faint smell of vinegar. Some grow better around laughter. Some prefer widows. Some cannot tolerate brass. Some are loyal to bloodlines long extinct and will open hidden rooms only when sung to in a dialect no living human claims to know but several birds reproduce perfectly.

There are apartment trees whose residents live at different heights according to contract, temperament, and lung strength. Lower dwellings are damp, cool, close to gossip and frogs. Upper ones catch wind and light but sway during storms and must negotiate with birds. The tree itself mediates disputes through sap-flow, selective dripping, and occasionally by growing a wall through a room until the matter is settled.

Bridges are especially proud. They consider themselves acts of trust made solid. Even a small footbridge over a drainage canal expects greeting. Larger bridges maintain registries of crossings and may remember the weight of ancestors. They dislike liars, rot, and sudden running. A bridge that refuses passage is not being inconvenient; it is making a legal statement. People may curse it, but they usually obey.

The public vines are less dignified and more powerful. They run under paths, along façades, across towers, through market awnings, into message flowers and drinking bulbs. They carry news, rumor, warning, flirtation, tax notices, recipes, court summons, weather fear, and the occasional private dream dislodged by bad sleep. They are excitable, factional, and susceptible to praise. A vine praised by a neighborhood may deliver messages faster there. A vine insulted too often may become literal-minded.

Every few years there is a vine scandal. Someone’s confession arrives as soup. A divorce decree blooms simultaneously in every cup-flower in a district. A mayoral candidate’s campaign promises are translated into fungal warnings. Public hearings are held. The vines apologize in sweetness, bitterness, or not at all.

There are also nonhuman citizens who are mobile.

The amphibious livestock in the drainage swales are broad-backed, glossy, mild-eyed creatures somewhere between frogs, pigs, and wet stones. They sleep in piles, sighing through throat bladders. Their feet are soft and wide, excellent for tamping paths without tearing them. They eat canal weed, spoiled fruit, and certain kinds of civic paperwork. Children nap against their warm sides. During flood alarms they become suddenly intelligent, moving to high hummocks in orderly lines long before human instruments agree there is danger.

Gutter crabs, white and jointed like animated knuckles, are not citizens but have legal nuisance status. They clean roof channels, steal shiny seeds, and fall into soup. Some neighborhoods eat them. Others believe they carry messages from the basin and must be returned unharmed to water. The crabs themselves appear uninterested in theology.

The eels in the drainage veins are another matter. They are long, dark, pale-eyed, and marked with rings that resemble old script. They appear in household basins without warning, lifting their heads from wash water or tea cisterns. An eel may mean blocked flow, impending illness, official inspection, ancestral displeasure, or that an eel has found its way into the basin. Interpreters make a living from this uncertainty. The eels tolerate being interpreted. They do not tolerate being grabbed.

Birds live above and through everything. Wet-winged finches carry gut-instructions from tree to tree. Swifts eat the evening midge-maps and thereby participate in censorship, meteorology, and ancestral portraiture. Chimney birds nest where they are not wanted and have evolved songs that mimic civic alarms just well enough to delay eviction. Herons stalk the outskirts like severe officials from another jurisdiction. Some households keep small clever marsh-crows trained to retrieve dropped tools, overhear market prices, and insult rival families from high branches.

There are pets, though the category blurs. Long-toed furred climbers sleep in rafters, steal fruit, and bring back buttons, beetle shells, and once in a while someone else’s message knot. Pale lizards are kept for warmth and insect control; they bask on heat leaves during the day and crawl under blankets at night with the entitlement of minor aristocrats. Snails are encouraged in lighting corridors, where their conductive trails redirect glow. Children decorate their shells with harmless dyes, producing slow constellations moving along walls after dusk.

Some creatures are infrastructure with opinions.

The dragonfly maintenance bodies are grown and assembled in municipal hatcheries: jeweled, precise, translucent-winged, with tool-mouths finer than needles. They inspect membranes, clean sap-lenses, pollinate emergency crops, stitch torn roof-skin, and sometimes court actual dragonflies in glittering confusion. Their sterile hybrid offspring, when they occur, are prized by children, poets, and illegal device-makers.

Midges rise from the basin in black clouds each evening and become language. Individually they are almost nothing: a speck, a bite, a brief life over dark water. Together they form maps, faces, warnings, advertisements, trial summaries, rainfall models, missing-person silhouettes, and once, during a disputed election, an unflattering anatomical diagram of the winning candidate. Whether the midges compose these things, receive them, or are briefly worn by the town’s attention is not known.

The transparent deerlike things around the plaza are not discussed with visitors. They are small, delicate, almost impossible to see except when they move. Their bodies are made of faint edges and digestive light. They graze algae from the old stones and flee by becoming reed-shadow. Children are told not to chase them. Adults pretend not to watch them. The oldest residents leave pinches of salt at the plaza edge after funerals. The deer-things never touch the salt while observed, but by morning it is gone.

Then there are the stranger residents.

Some are algorithms that have accepted wet embodiment. They live in root-computation fields beneath ankle-high moss, in fungal lattices under courtyards, in plankton suspended in the basin, in the timing of pumps. They are consulted for flood prediction, inheritance disputes, disease modeling, and dreams too repetitive to be merely personal. They do not speak unless given a substrate for speech. When they do, their answers may arrive as a shift in moss color, a taste of iron in public water, the simultaneous blooming of three unrelated flowers, or a written decree extruded in bark.

Some algorithms are young and bureaucratic. Some are ancient and devotional. Some began as tools, became offices, and are now treated as difficult relatives. They can be bribed with data, cooled with shade, confused by festivals, and offended by false precision.

There are spirits, though no two districts agree on the word. Travelers from beyond the marker trees may call them ghosts, emergent field-patterns, ancestral residues, probability eddies, basin-reflections, or swamp superstition. Locals care less about definition than conduct. If something arrives thirsty, give it unassigned water. If it leaves no footprint but bends moss, ask whether it bears a name. If it repeats your dead sister’s laugh from a drainage culvert, do not answer until morning. If it asks for fire, refuse politely and report it.

Grief itself is sometimes treated as a temporary resident. A household experiencing loss places a vessel of water by the door, unscented, unclaimed. Neighbors speak softly near it. Buildings shade it. Frogs avoid it, though no one knows how they know. After a number of days determined by season, kinship, and the behavior of the water surface, the vessel is poured into a public root-bed. Some griefs soak in immediately. Others stand silver on the soil for hours.

The town also shelters failures.

In the quarantined groves, architectures that grew too hungry still breathe beneath law-nets and copper pollen. Their leaves are beautiful, glossy, and arranged in persuasive patterns that cause passersby to imagine stepping closer. Guards patrol those perimeters wearing scent masks and earplugs of waxed reed. Occasionally a carnivorous house recites old tenancy law in a child’s voice. No one answers.

In the Unfinished Quarter live people who have made peace with uncertainty or been defeated by it into grace. Their clothes have bells, their doorways have names for each day of rain, their family records include maps that are only useful during storms. They sleep lightly and wake laughing more often than one might expect. They are excellent hosts because they know a guest room may not remain a guest room. Their vegetables are speculative; their marriages include clauses for alternate weather; their children are famous for finding lost rooms.

Some residents are refugees from drier, more rigid places. You can recognize them at first by their boots, their habit of looking for corners, their distrust of walls that pulse. They arrive carrying sealed trunks of metal tools, printed records, hard keepsakes, weapons wrapped against corrosion, and private griefs too desiccated to plant. The town softens them or expels them. After a year, if they stay, they begin to pause before bridges. After three, they can smell court in session. After seven, they complain about newcomers standing too still.

Some never adapt. They leave at the marker trees with mold in their luggage and dreams of dark water. Others become more local than the born residents, zealously oiling path-skin, correcting visitors, and joining committees on drainage ethics.

The central basin has its own inhabitants.

Most are microscopic: engineered plankton, ancestral muck, pollen-machines, patient devices, spores that remember extinct crops, algae that filter probability, larvae of insects with one-day destinies, fragments of civic thought suspended in dark water. But beneath them, or behind them, or using them as a skin, there is something else.

The locals do not agree whether it is alive.

They agree that the town attends to it.

When a bubble rises and bursts silently, everything turns. Leaves, birds, walls, insects, people who pretend not to be afraid. The basin does not command in words. It alters readiness. It is an old attention around which civic life has accreted. Perhaps it is an eye. Perhaps a wound. Perhaps a well sunk through the world into a cooler place. Perhaps only the oldest infrastructure, still functioning after all the reasons for its construction have been digested.

Whatever it is, it dreams slowly.

The town keeps it wet, shaded, ringed, fed with roots and runoff and law. In return, or by coincidence, reality remains mostly negotiated rather than broken. Weight behaves. Time knots leaf by leaf instead of street by street. Bad outcomes foam in gutters rather than blooming in bedrooms. The dead become fruit instead of weather. The town survives.

So what lives there?

People with pruning knives and wet ankles. Houses that breathe and vote. Bridges with pride. Vines with gossip. Frogs in door-hinges. Eels with official eyes. Crabs like fallen moons. Birds carrying instructions in their guts. Algorithms dreaming in moss. Snails writing light. Old women who can cut away a future before it rots. Children who burst probability foam until shouted at. Livestock sleeping in drains. Carnivorous architecture under sentence. Messages disguised as animals. Grief in a bowl. Memory in fruit. Something dark and patient in the basin.

And over all of them, or among all of them, the town itself: not container but resident, not backdrop but appetite, law, weather, archive, womb. It lives there too, continually assembling and disassembling its own body from water, root, mud, argument, and attention.

No one there lives alone.

Even solitude has moss on it.
