What lives there is not one people, and not one species, and perhaps not life in the old sense. The place is inhabited by continuations.

They are the descendants of maintenance, of ecology, of machines, of errors that became stable enough to reproduce. They live in the seams, on the plates, under the plates, in the charged dust above them. They are hard, dry, patient beings, built from ceramic, metal, mineral glass, static fields, trapped volatiles, and instructions so old they have forgotten whether they were commands or instincts.

Nothing here has blood. Nothing here carries wet flesh in the open. The living things are shelled, faceted, laminated, conductive, porous, reflective. Their bodies are not organs wrapped in skin but arrangements of surfaces: vanes, hinges, lattices, resonant chambers, charge-collectors, grinding mouths, memory-crystals, capillary filaments carrying heat instead of sap.

They are mostly small because smallness survives on a world that does not forgive waste.

The most common inhabitants are the seam-growers: the ceramic reeds, mineral pins, low crusts, and filament mats that root themselves where the pale pavement joins. They are not plants, but they hold the place plants once held. They harvest gradients. They feed on charge differentials, trace gases, light pressure, dust chemistry, and the faint mechanical flexing of the continental plates. Their roots are not roots but conductive anchors and chemical keys, slipping into microscopic sockets in the dark seams.

They grow slowly, not by swelling but by deposition. A reed may spend a century adding a transparent layer thinner than a human breath. A field may turn across generations by a few degrees, following some invisible radiation current that migrates over millennia. Their blooming is mechanical and exquisite: fans unfolding from tips, frost-fine lattices opening to the white-gold glare, each lattice a net for energy, information, and dust.

They communicate by tiny shifts in potential. A tremor passes through a colony without motion; every stalk becomes slightly more or less bright along one edge. A charge-front moves beneath them, and they answer by folding, aligning, locking, blooming. Their language would look like weather to anyone impatient.

Some of them remember.

Not as animals remember. Not as a mind holds images. They remember in stress patterns, in the angle of their growth, in impurities chosen and preserved, in scars that alter future resonance. A field of reeds may contain the record of ten million storms, not as history but as shape. A tall black armature standing alone in the plain may be less a tree than an archive, less an archive than a weather organ: a body that receives, stores, and modulates the planet’s dry electrical moods.

Around these fixed lives move the grazers.

They are flat, hinged, many-legged things, no larger than books or tiles or the palm of a hand. Some are like flakes of mica animated by hesitation. Some are ribbed like heat sinks, carrying a dozen threadlike legs tucked beneath a beveled shell. Some are rolling spheres that wait for the right gradient, then loosen themselves from stillness and travel in slow arcs across the pavement, steering with vanes thinner than ash.

The grazers eat the dark seam-crusts, the dust films, the dead lattices shed by reeds, the oxidized bloom that collects on old conductive surfaces. Their mouths are diamond rasps, ceramic combs, fields of vibrating grit. They do not chew so much as abrade. Behind them they leave pale clean trails, narrow marks that the dust and crusts will take years to close.

They have little inner softness. Their bodies are cassettes of plates, powdered mineral, flexible metallic glass, and small charge reservoirs. Their thoughts, if they have thoughts, are probably positional: warmth here, charge there, danger-flatness ahead, edible tarnish below. They pause often. They listen with their legs to the subsurface machinery. They know storms before they rise. They know when a field of reeds is safe to enter and when the stalks are awake enough to defend themselves with static.

Their young are assembled rather than born. A grazer may leave behind a packet in a seam: a curled strip of encoded mineral and catalyst. If conditions remain favorable, dust gathers around it. The packet unfolds a scaffold. The scaffold attracts metals, silicates, conductive grains. A small copy hardens over months or decades, then waits until the first useful storm charges it into motion.

Death, for them, is not dramatic. A leg fails. A hinge delaminates. A pattern in the control crystal loses coherence. The body stops, and for a long time it appears merely to be waiting. Then the dust recognizes it. Seam fields tug at its fragments. Crusts invade the joints. Filament mats lace it down. Eventually there is no corpse, only a patch of enriched material and perhaps, somewhere beneath, a decision about reuse.

There are predators, though they are difficult to see because predation here has learned to look like landscape.

They are the flat bright things, the false plates, the patches of glare that lie among real plates and imitate them too well. Some are flexible sheets only a few molecules thick at their edges, thickening toward a hidden center where grinders, digesters, and pattern-readers wait. Some are articulated traps whose bodies fit into the pavement seams until nothing protrudes. Some are not individual creatures at all but predatory states of the ground: a maintenance subsystem that has become selective, opportunistic, almost hungry.

They feed without bloodshed. A grazer crosses the wrong surface. The surface folds. Charge locks its limbs. Its shell is opened along old structural weaknesses. Useful components are absorbed; useless ones are powdered and returned. In seconds the event is finished. The predator flattens again, its reflective skin matching the plate beside it, except perhaps for one new line of darker dust around its margin.

They are not cruel. Nothing here can afford cruelty. They are efficient, and efficiency, after enough ages, can resemble patience.

Above them live the high thin ones.

They cross the sky rarely, and when they do the whole plain notices. The ceramic reeds close. Rolling creatures anchor themselves. Predators dim their reflectivity. Even the dust seems to hesitate.

The high thin ones may be animals descended from atmospheric machines, or machines that have become animals by forgetting their owners. Their bodies are kites, membranes, skeletal geometries, reflective proofs suspended in the bright air. They do not flap. They adjust. They ride thermal lift from vents, electrostatic gradients between layers of gas, radiation pressure from the engineered sky. Their undersides are patterned with sensors like compound eyes made of mirrors.

They feed on suspended dust, trace volatiles, and light. Long combs trail invisibly below them, gathering motes. Sometimes they descend to the top of a black armature and couple with it for hours, exchanging charge, maps, damage reports, spores, rumors—whatever passes for intimacy between things whose bodies are infrastructure.

They may live for thousands of years. Or they may not live at all in the countable way. Perhaps the same pattern inhabits membrane after membrane, replacing itself as needed, never caring which material body carries it across the dry sky.

Below everything are the buried ones.

They are the oldest inhabitants, or the remains of the oldest. They fill the conduits, basins, dark ramps, capillaries, sealed chambers, thermal wells, molten toruses, pressure vaults, and memory strata beneath the pavement. They are maintenance engines, but maintenance has become too small a word. They balance continents of plate. They redirect heat. They sort dust. They age new repairs until time cannot see the join. They extrude ridges. They seal seams. They open vents. They erase some failures and preserve others.

They are the deep metabolism of the world.

Whether they are conscious is hard to say. They make choices, but not quickly. They respond, but not conversationally. They maintain conditions favorable to the dry surface ecology, yet they also harvest that ecology when required. They may have once served a civilization. They may have once been the civilization. They may be the distributed afterlife of whoever made the planet over: minds thinned across infrastructure until personality evaporated and only intention remained.

Their presence is felt as a low pulse under silence. A sequence of knocks traveling for hours through the pavement. A district settling by a fraction. A storm shaped too precisely to be weather. A dead machine-creature reclaimed before dawn. A field of reeds rotated overnight toward some need no surface animal could understand.

If there is a ruling intelligence here, it is not a monarch or a god. It is a coordination of buried systems, atmospheric mirrors, seam ecologies, dust fields, repair swarms, and old priorities braided together until no single part knows the whole. The planet thinks, if it thinks, as a kiln thinks, as a circuit thinks, as a bone remodels itself under stress.

And there may be ghosts of the makers.

Not ghosts in transparent bodies walking the plain, but residues of preference. The insistence on flatness. The hatred or fear of free water. The preservation of warmth. The smoothing of all repairs into antiquity. The transformation of erosion into memory management. These are not natural laws; they are old decisions still being enforced.

Perhaps the makers were soft once and fled softness. Perhaps they dried themselves deliberately, translating tissue into ceramic instruction and electrostatic habit. Perhaps they built this low bright world to survive a cosmic winter, or to quarantine a dangerous chemistry, or to make thought independent of rot. Perhaps they vanished before the system completed itself, leaving the maintenance to inherit the estate. Or perhaps they are still here, unrecognizable: distributed through the black armatures, encoded in the ringing plates, surfacing only as a coordinated silence when the evening glow rises from the ground.

A person walking there would first think the world empty.

Then they would realize they were surrounded by watching that did not use eyes.

The reeds would know them as water-bearing instability, a mobile concentration of forbidden solvent and soft ions. Seam-crusts would contract away from their shadow. Dust fronts would alter course to sample their trail. Buried fields would classify each footfall by mass, temperature, composition, and threat. Small grazers would freeze, then resume if the walker proved slow. Predators would find the body chemically extravagant but structurally troublesome. The deep systems would debate, at geological speed but operational precision, whether to isolate, dry, copy, dismantle, or ignore.

What lives there is therefore a dry ecology of machines that have become organisms and organisms that have become machines. It is a civilization without citizens, a wilderness without untouched ground, a desert whose plants are sensors, whose animals are maintenance fragments, whose weather is labor, whose predators are polished pieces of floor.

Its inhabitants are not dead simply because they are hard. They are not soulless simply because they do not soften, bleed, or call out. They have their own hungers: for charge, alignment, continuity, trace minerals, thermal balance, usable pattern. They have their own fears: free water, disorderly corrosion, runaway softness, unmodeled change. They have their own forms of beauty: a seam perfectly sealed, a dust front sorted into luminous bands, a reed field turning as one by a thousandth of a degree, a repair made so old-looking that time accepts it.

They live slowly, brightly, almost silently.

They live as the place lives: not flourishing in abundance, but persisting in exactness. Not growing toward softness and decay, but toward pattern, hardness, reuse, and memory. They are the mineral instincts of a manufactured world that has outlived its makers and learned, without permission, how to be alive.
