What lives there is not a population so much as a single, distributed tenant: a wet green intelligence made of many plants, half-rooted in botany and half-rooted in the abandoned works it has inherited.

It is not one organism in the simple sense. If cut apart, its pieces would have names: fern, vine, moss, reed, fungus, strangler-root, succulent creeper, algae, liverwort, sedge, epiphyte, black mold, pale amphibious weed. But those names would be like naming the organs of an animal and mistaking them for separate creatures. The thing that lives there is the collaboration among them, the settled compact between growth and ruin. It is the grove-as-machine, the machine-as-grove.

It has no face. Or rather, it has many small false faces that appear and dissolve: a clouded gauge ringed by leaves like eyelids; a flywheel half-swallowed by vine, resembling a jaw; root curtains parted around a pipe-mouth like lips; white rootlets pressed against lamp glass like eyelashes. None of these is its true face. Its true expression is pressure. Green pressure. Slow, soft, constant insistence.

Its body fills the enclosure exactly because it has grown into every available measure of it. It lives in thickness rather than outline. The outer palisade of leaves and stems is its skin, overlapping, sweating, photosynthesizing, sealing the pocket from the ordinary weather outside. The root-mat is its muscle and memory, a springy brown-black fabric of old growth, living fibers, and buried structure. The pipes are veins it has chosen to keep. The concrete channels are intestines. The tanks are swollen organs, some holding water, some holding heat, some holding fermenting dark. The old wires are nerves, unreliable but beloved. The rusted frame under the roof is a skeleton it did not grow but has learned to wear.

It breathes mechanically because machinery once breathed there. A bellows, or pump, or sump system, or some remnant of them, still moves in slow increments beneath the root-felt. But the breath no longer belongs to industry. Warm vapor leaves pipe mouths when pressure gathers below. Hollow reeds shudder and exhale steam in sympathy. Leaves lift and settle. Condensation forms, loosens, falls. Its inhalation is seepage drawn into root and basin; its exhalation is humidity, green smell, vapor, the faint acid sweetness of rot being turned back toward life.

It feeds on water, light, mineral, rust, memory, and repetition.

The water is its blood-medium, though it rarely lies openly. It prefers hidden circulation: film, wick, capillary, drip, bead, root-thread, pipe-sweat. It drinks from below and above at once. Broad leaves catch light and condensation. Fine hairs gather around cracked insulation. Roots enter old conduits, travel through darkness, emerge thirty feet away with the accuracy of plumbing. In some places, where a pipe should be clogged, roots hold a narrow lumen open. In others, where flow would waste itself into useless hollows, the roots have packed the gaps shut with living felt.

It has learned the old factory’s habits and made them vegetal.

A drip falls into a pan.  
The pan tilts.  
A cable tenses.  
A pulley turns a little.  
The turning disturbs a vine.  
The vine releases droplets into a gutter.  
The gutter feeds a tray of seedlings.  
The seedlings lean, thicken, bind the gutter more firmly in place.

This is not consciousness as an animal would understand it. It does not think in images, plans, or sentences. It thinks in gradients: wet toward dry, light toward shade, current toward stillness, pressure toward release, mineral toward hunger, open space toward closure. Its decisions are made by growth. Its attention is shown by thickening. What matters to it becomes overgrown, held, entered, regulated. What does not matter is allowed to sink, soften, and become substrate.

It remembers through routes. A path is a remembered wound in its body, tolerated only because repeated passage has hardened the root-mat and trained leaves aside. Stop using the path, and the place forgets you. Ferns lean in first. Then root-hairs stitch the air. Then a vine finds the gap. In a season, the opening becomes an embarrassment corrected.

It is patient enough to seem merciful and possessive enough to seem cruel.

Small animals live there too, but mostly as punctuation. Pale amphibians inhabit the water-filled prints and algae-skinned hollows, soft as scraps of moon-flesh. Oil-black beetles travel the rusted beams, polishing their own backs against the wet iron. Blind gnats rise briefly from fungal seams and vanish into adhesive leaves. A translucent slug grazes on the green skin of concrete. Something light and arboreal moves above, perhaps a lizard, perhaps a mouse adapted to vines, perhaps only a seedpod springing open in stages. These creatures do not own the place. They borrow milliseconds from it. They pass through its damp processes like sparks through mist.

The dominant inhabitant is slower.

Its flowers are traps, signals, valves, and jokes. Some resemble pale moths folded asleep under leaves where no moths come. Some hang like little porcelain switches beside ceramic insulators. Some never open visibly but swell into bulbous seed-cases that ferment in shadow and smell of bruised fruit. Others grow around metal fixtures in rings, repeating the shape of washers, dials, bolt-heads, gear-teeth. The grove has learned symmetry from machinery and abundance from swamp-growth. It makes blossoms like parts. It makes parts like blossoms.

Its seedlings are especially strange.

Under the roof, in the trays on the old sorting bench, they seem almost organized. No hand arranges them, yet they sort themselves by appetite. Copper-colored sprouts cluster where the water has passed over rust. White thread-roots mass in trays fed by clean drips from the chain hoist. Tiny floating leaves collect in a shallow pan that trembles whenever the hidden pump strokes. They flex toward falling drops with the responsiveness of animals, then resume their vegetal stillness, storing each impact as information.

The chain hook, embedded in a woody collar, is one of the grove’s most important possessions. Once it lifted weight. Now it concentrates water. The metal sweats differently from the air around it; condensation gathers along the curve and falls from the point at reliable intervals. The plant that has swallowed the hook has not engulfed it randomly. It has shaped itself into a living bracket, a patient green fist around iron, keeping the drip aligned over the central tray. The hook is a stalactite, a teat, a clock.

The grove’s nerves are intermittent.

When a relay closes somewhere in the vegetation and a blue-white fleck appears, the whole place does not react. That would be too dramatic, too animal. Instead, minute regions respond. Tendrils near the contact tighten over hours. Leaves that face the wire shift their undersides toward it. Rootlets gather around ceramic insulators, not quite touching where the current still bites. Moss grows thicker beneath places where electrical warmth keeps surfaces fractionally more hospitable. A lamp flickers weakly under the roof, and tiny ferns crowd its glass not because they love light in any mystical way, but because it is warmth, rhythm, signal, a recurring difference in the wet.

To the grove, recurrence is weather.

A machine that turns once a day becomes a sun.  
A drip that falls every few minutes becomes a season.  
A pump that thuds irregularly becomes a kind of thunder.  
A human footstep, if repeated often enough, becomes a trail.  
A human body, if it stayed too long and ceased moving, would become architecture.

It is not hungry in the theatrical sense. It does not lunge. It has no need to. It possesses by incorporation. A fallen tool becomes orange with rust, then green with algae, then furred with moss, then threaded by roots, then indistinguishable from a mound. A plank softens into fungal sweetness. A dead beetle becomes mineral. A broken gauge becomes a niche for fern-spores. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is granted the dignity of remaining only itself.

If a person stood still in the center too long, the grove would not attack. Moisture would gather first on hair, cloth, eyelashes. Spores would settle. Seeds might adhere to mud on boots. A searching tendril, displaced by the body’s entrance, would resume its former arc and discover fabric instead of air. Leaves would rest against shoulders, not with malice but with the inevitability of closing water. The place’s curiosity is tactile and chemical. Salt on skin, warmth, carbon dioxide, the dark damp under a sleeve cuff: all would be information. All information, here, invites growth.

There may also be an older resident within it: the remnant rhythm of the factory itself. Not a ghost with intentions, but a mechanical habit too durable to die. The works once processed something local—perhaps reeds, oilseed, bark, dye-plants, medicinal roots, cane, peat, or fruit. Its apparatus was built to crush, steep, pump, strain, dry, condense. Those verbs remain, though their objects have changed. Now the grove crushes brick with roots, steeps metal in condensation, pumps water through reeds and pipes, strains light through leaves, dries almost nothing, condenses everything.

The machinery gave the plants a grammar.

The plants gave the machinery a metabolism.

Between them lives the present occupant: an engine-grove with no central throne, no master stem, no single brain. Its intelligence is infrastructural. It knows where water must go. It knows which hollows should be filled and which kept open. It knows that electricity is useful but dangerous, that rust feeds certain mosses, that shade preserves moisture, that collapsed roofing can become canopy, that a wheel need not spin quickly to remain alive.

Its moods are almost geological, but they exist.

After rain, it becomes expansive and glossy, loosening its smells, letting water speak loudly through every pipe and seam. In dry spells, if dry can be said of such a place, it tightens. Leaves angle inward. Pools shrink beneath skins of algae. The old pump sounds deeper, more effortful. Root-hairs thicken along shaded concrete. Electrical ticks become sharper in the less saturated air. During warm nights, it ferments. The unseen seedpods breathe out fruit-bitter fumes, and the trays under the roof seem crowded by morning with things that were not visible the evening before.

It reproduces cautiously. It does not fling itself outward in rampant conquest. The raised perimeter holds it, and perhaps it prefers containment. Inside the basin it has achieved a kind of completeness. Beyond the palisade, the world is too variable, too windy, too dry, too interrupted by ordinary weather and animals with hard hooves, sharp teeth, clean appetites. Here, every input is filtered. Here, the old works provide rhythm. Expansion would mean losing precision.

So it condenses.

It thickens old boundaries rather than seeking new territory. It sends roots deeper into channels, more finely around wires, more densely through frames. It revises itself inward. A young vine replaces an old one along the same cable. Moss grows over moss. Leaves fall and are not removed; they become floor, sponge, archive. Each year the space is less empty and more itself.

The scarce animals understand this in their simple ways. The amphibians breed only in footprints and tray-overflows, temporary waters created by movement and machine. The beetles use rusted beams like roads because the ground is too alive. The canopy thing, whatever it is, moves lightly because weight attracts response. Nothing nests conspicuously. Nothing chews too much. The grove tolerates tenants that do not interrupt its circuits.

At its deepest level, what lives there is maintenance without a maintainer.

It keeps the roof from falling entirely because the roof makes shade.  
It keeps certain channels unclogged because they carry water where roots want it.  
It allows the pulley to move because its movement creates drip-patterns.  
It preserves the lamp because its warmth gathers delicate growth.  
It swallows the rest.

If it has a desire, it is not to dominate, escape, or awaken. It wants conditions. Humidity. Repetition. Contact. Continuity. A world without crispness. A world where every hard edge softens enough to accept a root.

And if it has a voice, it is the whole enclosure sounding at once: drip on metal, drip on leaf, hidden water roaring in a shaft, pipe-gurgle, pulley-creak, reed-hiss, broad leaves rasping together, relay tick, pump stroke, the faint almost-speech of bubbles moving under mud. It speaks in processes rather than words. It says: remain wet. It says: close the opening. It says: use what is left. It says: nothing merely ends.

So the inhabitant of that place is a slow green factory-creature, a marshbound vegetal engine living off water, rust, electricity, and decay. It is a community that has become an organ. It is a ruin that has learned metabolism. It is not abandoned, not haunted, not wild in the old sense.

It is occupied by the thing the place became when every leftover function found a root.
