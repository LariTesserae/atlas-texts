What lives there is not one species but a whole under-ecology of things that have accepted machinery as weather.

The most common inhabitants are the workers, though they are less “native” than recurring elements in the system. They move through the region like a migratory maintenance fauna: insulated, hooded, lamp-faced, gloved so thickly their hands seem ceremonial. Their coats are patched with department colors dulled by condensation. Their boots are black with wet concrete dust. They carry tools on chest harnesses, radios sealed in rubber, tablets fogged at the edges, little chemical heat-packs tucked against their wrists. In the dimness, their faces appear only briefly—inside vehicle cabs, behind scratched visors, under the yellow refuge lights of shelters during ceiling-rain.

They speak quietly because the place is already speaking at full volume. They have learned the timings the way farmers once learned weather: west sump purge, roof-rain, compressor sleep, valve-thaw, shift-fog, the hour when the eastern channels reverse. They know which rails can be touched, which ladders sweat acid, which warning lights mean “leave now” and which are only old alarms repeating a condition that has existed for years. They read mineral stains like maps. They smell overheating insulation before instruments register it. Some can identify distant districts by vibration through a boot sole.

They are not sentimental about the place, but they are superstitious in practical ways. No one whistles in the late-sound rooms. No one sleeps facing a viewpoint. Chalk marks are renewed even after digital signs are installed. A forgotten cup on a control box is not moved until its owner is known, because every abandoned object might be an informal warning. The crews joke about Children’s Heat, but not when passing the arrows to it.

There are permanent residents among them, or nearly permanent ones: sump wardens, condensate farmers, corrosion readers, archive pump clerks, ice-scrapers, valve listeners, cable swallows’ keepers. These people have the pale, reddened look of those who live under lamps and cold drafts. Their eyes adjust too well to amber gloom. When they go above, if they go above, daylight makes them blunt and irritable. Open sky seems to them not freedom but exposure, a terrifying absence of ceiling.

Some were born on the surface and descended for wages. Some were reassigned and never quite rose again. Some belong to families that have worked in the under-region for generations, though “generation” is an imprecise word here: time is counted in maintenance cycles, outages, floods, retrofits, purges. They inherit routes rather than land, passwords rather than heirlooms, knowledge of dangerous valves rather than stories of hills and rivers.

But the workers are not the deepest inhabitants.

The place itself is lived in by a sparse, exact life that has become intimate with metal.

The black moss along the seams is among the oldest visible forms. It grows in ruler-straight lines where condensation repeats itself dependably, feeding on concrete salts, soot, iron dust, microscopic oils. In some corridors it forms commas and dashes, as if an invisible clerk has annotated the architecture. When rain passes, the moss darkens and swells; when frost returns, it tightens into a lacquered thread. Workers scrape it away from sensor housings and gasket seats, but leave it in harmless places because its presence reveals dampness better than instruments. The moss is a gauge, a script, a slow intelligence of moisture.

Around heat leaks live pale lichens, faint circular blooms on warm pipe jackets, transformer housings, and the walls behind operator rooms. They glow only by contrast, cream against black grime, but under ultraviolet inspection they show rings of blue and sickly green. Their growth records fluctuations in warmth: outages, repairs, months of overuse. Some maintenance crews consult them unofficially, reading the lichen halos around sealed conduits to learn which lines still carry hidden heat.

In cracks where wet dust has become paste, the transparent grasses tremble. They are fragile, bluish-jointed things, more like instruments than plants. Their blades lean according to airflow. Their roots grip rust flakes and powdered cement. They never form meadows. They appear in notations: seven blades under Stair K-14, a trembling comb along Basin Nine, a little glassy tuft at the marsh-edge where warm seepage passes beneath a cold slab. If removed into ordinary soil, they die. They require the poverty of this place: mineral water, vibration, machine draft, almost no light.

In the channels live the white crustaceans.

They are flat, blind, and nearly translucent, with delicate legs that scull sideways over algae film and mineral silt. They gather where warm discharge meets cold spillwater, where edible films grow on submerged grates. Inspection lamps send them flickering away like loose chips of porcelain. Their bodies contain traces of copper, nickel, and cleaning solvents; they are adapted not to purity but to a precise contamination. When sump purges come, they wedge themselves beneath bolt heads and into the perforations of drainage plates. Afterward they emerge and resume their small grazing, undramatic survivors of engineered floods.

The moths are more mysterious. They haunt warm junction boxes and old sodium lamps, gray-winged, powder-bodied, trembling with cold. Their caterpillars, if anyone has seen them, are mistaken for insulation fibers or fungus threads. They feed perhaps on lichen, perhaps on glue, perhaps on the damp paper of abandoned labels. Their wings leave dust on warning signs and on the inside of electrical cabinets. Some workers claim the moths appear before pressure failures, drawn by static changes; others insist they simply follow warmth. Either way, a cluster of moths on a panel is treated as a minor omen. Someone will open the casing and check.

There are rats, though not many. They are long, dark, and water-wise, with sparse fur slicked by condensation. They nest in dry cable voids, feed on worker scraps, dead moths, packaging starch, and whatever organic film the drains provide. Many are scarred by frostbite. Some carry bits of colored wire in their nests. They are wary of purges and know the high routes. One may cross a catwalk in the far distance and vanish so quickly it seems invented by the eye.

There are also maintenance dogs, though calling them dogs requires generosity. Some are living animals bred for cold, dark, and noise: low-bodied, thick-coated, white-eyed from lamp glare, trained to locate leaks, bodies, overheating cables, and unauthorized nests. Others are partly prosthetic by long necessity—joint braces, heated paw guards, respirator collars, implanted locators blinking under shaved fur. They move with grave competence through ankle-deep water and steel clutter, ignoring machine thunder that would break ordinary animals. They are beloved in a restrained, embarrassed way. Their names are stenciled on lockers. Their deaths are entered in logs.

And then there are the drones.

The articulated cable-birds called swallows are everywhere above, though they are noticed mostly by sound: dry metallic flutter, clasping feet on cable, the occasional click of a corrosion probe. They fold themselves narrow to pass through racks, then open their segmented wings to stabilize under vents. Their heads are small clusters of lenses and brushes. Their bellies carry chemical sniffers. They peck at rust, photograph seams, sample frost, measure the thickness of things meant to remain load-bearing.

The workers call them swallows because the name makes them easier to tolerate. In large numbers they resemble a flock, especially when a purge alarm sends them lifting from the low girders all at once. Some have been in service beyond their intended life. Their movements grow eccentric. They return to obsolete charging rails. They inspect beams removed years ago. One or two have been seen dipping toward the marsh-edge as if drinking, though there is no function in them for thirst.

More obscure are the feral inspection units: old crawler drones, pipe-mappers, wheel-bodied surveyors, and soft-bellied sensor machines lost between systems but still powered by scavenged induction fields. They live in the infrastructural margins. A crawler may drag itself through a conduit once a month, its brushes grown thick with moss, still muttering location pings to a network that no longer records it. A wheeled unit may appear at the far end of a tunnel, pause as if surprised, then reverse into fog. Some have accumulated debris—fur, tape, thread, labels, mineral crust—until they look almost animal. Workers do not interfere unless the machines obstruct flow. Anything still moving may be performing some forgotten task.

Beneath all of these lives the larger inhabitant: the under-region’s automatic will.

It is not conscious in any clean sense, but it behaves. It keeps time. It reacts. It closes gates, opens valves, condenses water, drains basins, chills rails, warms sealed rooms, rings sirens from district to district. It has reflexes older than many of its crews. It remembers instructions no one living understands. It preserves useless cycles because those cycles are coupled to vital ones. It purges because pressure rises. It rains because heat must go somewhere. It grows frost because machines steal warmth. It builds weather out of error and necessity.

The workers live inside this will the way coastal people live with tides.

There are settlements, though they do not look like towns. Human habitation is tucked into the intervals between systems: stacked bunk-cabins behind compressor courts, insulated dormitories bolted above dry sumps, mess rooms inside old control galleries, clinics beside battery exchanges, prayer alcoves made from abandoned relay closets, laundry racks strung near heat vents, schools-in-shifts held in windowless rooms where maps of the surface curl from damp walls. A lived-in district announces itself by small improvised softness: taped photographs, mismatched curtains across instrument glass, rubber mats over grating, a kettle wired illegally to a junction box, a row of gloves drying on a warm pipe.

Children do live there, though fewer than rumor says. They are quick, quiet, and overinstructed. They learn hazard colors before letters. They know not to chase a dropped toy toward a channel. They can sleep through stamping presses and wake at an unfamiliar drip. Their games imitate maintenance: closing gates, tagging leaks, timing imaginary purges. They draw skies as flat blue ceilings unless taught otherwise. Some have never seen rain fall from clouds, only from trusses.

Perhaps that is Children’s Heat: a nursery district, a recovered warmth line, an old welfare system, a thermal exchange that once kept subterranean housing alive. Perhaps the words remain because somewhere behind sealed doors there are still radiators breathing out the waste warmth of a school, a hospital, a dormitory, a vanished refuge. Or perhaps the name means only that warmth itself is childish here—rare, protected, easily lost.

At the marsh-edge, the living things become stranger because categories loosen.

The white crustaceans grow larger in the half-flooded construction rooms. The black moss hangs in submerged strands from rebar. Transparent grasses root in dust collected on floating foam. Moths drift dead on still water, becoming islands for mineral bloom. Swallows descend low enough for their reflections to look like real birds flying beneath the surface. Workers move more slowly there, speaking less, because the basin is full of unfinished intentions. It is not yet infrastructure and not yet ruin. It invites occupation by whatever can endure incompletion.

There may be people there who are not on rosters.

Every large system produces its unofficial inhabitants: former workers who missed too many departures, salvage families, debt-avoiders, route hermits, children grown beyond records, technicians attached to machines no department claims. They know the dry ledges above the purge line. They harvest condensate, trap crustaceans, trade copper fragments, sleep near transformer warmth, and paint private signs over official ones. Their lamps are dimmer. Their clothing is made from insulation felt, tarp vinyl, uniform remnants, and cable ties. They can disappear into service voids faster than a supervisor can call their names.

To the registered crews they are nuisances, ghosts, necessary informants. They know which doors are welded only on one side. They know where water runs uphill before the shift change. They know which viewpoints show weather and which show only black. They know the taste of safe condensate and the smell of a bad battery room. If something vital is lost in a flooded district, one of them may find it first.

What lives there, then, is adaptation without flourishing.

Nothing there is abundant except water, metal, labor, and cold. Life survives by becoming narrow, timed, procedural. Moss writes itself only where the drip repeats. Crustaceans shelter only where purge hydraulics leave a bolt-shadow. Workers walk only the routes that have not betrayed them. Drones continue because charging rails still hum. Rats, lichens, children, dogs, moths, grasses—all of them inhabit small permissions granted accidentally by the machinery.

The place does not nurture them. It permits them under conditions.

Yet over time they have made a kind of belonging from those conditions. The workers name machines. The moss maps damp seams. The dogs dream under humming pipes. The swallows flock along cables in the artificial dusk. The children learn the rain schedule. The unregistered leave offerings of tape and chalk at dangerous doors. Even the automatic systems, blind and cyclic, seem to have absorbed their inhabitants as variables: closing gates after sirens long enough for people to clear the channels, warming rooms never listed in modern plans, keeping certain lamps alive beyond their documented circuits.

No single creature owns the under-region. It is inhabited by a compact between wetness, cold, machinery, and whatever life can become small enough, clever enough, or obedient enough to remain.

They live there as annotations on a world made for other purposes.

They are the mold in the seam, the dog at the valve, the pale child counting purge lights, the drone fluttering under a beam, the worker with one hand on a frozen rail and the other raised to feel whether the ceiling-rain has begun.

They are what happens when an artificial lowland develops a climate, and the climate begins, reluctantly, to have citizens.
