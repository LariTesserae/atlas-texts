What lives there is not one creature but a tenancy: a whole drowned order, precise and opportunistic, arranged around pressure, vibration, shelter, and the surviving habits of machines.

The smallest inhabitants are almost the most numerous, though they are seldom noticed first. They live as films, veils, crusts, beads. Bacteria lace the rust and the mineral blooms. Algae glaze the lamps where a little light still leaks through, making greenish halos on glass already clouded by time. Slime thickens in seams, gathers under lips of metal, softens the edges of labels and screw heads. Worms live in pinholes and under old paint, withdrawing from the touch of current. Tiny white animals, almost grains until they move, graze along the pressure door’s seep, clustered there because something comes through from the other side: warmth, minerals, a trace of richer water, the faint continuous gift of elsewhere.

Shell life claims the structure with the patience of a second construction crew. Mussels fasten themselves in dense black-blue knots along grates, brackets, and shaded pipe runs. Barnacles whiten the underside of handrails and the lips of flanges. Limpets and snails browse the tiled walls, leaving delicate clean paths through the stain. Where men once sealed the chamber with gaskets and pitch, animals now seal it with shell, thread, and calcified persistence. They do not repair the station, exactly, but they give its failures a living lining.

Shrimp live in the pauses.

They hover behind instrument boxes, under loose plates, inside the square shadows cast by pipe supports. Their transparent bodies catch the lamps and disappear again, antennae writing nervous script into the water. When the turbines thud, they compress themselves into cracks. When bubbles climb from the ruptured valve, they come out to pick at the disturbed silt. They know the chamber as a map of leeward pockets, safe angles, soft deposits, and sudden betrayals. A click from a relay can empty an entire wall of them.

Crabs patrol the made surfaces as if they were natural rock. They step over bolts, cross warning stripes, climb mesh, test sediment with their claws. Some are mottled the color of corroded iron; some carry scraps of weed and sponge on their backs until they resemble broken pieces of the room itself. They gather at the pressure door’s seep, quarrel in slow motion beneath the valve wheels, and wedge themselves into cable trays when the current strengthens. Their lives have a bureaucratic quality: inspection, extraction, withdrawal, return.

The eels belong to the pipes.

They occupy intake mouths, drainage holes, broken conduits, and the dark round interiors of machinery whose purpose has been forgotten. Their heads appear from black circles like thoughts emerging from old systems. They open and close their jaws, not in threat but in respiration, and their eyes hold the dim light without seeming to reflect it. Some pipes contain only one eel, large and proprietary. Others have smaller ones braided deep inside, bodies vanishing through bends that no swimmer could follow. They have inherited the plumbing more completely than any other animal. The station’s veins are theirs now.

Fish make the open water visible.

Small silver fish collect around the remaining lights, turning together in pulses, making and unmaking a body larger than any one of them. They are not permanent in shape. They condense under a lamp, thread through a railing, burst apart at a pressure change, reform behind the turbine housing. In the green-black water they appear less like animals than like decisions being reconsidered. Their sides flash briefly, then go dull. They feed on whatever the light and seepage gather: larvae, specks, plankton, loosened life from the walls.

Larger fish move more slowly, as if they understand the dignity of weight. The broad mottled one beneath the central turbine casing is less a resident than a concealed event. It lies where rust, shadow, and silt make one color. Its fins barely stir. Smaller fish pass near it, misjudge it, forget it is separate from the machine. Then it opens into motion. For one second the whole chamber knows it: the lunge, the scattering, the impact of bodies against tile and grate. Afterward it settles back beneath the casing, and the room resumes around the absence it has made.

There are octopuses too, though not always the same ones.

They come through gaps no engineer intended for passage, pour themselves behind panels, beneath stair landings, into valve housings. Their arms appear first: pale, flexible, exploratory. They touch gauges whose needles still tremble, press suckers to enamel labels, open crab shells under a loose cover plate. They know corrosion from living shell, hollow from blocked, danger from mere movement. The chamber is suited to them because it is full of doors that no longer open and spaces that no longer belong to their original purpose. To an octopus, every failed mechanism is a question with food behind it.

They are the most curious inhabitants, but not fancifully so. Their intelligence is practical, tactile, unsentimental. They do not wonder what the station was. They know that a cable tray gives shade, that a cracked junction box can hold eggs, that the old stair has many undersides, that a twitching servo should be avoided until it has finished remembering work.

There may be seals sometimes, or something of that size, but only as visitors. The chamber is too cramped, too obstructed, too loud with old pressure to be a comfortable home for anything that must move broadly and breathe air. A seal might enter through a larger breach, hunt, nose the trapped air at the ceiling, disturb the schools, and leave. The true residents are those able to use narrowness, dimness, repetition.

The chamber itself also seems to live, though not as an animal.

Its life is residual and mechanical: the boom of the turbine beyond the wall, the cough of a pump, the tremble of a gauge needle, the exhausted twitch of a servo. These motions govern the animals as weather governs surface creatures. A relay click becomes alarm. A lamp warming by one degree becomes a feeding ground. A pipe’s vibration draws larvae, then growth, then grazers, then predators. The machines are no longer tools, but they are still ecological facts. They make rhythm, shelter, heat, hazard. They sort the living.

So the place is inhabited by a community that has mistaken nothing. It has not misunderstood the room as a reef or a cave; it has accepted it as what it now is: a drowned apparatus full of hollows, edges, weak light, mineral food, and predictable disturbances. Life here is not lush. It is edited, technical, almost austere. It does not cover the old human intention. It threads through it.

The residents are tenants of failure.

They live in the parts made useless to people: the pipe mouth, the gauge recess, the underside of a stair, the seep beneath a sealed door. They feed where warnings peel. They spawn in control boxes. They shelter behind manual overrides. They make nurseries of drains and hunting blinds of turbine casings. They have no reverence for the chamber, but their occupation gives it a strange composure, as if abandonment were not an ending but a change of management.

And under all of them, around all of them, is the water: the oldest resident, though it arrived after construction. It fills every office of the place. It reads every label with its slow pressure. It lifts sediment, lowers it, carries larvae in, takes flakes of paint away. It touches the rust-colored fish and the mussel seam and the fixed wheel of the pressure door with the same cool patience.

What lives there, finally, is a drowned ecology of use without purpose: shell, slime, eel, crab, shrimp, fish, octopus, current, rust, and the stubborn half-life of machinery. A population assembled not by design but by available shelter. A society of the narrow gap and the failing light.
