What lives there is not a single people, nor a single kingdom of beasts, but a whole civilization of patience: animals, plants, stones, shadows, fossils, and a few quiet hand-making beings all sharing one ancient habit of endurance.

The inhabitants have become less like creatures *in* a landscape than like functions of it.

The tall salt-rasping ungulates are among the most visible. They stand on the brittle flats like narrow monuments, ribbed, parchment-colored, with wide cloven hooves and bony muzzles made for scraping mineral skin from basins where no water ever gathered. Their herds do not flow; they accumulate. Generations may have used the same crossings so faithfully that the crust has learned their weight. Their calves grow in the shadow under their mothers, and the mothers themselves seem half-built from pale rock, dust settling along their spines until their outlines blur into the flats behind them.

Around and beneath them lives a second, denser nation: small runners, armored crawling mammals, translucent-throated lizards, blind velvet diggers, insects folded into stone cracks like mica scales. These are not scavengers in the frantic sense. They are minute custodians. They browse films, polish bones, clear burrows, inhabit carcasses without haste. A dead animal is not consumed so much as reassigned. First it remains standing. Then it becomes shelter. Then perch, salt source, shade marker, insect city, lichen trellis, cairn.

The birds are almost priests of the place, though nothing here would name them that. They occupy ledges, hut roofs, towers, sky-surfaces, and impossible perches. They are quiet enough to seem ceremonial. They click, scrape, descend one note at a time. Some have learned to stand upside down against the blue, feeding on whatever gathers on the underside of the sky. They remember directional taboos. They announce danger not by alarm, but by unanimity: all heads turned the same way, all doorways suddenly made questionable.

The predators are patient beyond hunger. Ash-gray cats, clay-cracked and long-bodied, sleep at the mouths of unused dwellings or beside ancestral paths, becoming part of the architecture until prey forgets they are not stone. They do not chase much. Chasing would belong to a wetter, younger world. They wait where bodies must eventually pass. The mantid things are even more nearly mineral: stalk-limbed, blade-armed, indistinguishable from thorn and shale until their heads adjust by a few degrees. Their violence is precise, infrequent, and already included in the peace of the plateau.

The plants are not greenery but contracts. Thorn mats stitched into cracks. Brittle jointed stalks with ceramic cups. Dry lichens that clench when touched, slowly enough to make touch feel like an event from another century. They do not promise abundance. They gather salt, heat, old dung, mineral trace, and sun-angle. They survive not by growing upward into weather, but by making themselves small enough for a world without weather to overlook.

And among all this, there are people, or something close to people.

They are few now, or else so well adapted that they no longer present themselves plainly. They are probably narrow, dry-bodied, dust-colored, with long hands and careful feet. Some have more fingers than expected, unless the handprints lie, or unless “expected” is a lowland prejudice. Their faces would be wrapped or shaded, not against storms but against glare, powder, and the dangerous intimacy of looking too quickly at impossible things. They would move in pauses. They would seldom gesture broadly. A child here would be trained first not in speech, but in discrimination: which crust has been tested by hooves, which echo is safe to ignore, which bone must not be moved, which empty basin is truly empty only from above.

Their dwellings show no desire to conquer the plateau. They are not fortresses. They are arrangements. Low stone rooms tucked into ledges. Shade cells. storage jars sealed with clay. Bone ladders pegged into cliff steps. Walkways of shale. Towers offered to birds. Entrances left for burrowers. Walls that steer herds rather than exclude them. A village here is not a human claim against wilderness; it is a negotiated widening in the traffic of animals.

These people would possess a technology of exact sufficiency. Scrapers, awls, blades of stone, worked bone, thorn-fiber rope, sinew lashings, fired bowls, hide screens, mineral pigments. Their hearths may be ashless because flame is rare, unnecessary, or too transformative. Their chimneys are for light, angled to the known sun. They cultivate, if that is the word, pale mineral-fed fungi in chambers where warmth and shadow arrive on schedule. They store salts as carefully as grain. They read cracks as others read weather.

They do not have seasons. They have returns.

The return of a certain bird to a certain hole.  
The day a cup-plant opens a finger-width early.  
The molt of the mica insects.  
The widening of a salt fracture.  
The first calf of a herd born under the east ledge.  
The shadow of an extinct thing appearing beside a stone at noon.  
The interval between one harmless contradiction and another.

Their laws are practical, inherited, unsentimental. They know that not every impossibility is dangerous. Some are merely local. Some are useful. Some must be greeted with silence. They teach children to look through perforated cairns before passing, to wait if the self appears on the far side, to avoid answering repeated sounds, to follow animals over flats and humans along edges. Their wisdom is not mystical in their own minds. It is route knowledge.

They may have stories, but the stories would not be dramatic. Drama implies change arriving from outside. Their myths would concern arrangements: why the first herds learned the safe crusts; why birds may stand on the sky but people may not; why fossils remember forward; why a doorway facing the same direction as all nearby birds should be left alone. Their ancestors are perhaps not dead in any simple way. Some are silhouettes inside bisected huts, seated in absence. Some are handprints pressed from the inner side of stone. Some may be reachable only by waiting at a cairn until the wrong distance repeats correctly.

They likely speak softly, if at all. A language suited to this plateau would have many words for stillness, for kinds of dryness, for old polish, for safe and unsafe shadows. It would distinguish between a hollow that is shallow, a hollow that only looks shallow, a hollow that is deep but permits crossing, and a hollow that is hungry. It would have verbs for the movements of things that do not seem to move: dust settling, lichen tightening, a predator deciding, a herd changing its mind over several days.

But the people are not masters of the place. They are one guild among many.

The ungulates know surfaces better.  
The birds know orientation better.  
The burrowers know coolness better.  
The insects know death’s interior uses better.  
The fossils know what has not happened yet.  
The stones know which shadows belong to them and which do not.

Everything living there participates in a shared restraint. Birth happens, but not as expansion. Hunting happens, but not as disruption. Building happens, but not as conquest. Death happens, but not as decay. Even the contradictions of reality do not erupt; they persist as features, as dependable as ledges.

The true inhabitant of the plateau may be equilibrium itself: an old, dry, exact intelligence distributed through hoof-paths, salt crusts, bird ranks, handprints, cracked bones, and quiet rules.

It lives as herds that barely advance.  
As cats asleep for days beside a path.  
As lizards on altars.  
As people repairing the same rope ladder their grandparents repaired.  
As plants opening their cups to the same angle of sun.  
As birds pecking at the blue underside of heaven.  
As shadows arriving early and being given room.  
As fossils stirring when their living counterparts pass.

Nothing here is alone, and nothing here is in a hurry to become something else. The inhabitants have survived by surrendering ambition to precision. They do not thrive in the lush sense. They persist in the geological sense.

They are the patient living remainder of a world after ending: dry, crowded, exact, and calm.
