What lives there is not a population so much as a pressure—life packed so densely into dryness that it has become climate, masonry, rumor, and hunger.

The basin’s first inhabitants are the growths themselves: the dry green makers, the wax-skinned and salt-veined plants that are not quite plants. They do not drink. They conduct. They feed on heat, mineral radiation, friction, charge, animal passage, powdered remains, and the faint sift of light leaking through the roof of the world. Their bodies are built from brittleness and tension: gas-swollen chambers, resinous membranes, carbon threads, powdered nutrient veins, crystal nodes, insulating dead sheaths layered over living cores.

They are the ground underfoot, the bridges overhead, the false walls, the listening crusts, the curtains, the traps, the plazas, the lamps.

Some are quick and small: blue-white films that lie flush against the floor until a warm body pauses above them; bronze leaf-plates that grow overnight across a trail; black thermal fronds that slowly turn their faces toward unseen vents. Others are massive, slower only by comparison: trunk-vaults wide enough to shelter caravans, ribbed chimneys that exhale pollen storms, root-cables stretched so taut between cliffs that they sing when touched.

They do not merely occupy the cavern. They enlarge it. Their root-fingers wedge into stone, pry open gypsum, peel basalt, stitch cracks, brace collapses, and make paths before any hand has the idea of a road. They are builders without plans, or with plans too distributed to be recognized as such. A bridge may be a feeding organ. A corridor may be a digestive experiment. A plaza may be a scar held open by repeated grazing.

Beneath them, deeper than the visible thickets and crusts, live the old root-minds: regional bodies spread through warm underlayers, too broad to meet as individuals. They taste mineral seams. They feel herds as tremors. They conduct decisions through pulses of pressure, electrical irritation, scent, and the slow arithmetic of tension. When one of them wakes, flowers appear kilometers away. When one tightens, ledges split. When one fruits, animals migrate before knowing they have been summoned.

They may not think in words, but they remember in pattern: where feet pass, where salt is exposed, where a predator fed well, where water once fell and killed a grove.

Among this architecture move the grazers.

The commonest are the basket-beasts: pale, many-jointed animals with long stilt legs and open rib cages full of symbiotic fronds. At a distance they look unfinished, like skeletons woven into wicker panniers. Their bodies are dry-scaled and dust-colored, always stained by the groves they have passed through—copper, chalk, green-black, rust-orange. Their sideways jaws rasp leaf-plates, salt blooms, wax curls, and mineral-rich sheaths. The fronds in their ribs help them process what they eat, exchanging heat and charge for nutrient dust.

They travel in loose, clattering herds. Their young shelter inside the elders’ rib-baskets when storms rise. Their dead are rarely allowed to touch the floor for long; opportunistic vines lift them into hanging nurseries, where larvae, mites, and seedlings inherit the body.

There are heavier grazers too, shell-backed and low, like walking millstones plated in horn. They grind old stalks into meal and leave behind broad migration grooves that later harden into roads. Their mouths are circular rasps. Their dung is not wet but compacted cakes of mineral-rich powder, prized by insects, people, and certain patient plants.

There are spindle herds: narrow, high-shouldered things moving on dozens of clicking legs, cropping ceiling curtains by rearing in coordinated waves. Their hides collect static until they crackle blue in darkness. Predators follow them because their passage wakes dormant pods.

Above the floor drift the ballooning animals.

Some are fruit-sized, almost invisible except where their veined sacs catch amber light. Some are broad as tents, translucent and slow, with digestive threads hanging beneath them like delicate fishing lines. They live in the warm updrafts, feeding on pollen, seed-flour, tiny flyers, and each other’s shed films. They rarely land; the floor is too full of mouths.

When startled, they jet sideways on bitter gas, bouncing off curtains, rupturing pods, tangling in root-bridges. In storm hours they rise by the thousands, forming pale constellations in the haze. Young ballooners hatch from hard mineral seeds lodged in warm cracks, inflate over several days, and lift free once their internal gases ripen.

Some larger kinds carry riders unintentionally: mites, threadworms, seed-clamps, tiny parasitic wasps, and occasionally human charms tied to their trailing filaments to mark air currents.

The insects, or things close enough to insects to earn the name, are the true multitude.

They live in rivers, sheets, knots, blooms, and sudden black boils. Lacquer-backed cutters march along root-bridges in braided lanes, each carrying flakes of plant-skin or crystal sugar. Amber-jawed dust-packers bite grooves into living tissue and fill them with mineral powder, inducing the growth of chambers useful to their colonies. White eyeless borers move through dead sheaths by sound. Copper-winged shriekers gather around vents and scream at pressure changes before the basin roars.

Their nests are seldom separate from the plants. They persuade living surfaces to swell, fold, blister, hollow, or scar. A colony may begin as a chemical wound and become a city of nodules, galleries, brood-combs, fungusless farms, and dry granaries packed with seed-dust. Some insects farm scale-creatures beneath leaves, milking oils rather than nectar. Some herd mites across grazing animals. Some cultivate explosive seed-pods as defenses, stroking them with tuned antennae until they remain armed but do not fire.

There are termites of salt, glass-bodied aphids, thorn-legged mantids, moths with powder wings that disintegrate at mating, beetles whose wing cases produce sparks in dust storms, and blind locusts that navigate by tasting old echoes trapped in fibrous bowls.

Predators live everywhere, though few advertise themselves.

Stem-hunters resemble fallen bundles of dry vegetation until they open. Their limbs unfold one after another, too many and too quietly, and then they run flat across the crust without disturbing dust. They prey on young grazers, workers at the edge of human shelters, and ballooners forced down by bad air.

Ceiling hookers hang among root-filaments like knots of shadow. Their bodies are narrow and calcified, their forelimbs jointed into barbed drops. They fish in swarms, lowering hooks into clouds of wings, then hauling up struggling clusters to be processed against their mouth-plates.

There are throatless mimics that hunt by vibration. They pluck plant-cables to imitate ripening, distress, mating calls, or the low comfort-hum of a safe herd. Grazers enter the corridor. The walls tighten. The mimic feeds only after the plants have crushed the prey, licking nutrient dust from the seams.

Other predators are almost botanical. Pit rosettes form beautiful arrangements of coppery leaves around warm hollows. Their interiors are lined with static hairs. Small animals descend toward the heat and cannot climb back through the clinging charge. Over days, rasping root-teeth mill them into meal.

Thread lions live in chaff dunes, their bodies a mat of pale tendrils around a hidden mouth. They wait for footsteps, then rise as a net.

Among the dead and nearly dead are the millers: beetles, raspers, root-teeth, mites, shell-worms, and herds of small chitinous mouths that reduce everything into usable powder. There is little rot here. A corpse dries, cracks, is scraped, lifted, bored, powdered, sorted. Bone becomes tools or calcium dust. Hide becomes shelter or larval casing. Horn becomes blade. Memory becomes meal.

The scavengers are therefore also archivists. What they cannot eat, they distribute. A dead grazer may become a bridge coating, a nursery wall, an insect road, a human mask, a seed trigger, and a faint smell in the air that guides predators for weeks.

The air itself is inhabited.

It carries seed-dust with static hairs, drifting reproductive engines, scent-codes, electric irritations, shed cuticle, powdered salt, animal musk, microscopic hooks, and spores that are not wet spores but mineral intentions. Tiny flyers swim through it in such numbers that open space has texture. Some live their entire lives without touching a surface, hatching from dust motes, mating in turbulence, and dying into the same current.

During pressure storms, the air becomes a body shared by the basin. Plants open vents. Animals rise, flee, couple, molt, or feed. Insects pour up walls. Seed-pods fire. Ballooners collide. Human shelters seal their cracks. The old root-minds pulse below. For an hour, every organism becomes part of one dry respiratory act.

Then the storm passes, and the survivors inhabit a slightly different world.

People live there too, but carefully.

They are not masters of the basin. They are tolerated negotiators, cutters of temporary absences, keepers of thresholds. They wrap themselves in wax-glazed fiber veils and dust-cloaks. Their eyes are darkened against glare. Their lips and nostrils are protected by filters of woven filament and resin. Skin exposed too long becomes seeded, irritated, claimed.

Their settlements are agreements rather than possessions: a chamber kept open by daily scraping; a salt-brick hut rebuilt every season as the wall behind it grows; a platform lashed to a mineral pillar with living rope that must be fed smoke and bitter oils so it does not constrict; a cleared shrine around an ancient seed-stone polished by hands, hooves, and mandibles alike.

They carry hooked poles to test crusts and pull aside curtains, seed rattles to warn pods or placate guard-insects, resin plugs for storms, obsidian blades, bone needles, thorn combs, and sealed moisture pouches treated with the gravity of relics. Water is medicine, oath, threat, emergency, and sometimes execution. To spill it carelessly is to invite bloom, poison, panic, or taboo.

Their maps are cords: knots for bridges, roughened sections for chaff dunes, scent-stains for predator corridors, embedded seeds for seasonal hazards. Drawn maps fail because the cavern revises itself too quickly.

They speak with hands in loud zones, with touch in storm hours, and with song only where it is safe to let vibration travel. Children learn early not to shout under pods, not to linger on warm floors, not to trust a new bridge, and never to cut a vine without knowing where its tension sleeps.

Some clans maintain relationships with insect colonies, feeding them powdered meal in exchange for guarded ramps. Some harvest luminous parchment growths from walls. Some herd basket-beasts through safe corridors. Some specialize in catching edible dust on hide screens. Others are seed-brokers, resin-smokers, storm-listeners, bridge-testers, or keepers of forbidden moisture.

Their dead are wrapped dry and suspended where chosen vines can take them. To be milled into the basin is not always a horror. It can be a return, a contract fulfilled.

There are also stranger residents at the edges of notice.

Sound-keepers live in fibrous hollows where cries collect and later emerge. Whether they are animals, plant-organs, or acoustic accidents is debated by anyone who survives them. They release old herd calls, false footsteps, human weeping, mating shrieks, warning songs from years before. Predators use them. Travelers fear them.

Imitation pods germinate into replicas of touch: a hoof made of green tissue, a hand with no bones, a beetle too still, a tool grown uselessly from a seed. Some people read these as omens. Some insects use them as decoys. The root-minds may use them as questions.

Black-leaf fields arrange themselves into patterns when swarms pass overhead. Their surfaces flip and tilt, making temporary script out of shadowless light. No one agrees whether the fields are recording, luring, calculating, or merely reacting. Human lineages have spent generations copying the patterns into knot-cords and still cannot decide if they are warnings or weather.

Deep cracks hold older, hotter beings: pale thermal worms armored in salt; gas-breathing blind things that surface only during pressure falls; larval chambers that open once in a century and empty winged adults into the basin by the million. There are stories of creatures that live beneath the vegetative crust entirely, grazing on roots the way surface herds graze leaves, their backs mistaken for warm ground until they move.

And everywhere, in every niche, live the small dependencies: eyelid mites, knee-worms, belly seed-carriers, hide-lice, dust minnows, scale colonies, filament fleas, resin bees, static gnats, salt leeches that drink charge instead of blood, and pale larvae that wait inside old pods listening for the footfall that means world.

What lives there is therefore a civilization without a single species to claim it.

A basket-beast is also a garden.  
A bridge is also a muscle.  
A house is also a wound held open.  
A storm is also a mating.  
A corpse is also construction material.  
A plant is also a road, a trap, a lung, a law.  
A person is also a temporary clearing.

The basin’s inhabitants all share one discipline: they know how to live without softness. They have replaced water with heat, wax, charge, dust, gas, pressure, and appetite. They bloom by abrasion. They communicate through tension. They build by swelling and splitting. They remember through residues.

Nothing there simply lives in the place.

Everything lives as part of the place’s ongoing attempt to become larger, louder, greener, drier, and less finished.
