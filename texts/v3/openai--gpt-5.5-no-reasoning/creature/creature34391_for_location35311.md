What lives there is not a people in the usual sense, though there are builders among them, or inheritors of building, or animals whose habits have hardened into architecture.

The basin is inhabited by a whole heat-bound community: a crowded, plantless fauna adapted to stone, salt, darkness, and dry breath. They are creatures of pressure, mineral, friction, and stored warmth. Most are low to the ground. Most are armored or plated or jointed. Many are blind, or nearly blind. Their bodies read the world through heat gradients, vibration, gas taste, and the remembered polish of paths worn over generations.

There are the mineral-grazers, broad and many-legged, that move slowly across the basin floor with the patience of tools. Their mouths are fitted with scraping plates or rasping rims, and they feed on the black films that grow on shaded stone. Where they pass, they leave bright arcs and crescents on salt and slag, as if someone had polished the rock with a dull blade. Their shells are dark, but along the edges they are often powdered white with salt. Some carry colonies of smaller life in the seams of their armor: mites, spore-crusts, pale hitchhikers that clean their joints.

There are file-runners, narrow animals with overlapping backs and precise legs. They travel in repeated lines, one after another, always along the same heated cracks. Their routes are so fixed that the ground has accepted them as features, grooved and smoothed by countless passages. They do not call. They communicate by pauses, taps, and the brief touching of antennae or facial fringes. When danger crosses a path, the whole file stops as one body, then spills sideways into cracks too thin for larger hunters.

There are pale chimney-dwellers, blind, jointed, and rootlike, though there are no roots to resemble. They live in the holes of the salt chimneys, extending their fringed heads into the air to taste minerals, heat, and breath. In colonies, they open and seal their holes in waves, producing a silent rippling effect across a tower of stone. Their bodies are translucent enough that swallowed dust can be seen inside them as faint gray threads. They may be prey, but they are not helpless; at a nearby tremor they vanish instantly, and some passages close behind them with hardened plates like eyelids.

There are heat-crouchers, heavy squat beasts that spread their bellies against warm ground. They seem almost architectural when at rest, like dark bolsters or fallen blocks, until their side vents open and release a slow dry exhalation. Their skins are thick and cracked like old leather. They gather near vents when the temperature rises but must retreat before the ground becomes lethal. In the cooling hours they radiate stored heat, and smaller creatures sometimes cluster near them, risking being eaten for the chance to survive the cold.

There are basalt-flat predators, slab-colored animals that mimic pieces of broken volcanic stone. Their bodies are thin, hinged, and patient. They may wait for days beside a grazer’s route or under a dusting of powdered salt. When prey crosses them, they fold upward and inward, making no leap, no chase, only a sudden correction in the shape of the ground. What was pavement becomes mouth. What was shadow becomes enclosure.

There are hook-footed wall hunters, climbers that live along the steep basin walls and the undersides of basalt shelves. They hang inverted in the dark, gripping with curved claws or adhesive pads tough enough not to blister on heated rock. Their bodies are narrow and ribbed. Their heads turn slowly, listening downward. They take the unwary from vertical paths: chimney-burrowers, runners, young grazers, even hatchlings from nests built too near the wall. They are among the few animals with visible eyes, though the eyes are dull and ember-reflective rather than bright.

There are dust-catchers, animals that open dorsal slits during pressure flows. They stand motionless while salt dust slides through the basin, filtering useful mineral particles from air too dry to breathe comfortably. Some resemble folded shields on stilts. Others are wormlike and bury themselves, leaving only horns, vanes, or sensory combs exposed. After dustfall, the ground seems freshly patterned with half-buried life.

There are shell-bearers and draggers, whose undersides polish the old paths. There are burrow-mouthed things that live beneath the clinker and feed upward through cracks. There are small jointed scavengers that dismantle the dead before decay can soften them. There are molters that wedge themselves into triangular openings in the old stone stacks and split out of their hardened skins by rubbing for hours against the same polished edge. There are nest-keepers, not quite social and not quite solitary, who guard hollows full of mineral-coated eggs. There are parasites that live in side vents, heat blisters, mouth plates, and shell seams.

The simplest lives of the basin are not plants, but they form the base of everything. Black mineral films grow under stones and near warm cracks. Pale rubbery knots occupy cavities and exude spores, salts, or edible crust. These organisms feed on heat, chemical seepage, gases from below, and the slow offerings of dead bodies. They do not make the basin lush. They make it usable. They are the first skin over the stone, and the grazers harvest them constantly.

The inhabitants have no softness to spare. Their bodies keep moisture hidden deep inside. Skins are sealed, plated, waxed, scaled, or mineralized. Many excrete dry pellets or salt flakes. Blood, if spilled, clots quickly into dark paste. Eggs are armored. Young emerge already tough, small versions of endurance. Death is stripped clean, and bones, shells, plates, and horns become part of the terrain, sometimes fused into arches, frames, shelters, or mineral growths.

Some animals use the old structures. Some may have made them.

The small black stone stacks with triangular openings are occupied by creatures of many sizes. A solitary ambush animal may lie within one, its mouth just behind the entrance. A colony of grain-sized runners may fill another, erupting and withdrawing like spilled ash. Larger beasts guard stacks they could not possibly have built, suggesting the structures are older than the current owners. The arch-like frames of mineral and bone may be accidental accumulations, but they recur too often to feel entirely accidental. Something here understands placement, if not design: shade made by weight, passage made by repeated use, shelter improved by pushing one more plate into place.

If there is intelligence in the basin, it is not loud. It is tactile, inherited, and local. It may belong to the animals that arrange stones by leaning against them generation after generation. It may belong to colony-creatures whose collective routes produce walls and openings without any single mind intending architecture. It may belong to vanished makers, with present life merely inhabiting their remains. Or it may be that the distinction between builder and dweller has eroded here, just as the structures have eroded into geology.

Everything lives by austerity.

Shade is property. Cool stone is wealth. A crack of the right temperature is a nursery, trap, refuge, and battlefield. The dead are resources. Molted skins are eaten. Breath is information. Vibration is warning. A careless call is an invitation to be found. So the basin’s life keeps quiet. It scrapes, taps, clicks, exhales, folds, withdraws, and resumes.

The dominant impression is of an ecosystem packed to its limit. There is no empty niche, no unused crevice. Every patch of ground has a history of occupation. Every route has been worn by bodies. Every shelter has been claimed by something, abandoned, fouled, cleaned, inherited, and claimed again. The animals are not visitors to the basin; they are its moving surface. They make the hollow seem less like a barren volcanic bowl than a sealed organ: dry, hot, dark, crowded with specialized forms, and alive in nearly every crack.

They are creatures of the crowded dark: mineral-fed grazers, silent runners, heat-hoarding crouchers, pale chimney colonies, slab predators, wall hunters, dust-buriers, scavengers, molters, nest-guards, and perhaps old builders whose intentions have become indistinguishable from shelter.

They live without green things, without rain, almost without sound.

They live by heat, stone, salt, touch, and patience.
