The ones who live there are not cave-people in the old story sense. They are not pale, blind, secret, or estranged from the sun. They are hill people who have learned to turn the underside of land into dwelling. They are practical before they are strange, and whatever strangeness they have comes from habit pressed long enough against earth.

They are compact in their movements. Even the tall among them learn early to duck without thinking, to turn a shoulder before a tight bend, to feel a ceiling’s height in the skin of the scalp. Their bodies carry the town’s architecture in muscle memory: one hand lifted before a low lintel, feet placed along the raised dry edge of a passage, elbows tucked in crowded stairwells, voices lowered automatically beneath certain roofs. Children learn this before they learn many words. They learn that space belongs to everyone and to no one, that a person passing with water jars has right of way, that one does not stop beneath a weak chalk seam, that a body must become narrow in public ways.

They tend to be wiry rather than large, not from any inborn smallness but from the life of carrying, bending, climbing, bracing, hauling baskets, working in half-light. Their hands are the most expressive part of them: nicked, broad-knuckled, clay-packed, quick with gestures. They point with two fingers toward wet cracks, tap warnings on doorposts, sign across noisy work courts, measure walls by span and thumb, test plaster with the heel of a palm. Their fingernails are often rimmed in pale chalk or brown clay. Lime roughens their skin. Smoke yellows the edges of sleeves. Wool grease, ash, damp earth, and lamp fat cling to them.

Their clothing is layered and serviceable. Wool tunics patched at shoulder and knee; hide vests darkened by wear; wrapped leggings against the chill of lower passages; belts crowded with knife, awl, cord, tally stick, lamp hook, bone needle case. In the colder months many wear felted caps or hoods that make them look still more burrow-shaped, moving through amber lamplight with their heads tucked. The outer garments smell of rain, goats, smoke, and clay. Fine clothing exists, but it is rarely flowing or impractical. Decoration is compressed: a carved toggle, dyed thread at a cuff, a bronze pin, beads stitched inside a collar where they will not catch on wicker screens.

Their faces are weathered in two different ways. Those who work above have wind-reddened cheeks, narrowed eyes, cracked lips from the upland. Those who spend most time below have softer pallor under the grime, eyes accustomed to reading low contrast: damp darkening in plaster, a faint smoke backdraft, a trickle where no trickle should be. Nearly everyone can move between light and shadow with patience. They pause at shaft mouths, not dramatically, simply giving their sight time to change.

They are not afraid of the underground, but they respect it with something deeper than fear. To them the hill is not dead matter. It is not exactly alive either. It is a body of tendencies: certain seams drink, certain ceilings sulk after rain, certain roots should be thanked or left alone, certain hollows remember water. The people speak of walls almost as neighbors. “That west face is restless.” “The yellow clay is holding.” “The chalk is lying today.” “The old root has taken the weight.” Such phrases are not metaphor to them, not entirely. They are the ordinary language of survival.

Their households are close, materially and emotionally. Privacy exists, but it is thin and negotiated: a hanging mat, a turned shoulder, a lowered voice, a sleeping shelf with a curtain, a niche claimed by generations of hands. A family’s wealth is partly measured by dry storage, by a dependable smoke draw, by an inherited chamber whose roof has never complained. Space is inheritance. A good alcove, a safe shelf, a seep jar that fills in spring, a right to use a certain drying rack near a shaft: these are things people quarrel over, marry into, remember bitterly.

They are makers because the town allows no other kind of person for long. Even the very old sort wicks, mend baskets, watch drips, teach symbols to children, strip fibers, mind embers, cut mold from cheese, recite which passages flood. Children stomp straw into plaster almost as play. Adolescents carry spoil, polish lamp horn, twist rope, chase chickens from the shafts, learn to listen for rats, learn which adults may be mocked and which must never be interrupted when testing a wall. Every inhabitant is at least partly builder, plumber, porter, fire-keeper, and witness.

Some among them are specialists, though specialization remains porous.

There are diggers, whose status is higher than outsiders might expect. They know the color of safe clay, the sound of a pick entering a hidden gravel pocket, the smell of damp before seepwater appears. Their ears are trained. They place a cheek or brow against a wall and listen while others fall silent. They are not merely laborers but interpreters. A reckless digger can kill a household; a good one can make a lineage’s future.

There are shorers and timber-judges, jealous of beams and ruthless about waste. They know which trunk may bend, which must not be trusted after beetles, which old lintel can be turned and used again. They have arguments that outlast marriages. They mourn good timber burned by fools.

There are water-keepers, often older women but not only women, who oversee cistern lids, settling jars, channel rights, and the moral order of drainage. They can shame a grown man into clearing a clogged groove. They know whose children muddy the jars, whose household takes more than it carries, who has been rinsing hides too near a clean runnel. Their authority is quiet until challenged, and then it becomes communal law.

There are potters and kiln-tenders, smoky-eyed and patient, living near heat that never quite warms them enough. They shape the settlement’s daily vessels: seep jars, lamp cups, drain collars, storage crocks, little offering bowls placed in wall niches. Their quarter smells different—hotter, denser, mineral, almost dry. They are accused of hoarding good clay and of knowing more than they say about certain sealed chambers.

There are weavers and mat-makers, whose work is everywhere and therefore half-invisible. They make the town’s softness and its boundaries: screens, sacks, bedding, reed doors, carrying straps, wattle, rope, filters, ladder lashings. Without them, there would be no walls smooth enough to live beside, no privacy, no grain carried safely, no drains screened against leaves. Their fingers move constantly, even while talking.

There are surface-goers: herders, thorn-cutters, grass-gatherers, hunters, traders, people who know weather before it reaches the shafts. They descend smelling of wind and open cold. They bring news from the bright world: a fox den, a broken path, strangers on the ridge, frost coming, a tree fallen in the sparse grove, the possibility of barter. Below, they seem loud. Above, perhaps, the town-dwellers seem hunched and blinking. But they are the same people, divided by daily task rather than tribe.

There are shrine-keepers too, though their religion is practical and embedded. They tend low places where grey daylight falls on clay figures, root bundles, smooth stones, ancestor pins, tiny bowls of grain, scraps of wool, broken tools retired from use. Their spirits are household spirits, roof spirits, water-under-stone, the old dead sealed behind walls, the patience of clay, the danger of unsupported space. Prayers are brief and often sound like instructions: hold, drain, dry, let smoke rise, let the child’s fever pass, let the rain come gently, let the roof speak before it falls.

They are not gloomy people, though outsiders might think so. Their humor is dry, close, and situational. They laugh at echoes, at someone hitting the same low beam for the hundredth time, at goats eating legal notices, at children emerging from plaster work indistinguishable from lumps of wall. They tease newly married couples about finding room to quarrel. They imitate the solemn voices of planners whose measuring cords must bend around some grandmother’s ancient sleeping niche. They have songs, but many are made for enclosed spaces: low, rhythmic, good for work, with refrains that travel better than words.

Their music favors pulse over volume. Hand against beam. Knuckles on a storage chest. A loom’s clack worked into melody. Soft flutes of bone or reed, quiet enough not to anger sleepers. In upper courts during market days they may sing louder, but below their songs narrow themselves, winding through passages as humming, fragments, a remembered line. Children know tunes as routes: the stair song, the cistern song, the plaster-stomping song, the rain-watch song.

They are communal out of necessity, but not ideally harmonious. The town makes cooperation unavoidable; it does not make people kind. They feud over smoke, seepage, inheritance, stolen dry straw, misplaced waste, blocked corridors, noisy chickens, bad repairs, marriage promises, and whose grandfather first cut a chamber now needed for a public drain. They are expert at grievance. They remember exact inches: a shelf extended too far, a curtain hung across a common turning, a storage jar placed where a stretcher once had to pass. Their disputes are spatial because their lives are spatial.

Their law is embodied in maintenance. A person who lets a roof fail, fouls water, steals supports, or blocks a drain has not merely broken a rule but endangered the shared possibility of living under the hill. Punishment may be labor, loss of storage rights, public scolding, exclusion from a water point, or being assigned the filthiest clearing after rain. Shame matters because everyone sees the results of everyone’s negligence. Walls remember. So do neighbors.

They have leaders, but leadership is likely distributed: elders of chambers, water arbiters, master builders, market speakers, kin heads, ritual keepers. A person may be powerful in one court and irrelevant three turns away. Authority follows competence. During dry weeks, the water-keepers matter most. During expansion, the diggers and timber-judges. During sickness, herb women and bone-setters. During heavy rain, everyone listens to those who know the lower drains.

Their children are sharp, spatial, and grubby. They run in routes adults barely fit through, climb galleries, drop pebbles down shafts, make toy towns from clay scraps, float reed boats in drainage channels until scolded, and dare one another to touch sealed walls. They can identify households by smell: cheese room, wet wool, lamp fat, tannery, new plaster, boiled grain. They know which old women guard herbs, which dogs bite, which stallholder gives cracked cups to careful hands. They grow up with danger as instruction rather than spectacle. “Do not go there after rain” is as basic as “do not put your hand in fire.”

The elderly become maps. Their bodies may weaken, but their memory gains civic weight. An old man who can no longer carry spoil may still remember the buried course of a drain filled twenty years ago. An old woman may know that a chamber wall was once an outer face and should not be cut. They remember abandoned rooms before they were sealed, the first smoke shaft in a court, the family that ignored a seep and lost two children to collapse or fever. They sit in warmish niches and correct everyone.

They eat what can be stored, carried, dried, fermented, and protected from damp. Grain ground on querns. Flat breads. Gruel. Root stews. Goat milk when available, cheese aged in cool cellars, onions, bitter greens from pots, smoked fish, occasional small stream fish eaten whole, eggs from shaft chickens, dried berries or herbs from above. Meat is not absent, but it is notable. A slaughtered goat changes the smell of a court. A successful hunt becomes news. Salt is precious enough to be counted and watched.

Their sense of wealth is unlike that of open-field villages. Dryness is wealth. Airflow is wealth. A chamber with morning light is wealth. A reliable roof is wealth. Good storage jars, sound ladders, uncontested water rights, a smoke vent that draws, a marriage into a family with surface plots, a beam of straight timber—these are fortune. Ornament exists, but the deepest luxury is a space that does not need immediate repair.

They sleep in layers of sound. Not silence, exactly, but a known hush: breathing behind mats, water ticking, rats troubling traps, embers sighing, timbers adjusting. A wrong sound wakes them. A drip where no bowl has been set. A new creak. Water moving without rain. The absence of a familiar trickle. They are light sleepers because the hill is never entirely still.

They do not think of themselves as hidden, though they are hidden from the surface. The upper world is part of their town: vents, paths, thorn patches, grazing, gardens, graves perhaps, watch places, weather signs. But home begins where light softens and the wind stops tearing words away. Outsiders entering may feel enclosed, compressed, watched by earth. The inhabitants feel held—sometimes too tightly, sometimes precariously, but held.

Their myths, if asked, would not be about underworld kingdoms. They would tell of the first cut into the bank, the first chamber that stayed dry, the rain that nearly drowned the lower court, the woman who heard water behind a wall and saved three households, the foolish family who cut a root and brought down their roof, the child found alive in a sealed pocket of air, the beam reused from a dead man’s house that still groans in storms. Their legends are maintenance stories made memorable.

They are a people of thresholds: between surface and underside, wet and dry, dug and built, private and shared, danger and shelter, new chamber and grave. They live in a town that is not yet finished, and so they themselves seem unfinished in a generative way. Their customs are still hardening. Their routes are still becoming streets. Their symbols are still becoming writing. Their warnings are becoming law. Their repairs are becoming architecture.

What lives there, then, is a dense, cooperative, irritable, skilled human community adapted to the shallow dark without belonging wholly to it. They are neither subterranean creatures nor ordinary villagers merely tucked underground. They are makers of an in-between habitat: clay-handed, smoke-scented, damp-wise, frugal with flame, suspicious of waste, tender toward dry straw, reverent toward good drainage.

They live by listening. They listen to walls, rain, rumors, babies, beams, jars filling, drains clearing, neighbors shifting behind mats. They listen because the town speaks softly and consequences arrive through matter before they arrive through words.

And beneath all their quarrels and routines there is a shared, almost bodily knowledge: the hill permits them, but does not promise them. Each day they answer by shoring, scraping, digging, drying, patching, carrying, warning, remembering. They are the town’s inhabitants, but also its organs—hands, ears, lungs, skin. Without them the hollow would slump closed, fill with wet, go root-dark again. Without the hill they would scatter across the wind-bitten grass above.

Together they are becoming something neither could make alone: a buried people in a young town, learning how to endure just under the light.
