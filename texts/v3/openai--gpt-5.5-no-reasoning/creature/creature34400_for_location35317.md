What lives there are not invaders, and not wildlife in the old sense. They are the inheritors of surfaces.

They live as if the made world were a reef, a marsh, a carcass, a parent body, and a weather system all at once. Their ancestors may have been animals released by accident, or designed organisms seeded deliberately into the infrastructure, or scavengers that arrived through drains and cargo locks and adapted faster than anyone expected. But by now that distinction has blurred. They are no longer “animals in a machine.” They are the soft, jointed, breathing population of the machine’s skin.

Most of them are low creatures.

They keep their bodies close to warmth, moisture, vibration. They press themselves into service grooves and cable shadows, into the undersides of catwalks, into sumps shallow enough to warm through the plating. Their world is measured not by north and south, not by weather or season, but by gradients: a warmer conduit, a saltier trickle, a vent that exhales protein-thick vapor every nineteenth hour, a wall whose ceramic face holds condensation longer than the next.

Many are pale. Not purely white, but the colors of things that live under panels and inside tanks: milk-grey, translucent beige, bluish cartilage, wax yellow, the faint pink of organs visible through skin. Others are black and lacquered, matching the composite ground so completely that they are noticed only when their eyes open or their plates rise to breathe. A few carry warning colors borrowed from machinery itself: hazard orange throats, blue-lit flank organs, yellow stripes along hinged limbs, not painted but grown.

Their bodies often echo the infrastructure around them. Crabs have legs like folded brackets and claws shaped for gripping flanges. Eel-things wear rings of hard tissue at regular intervals, like flexible conduit. Grazers flatten themselves into gasket-like ovals, sealing their mouths against panels while they scrape invisible films. Roosting fliers fold into compact, angular bundles under beams, becoming almost indistinguishable from cable clusters until one turns its eyeless head.

There are creatures like maintenance tools and creatures like spills.

The tool-creatures are articulated, deliberate, exact. They walk on narrow feet that fit old bolt sockets. They climb ladders better than hands could. They hang beneath rails with magnetic pads or hooked cartilage. Some have mouthparts that ratchet open and closed with a small clicking, as though they were tightening the world one bite at a time. Their carapaces are smooth, sealed, and beaded with water. When they die, their bodies look less like corpses than discarded parts.

The spill-creatures are soft and adaptive. They pour through grates. They occupy the shapes given to them. In a rectangular sump they become rectangular. In a pipe elbow they coil and thicken. In a shallow basin they spread wide and thin, a membrane with mouths along its rim. Some are nearly transparent except for threads of darker organs pulsing inside them. Some tremble continuously, not from fear but from filtration. They feed on mineral films, on bacterial sheens, on whatever nutrient the system releases with its warm water. They are less like animals than like digestion made visible.

There are grazers in immense numbers.

Blind, patient, heavy-bellied things cling to condensation-slick plates and rasp. They move so slowly that a painted arrow may vanish beneath a herd over the course of a day and reappear polished clean a week later. Their mouths are wide, soft disks full of mineral teeth. Their backs collect droplets, dust, and the shed skins of smaller organisms. Tiny commensals live in the seams of their armor, feeding on the films they loosen but do not swallow.

Some grazers are no bigger than a palm and travel in curtains over vertical surfaces, leaving behind matte trails. Others are the size of vehicles, low and plated, standing in ranks on service roads where heat rises from buried engines. They have no urgency. Their heads are little more than sensory lips and scraping jaws tucked under the forward edge of their bodies. Their eyes, when they have eyes, are small and lateral, made for noticing shadows passing overhead rather than destinations.

In the warm canals live breathers.

They rise together as if summoned. A whole channel may appear empty, only dark water and reflected pipework, until a signal passes through it. Then dozens, hundreds, thousands of narrow heads break the surface in rows. Their mouths open with tiny metallic ticks. Some gulp air. Some vent gas. Some taste the chemical state of the atmosphere before sinking again into the amber-green light below the grates.

Their bodies are long, eel-like, but not fish. They have many small fins, or ciliated folds, or clasping appendages along their sides for anchoring themselves during flushes. Their skins are smooth and mucous-bright, often patterned by faint longitudinal seams, as if grown around old cables. In low water they knot together under bridge shadows, breathing in relays. In deeper reservoirs they form vertical columns, rising toward warmer layers and descending when pumps tremor beneath them.

They are prey to almost everything.

The circular basin-creatures feed on them from below. The stilt-hunters pluck them from surface breaks. Crabs hook them from drain mouths. Even the roosting fliers take the smaller ones, dropping silently from under catwalks to skim the channels with narrow jaws.

The fliers are not birds, though some have become bird-like because beams and towers favor bird-shapes.

They roost in huge numbers, packed shoulder to shoulder under gantries, along crossbeams, beneath eaves of exchanger housings. Their wings are narrow and leathery or feathered in dry white filaments that never quite look clean. Many have no visible eyes. They navigate by heat, pressure, electromagnetic hum, the minute differences between one tower’s vibration and the next. Their claws are specialized for the built world: magnetized pads, hooked toes for mesh, split talons that lock around cable.

They are quiet because loudness is unnecessary here. Sound does not need to travel through forest or wind. A scrape, a tremor, a change in pump rhythm carries far enough. When a flock lifts, it does not erupt in panic; it peels away from the structure in layers, circles once through warm fog, and settles again a short distance off. Their colonies smell ammoniac and sweet, but their waste does not remain. Cleaning arms, rain events, and other animals erase it with administrative regularity.

Some fliers nest in instrument housings. Some lay eggs in the warmth behind warning lamps. Some suspend their young in hammocks of hardened saliva beneath railings, each larva wrapped against condensation and fed on regurgitated canal-things. When the maintenance crawlers pass, the adults lift just enough. The young that fail to lift become part of the floor ecology.

There are crabs along the pipe-racks.

They are pale, long-legged, and deliberate, living in the narrow architectural jungle above the wet plain. Their limbs are too many or seem too many because they fold and unfold with such complexity. Each foot has a different purpose: clamp, hook, probe, magnet, blade. They sidle along cable trays, slip through ladder cages, nest in clusters around warm junction boxes. Some are scavengers. Some harvest the grey films inside vents. Some are active predators, waiting beside condensation lines for smaller animals to come drink.

Their shells bear serial-like patterns, not true numbers but rows of raised pores and sensory pits. Water beads on them and runs down the grooves. When threatened, they flatten against pipes and become brackets. When feeding, they work with uncanny neatness, disassembling a corpse into clean segments, passing soft tissue from claw to mouth while discarding shell and cartilage into drainage slots.

Their young hatch in sump-water and climb as soon as their limbs harden. The smallest ones swarm over warm bolts in glittering clusters, feeding on microbial slicks. Many are eaten by the fliers, the breathers, the membrane things, and by larger crabs that do not distinguish kin from resource.

The predators are quiet because the whole world teaches quietness.

The tall stilted hunters are among the most visible. They move like instruments walking. Their bodies are high, narrow, and suspended between impossibly thin legs jointed for stepping over rails, pipes, and sleeping herds. Their feet are needles or soft gripping points, placed into old dents, gaps, and bolt recesses with exquisite care. They can stand for hours over a channel, every membrane on their head listening.

Their heads are not faces so much as sensor arrays grown in flesh: slit nostrils, heat pits, stretched tympanic sails, chemical whiskers trembling in vapor. Some have no jaws visible until they strike. Then the lower head opens sideways, or downward, or unfolds into a hinged trap. The strike is brief and precise. A splash. A snap. A convulsion traveling through nearby animals. Then the hunter folds itself back into stillness.

They do not chase unless forced. Chasing is expensive in a crowded machine-marsh. They wait for schedules: a flush that disorients canal prey, a vent that draws grazers, a rain event that brings fliers low, a cleaning arm that scatters clingers from a tower skin.

There are also predators that are almost invisible.

Flat things under puddles with transparent backs and mouths facing upward. Cable-colored serpents living inside open conduits. Clusters of harmless-looking nodules around warm outflows that close like fists when touched. Basin rims that are not rims but living lips. Doorsills that pulse once, slowly, when an animal steps across them.

Some predation looks like accident. A small grazer crosses a membrane it mistakes for flooring and is simply no longer above the surface. A bird settles on a warm housing and the housing’s “stain” folds around its feet. A crab reaches into a vent for film and withdraws without a limb.

The largest creatures are mostly aquatic or semi-aquatic, because low worlds make size easier in water.

In the wide reservoirs lie the living manhole covers: broad circular animals just beneath the surface, as regular as manufactured plates. Their rims flex and breathe. Their upper skins collect silt, droplets, and reflected sky until they are nearly indistinguishable from access hatches. Beneath, they may be all stomach, filter, and slow muscle. They feed on swimmers, on settling organic matter, on the outflow from towers. Occasionally they release huge bubbles from deep internal chambers, and the water domes before breaking with an oily sigh.

Some are solitary and territorial, each occupying the warm ellipse beside an intake. Others form fields, overlapping at the edges, a second floating pavement alive with slow contractions. Smaller animals walk over them carefully. The largest predators avoid them. When one dies, if they truly die in any ordinary sense, its body does not rot openly. It delaminates. Layers loosen. Crabs, films, eel-things, and soft spill-creatures enter it until it becomes a temporary district.

In the dry-hot cells live different beings.

They are harder, darker, less moist. Low armored herds gather over turbine housings and radiant plates. They absorb heat through black carapaces and store it in fatty internal banks. At cooler hours — if hours matter — they move toward wet edges and release warmth into egg clusters, larvae, or symbiotic films. Their bodies are blunt and patient, often mistaken for housings or equipment covers. Many have transparent eyelids permanently filmed with condensation.

Around them live heat mites, little articulated specks that travel in their armor seams. There are also needle-mouthed parasites that drill between plates and drink mineral-rich blood, and small cleaner animals that pick those parasites off in exchange for access to warm skin. Entire micro-ecologies move with each herd, not unlike old savanna animals with ticks and birds, except here the savanna is a service road and the birds hang from gantries.

There are organisms that may not be animals at all.

Grey films inside shaded vents pulse faintly when warm air passes through them. Wax-like nodules around outflows open pores in synchronized patterns. Sump membranes thicken after feeding and thin after rain. Translucent sheets creep over panels at night-reduced light, then withdraw into seams before cleaning cycles. They may be engineered microbial mats. They may be fungal descendants. They may be parts of the system’s own maintenance ecology, given enough independence to seem alive and enough regularity to seem mechanical.

The animals depend on them. Grazers scrape them. Filterers strain their spores or cells. Larvae embed in them. Predators wait where they bloom because prey gathers there. The films are the closest thing this world has to pasture, moss, fallen leaves, fruiting bodies. They are the green that is not green.

Reproduction follows the machine’s calendar.

There are no springs. There are hatchings after scheduled warmth. Swarms after venting. Egg-laying during long condensation events. Metamorphosis in conduits held at exact temperatures by processes no animal understands.

Some creatures deposit eggs inside unused cable channels, where the insulation keeps them warm and dry enough. Others glue translucent capsules beneath handrails, each capsule with a bead of water trembling at its tip. Canal-breathers release clouds of young during basin flushes, trusting the current to distribute them through gridded channels. Fliers lay in lamp housings. Crabs carry larvae in wet folds under their abdomens until a particular pump vibration causes them to release all at once.

Many young are larval in the extreme: blind threads, pulsing beads, soft commas drifting through drainage mesh. They live first on films, on heat, on dissolved chemicals. Only later do they become recognizable as the crabs, grazers, fliers, breathers, plates, stilts, masks, and armored sleepers of the visible world.

Death is absorbed quickly.

There are too many mouths, too many wet processes, too much warm chemistry for a body to remain itself for long. A dead flier below a catwalk becomes a trembling knot of small animals. A stilt-hunter carcass draws crabs from high pipes and eel-things from low drains. The membrane of a sump may rise to meet fallen flesh. Cleaning systems sweep fragments into channels, where filterers take them. Even bones and shell are rasped, dissolved, or incorporated into nests.

Nothing accumulates except population.

The inhabitants know the place through timing. Not intellectually, perhaps, but bodily. Their nerves are tuned to the schedule of gates, pumps, vents, heat cycles, misting events, cleaning arms, pressure hums. A tremor passing through a channel can lift thousands of heads. A relay click behind a sealed wall can send crabs withdrawing into brackets. A change in the taste of condensation can start a migration from one basin to the next.

To live there is to be precise. Not clever, necessarily. Precise.

A creature that rests beneath the wrong gate is crushed. One that breeds before the warm flush loses its eggs to cold water. One that feeds from the wrong tray is poisoned or sterilized. One that mistakes a dormant maintenance crawler for terrain is carried kilometers from its niche and dies among unfamiliar schedules. The world is indifferent, but its indifference is patterned, and life has filled the pattern.

Over time, many animals have become less exploratory than tuned. They do not wander widely unless their kind is made for it. They repeat circuits. They occupy gradients. They return to the same rail, the same vent, the same sump lip, generation after generation, because the machine has not changed and rewards fidelity.

And yet the crowding gives the place a strange abundance.

There are tiny transparent organisms clustered over warm vents like beads of living glass. There are whiskered swimmers nosing through blue inspection light. There are heavy blind grazers shining with condensation. There are pale pipe-crabs folded into the geometry of clamps. There are soft basin disks breathing under reflected sky. There are white roosting multitudes lifting and settling around indifferent cleaning arms. There are tall hunters listening to water. There are armored heat-herds standing on black roads as if parked there by some forgotten operator.

They are not pastoral, not lush, not wild in a green way. They are industrial fauna: humid, mineral, noctilucent, plated, slick, exact. Their paradise is a drainage slot. Their forest is a gantry grid. Their meadow is a warm ceramic panel filmed with invisible growth. Their river is a coolant channel. Their cliffs are tank walls. Their caves are conduits. Their moon, if they need one, is a warning lamp glowing forever behind damp glass.

If there are any descendants of people there, they are not obvious as people.

Perhaps something small and clever sleeps in control-room footwells, using abandoned chairs as shelter and sealed consoles as heat. Perhaps thin-handed scavengers move only during low-glare intervals, drinking from condensate trays and reading the old warning glyphs as instinctive taboo rather than language. Perhaps the handrails and doors, still sized for vanished bodies, exert a ghostly evolutionary pressure: perches at a convenient height, shelters with convenient handles, chambers whose warmth suits denning.

But the dominant impression is not of humanity surviving. It is of human intention outlasting humans, and life learning to live on that intention.

The things that live there are the population of a finished system: animals adapted not to change, but to maintenance; not to seasons, but to cycles; not to soil, but to sealed surfaces; not to wilderness, but to infrastructure so vast and stable it has become nature by duration.

They are the marsh’s answer to metal.

They are what grows when nothing grows.
