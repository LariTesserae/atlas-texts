They are small in number, or else they have learned to behave as if they are.

The inhabitants of the cleft-city are not a crowding people. Even when many of them live there—behind the stacked windows, inside the sleeping cells, below the sealed hatches—the visible impression is of restraint. They pass one at a time along narrow bridges. They pause at thresholds while air pressures equalize. They do not gather in broad public knots, because there is nowhere broad enough for that, and because their city has taught them that every body is heat, vapor, mass, contamination, interruption.

They are people of edges.

Their lives are shaped by railings that appear only when needed, by paths just wide enough for careful feet, by doors that wait to be resealed before another opens. Their bodies have adapted culturally, if not biologically, to precision. They do not swing their arms freely. They do not lean unless they have checked the surface first. They know where ice forms invisibly, where the basalt sheds flakes after freeze-thaw stress, which gutters overflow after cloudbursts, which bridge flex means ordinary loading and which means report it immediately.

They move as though the whole city is an instrument that can be put out of tune.

Their clothing is mostly sealed skin: pale insulated suits, flexible at the joints, dark at the palms and soles, sometimes patched with newer material that never quite matches the old. They wear hoods or close-fitting caps in the public cold, face shields when the air is abrasive with ice dust, breathing collars when upper drafts turn thin. Their garments are not bulky in the old mountain-climber way; they are layered and technical, filled with small heat channels, moisture traps, pressure membranes. The suits take care not to waste the warmth of their occupants. At cuffs and throats, vapor is caught before it can escape freely into the cleft.

Bare skin appears only briefly: a hand inside a service glove, a cheek behind a half-open mask, eyes reflected in transparent ceramic.

They are likely human, or descended from humans, but the place has edited them. Not grossly. Not into monsters or myths. The changes are subtle enough that an outsider might not understand them at first. Their faces tend to be narrow from generations in rationed light and sealed air. Their eyes are pale-adjusted, quick to read low contrast, good at judging wet shine from dry ceramic, frost from merely cold metal. Their hands are dexterous and patient. They can work through gloves with the precision of surgeons. Many have a habit of holding their breath when listening to machinery, as if even lungs are an interference.

Children, if there are children, are rare and intensely noticed.

A child in the cleft would be a logistical event: heat budget, air allocation, growth space, immune management, sound. They would be taught early not to run except in designated warm corridors, not to put their tongue or fingers to cold metal, not to shout near acoustic sensors, not to hide in ducts, not because adults dislike mischief but because the city has too many places where mischief becomes hazard. Their games would be miniature versions of survival: tracing clean water through transparent floor channels, identifying alarm colors, balancing along marked safe seams, learning to read the sky-strip as others learn clouds over fields.

They would draw mountains as walls, not horizons.

The adults do not seem sentimental, but that may be misleading. In a place where nothing disperses, feeling has also learned to circulate through narrow channels. They do not waste words publicly. Their greetings are small: two fingers briefly against the wrist seal, a tilt of the head toward someone’s thermal hood, a pause long enough for recognition before continuing past. Affection may be practical. Someone has warmed your gloves before a shift. Someone has cleaned the mineral film from your window. Someone has transferred their water allowance to a fabrication bath so your broken knee joint can be regrown or replaced. Someone has checked the ice above your route without saying so.

Their tenderness is maintenance.

Their society is probably organized less by ownership than by responsibility. To live there is to be accountable to a system too small for neglect. There are water stewards, pressure engineers, mirror tenders, ice auditors, thermal accountants, basalt listeners, fabrication cooks, atmospheric physicians, drone shepherds, archivists of condensation. Some professions may sound ceremonial because, over time, necessity has acquired ritual. The person who calibrates the central basin is not merely a technician; they are one of those entrusted with the city’s pulse. The person who clears the upper rim after storms has the status of someone who negotiates with weather.

No one simply “works in utilities.” Utilities are life.

The inhabitants understand water with a granularity that agricultural people once reserved for soil. They know water by taste, conductivity, temperature lag, mineral ghost, bubble size, freezing behavior, memory of pipe and stone. They can hear when a drip is falling onto ceramic rather than basalt, when a catchment is nearing overflow, when a pump’s pulse is damped by slush. They talk about water’s path the way sailors talk about wind. A person may be praised for “keeping a clean return” or criticized for “letting the flow go blind.”

The central basin is not worshipped exactly, but it is approached with the quiet given to places where error matters.

Those who tend it wear darker suits, perhaps to conceal wetness or perhaps because their tools must read them clearly against the smoked glass lining. They kneel beside the low instruments and speak very little. Their work involves samples, phase shifts, acoustic readings, perhaps conversations with the engineered water itself. When geometric ripples cross the basin, people nearby may pause—not in fear, but in attention. Some decision has moved through the city’s deepest layer. A salinity correction. A load prediction. A memory retrieval. A warning from the rock.

For the water here is not passive. It is processed, measured, taught, induced to carry data in transient structures: suspended microbubbles, crystalline alignments, molecular tags, acoustic resonances. The inhabitants may not distinguish cleanly between plumbing and computation. Their city thinks partly in water. It stores not whole minds there, perhaps, but probabilities, forecasts, histories of flow, the health of seals, the stress of bridges, the breath of its residents condensed and re-entered into the common cycle.

To them, exhalation is not metaphorically communal. It is actually communal.

This makes privacy a complicated matter.

They live in small sealed rooms recessed into the stone, stacked in pale grids along the cleft walls. A sleeping cell may be no larger than a berth, with a fold-down surface, a thermal cocoon, a wall of soft light, a personal archive projected onto the curve of a membrane. Larger family chambers exist deeper in the rock, warmer and more humid, lined with wipe-clean materials and condensation collectors disguised as decorative ribs. There are no carpets, no books left open to the air, no wooden furniture, no loose cloth if it can be avoided. Personal possessions must justify volume and outgassing.

A keepsake might be a stone flake from the original opening of the cleft, sealed behind resin. A ribbon of obsolete circuit. A vial of water from a first snowfall. A recording of wind over the upper lip. A printed image of a tree, though many residents may never have smelled one.

They know of trees. They know of lowlands, oceans, warm rain, insects in swarms, soil thick with roots, cities spread flat under open sky. Some have come from those places and never fully adjusted. These outsiders are recognizable by how they look upward too often, or how they overheat rooms, or how they use too much water when washing, or how they speak of weather as something that arrives rather than something that must be captured, sorted, and survived. Long-term residents find such people both wasteful and moving.

The cleft-born may feel uneasy in open landscapes. Too much sky would be a kind of exposure. Too much green, a kind of noise.

Their bodies may carry implants, but not decorative ones. Subdermal thermal regulators. Blood-oxygen monitors. Mineral exposure tags. Balance aids for vertical work. Identification patterns readable by doors through gloves and frost. Some may have auditory filters tuned to distinguish machine distress from the endless ticking of water. Their medical care is excellent in narrow ways and limited in broad ones: superb at hypothermia, decompression, respiratory imbalance, fracture repair, sterilization, organ monitoring; poorer at the diseases of crowds, pollen, parasites, sunlight excess, soil microbes.

They are not fragile. They are specialized.

The city’s engineers are not rugged miners, though they know stone intimately. They do not conquer the mountain. They listen for expansion, shear, sublimation, meltwater pressure. The basalt is young and still settling. Sometimes the rock speaks in pings, ticks, tiny reports of stress. In deeper galleries, basalt listeners place sensors into cracks and interpret hours of data like physicians reading a difficult pulse. When the mountain shifts, the city alters its gait: bridges close, water is rerouted, warm chambers cool down, drones wake, residents sleep in different cells until the fracture’s mood is understood.

The mountain is not worshipped either. Reverence here has been replaced by ongoing negotiation.

There are machines among them that nearly count as citizens.

Drones live in wall niches, folded like insects, their bodies matte and jointed, their rotors or field vanes hidden until deployment. They tend mirrors, clear ice, inspect upper cracks, carry parcels through mist shafts, enter spaces too cold, too narrow, or too unstable for people. Their movement is quiet enough that residents notice them mostly by displaced droplets and the brief dimming of reflected light. Larger maintenance forms move only rarely, unfolding from lower vaults like pale crabs or skeletal hands to repair anchor points or scrape accreted mineral scale from conduits.

The tiny engineered custodians in the drains are treated with less affection but more superstition. They are not alive, officially. They are programmed assemblages: jointed beads, translucent slivers, mineral-eating cleaning bodies with simple decision loops. Yet children name them. Adults pretend not to. If one appears in a visible channel during clear-flow hours, people watch it until it vanishes into a slot. A custodian outside its proper route is an omen of clogging, bad calibration, or unauthorized release.

The inhabitants’ artificial intelligences are likely diffuse rather than enthroned. There is no single bright oracle speaking from the central basin. Instead, intelligence is distributed through water tables, wall sensors, door logic, climate predictions, mirror alignments, medical monitors, and drone swarms. The city answers in changed light, warmed floors, sealed hatches, altered flows. It does not need to speak often because its residents have learned to read its body.

If it does speak, it probably does so softly.

A voice in the cleft would be economical, genderless or variable, localized to the listener’s hood or the nearest panel. “Upper path closed.” “Return flow mineral-heavy.” “Wait.” “Your left glove is losing heat.” “Do not cross.” Such messages have the intimacy of a hand on the shoulder. The city is less a ruler than a nervous system shared by architecture and inhabitants.

There may be factions, but they would form around principles of allocation.

Those who believe in expansion: cutting deeper cells, adding mirrors, widening warm chambers, increasing population. Those who argue for austerity: less heat, fewer births, stricter water cycling, no unnecessary signal traffic. Those who want more contact with the outside, trading data, rare minerals, climate engineering, refuge space. Those who insist hiddenness is survival. In such a compressed place, disagreement cannot be allowed to become theatrical. Debate would happen in scheduled chambers with condensers running high to reclaim the heat of argument. Raised voices would be remembered.

Politics is probably measured in liters, watts, kilograms of ice load, hours of sunlight, grams of imported organics.

Food is one of the few things not evident in the cleft, which means it is grown or made out of sight. The inhabitants eat from sealed cultivation rooms deeper in the mountain: algae sheets under calibrated light, fungal proteins in sterile racks, cultured tissue, mineral-fed microbial pastes refined into something more dignified, perhaps small hydroponic chambers so precious that entry is restricted. The absence of plants in the public city does not mean the people are plantless. It means vegetation is too vulnerable and too contaminating to be exposed. Leaves, if they exist, live behind doors like patients.

A fresh herb might be festival food.

They may celebrate not with abundance but with permitted waste. A warm room opened for longer than usual. A fog chamber filled for children to run through. A communal meal with visible steam. Music allowed to resonate across the cleft for one hour while sensors compensate. Mirrors angled to bring an actual blade of sun to the lower basin on the longest day. Snowmelt tasting: first runoff from the season passed through transparent vessels, compared, recorded, blessed in secular terms.

Their festivals would be quiet by lowland standards. But in that city, a dozen people gathered without masks in a warm wet chamber would feel extravagant, almost dangerous.

Death, too, must obey the city.

Bodies cannot simply be buried; there is no soil. They cannot be burned casually; heat and particulates are too costly. The dead are likely processed with ceremony and precision. Water reclaimed. Elements separated. Bone mineral returned to fabrication stock or sealed into memorial tiles. Names stored in the basin’s archive, in wall light, in the pattern of a bridge’s maintenance code, in a small change to a mirror’s annual calibration. To outsiders this might seem cold, but to the inhabitants it is continuity. Nothing disperses. Even grief is recycled into structure.

A memorial may be a window that remains dark for one day each year. Or a drop of treated water released at the top of the central well, allowed to hesitate in the impossible column before joining the basin below.

They are not emotionless. They are not merely efficient. They live in a place that punishes carelessness and therefore has made care into the core of culture.

They are patient with objects. They repair rather than replace. They notice seals aging, hinges frosting, surface textures wearing smooth under gloves. They dislike dust because dust means uncontrolled breakdown. They dislike unnecessary heat because heat means imbalance. They dislike open flame instinctively, almost morally. They trust redundancy more than heroism. A person who saves the city in a dramatic crisis would be honored, but the greater honor goes to the person whose thirty years of boring calibrations prevented the crisis from ever occurring.

Their heroes are those who kept things uneventful.

The visible figures in insulated skins, moving slowly along catwalks, are therefore not merely workers. They are the city’s outward-facing organs: fingers, eyelids, immune cells. One checks the transparent channel underfoot and marks a discoloration with a wrist light. Another pauses beside a railing that has briefly appeared, tests the lattice with a gloved knuckle, logs the delay. A third receives a drone on a narrow balcony, removes a frost-cracked sensor from its belly, and sends it back into the mist. Across the cleft, behind a pane, someone presses both hands to the glass and watches.

Perhaps an outsider has arrived.

Visitors would be uncommon and treated like weather: assessed, contained, filtered, integrated only if safe. At arrival locks, strangers are stripped of excess moisture, spores, heat signatures, loose fibers, unknown microbes. Their belongings are scanned for soil. Soil is a threat here—not because it is dirty in the crude sense, but because it is alive in too many unscheduled ways. Seeds, molds, mites, bacteria, pollen: all the lowland’s abundance would be chaos in a sealed mountain instrument.

The inhabitants may ask visitors what rain smells like on warm ground. They may pretend not to care about the answer.

Some among them have never left the cleft and never will. Others serve in outside teams, climbing to upper collectors in storms, traversing snowfields to maintain beam relays, entering old tunnels to trade with hidden supply routes. These exterior workers are regarded with a mix of admiration and concern. They bring back ice stories, ridge-light, sunburn, torn suit membranes, rumors from other settlements. They know the sound of wind not filtered through the city’s vertical throat. They also know how exposed life is beyond the basalt walls.

There may be other cities like this, hidden in other ridges, each fitted to its own fracture, each with its own water customs and thermal dialect. The cleft’s people might exchange packets of climate data, genetic diversity, engineering refinements, songs compressed into tight beams when clouds part. The mountain walls damp broad communication, so contact is deliberate and brief. Messages are not chatter; they are released like precious gas.

Their language, over generations, has probably changed.

It would be rich in terms for states of wetness, cold, pressure, phase, visibility. It might distinguish between falling mist and wall-condensed mist, between safe frost and treacherous glaze, between water that has passed through living breath and water newly melted from snow, between quiet machinery and too-quiet machinery. Silence itself may have categories. A normal hush. A sealed hush. A failing hush. The hush before ice sheds. The hush after a pressure door closes and someone is on the other side.

They speak in low voices because the cleft carries sound oddly, and because loudness has few uses. But they are not mute. In warm chambers they may sing—close, layered songs shaped to resonate without wasting breath. Their music would use the city’s acoustics: bottle-wind from above, droplets ticking, bridge vibration, the soft pulse of pumps. It might sound sparse to outsiders, but inside it would be full of references: a rhythm of thaw valves, a melody like upper-rim warning tones, a bass line imitating the central well’s frozen night ticking.

Their art hides in systems because wall space is valuable.

A sequence of corridor lights that shifts by one hue during a birth month. A bridge surface etched with maintenance grooves arranged in inherited patterns. Condensation trails encouraged to bead along certain ceramic textures, forming temporary drawings that vanish into gutters. Personal marks visible only under thermal contrast. Poems encoded into flow reports. Names given to drones that officially have serial numbers. A mirror alignment that, for one minute each winter, lets the sky-strip fall perfectly onto a sleeping cell door.

Nothing ornamental, perhaps, but nothing untouched by intention.

The nonhuman residents—the drones, custodians, distributed intelligences, water-borne algorithms—do not “live” there as animals live, yet they are part of the habitation. The city’s life is composite. Human breath warms sensor housings. Machines decide where frost may form. Water carries computation. The mountain constrains every habit. The inhabitants are not masters of the place so much as participants in an engineered metabolism.

If asked who lives there, the simplest answer would be: careful people, and the careful machines they have made necessary.

But that is too simple.

What lives there is a community that has traded spaciousness for continuity. A population that has made a virtue of not overflowing its container. A culture of maintenance, condensation, calibration, and held breath. Human beings whose domestic weather is manufactured, whose public squares are bridges over dark wet air, whose gardens are sealed vats behind pressure doors, whose sky is a pale line far above. They live close to danger but not romantically; danger is just another variable to be reduced.

They are cold-adapted without being cold-hearted. Spare without being empty. Hidden without being primitive. Technical without being sterile.

They are like the city itself: folded, stacked, nested, recessed. More present than they first appear.

And among them lives something larger than any one resident but smaller than a nation: the cleft’s shared intelligence, partly civic system, partly weather engine, partly memory. It breathes in vapor, thinks in droplets, speaks in light, dreams in the dark basin at the bottom. It does not rule them. It keeps faith with them, as they keep faith with it.

Together they inhabit the mountain’s wound.

They keep it from freezing solid. They keep it from flooding. They keep it hidden. They keep it running.
