What lives there is not one species, not even one kingdom, but a dry intelligence distributed through root, shell, horn, dust, and rite.

The inhabitants of the ring are the **children of thirst without water**.

They are crisp-bodied, salt-fed, electrically attentive things. They do not drink. They do not weep. Their wounds seal into resin or crystal. Their births are dry, beadlike, rattling. Their deaths leave architecture.

The dominant life is the grove itself: a community of basalt-rooted plants that behave less like vegetation than like instruments tuned to charge, heat, mineral drift, and pressure. They rise in separate stations from the black stone—fans, combs, cages, bladders, pillars, needles—each holding its own territory, each connected to the others by pale root-threads stretched across the ground. Together they breathe in convulsions. They lift dust, sort it, feed on it, seed through it, and speak through it in rattles, snaps, hums, and storms.

They are not passive plants. They listen. They remember cuts. They recoil from moisture. They reward patience with edible scales, resin curls, powder-nodules, and charged blooms. They punish clumsiness with teeth, thorns, filaments, sealed footprints, and glass reeds that sing over the vanished.

Among them live animals shaped by the same covenant.

There are small six-legged runners that pause like weather-vanes to raise sail-backs into the charged air. Pale lizards with feathered jaws lick salt and pollen from the fan-plants. Thumb-sized winged creatures, half moth and half seed, erupt from amber sacs in shrilling clouds. Goat-sized grazers scrape mineral flesh from stems with narrow heads and translucent horns. Armored scavengers fold themselves into wheels and roll from danger. Ribbon-bodied predators pass partly through the web of root-threads, making the plants tremble ahead of their arrival.

Their bodies are dry and exact. Blood clots into fragrant resin. Waste falls as crystal. Eggs are laid as polished beads. Nests are placed in vibration instead of softness. Parasites are shaken away by sound. Food is not flesh alone, but charge, oil, dust, salt, stored heat, and the chemical patience of stone.

There are also people, though perhaps not permanent people, and perhaps not entirely separate from the grove anymore.

They come masked and goggled, wearing layered fibers and carrying immaculate tools: flint knives, bone needles, wooden tongs, stone weights, braided seed-nets. They are gatherers, tenders, listeners, ritualists of dryness. They know the manners of the ring. They know not to bring water through the wall. They know which plant is offering and which is armed. They know that a cleared circle of dust belongs to something still beneath the ground. They know that the hut’s clicking should not be answered by strangers.

They do not rule the garden. They negotiate with it.

Their settlement is intermittent: camp along the inner wall, harvest, repair, sing, listen, depart. On certain days they gather as if at a market, though what passes between them is not merely trade. They exchange powders, warnings, animal plates, songs, cord, timing, permission. They hang offerings in the notches of the wall and wait for the grove’s response: scales, color, seed, silence.

And beneath all these visible lives there is another inhabitant: the place as a single reacting body.

The wall is its rim. The hut is its ear or throat. The root-threads are nerves. The plants are organs. The animals are moving appetites and pollens. The people are sometimes parasites, sometimes caretakers, sometimes memory. The dust storms are breath. The dry lightning is bloodless thought.

It lives by refusing water. It has made lack into law, and law into abundance.

So whoever lives there is sharp, loud, mineral, old. A whole society of dry beings, tended by masked visitors and governed by a grove that breathes dust instead of air.
