What lives there are not citizens in the human sense, but the city has citizens nonetheless.

They are beings of nearness: small, translucent, blind or nearly blind, shaped by pressure, hunger, current, and stone. Most of them do not travel far. Many will never know more than a ledge, a crack, a dim chamber, a single wall furred with edible film. Their lives are measured not in streets crossed but in gradients followed: oxygen thickening, mineral sharpening, rot sweetening the water, danger arriving as vibration.

They are animals almost without assertion. Amphipods pale as fingernail parings. Worms like animated threads. Seed-sized bells that open and close together. Flat grazers pressed to walls as if printed there. Tiny crustaceans whose legs are so fine they seem made of current. Larvae suspended like punctuation in the blue-dark water. Fish-like forms with skin over their eyes and mouths that take the invisible into themselves all day.

Some are clear enough that their meals are the brightest part of them.

Some carry a cold, private light beneath the jaw or in a bead along the flank—not a lamp, not a signal meant for distance, but a minimal declaration: here, here, here. The light does not reveal the city. It only appears and vanishes inside it, like thought in a sleeping body.

They live by filtration, grazing, scraping, waiting, folding around prey, withdrawing from touch. Predators are present, but not theatrical. A hunter here is only a more decisive softness. It angles itself differently in the current. It pauses where another creature will pass. It opens. Something smaller becomes an absence.

The slowest inhabitants are the films.

They cover the city more faithfully than any animal. Amber slicks, white lace, gray velvet, milky curtains, black-green stains, bacterial skins spread across mineral thresholds. They are the city’s first settlers and its archivists. They taste the seepage from the stone. They thicken where a dead thing rests. They map the invisible chemistry of each room. They make food out of almost nothing, and then nearly everyone else makes food out of them.

The mussel-like shells live as a municipal patience. Sealed black against the cold, they wait for pressure, for current, for the right passing condition. Then they open together by a fraction and expose pale interiors. They do not speak, but their synchronized hunger gives the chamber the feeling of governance. Water enters them fouled and leaves altered. They clean by desiring.

The worms are the city’s nerves. They inhabit the smallest ducts, hanging from cracks in pale bunches, tasting with their whole length. A touch passes through them as withdrawal, and withdrawal passes through them as news. One body vanishes into the wall, then ten, then a hundred: a district closing itself without doors.

The amphipods are the visible public. They move along ledges in pale, busy processions, scraping the films, detouring around danger, gathering where mineral seep meets old organic dust. They seem purposeful because every motion is an answer. They do not know market or road, but they make both by repetition.

The larvae are the city’s suspended weather. They gather in rings, in eddies, in blue-shadowed plazas where a slight current can hold them as if in ceremony. They face into movement. They replace one another without signal. Some are eaten. Some vanish upward into cracks. Some settle and become fixtures, mouths, bells, shells, tubes, futures.

There are sponges or sponge-like colonies too, porous towers living as architecture. Their bodies are buildings that breathe water. Inside their pores smaller lives move, and on their surfaces smaller gardens root, and inside those gardens smaller hunters wait. Nothing here is single. Every inhabitant is also habitat.

Even the dead continue to live as neighborhoods.

A shed shell becomes a chamber. A fish’s body becomes a district of feeding. A fallen grain of sand creates a blockage, a boundary, a new underside. A fragment of leaf from the far upper world arrives like a god or catastrophe: soft, foreign, warm with stored sunlight. Around it the city changes shape. Grazers find it. Films bloom over it. Predators come for the grazers. Minerals begin to sheath it. In time, the leaf stops being leaf and becomes history: a dark inclusion, a founding layer, a cause no living thing remembers.

What lives there, then, is not one creature but a scale of lives nested inside one another.

Blind fish know the midwater avenues.

Amphipods know the ledges.

Worms know the cracks.

Bell colonies know the ceilings.

Bacteria know the chemical borders.

Shells know the pressure.

Mineral-growing animals know the surfaces that can be made into body.

Predators know interruption.

Eggs know only drift.

The water knows them all.

None of them sees the city entire. There is no creature there large enough for overview, no eye that holds the plan. Each inhabitant possesses a fragmentary map written in touch, current, taste, and recoil. To one animal, the city is a feeding wall and three safe cracks. To another, it is a vertical shaft of edible particles. To another, it is a black chamber to avoid because the water burns faintly with metal. To another, it is only the direction in which oxygen improves.

They are not primitive in the sense of unfinished. They are exact. Each is refined to the smallness and coldness of the place. Their bodies are answers to questions the city asks constantly:

Can you hold position?

Can you eat what settles?

Can you sense without seeing?

Can you wait longer than hunger wants?

Can you become transparent enough not to matter until you strike?

Can you leave offspring in a current and trust the architecture to catch some of them?

The deepest resident may be the city itself: not conscious, perhaps, but alive in the broader grammar of growth. It secretes rooms. It closes passages. It opens new ones by fracture and seep. It feeds its inhabitants with surfaces, shelters them with pockets, kills them with dead zones, preserves them as layers. It is reef, nursery, stomach, archive, and machine without parts.

And among all these lives there is no loneliness, because there is no empty distance. Everything touches eventually. Water touches stone. Stone touches film. Film touches mouth. Mouth becomes prey. Prey becomes sediment. Sediment becomes wall. Wall becomes ledge. Ledge becomes crowded. Crowding becomes city.

So whoever lives there is a multitude of small cold beings, mostly blind, mostly soft, many transparent, all fluent in pressure and scarcity.

They are the citizens of a drowned, unborn metropolis.

They do not build it with hands.

They inhabit it until their bodies become part of its architecture.
