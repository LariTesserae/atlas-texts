What lives there are not inhabitants in the usual sense. They are almost allowances.

Life in the mineral marsh is thin, patient, and shy of form. It does not stand upright, does not spread green, does not claim territory with trunks, burrows, calls, or nests. It keeps to contact zones: the undersides of lips, the seams where water presses through stone, the silt just below a translucent crust, the cold film between a black slab and the clay beneath it. It lives where mineral change is slow enough to shelter it and water is constant enough to lend it a body.

Most of it is small beyond easy noticing.

In the shallow pans there are colonies like grey lace, though lace is too generous a word. They are veils, nets, stains with intention. Spread across clay or glassy stone, they look at first like residue left by evaporating water: a faint branching dullness, a frost of matter in the wrong temperature. But shadow makes them gather. A dropped pebble makes them contract. Each pale thread thickens, beads, and withdraws through pores so small they were invisible before the colony moved. A pool that seemed occupied is suddenly bare, except for a few pinholes breathing dimly in the clay.

They are neither plant nor animal as those categories would like to be understood. They do not photosynthesize in any lush, sunward way. They feed on seepage: dissolved salts, metals, sulfurous traces, the slow edible difference between one layer of stone and another. Their bodies are temporary agreements among mucus, mineral dust, and translucent tissue. When water is plentiful, they spread flat to taste it. When the pool shrinks, they thicken into beads and sink into the sediment, waiting as little wet knots, almost stones themselves.

There are pale disks in some pools, coin-sized or smaller, soft around the edge and faintly ridged underneath. They turn over very slowly, not by flipping but by passing a ripple through the rim of themselves until the upper surface becomes the lower. The motion is so gradual it can be mistaken for the shifting of light. These disks graze the mineral films from submerged rock. They leave behind clean arcs, darker than the surrounding surface, as if a fingertip has wiped condensation from glass.

When disturbed, they do not flee quickly. Speed would be wasteful here. Instead they loosen their outline. Their bodies cloud outward, flatten, and become nearly indistinguishable from silt. A moment later they gather again several inches away, incomplete at first, then circular, then ridged, then still.

In cracks and under overhangs live thread-creatures: long, white-grey, flexible things no thicker than wire. They anchor themselves at one end in a seam and let the rest of the body drift in shallow water. When droplets strike nearby, the threads taste the tremor. They recoil into the crack with a clean, instantaneous withdrawal, vanishing so completely that the eye doubts they were ever present. At night, or in the basin’s version of night, they extend farther. Hundreds may fringe the submerged edges of a stone shelf, quivering in the drainage flow like rootlets without a plant.

They feed on what the water carries. Powdered clay. Microscopic organisms. Metallic precipitates. The faint organic leavings of other sparse lives. Their mouths, if they can be called mouths, are not at the end but along the body: small soft openings that open and close according to the chemistry of the passing film.

There are also bead-clusters, usually mistaken for bubbles beneath mineral skins. They cling to the underside of salt crusts and gypsum sheets, each bead clear with a smoky center. When conditions are right, a whole cluster pulses together. The beads squeeze and relax, passing fluid between them. Sometimes they detach and roll under the crust like drops of mercury, joining other clusters in cooler hollows. If exposed to open air, they harden within minutes into dull grains. Later, when wetted again, some of those grains soften and begin to pulse.

These are the basin’s common lives: films, disks, threads, beads. They are plentiful only when one learns how to look. They do not fill the place with biomass. They embroider it lightly, almost apologetically, at the margins of water and stone.

The larger creatures are rarer.

One of them is low and many-legged, though “many” is difficult to count because its limbs are often hidden beneath its body. It moves like a wet shadow with grit in it. Its back is broad, flattened, and plated with dark mineral fragments embedded in a flexible hide. Not true armor, perhaps, but accumulated protection: flakes of black stone, pale crust, iron grains, all held in a slick secretion that dries at the edges and is renewed by passing through water.

From a distance it resembles a slab that has decided, reluctantly, to cross from one pool to another.

It emerges mostly in the deeper dimness, when condensation is heavy and the flats shine. Its legs end in small hard points that click on stone and sink silently into clay. Between those points are softer pads that test the ground before weight is committed. It never trusts the crust. It advances by placing, waiting, tasting, then placing again.

Its head is not distinct. The front of the body narrows into a blunt wedge with several small dark pits along the underside. These pits drink information from wet surfaces: salt, temperature, the presence of film-colonies, recent movement, the faint electrical unease of seepage through mineral seams. It feeds by pressing its lower body against stone and slowly rasping. Behind it, surfaces are left dulled and clean. A bronze film disappears. A grey lace colony vanishes. A sulfur bead field is reduced to wet specks.

It is not a hunter in any dramatic sense, but it consumes whatever cannot withdraw in time.

When alarmed, it becomes flatter. Its legs fold under. Its wet, mineral-coated back settles flush against the ground, and because it has gathered the basin onto itself, it becomes nearly invisible. Droplets strike it and ring faintly from embedded stone. Thin water runs over it as over any other low plate. It may remain this way for hours while fog moves around it.

Another larger inhabitant lives nearer the weeping bluffs. It is softer, paler, and less often seen whole. One notices first the trail: a broad moist drag through clay, edged by rows of delicate punctures. Then a slow intake sound from under a tilted slab. Then perhaps a glimpse of a body withdrawing backward, boneless-looking but not weak, the color of wet chalk veined with grey.

This bluff-creature occupies rubble passages and black alcove pools. Its body is elongated and compressible, able to pour through openings much smaller than its resting shape. Along its sides are hard nodules, not limbs exactly, that can protrude and grip. When moving over exposed stone it raises itself on these nodules and makes the clicking pauses heard across the basin: touch, brace, draw forward; touch, brace, draw forward.

It feeds in the dark seam-water beneath fallen slabs. Its mouth is a circular pressure, used to pull mineral skins, microfauna, and soft deposits from surfaces. It also drinks from the cold black pools under the bluffs, though not often. Those pools are heavy with something the creature needs but cannot take too much of. After drinking it lies motionless, half-submerged, while faint waves travel through its pale body.

There may be only a few of these in a large region. Their growth is slow. Their reproduction, if it occurs at all, is tied to rare floods that connect the bluff pools with the central flats. In such seasons they leave behind strings of cloudy capsules glued under overhangs, each capsule with a dark mineral grain at its center. Most dry, dissolve, or are eaten by smaller things. A few wait through many cycles of wetting and exposure before opening.

There are creatures that are almost sound.

Inside hollow nodules and resonant stone plates live tiny shelled things, no larger than seeds. They occupy cavities where condensation collects but open water does not linger. Their shells are asymmetrical, shaped by the chambers they inhabit: flattened spirals, hooked cups, little angular helmets of translucent mineral. They scrape moisture from stone and feed on the residue left by evaporating seepage.

During rain they extend hairlike limbs through pores and cracks. Droplets striking nearby make them retract all at once, and the motion produces a faint chirr or ping from within the nodule. Some of the basin’s music is not stone alone but these hidden colonies responding to impact. They do not sing intentionally. Their bodies make instruments of their shelters.

Where the waist-high piers cross the flooded flat, another form gathers in the carved bowls. Each bowl holds its own small ecology. In the higher bowls, where water comes and goes, live hard little crescent organisms that cling to the stone like chipped fingernails. In the lower bowls, more constantly wet, translucent specks gather in loose swarms. They contract when shadow passes over them, drawing together into a single quivering pellet before relaxing again into separate points.

When heavy rain links the bowls and water spills from one pier to the next, these specks travel. Some are carried onward. Some cling to the overflow rims. Some are stranded in bowls where the chemistry is wrong and vanish within days, leaving only a powdery ring. The line of piers is therefore also a corridor, but a harsh one: a migration route measured in storms.

If anything built the stone arrangements, it may no longer exist in a recognizable form.

Perhaps the builders were not separate from the basin’s wet mineral life. Perhaps they were larger, slower relatives of the plate-backed grazers or the bluff dwellers, creatures with enough patience to place stone by repeated nudging over generations. Perhaps the piers were not made with hands, but with bodies that favored edges and bowls, bodies that secreted binders, dragged blocks, wore grooves by returning to the same paths. Intelligence here, if it occurred, may have resembled erosion more than planning: not invention, but preference repeated until the landscape recorded it.

Or perhaps there were once deliberate people of weight, shape, and stone. If so, their descendants have retreated into smaller margins, or dissolved into the living films, or left entirely when the water changed. The present inhabitants do not explain the structures. They only use them. Disks graze inside carved cups. Thread-creatures hang beneath causeway stones. Plate-backed animals shelter in broken rings. Bead-colonies pulse under the softened blocks. Whatever meaning the structures had has become habitat.

The dominant presence, though, is something broader than any creature.

The basin itself behaves almost like a living thing, though not one with organs or desire. It breathes through holes in the clay. It sweats from bluffs. It rings when struck. Its pools dimple in sympathy. Its seams carry signals faster than water should. Life here has adapted to that near-living mineral order. The creatures do not oppose the place; they borrow its habits. They pulse when pools pulse. They hide when stone sings in certain tones. They emerge when condensation reaches a particular density. Their senses are tuned less to light or scent than to vibration, pressure, and dissolved matter.

They are tenants of resonance.

None of them call. None display. There are no mating songs across the flats, no territorial cries, no bright bodies meant to be seen from far away. Visibility is dangerous and mostly useless. Communication, where it exists, travels through water films and stone contact: a tremor along a seam, a chemical loosening in a shared pool, a synchronized contraction under salt skin, a faint tapping from inside a hollow plate.

To live there is to be quiet in a loud world.

The marsh’s inhabitants are therefore small, mineral-colored, and discontinuous. They can wait as stains, grains, knots, crusts, and shadows. They can become active for an hour and dormant for a season. They do not conquer the bare ground. They do not green it. They scarcely soften it. But they are there, in the crowded seams and shallow bowls, in the breathing pinholes and black water under slabs: lives older in style than forests, nearer to chemistry than flesh, making do with seepage, darkness, salt, and the endless articulate speech of water on stone.
