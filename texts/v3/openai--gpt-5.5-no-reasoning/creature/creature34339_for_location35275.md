What lives there is less a roster of species than a slow agreement among wetness, height, rot, and green endurance.

The plateau is inhabited by beings that do not insist on themselves all at once. They reveal an eye, a flank, a movement in moss, a breath under a floating lawn. Most of them have learned that in such a country, to live is to cling, seep, browse, wait, root, soften, and become useful to something else before death and after it.

There are animals, certainly. There are small ones everywhere.

On the slick trunks and sweating stone ledges live many-legged grazers no longer than a finger, with flattened bodies and soft jointed plates dark as wet bark. They move in patient processions over moss and liverwort, combing films of algae and mineral bloom with brush-like mouthparts. Behind them they leave clean shining trails, narrow calligraphy across the green. Some carry translucent larvae beneath their bellies in damp pouches. Some are themselves gardens: tiny ferns and fungal beads grow from pits in their backs, disguising them from the small hooked birds that pry among the stems.

In the moss meadows live creatures like snails without shells, or worms pretending to be leaves. They are broad, ribbed, and green-brown, with fringes along their sides that drink dew directly from the air. At rest they are indistinguishable from fallen plant matter. When they move, whole patches of ground seem to reconsider their position. They rasp at old leaves, soften them with saliva, and leave behind pellets that sprout with fungus by morning.

The peat is full of larger, paler life. Long worms, some the length of vines, pass through the ancient layers with nearly geological patience. Their bodies are milk-colored, veined faintly with rose and amber, and ringed with stiff sensory hairs that test acidity, warmth, root pressure, and vibration. They do not so much burrow as persuade the peat to part. In their wake, buried water finds new paths, old seeds wake, fungal cords bridge fresh distances. The plateau depends on them more than it admits. They are engineers without intention, priests of aeration and collapse.

There are amphibians in astonishing variety, because the world is built almost entirely of damp thresholds.

Some sleep curled in leaf cups, plump and slick, their skins mottled in patterns of lichen, bark, and mineral stain. Their eyes are golden-brown, tea-dark, or cloudy green. They wake when the cups overflow, drinking through their bellies, then climb by slow suction up stems beaded with rain. Their calls are low, interior sounds: pulses, gulps, soft wooden knocks. In dusk-filled hollows, dozens answer each other until the ground seems to have developed a second heartbeat.

Others are long-limbed and almost transparent, living inside the ribbed basins of giant leaves. Their bones show as pale blue strokes. They lay eggs in strings under the water’s surface, where the embryos cling like beads of glass. These tadpoles do not swim freely; they fasten to submerged leaf hairs and filter the brown water, growing in the suspended dark until their legs unfold.

In the black marsh pools live soft-shelled animals with wide mouths and narrow patient eyes. By day they bury themselves in silt beneath floating mats, leaving only a pair of nostrils at the surface. At night they rise to breathe through red, frilled openings along their throats and flanks. The frills pulse like underwater flowers. They eat whatever comes too near: blind fish, fallen insects, froglets, the toes of careless browsers. Their shells are not hard plates but leathery domes covered in algae and tiny rooted plants. Old ones become moving islands. Seedlings sprout on them. Leeches graze them. Birds stand on them by mistake.

There are fish, though many are fish only by courtesy. Some are eel-long and root-pale, slipping through drowned root lattices with whiskers extended. Some are flat and leaf-shaped, lying among submerged debris until a larva passes overhead. Others gulp air in warm, stagnant pools and can survive for days within wet moss when water withdraws. Their scales are dark bronze or tea-black, useful in tannin water. Many have small lights under their jaws, dim as fungus glow, not enough to illuminate the water but enough to confuse, signal, or lure.

The insects of the plateau are quiet compared with the dry and sunstruck insects of lower worlds. They are not absent; they are innumerable, but their languages are suited to saturation. They tap. They tremble stems. They drum inside hollow bark. They release vaporous scents that hang in the warm stillness for hours. Moths with sodden velvet wings rest under leaves like folded scraps of night. Beetles polish the interiors of rotten logs. Needle-bodied midges hover in shafts of fog, rising and falling as if suspended from invisible threads. Wingless crickets live in the moss layer and communicate by shivering their abdomens against root fibers, sending messages through the ground that amphibians, worms, and hunting plants can also hear.

Some insects have entered treaties with plants so old that neither partner could survive alone. There are little blue-black beetles that live only inside the swollen bases of certain shrubs, cleaning fungal growth from water chambers and receiving sugary seep in return. There are pale flies whose larvae grow in cup-plants; the plant digests some, shelters others, and blooms only when enough larvae survive to gnaw a channel in the flower stalk. There are stem-tapping insects that call mates by rhythm, and nearby leaves that mimic the rhythm to draw them into sticky folds.

The plants here are not passive scenery. Many behave with such slow specificity that animal and plant become less separate than elsewhere.

Mosses withdraw from poisoned water. Fern colonies close ranks against trampling. Certain vines tighten at repeated vibration, making old animal paths into tunnels and unused ways into walls. Cup leaves tilt during rain to share overflow with seedlings growing in their shade. Some shrubs, swollen with stored water, split their bark during dry intervals so small mammals and birds can lick the seep; those visitors carry the shrub’s seeds in mats of fur or gut.

There are trees that are almost groves pretending to be individuals. Their trunks are bundled columns, fused roots, old stems, younger stems, hollowed dead cores and living sheaths. Inside them are chambers warm with decay. Bats or batlike things roost there, if bats exist in this world; otherwise something analogous does: damp-winged mammals with blunt faces, large nostrils, and fur that beads water instead of shedding it. They hang in clusters from root-struts and emerge at night to take moths, fruit, and the nectar of flowers that open with wet clicks.

Many trees host whole vertical marshes. In forks where old leaves collect, sedges grow. In sedges, frogs breed. In frog pools, small red worms churn the muck. In the worms live glittering parasites. In the moss around the pool, orchid-like flowers bloom from nutrients washed out of bird droppings and drowned insects. A branch may hold an ecosystem older than a human city.

The larger animals are seldom seen clearly. They have no reason to expose themselves.

The marsh deer are warm-bodied, soft-footed, and low to the ground. Their hooves spread wide like dark leaves, flexible enough to cross floating mats without breaking them. Their coats are not sleek but deep and absorbent, colored peat-brown, olive, rust, and gray-green. Moss and algae grow along their backs and in the velvet of their low palmated antlers. In some individuals this growth is mere camouflage; in others it may be cultivated. Small birds pick among it. Insects nest there. During the wet season the antlers can look like little traveling bogs.

They move in loose, quiet groups, browsing water shrubs, fern tips, and the swollen fruits of low canopy vines. Their young are born on floating islands, hidden among sedges, and remain almost scentless for weeks. When frightened, they do not bolt far. Speed is dangerous here. Instead they sink, kneel, flatten, or step sideways into vegetation that seems to close around them. Often the only sign of their passage is a tremor through the moss and the slow return of bent stems.

In the trees live slothful mammals whose bodies have yielded to the pace of the place. They hang beneath branch roofs and vine bridges, long-limbed, hook-clawed, thick-furred, and greened by algal bloom. Their faces are blunt and solemn, with nostrils that close against dripping water. They feed on leaves too bitter for most creatures, fermenting them in slow internal chambers. They descend rarely, and when they do the journey may take a day. Orchids sprout in their fur during the wettest months; small insects graze those orchids; the mammals scratch only when the gardens become inconvenient.

There are gliders, too: membrane-bodied creatures folded against trunks by day, leaf-colored and nearly flat. Their eyes are large, dark, and side-facing. At dusk they let go. For a breath they are only falling bark; then their limbs spread and a skin unfurls from wrist to flank to ankle. They cross gaps in silence, damp air holding them up just long enough to reach another column of roots. Some feed on fruit, some on insects startled from leaves, some on the nectar of night-opening flowers. Their young cling to the underside of the mother’s membrane like curled seeds.

Birds are present but restrained by the thickness of the world. There are few open skies for soaring. Instead there are short-winged, strong-legged birds that creep along branches and probe moss with curved beaks. Some have calls like single flute notes, repeated after long intervals from hidden perches. Some are nearly mute, communicating with flashes of pale throat skin or the lifting of feather crests. Their feathers are often water-shedding, oily, dark above and dimly iridescent below. A few specialize in following the marsh deer, plucking insects from their living coats. Others nest inside old stone rings, in the dry pockets beneath fern roots, where the ancient human or prehuman gestures still make useful shelter.

Predators exist, but they too are shaped by patience.

In the peat are rootlike hunters, pale and muscular, that extend from burrows at night. They resemble roots until touched. Then they close. Some are animals with branching appendages; some may be plants with animal habits; some may belong to a category the plateau has made for itself. They wait for vibration: the tread of a frog, the probing snout of a grazer, the wingbeat of a fallen glider. Their strike is not dramatic. A loop tightens, a body vanishes into moss, and the surface slowly lifts back into place.

There are larger hunters that move through shrub walls with barely a sound. Perhaps they are catlike, but lower, longer, more amphibious. Their fur lies in slick cords patterned like wet bark. Their paws spread wide; their whiskers are long enough to read tunnels before the face enters. They hunt by smell, pressure, and remembered routes, using old fallen trunks as causeways. They do not roar. At most they make a cough deep in the chest, a sound swallowed almost immediately by moss and vapor.

In the pools, danger is horizontal and sudden. Long-jawed things rest beneath rafts of floating root, their backs furred with green. Their eyes sit high. They know the regular crossings of deer and the night descents of tree mammals. They can wait days in warm black water, then lift with no splash, only a widening ring and the brief tearing of sedges.

Carrion does not remain carrion long. Scavengers arrive in layers: first scent-flies and beetles, then soft-mouthed mammals, then fungi, then rootlets, then moss. Bones soften under acid and vanish. Horns grow lichens before they fall apart. A dead deer becomes a raised green mound, then a shrub nursery, then a place where frogs call after rain.

The ruins have residents of their own.

The low stone rings on the ridge are inhabited by things that prefer edges between mineral and moss. Blue-black bog plants bloom in the circular wet centers, their flowers glossy as bruises. Frogs lay eggs there because the water warms a little over stone. Small burrowing mammals nest beneath tilted slabs, lining chambers with sedge down and dry fern. Certain beetles occur nowhere else, feeding on mineral salts leached from the old stones. Roots have pried the rings open, but not erased them; life here does not destroy memory so much as occupy it.

The knee-high wall that vanishes into shrubs serves as a migration route for tiny animals. Its stones hold heat after rare brighter hours, so lizards or lizardlike amphibians bask there in the dim warmth. Moss grazers follow its length. Vines use it as a buried spine. Birds perch on its highest slabs to give their sparse calls into fog. Whatever built it is gone, but the wall is still a kind of instruction: a line through a world that dislikes straightness, now softened into usefulness.

And beneath all visible inhabitants lives the greater resident: the plateau’s distributed green intelligence, not a mind in the quick animal sense, but a memory held in roots, fungal cords, chemical gradients, inherited slopes, and exchanged water.

The forests remember vanished mountains. Groves rise along mineral ghosts. Root networks follow ravines filled ten thousand lifetimes ago. Fungi connect shrubs to trees, trees to moss, moss to peat, peat to water. A drought in one basin changes flowering on a ridge days later. Fog from a particular hollow wakes blossoms that remain closed to all other mist. Plants feed unrelated seedlings because shade now may mean stability centuries hence. The place does not think in words or images. It thinks in wetness, acidity, pressure, seepage, shade, and return.

If there are people here now, they are not builders in the usual sense. They would have to be few, quiet, and partially surrendered to the plateau’s terms.

They might live on firmer ridges in houses grown more than made: frames of bent root, walls packed with moss and bark, roofs of living thatch that must be trimmed like an animal’s coat. They would travel by smell, slope-memory, and the flowering of guide plants. Their tools would rot unless constantly tended. Their paths would close if not walked. They would know which floating islands are trustworthy, which cup leaves hold clean water, which stone rings are unlucky because the peat-hunters nest nearby. They would not clear the land so much as bargain with it.

But perhaps there are no people left, only the gestures in stone and whatever descendants of their commensals remain: ridge mice in the walls, fruit shrubs spread from old camps, a moss that grows best where ash once fell.

The truest inhabitants are those that accept being temporary shapes in a long, damp continuity. A deer is partly a garden. A tree is partly a corpse. A fallen trunk is partly a road. A pool is partly a mouth. A ruin is partly a bog. An animal dies and becomes architecture; a plant grows and becomes weather; stone weeps and becomes root.

Everything that lives there is softened, patient, and intimate with decay. Nothing remains merely itself for long. The plateau allows life, but only on the condition that life be willing, eventually, to become ground.
