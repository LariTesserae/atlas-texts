The beings who live there are not delicate, though at first glance many of them seem soft.

They are a low people of the basin: broad-handed, thick-ankled, damp-skinned, accustomed from infancy to slick footing and bad air and noise so constant that quiet would feel like suffocation. They move with a peculiar certainty, not graceful in the dry-land sense, but sure. Their balance is intimate with wet tile, swelling clay, buckling plank, leather lashings gone slack from humidity. Children learn to walk by clutching rope railings over drainage cuts and by falling harmlessly into shallow black water under platforms. Old people test steps with the side of the foot, never the toe. Everyone knows the sound a roof makes before it sags, the smell of a gutter before it backs up, the pitch of a wheel axle that needs grease.

Their bodies have been shaped by the place as much as their tools have. Most are compact and strong, with rounded shoulders from lifting buckets, hauling wet hides, carrying clay in slings, turning crank bars, scraping mineral paste from troughs. Their palms are pale where the skin is thickest, split by dark lines of pitch, lime, blood, soot, and clay that never fully wash out. Their fingernails are short, worn, often rimmed in white dust or black sealant. The skin of their feet is tough, but the rest of them is always damp: temples beaded, hair slicked flat or bound away from the face, clothing clinging at the ribs and thighs. Many have a faint mineral bloom on their skin by the end of a shift, a whitish crust at the collarbone, behind the ears, along the creases of the arms.

They do not dress for display first, but for water, labor, and repair. They wear leather aprons gone glossy with use, short wrapped tunics of hide or imported cloth patched almost beyond recognition, belts heavy with bone tools, hooks, clay knives, plug-stoppers, measuring cords, waxed pouches of tokens. In work districts, people bind their calves with strips to keep mud from sucking at the legs. In kiln quarters they wear thicker soles and shoulder capes stiffened against sparks. Those who tend animals are marked by salt and dung; those who tend plaster by white hands; those who work pitch by blackened wrists; those who manage water by the permanent soaked darkness of their hems.

No one living there is entirely clean, but cleanliness matters deeply. Not purity, not perfume, but practical cleanliness: sluiced floors, scraped hooves, washed tools, cleared troughs, hides hung above the splash line, blood sent at once into channels, clay kept from souring, food jars sealed against insects. Their city would drown in filth within a week if they were careless. So they are not careless. Even the most exhausted person, returning home through the bronze damp, will pause to hook a rag from a drain grate, right a tilted jar, kick a brick back into place at the edge of a runnel.

Their lives are rhythmic rather than seasonal. There are no green springs, no harvest gold, no leaf-fall. Time is measured in firings, calvings, repairs, water levels, curing periods, market counts, animal illnesses, the souring and ripening of vats. A family may say, “That was before the west kiln cracked,” or “during the long white plastering,” or “when the third wheel had to be rebuilt,” rather than naming years. Children are aged by tasks: old enough to skim troughs, old enough to turn small wheels, old enough to walk the roof ropes, old enough to enter the drainage quarter, old enough to stand night watch at a firing.

They are loud because they must be. Their voices carry, rough and projecting, trained to cut through water thunder and animal cries. A greeting is often half shout, half signal. Conversations happen in bursts across lanes, from roof to courtyard, from one end of a work line to another. They use many gestures: palm flat for stop-water, two fingers hooked for clog, fist over heart for agreement, knuckles tapped twice on wet stone for warning. In the worst quarters they communicate by rhythm, striking pipes, beams, tile stacks, or stretched hide drums. A wheel crew can coordinate gates and bucket chains by knocks alone. The people hear the city the way sailors hear wind.

They are not a mystical folk, but they are reverent toward continuance. Their sacred acts are maintenance acts intensified: clearing, sealing, balancing, measuring, sluicing, mending before failure. They do not pray for abundance in fields. They pray that water rise no higher than the mark. They pray that clay dry evenly, that a birth not sour, that rot stay outside the wall, that the hidden channels remain open, that the herd take mash, that the kiln fire not blister the load. Their gods, if they call them gods, are not sky kings or forest mothers. They are levels, thresholds, mouths, hinges, basins, sealed seams, the heat inside clay, the patience of animals, the hand that repeats a needed motion.

At the city’s center, the waist-deep basin is tended by people of particular standing: water-keepers, oath-witnesses, tool-washers, fever-coolers. They are not priests in embroidered robes. They wear dark waterproofed aprons and keep shaved forearms. They know how to read the surface of the central water by lamplight: whether fine silt has entered, whether underground seepage has warmed, whether some hidden crack is changing the balance. Disputes are settled there because the basin is the city’s image of rightness: what enters must leave; what is used must be restored; what is promised must be maintained.

Their houses are deep, low, and layered by function. A dwelling is rarely only a dwelling. It may include a sleeping platform, two animal stalls, a drying alcove, a small oven, a repair wall hung with tools, a vat corner, a roof pen for birds, and a shared drain access. Privacy exists, but it is partial. Families hear one another breathing through walls, arguing through courtyards, laboring under roofs. A person belongs not merely to a household but to a run of water, a cluster of walls, a shared kiln, an animal route, a repair obligation.

Kinship is practical and expansive. Children are watched communally because every adult’s hands are often occupied and every lane contains hazards. Apprentices sleep in workshops; widowed elders sit near sorting trays and teach by correction; cousins may be defined as much by shared labor as by blood. To be kin is to know which roof you may cross, which bucket you may borrow, which animal you may calm, which wall you are expected to help plaster when it weeps.

They are suspicious of idleness but not of rest. Rest is necessary, like drying time. People sleep in short stretches around firing schedules, animal needs, drainage shifts. Many nap in the humid dusk under hide screens, one hand still on a tool or rope. Their leisure is noisy too: pipe flutes of bone, stamping games on dry platforms, sung work chants turned comic after hours, gambling with clay counters, contests of balance over narrow wet beams. Stories are told loudly, often collaboratively, interrupted by corrections from listeners who know the same old cycles.

Their beauty is not floral, not airy. They admire compactness, endurance, competent hands, a voice that carries without breaking, scars from useful accidents, hair oiled and tightly braided out of the work, a well-fitted leather belt, a clean repair, a perfectly trimmed hoof, a jar whose lip pours without dribble. A handsome person is “well-set,” “true-footed,” “good in the wet,” “clear-throated,” “not wasted in motion.” Jewelry exists, but it is heavy and close to the body: shell discs, fired-clay beads, bone pins, horn toggles, black stone weights polished by touch. Bright plant dyes are rare and treasured. Most color comes from mineral pigment: ocher, soot, lime white, brick red, blue-gray wash.

They eat dense, salted, fermented things. Warm mash, curds, broth thickened with stored meal, fish paste, shell meat, blood cakes, rendered fat, sour vat loaves, eggs, milk, dried imports softened in steam. Food smells strong in that damp air. Meals are often communal because fire, fuel, and dry storage are precious. Nothing is wasted. Bones become stock, then tools, then fragments for lime or charm markers. Hides become doors, buckets, lashings. Dung becomes fuel. Broken pots become drainage fill, paving, temper for new clay.

Their animals are not background property; they are co-inhabitants, wealth, labor, warmth, hazard, and kin of a different order. A household may be known by its donkeys, its wide-hooved turning beasts, its roof dogs, its milk goats, its white fowl that nest in chimney niches. Children sleep beside calves in cold damp spells. Old draft animals are spoken to with respect and sometimes fed mash by hand long after they cease useful work. Slaughter is common and unsentimental, but never careless. The killing rooms are washed immediately; thanks, if given, are brief and procedural.

Dogs in the city are narrow, scarred, and clever, with waterproof coats and curled tails, bred to patrol refuse lanes, harry rats, guard pens, and bark from roofs at strangers. Cats are soot-colored, brick-colored, shell-white, kiln-warm, half domestic and half spectral, appearing in lamp-glow near ovens. The large turning animals are placid until they are not: low-backed, broad-skulled, mud-loving, with feet like mallets and eyes that reflect fire. They know their circular paths better than some children know alleys. Their handlers sing to them in heavy repetitive phrases that match the groan of the wheel.

There are also the small citizens: rats slick from drain mist; pale lizards on warm walls; beetles in rope coils; crabs under grates; blind or half-blind eels in cistern gloom; roof birds that build nests from hair, cord, leather shavings, and stolen thread. The people classify them by usefulness and nuisance, not by wildness. Some are trapped, some tolerated, some encouraged. Certain water insects signal foulness; certain lizards keep biting flies down; certain birds warn of bad smoke before humans feel it.

The human inhabitants have little sense of wilderness. Outside the city is not nature to them, not freedom, not green distance. It is raw material, exposure, glare under vapor, uncertain footing, brine pans, clay cuts, shell heaps, black seams of pitch. To leave the maintained city is to step out of protection, out of the web of cleared drains and known sounds. Travelers exist, traders and haulers from uplands or unseen waters, but they are regarded with fascination and pity. Dry-land people seem brittle, over-tall, oddly quiet, ignorant of how much work the world requires to keep from dissolving.

Yet the basin people are not trapped in misery. The city is hard, but it is theirs in the fullest bodily sense. They know it through soles, palms, nostrils, shoulders, and ears. They can identify districts by the echo of hooves, by the mineral taste on the tongue, by the warmth of paving beneath a foot, by whether the drip from a roof edge is lime-sweet or pitch-bitter. A blind person could navigate much of it by sound and slope. A child can tell which kiln is being opened by the change in the air.

They are conservative because error is expensive. Innovation is not forbidden, but it must prove itself without upsetting levels, routes, loads, breeding, firing, drainage. A new tool is tested beside the old one for many cycles. A new wall mixture is watched through rains. A new vat food is fed first to animals, then to apprentices, then to everyone else if no one sickens. Their law has the same character: precedent as patchwork, obligation as shared maintenance. Theft is serious less because of ownership than because a missing tool may clog a system; negligence may be punished more harshly than rage.

Their children are wonderfully unruly within narrow constraints. They run roof to roof where adults allow, race floating chips in gutters, catch drain crabs with bent pins, dare one another to touch cooling kiln bricks, sculpt animals from scrap clay and leave them in niches. They know which puddles are safe and which are deep with suction. They imitate the calls of wheel bosses and animal handlers. Their lullabies are nearly shouted over water, and their nightmares are of silence, dry cracking, walls unweeping, troughs empty, animals gone.

The old become keepers of memory, but memory here is not a grand tale of conquest or migration. It is a catalogue of how things fail. An elder knows the corner that bulged once and may bulge again; the drain whose stone lip must never be replaced with brick; the family line of a stubborn cow breed; the kiln that prefers a slow night fire; the year a certain imported wood swelled and jammed three doors; the fever that came from a sealed cistern. They sit on raised benches in damp halls, wrapped in patched hides, watching young hands work, correcting knots and mixtures and postures. Their authority is technical, therefore sacred.

Death is handled with the same practical solemnity. In a wet city, bodies cannot simply be left to earth that has not learned to receive them. Some are fired to bone ash in special kilns, mixed with lime, sealed in jars, built into threshold walls or placed near household drains as guardians of passage. Some bones are cleaned, marked, and kept in family niches. There may be a belief that the dead should continue to support the living structure—not metaphorically only, but materially. A wall containing ancestors is trusted differently.

They fear imbalance above all: rot in hidden beams, sickness moving through herds, a blocked drainage quarter, spoiled ferment, a kiln collapse, imported dry plague, sudden silence in the insects, water rising without cause or falling from the central basin. They fear fire too, but not as dry cities do. Fire is a servant that must be fed in wet air, coaxed, shielded, watched. A runaway fire is rare; a failed fire is commoner and more dreaded among potters, lime burners, cooks, and plasterers. Unfired clay returning to sludge is a small humiliation; a whole load lost is grief.

Their humor is earthy, technical, full of insults about bad knots, crooked walls, weak buckets, animals smarter than their owners, people who slip on dry ground. They mock uplanders for wasting wood and for speaking softly. They mock one another constantly, because competence must be sharpened in public. But praise, when given, is plain and powerful: “It held.” “The level did not move.” “No beast sickened.” “Your seam stayed shut.” “Good hands.”

There may be people among them marked apart by craft to the point of strangeness. The vat-keepers, pale from indoor ferment rooms, smelling of sour warmth and salt. The shell-burners, eyebrows singed, skin dusted white. The pitch cutters, black to the elbows, half miners, half priests of sealing. The drain crews, strongest and loudest, with legs scarred by hidden grates and biting trench things. The counters, who sit in damp courts over clay tokens and tallies, their fingertips precise rather than powerful. The animal speakers, who can quiet a bellowing turn-beast with one phrase. The night firers, red-eyed from lamp and kiln, sleeping through the loudest parts of day.

If there are rulers, they do not live high above the city, because height means little here. Authority belongs to those who coordinate systems: water, fire, animals, stored food, clay, repair labor. A council might meet in the low pillared hall with wet floors, not on thrones but on stone benches raised just above seep level. Their power is distributive and managerial. They allocate fuel, settle wall claims, assign drainage watches, regulate slaughter days, maintain causeways, count imported plant matter, decide when a district must pause ordinary work for emergency replastering. A bad ruler would be one who lets maintenance lag. A tyrant would be one who hoards dryness.

They are intimate with impermanence but live as if permanence can be made by repetition. This is their deepest trait. They know everything softens, cracks, rots, clogs, slips, breeds, fouls, leaks. They do not expect the world to stay sound on its own. Soundness is an action. A wall is not a thing built once; it is a relationship between clay, water, heat, hands, and attention. A city is not founded; it is kept founded.

So the inhabitants are keepers of the not-yet-eroded world: mud-workers, animal-tenders, water-lifters, kiln-watchers, patchers, counters, gutter-clearers, vat-brewers, hide-cutters, shell-burners, roof-crossers, oath-witnesses. They live crowded among beasts and tools in a leafless ecology of seams. Their culture is humid, loud, practical, communal, suspicious of neglect, reverent toward balance. They are not waiting for the city to become finished. They are the process by which it remains exactly unfinished enough to live.
