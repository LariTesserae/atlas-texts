What lives there is not one species, or even one people, but an entire wet, breathing civilization of animals, keepers, engineers, vats, and old domestications.

The first impression would be size: not because every creature is enormous, but because life there has arranged itself by scale. The inhabitants are distributed vertically and socially according to body, heat, temperament, water-use, and the strength of bridges. Some live in plazas. Some in cisterns. Some in roof seams. Some in the service tunnels under the city, touching their way through warm runoff. Some live only in law: breeding lines, feed rights, permitted crossings, the recorded habits of herds dead for centuries but still shaping architecture.

The dominant life is animal life. Not wild in the forest sense, because there is no forest; not tame in the pastoral sense, because there are no pastures. The fauna belongs to the city the way pipes and stairs do. It is managed, inherited, feared, loved, taxed, repaired around. Animals are not decorations or pests, though some are both. They are transport, waste processors, heat sources, food, labor, weather-sensors, status, danger, memory.

The great lower-avenue beasts are perhaps the most visible. They are vast, patient quadrupeds with thick gray hides that resemble rain-dark stone. Their backs are broad enough for platforms, their shoulders plated with calloused ridges where harness frames rest, their heads low and heavy, with small wet eyes protected by folds of flesh. They move slowly, but not stupidly. Their feet are wide and padded, made for slick paving, and their weight has polished the city’s main routes into shallow troughs. When one turns, traffic must obey. When a herd of them crosses a plaza, the city seems to tilt its attention around them.

They haul freight between terraces: ore, ceramic pipe, pressed feed bricks, bone sacks, engine housings, sealed vats, carcasses wrapped in treated cloth. Some are publicly owned, their flanks marked with mineral paint and brass tags. Others belong to old animal houses, dynasties of keepers who know bloodlines better than family names. Their moods are studied like weather. A beast refusing a ramp may mean a loose paving slab, a tremor in the cistern below, a smell of illness, or merely ancestral stubbornness. No one ignores them.

Around them move the smaller labor breeds: spined pack animals that kneel on command in loading yards; six- or eight-limbed haulers bred for narrow stairways; blunt-snouted sledge pullers with hides always shining from grease and rain; sure-footed wall-climbers used to repair gutters and ceramic conduits; warm-backed tunnel stock that can work in flooded lower passages without panic. Many of these are not beautiful in any ordinary way. They are jointed, folded, muscled low to the ground, with nostrils that seal against spray and eyes clouded by generations of steam. Their beauty is functional: the angle of a load-bearing spine, the calm spread of toes on wet stone, the practiced lowering of a body beneath a crane hook.

Above them live the clingers and roost animals.

The walls are never still. Under cornices, in the sheltered backs of statues, beneath abandoned aqueduct lips, narrow-bodied creatures hang in clusters. They have ribbed torsos, hooked limbs, and skins mottled to match limestone, soot, and old brick. During the day they sleep folded flat against masonry, indistinguishable from bundled tools or strips of wet leather. At dusk they loosen themselves and move sideways over the buildings, licking mineral crust, hunting drain-creatures, stripping protein films from condensation plates, stealing from feed gutters if the keepers are inattentive.

Some are tolerated. Some are licensed. Some are sacred to particular guilds because they eat destructive mites from pipe seals or give warning before pressure failures. Children learn early which wall-creatures can be touched, which must be driven away with clappers, and which should be reported to the nearest animal office because they carry rot into the roost towers.

The air belongs to harsher things.

Flocks of pale warm-blooded fliers wheel through the soot and vapor. They are not quite birds, though many have beaks, or beaklike shears, and many have feathers in some places and naked skin in others. Their calls are metallic, coughing, argumentative. They nest in roost-towers built with ledges, heat shafts, and waste chutes. Some feed on vat-cakes thrown from balconies at appointed hours; some skim cistern surfaces for blind fish; some follow herds to pick parasites from their backs; some descend on refuse yards in boiling white storms.

There are also heavier aerial animals, seen more often as shadows than bodies. At night their wingbeats move the vapor over the roofs. They roost on broken bridges and high furnace stacks, where they spread heat-scarred membranes to dry. Their droppings build calcified shelves on old stone. Workers harvest these deposits with masks and chisels. It is said that certain districts were founded where the great fliers chose to roost, because their waste enriched the microbial pans and their bones, when they died, supplied a lime prized for waterproof mortar.

The water holds another population entirely.

The reservoirs are opaque, mineral-heavy, warm in layers. Things surface there carefully: nostrils, eyes, ridged backs, whiskered mouths. Canal-beasts move beneath floating scum and condensation slicks. Some are draft animals for barges, blind or half-blind, guided by bell tones and pressure taps on the water. Some are food stock, raised in dark cisterns until harvest. Some are older than the cisterns themselves, or believed to be: broad-backed, slow-breathing animals that occupy municipal reservoirs by treaty rather than ownership.

They are fed according to schedule from gantries. Their teeth, if they have teeth, are inspected by crews with long hooks. Their mating is restricted with gates and sonic horns, because an unplanned breeding in a pressure basin can ruin a whole district’s water discipline. Reservoir keepers are a specialized class: quiet, damp-skinned people with excellent balance, able to read ripples under lamplight and identify individuals from scars glimpsed for half a second in brown water.

Then there are the drain lives.

Every gutter, culvert, tiled channel, and warm outflow has its own quick ecology. Slick armored things that flash under grates. Soft translucent bodies pressed flat in seams. Blind chain-creatures traveling nose-to-tail through side channels, grazing the films that thicken there. Pale jointed scavengers that emerge after rain to consume shed skin, spilled feed, and the soft edges of waste. Fat-bodied filter animals in settlement tanks. Wax-skinned worm masses in digestion houses. Tiny clicking creatures in the condensation lines. Unnamed things that belong to the maintenance crews more than to naturalists, because they are known by what they clog, clean, stain, or predict.

The people—if they are people in the familiar sense—live among all this as a smaller, more deliberate animal.

They are not the masters of the place in any simple way. They are coordinators, patchers, counters, feeders, law-keepers, machinists, surgeons, singers, slaughterers, breeders, hydrologists, and clerks of appetite. They are physically modest compared with the great beasts and architecturally dependent on raised walkways, arcades, grates, shutters, warning horns, and walls thick enough to make coexistence survivable.

They dress for wet stone. Oilcloth, treated hide, waxed seams, ceramic buttons, felted microbial cloth, boots with broad soles or hoof-cleats for slick ramps. Their clothing often carries guild markings: pump blue, roost white, furnace red-black, cistern gray, bone-mill ocher, herd brass. Many wear masks, not against plague necessarily, but against vapor, dust, dung-sour air, fungal feed spores, and mineral fumes. Children wear harness-belts near animal avenues. Old people carry hook-sticks useful for balance, valve-turning, and discouraging smaller scavengers.

Their bodies may have adapted to the plateau: broad lungs, strong calves, skin accustomed to damp, eyes narrowed against glare from wet paving. They speak loudly when outdoors but softly indoors, because sound behaves strangely there. They know horn codes before they can read. They can identify neighborhoods by smell: hot iron rain, curing hide, vat-sour feed, reservoir mud, lamp oil, disinfectant, wet brick, animal sleep.

Their society is probably organized less around land than flow.

Water rights matter. Heat rights matter. Feed chains matter. Passage rights matter enormously. A family might not own much floor space but may possess hereditary access to a condensation gallery, a share in a herd, a license to scrape microbial crust from municipal pans, or the right to guide certain beasts across a particular bridge. Guilds are powerful because no one survives without pumps, drains, feed vats, and animal management. A clogged channel can threaten a block. A spoiled vat can starve a district’s smaller stock. A misrouted herd can kill hundreds, not out of malice but mass.

There are animal courts in both senses: vast enclosures and legal institutions. Disputes over a beast’s injury, a broken hoof, a polluted trough, an unauthorized mating, or a dangerous temperament may be heard with as much seriousness as disputes over inheritance. Some animals are property. Some are municipal infrastructure. Some belong to temples, factories, military houses, or old civic trusts. Some, especially the reservoir elders or great roof fliers, may be legally unownable.

The emotional life of the people is entangled with creatures.

They sing to herds, but the songs are rhythmic commands, timing devices, and inherited comforts all at once. They mourn valuable beasts with processions through wet streets, the carcass carried to rendering not as disposal but as civic return: hide to membrane, bone to meal, fat to lamps, horn to tools, gut to cord, mineral ash to mortar. Children keep small gutter animals in ceramic boxes, though parents object because they smell and breed in drains. Lovers meet in condensation galleries or under roost towers at the hour when the pale flocks lift. Old keepers can become famous for calming a dangerous turn-beast, hatching a difficult clutch, or predicting a migration shift from the taste of morning water.

The city’s food is almost entirely unleafed.

People eat fish from cisterns, cultured mats cut into slabs, rendered fats, vat-grown fungal loaves, broth thickened with microbial meal, eggs from roost animals, meat from managed stock, mineral salts, fermented protein pastes, pressed cakes, marrow, shell-flour, and things that would seem industrial elsewhere but are culinary here. Food culture is probably elaborate despite its narrow biological base. Texture matters: gelatin, crisped skin, steamed fungus, smoked fat, sour vat-curd, broth clarified through chitin filters. Color is muted: gray, amber, ivory, brown, black, rust-red. Green, if it appears at all, is alarming.

The prohibition against green life hangs over them.

Perhaps they do not know why plants are forbidden. Perhaps they know too well and no longer speak of it plainly. The ban may be theological, ecological, historical, or practical. It may commemorate a plague of roots that cracked cisterns, a toxic bloom mistaken for salvation, a failed agricultural empire, or an ancient covenant with the animal systems that replaced vegetation. Whatever the origin, the absence of leaves has become part of identity. To cultivate green would not merely be illegal; it would be an offense against the city’s understanding of itself.

Still, there must be rumors. A child claiming to have seen a blade in a sealed courtyard. A smuggled seed kept dry in a bone tube. A forbidden greenhouse in the house of a rich eccentric. A lower world beneath the clouds where trees exist. A dead district where roots once entered the drains and the walls had to be burned with lime and sealed behind iron. The inhabitants may laugh at such stories in public and dream of them privately.

Beneath the official civilization live other human or near-human populations.

Tunnel crews who spend so long below that their dialect is full of bell codes and echo-clicks. Vat tenders pale from amber lamp-light. Slag-field gatherers who follow burrowing animals and harvest shed plates from warm metallic dunes. Roof people who maintain roost towers and move across high bridges with casual fearlessness. Reservoir families who marry among themselves because outsiders cannot tolerate the smell or the danger. Render-yard clans marked by grease, smoke, and social necessity. Clerks in pump offices whose ledgers determine whether a neighborhood receives water, heat, or feed slurry before a storm front arrives.

And there may be feral people too: not savages, but those living outside registry, among ruins and animal routes. They might occupy cracked warehouses, abandoned furnace galleries, old market roofs, surviving by scraping films, trapping drain life, scavenging from herd passages, and trading information to official keepers. In a city that incorporates ruin, abandonment is rarely absolute. Someone always lives in the half-collapsed places: people, animals, cultures, laws no longer written but still obeyed.

The microbial life is the hidden foundation.

It is not plant life, but it has taken the ecological place of green in a stranger, flatter way. Films, mats, crusts, membranes, vat cultures, pale colonies under lamps. Some are cultivated in tiled houses, their growth measured by heat and mineral feed. Some line runoff pans and are scraped with wooden or ceramic blades. Some pulse on underground walls, protected because blind animals depend on them. Some are nuisances that foul valves, sour cisterns, poison stock, or make floors treacherous. Some may be ancient domestications older than the current city, preserved through collapse after collapse because without them the animal abundance would fail.

They do not reach upward. They spread sideways. They thicken where there is warmth, wetness, waste, mineral, and managed light. In that sense, they are the plateau’s true pasture: a pasture turned horizontal against stone, hidden in vats, drains, pans, cisterns, and warm seams.

Nothing living there is innocent of infrastructure.

Even the wildest animals know bells. Even pests follow heat routes created by engines. Even the people’s myths likely involve pumps, gates, and rain. Birth and death are scheduled around drainage, breeding windows, vat readiness, bridge capacity, furnace heat. The city has no countryside to retreat into, no leafy outside that renews it. Its renewal is circular, mechanical, animal, microbial, civic. It eats itself and survives.

The inhabitants, taken together, are a wet urban biome with memory.

They are gray beasts steaming at dusk. Pale fliers screaming over chimneys. Blind cistern bodies turning under opaque water. Wall-clingers asleep beneath cornices. Drain things clicking under grates. Keepers in oilcloth chanting a herd through a narrow turn. Pump clerks listening to rain on ceramic roofs. Children learning that no flat roof is safe, no blocked gutter harmless, no animal avenue truly empty. Engineers who treat ancient engines like organs. Slaughterers who waste nothing. Priests or magistrates standing below carved warnings against green life, repeating laws whose origins have become mist.

They are not a fallen people living in ruins, though ruins are everywhere. They are not nature reclaiming the city, because nature here never became leaf and root. They are what happened instead: a civilization that made its animals into streets, its waste into food, its moisture into law, its engines into weather, and its absence of plants into a principle.

What lives there is a city that breathes through beasts.
