What lives there is not a single species but a whole dry congregation: a sealed ecology tuned to heat, salt, vibration, charge, and old machinery.

At first the inhabitants look like fittings. They have learned the shapes of bolts, washers, enamel cracks, lamp brackets, pipe barnacles. The smallest are brass-backed many-legged creatures lined along warm seams in exact rows, each foot spread against ceramic or metal, feeding not on flesh or leaf but on tremor. They drink the engines through their limbs. When disturbed, they do not flee; they become more object-like, locking down until their shells read as cast ornament or mineral crust.

There are pale lamp-moths too, though they are not really moths in any ordinary floral sense. Their wings are dry membranes, powdery and veined in patterns like broken circuitry. They move slowly through the amber and green industrial light, not seeking nectar but mineral bloom. They settle on bristle-plants and scrape crystalline dust with file-like mouths, carrying charged powders from one black-green growth to another. Their pollination is mechanical, abrasive, almost clerical.

The plants are barely plants. They are heat-roots, wire-lichens, bristle colonies, rubbery black-green conductors braided through cracks and around rails. They do not reach toward light. They lean toward leaks: warmth, static, vibration, the faint electrical seepage of buried systems. Their tissues are waxed and mineral-fed, dry as insulation. Some grow as cords. Some as stiff black fans. Some as small ruffs around bolts. They are the chamber’s slow nerves, joining stone, machine, and animal into one exchanging body.

In hotter recesses live larger plated things, neither lizard nor insect but something between furnace-creature and instrument. Their bodies are flat and jointed, made for sliding beneath machine housings. Through translucent armor, internal chemical sacs glow faintly orange, violet, or sulfur-yellow. They pulse in time with the engines, as if their organs have accepted the machinery as a heart outside the body. They do not hunt dramatically. They wait, absorb, rasp, shed, and pulse.

From cable bundles hang blind soft-skinned animals in sleeping knots. They are almost avian in posture, almost mammalian in their throats, almost larval in their skin. Their eyes have sealed or never formed. Their bodies are pale, warm, and folded, suspended upside down in dense clusters where cables sag near the ceiling. Their throats inflate and deflate continuously, producing low tones that merge with the chamber’s sound. They are not asleep because it is night; they are suspended in a permanent interval, singing or breathing into the second engine’s hidden rhythm.

The burrowers live wherever the chamber is already hollow: faults, ducts, old conduit interiors, hexagonal pores in slag, gaps behind capped pipes. They do not make soil. They polish entry rims. They pelletize salt. They appear as eyes in black seams, then vanish backward without scraping. Some are seed-sized and numerous. Some are long, pale, segmented things that move only when vibration masks them. Others are shell-capped and wedge-shaped, built to pry flakes from mineral plates and carry them away piece by piece.

Around the circular hatch in the floor, the life becomes ceremonial. Brass-backed feeders form rings. Blind hangers face inward from their cables. Bristle-plants subtly lean toward the rim. Small animals tend the troughs of dry crystalline pellets, removing some and returning others, keeping the level exact. They behave less like workers than like parts of a regulator. Whether they understand the hatch as sacred, warm, nourishing, dangerous, or simply necessary is unclear. Their ritual may be instinct. Their instinct may be maintenance.

There are also the singers hidden inside housings: ceramic-throated organisms wedged in transformer ribs and pipe cavities, producing tones like bearings, valves, or distant rotating parts. Some may be animals imitating machines. Some may be machines colonized by throats. Some may be neither distinction anymore. In this chamber, a living body and a working component can occupy the same role for so long that identity becomes secondary to function.

They all live by balance.

Nothing here blooms wildly. Nothing overruns the room. No mold surges, no swarm erupts, no predator breaks the pattern. Appetite is measured. Growth is exact. Waste becomes crust, pellet, husk, powder, charge, song. Death does not rot; it dries, curls, laminates, and is filed back into the system. A body becomes salt-thread, shell-flake, alkali dust, insulation nest, mineral feed. Even decay has edges.

They fear water, if fear is the right word. More likely they have no category for it except catastrophe. Their skins, shells, roots, eggs, and feeding structures belong to a world where solvent is forbidden. Moisture would be poison, flood, weather, dissolution, short circuit, and resurrection of dangerous chemistry. They are not desert creatures exactly; deserts still remember rain. These beings are inhabitants of a place that has never planned for wetness.

Their senses are tuned away from light and air. They listen with feet, shells, throats, bristles, mineralized jaws. They feel the chamber as pressure, rhythm, voltage, warmth. The second engine beneath the main sound is their moon, their tide, their buried sun. They orient to it. They thicken around its nodes. Their bodies make maps of its unseen rotation.

What lives there, then, is an engine-reef.

A salt-dry population of shell, filament, wing, throat, plate, bristle, larva, and root.

A society without haste, without weather, without softness.

Life that has accepted constancy as climate and machinery as geology. Life that does not break the silence because there is no silence to break. Life that holds still not from death, but from precision.

Everything there is listening.

Everything there is being counted.
