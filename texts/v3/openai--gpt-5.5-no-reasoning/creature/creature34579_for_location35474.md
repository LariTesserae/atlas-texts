What lives there is not a single species so much as a pact: a vertical population of workers, instruments, scavengers, breeders, half-domesticated cave animals, and mineral-adapted organisms all caught inside the same narrow discipline of dryness.

The people—if people is the right word—are slight, economical beings. Not fragile exactly, but spare in the way of things made by altitude and rationing. They do not waste breath, water, gesture, or heat. Their bodies have learned the cleft’s rules: narrow shoulders for ladders and service gaps, strong fingers, compact chests, dark-adapted eyes under protective lenses, skin roughened or sealed against abrasive dust. They dress in close layers of waxed fabric, ceramic scales, patched leather, and metal clips. Everything on them fastens twice. Belts carry hooks, chalk, coded tags, mask filters, tapping pins, folded gloves, and little sealed food tablets that look more like industrial washers than meals.

They are not cave primitives. They are machinists, pressure-readers, animal-keepers, dust chemists, lift tenders, mirror cleaners, welders, archivists, scaffold-priests, auger surgeons, counterweight inspectors. Their civilization is intensely technical, but not sleek. It is practical to the edge of superstition. Every tool has a history of accidents built into its shape. Every railing bears rings because someone once failed to clip on there. Every hatch has a charm of etched metal beside its pressure gauge, not because they believe the charm replaces maintenance, but because maintenance itself has become a ritual form.

They speak quietly. Their languages are full of clicks, taps, held consonants, breathless syllables, and hand signs designed for masks and darkness. Much of their communication is conducted through the city itself: three knocks on a pipe; a shuttered lamp blink; a colored dust tag hung from a cable; a change in the rhythm of a conveyor. Children learn district-sounds before they learn maps. They know where they are by the pitch of a grating, the smell of a reagent line, the coldness of a wall under the palm.

Their faces are often partly hidden. Masks hang below their chins when not sealed over the mouth. Goggles make their eyes look large and insectile. Their hair, when they have it, is braided tight, shaved, wrapped, or powdered with anti-static ash. Beards are uncommon near machinery. Loose cloth is taboo. Ornament exists, but it is small and tactile: carved grip-rings, ceramic beads clipped flat to cuffs, family grooves filed into tools, colored insulation threaded through harness straps.

They do not think of the mountain as home in a sentimental way. They think of it as pressure, resource, danger, and enclosure. It is not mother or god. It is the thing around them that must be answered correctly.

They are born into verticality. Infants sleep in suspended cradle-cells with mesh lids and grounding wires. Toddlers are tethered before they can walk far. Their games involve sorting mineral grains, naming warning hums, balancing washers on vibrating plates, teaching tame scavengers to follow light signals. A child who can climb from one deck to another without touching a forbidden pipe is praised more than one who can recite dates. Height is not awe-inspiring to them; it is infrastructure.

They have little concept of open distance. A horizon would disturb them. Rain would horrify them.

The idea of water falling freely from the sky belongs, for them, to disaster tales.

Their elders are not necessarily old in years, but old in exposure. They have scarred knuckles, silvered lungs, damaged hearing, or a faint tremor from too many static storms. They can tell by a draft whether an upper intake is choking. They can sleep through ordinary machinery but wake instantly when a counterweight chain misses a beat. Their authority is practical and immediate. In a place where the city rearranges itself constantly, memory is a survival organ.

Among them live the animals, and the animals are everywhere.

The smallest are almost invisible: powder-mites, heat-lice, static fleas, pale joint-specks that cluster in warm seams and feed on films no human can see. They are tolerated until they gather in numbers large enough to foul a relay, then discouraged with tuned vibration, bitter dusts, or the release of needle-mouthed grazers. Children collect their shed casings in matchbox trays. Technicians read infestations as diagnostic signs: too many amber mites means a junction is over-warm; silver dust-crawlers indicate a leak in the catalytic beds; black-eyed pin-worms in a conduit suggest insulation fatigue.

Larger scavengers haunt the walkways. They are long-bodied, low, and careful, with shell backs the color of unglazed porcelain or old bone. Their legs fold sideways like delicate tools. Their mouths are not jaws so much as rasping plates, built to scrape chemical residue from ceramic, metal, and glass. They move with solemn industry, cleaning spills before workers reach them. Some wear tiny stamped tags riveted harmlessly into their dorsal plates. Some are feral. The tagged ones know feeding chimes, danger lamps, and the difference between permissible dust and poisonous powder.

There are carriers too: pale, many-limbed haulers with patient temperaments and dull jewel eyes. They pull miniature carts along narrow rails, carry sealed capsules through passages too cramped for workers, or cling upside down beneath bridges with loads strapped to their bellies. They are not affectionate, but they are loyal to routine. Feed one at the wrong hour and it will follow the smell of your gloves back to your sleeping cell, waiting outside with awful stillness. Their keepers speak to them through touch-patterns along the shell and low clicks made behind the teeth.

The flutterers live under catwalks in folded colonies. They are blind or nearly blind, all membrane, ribs, and powdery hide. Their wings are not soft like bats’ wings but dry and faintly translucent, opening with a papery snap. They wake during pressure changes, not at dawn. When the cleft inhales or the upper filters shift, colonies ripple awake and pour into maintenance shafts, reading pressure gradients with organs along their throats. Workers watch them carefully. A sudden migration of flutterers can mean a blocked vent, a coming inversion, or a deep rock movement not yet felt by instruments.

The grazers are rat-sized, but not rats. They have blunt triangular heads, grinding mouths, and foreclaws adapted to cling to heated plates. Instead of plants they eat mineral bloom, cultured film, wax-protein residue, and the thin chemical skins grown deliberately on warm ceramic racks. Their bellies glow faintly after feeding. In the dark, a herd of them moving through a service gallery looks like embers crawling low to the floor.

There are also things that do not fully belong to the city but have become part of it.

Translucent burrowers slide through insulation and soft packing compounds. They are visible only as distortions: slow, muscular shadows passing behind pale panels. Workers dislike them, but cannot eradicate them, because the burrowers also consume worse pests and keep certain packed layers aerated. They are managed by heat, scent, and sacrificial tunnels. If one emerges openly, people make room. Its body is too delicate to touch and too chemically active to anger.

In the Deep Racks, older creatures remain from before excavation. They came from sealed pockets in the young mountain: dry cave fauna that had lived on mineral seep, trapped gases, metallic salts, and one another. Some are eyeless white centipede-things as long as a forearm, with whiskers that spit blue sparks. Some are plated nodules that pretend to be stones until exposed to body heat. Some are tall, trembling, many-kneed organisms like walking root systems, though there are no roots here and never were. These are not pets. They are neighbors under uneasy treaty.

The city has learned which of them can be harnessed, which can be lured, which must be sealed away, and which must be left alone because they know passages the builders do not.

Above all lives the swarm-life: not one swarm but many cycles of emergence, shedding, nesting, and retreat. Every district has its expected population. The Loom has wire-nesters that braid their egg cases into warm cable bundles. The Foundry Shelf has ceramic-backed heat drinkers that cling near vacuum ovens. The Upper Intake has dust skimmers with charged hairs that spread themselves across filter screens during storms and must be combed off with grounded rakes. The Deep Racks have cold lantern grubs that pulse when pressure drops, used by some workers as living gauges.

Nothing in the city is merely animal, pest, machine, or citizen. Categories blur because usefulness is a form of citizenship there.

A cleaner-beast that keeps a stair from becoming slick with reagent dust is part of public safety. A colony of flutterers nesting under a bridge may be protected by municipal rule because their panic once warned of a collapsing pressure curtain. A mite infestation may be logged as a maintenance symptom rather than exterminated outright. Children are taught not “this creature is good” or “this creature is bad,” but “this creature means heat,” “this creature means leak,” “this creature means wait,” “this creature means clip on now.”

The human inhabitants live by the same logic. A person is known by what systems they maintain.

The intake clans are wind-thin and frost-dusted, always smelling of filters and outer grit. They climb nearest the sky-slit, masked and clipped, harvesting charge from storms and clearing mineral crust from scoops. Their eyes are narrowed from glare, though they live mostly in dimness. They are proud in a quiet way because they touch the outside most often and survive it.

The Loom technicians are wiry, patient, and almost unnervingly calm. They spend hours wedged inside walls of cables and tubes, reading the city’s pulse through gloved fingertips. They carry long flexible mirrors, ceramic knives, and spools of coded thread. Their hands are webbed with old burns and static scars. They know more secrets than anyone because all messages, fumes, power, and pressure eventually pass through their district.

The Foundry workers are broader where others are narrow, their clothes stiff with baked dust. They understand heat without flame, fusion without pouring, manufacture without liquid. They handle powders as carefully as priests handle relics. Their songs, when they have them, are timed to press cycles and cooling ticks. They make the parts that allow the city to keep overtaking itself.

The Deep Rack families are pale even by the city’s standards. They tend storage, archives, animal warrens, crystal bins, sealed food matrices, old survey routes, and places where the fresh mountain keeps intruding. They are good at waiting. They know which cracks are old enough to ignore and which hairline mark will become a district problem by morning. Some claim they can hear the deep engines under the rock, though no one agrees whether those engines are built, natural, or something between.

There are outsiders too, but few. Traders from lower altitudes arrive rarely by cable lift, pressure capsule, or guided climb through routes that are not shown on ordinary maps. They bring sealed foodstock, replacement lenses, memory plates, medicines, cloth, and stories too wet with rivers and forests to be believed. They leave with refined powders, ceramic-metal sheets, charged salts, catalytic dust, engineered animals in transport cages, and an uneasy feeling that the city watched them the entire time.

Outsiders are tolerated, never fully trusted. They sweat too much. They breathe too loudly. They ask for water.

The inhabitants are not humorless. Their humor is dry in every sense: small, precise, often based on near-disaster. They joke about people being “poorly fastened,” about marriages needing counterweights, about children who shed less neatly than carrier beasts. They name dangerous corridors affectionately and affectionate animals insultingly. They celebrate successful repairs, first solo climbs, pressure storms survived, new districts stabilized, and the rare day when nothing falls upward.

Their festivals are not lush. No garlands, no feasts spilling over tables, no fountains or bonfires. Instead: lantern sequences down the central void; choreographed lift movements; polished reflective plates hung to multiply the sky-slit; harmless colored powders released in controlled spirals; animals fitted with tiny mirror tags so the lower dark fills with moving stars. They gather on balconies and bridges, tethered in formal patterns, and make the city visible to itself.

Their dead are not buried. There is no soil. Nor are they burned openly, because flame is wasteful and dangerous. Bodies are dried, rendered, mineralized, incorporated. Bone becomes ceramic additive or memorial tablets. Metals from implants or tools are reclaimed. Names are etched into railings, counterweights, door grooves, pressure bells. The dead become touchable geography. To pass through a district is to lay hands on generations.

Their religion, if it is religion, centers on tension.

Not gods enthroned above the mountain, but balances: weight and counterweight, breath and filter, dryness and contamination, motion and fastening, crack and brace. They trust nothing unsupported. They distrust emptiness unless it is measured. Their shrines are small alcoves containing plumb lines, old broken hooks, crystal growths, failed bolts, fragments of first scaffolds, and animal skulls with conductive whiskers still attached. Prayers are often indistinguishable from checklists.

Keep the hatch true.  
Keep the line dry.  
Let the dust fall downward.  
Let the chain remember its teeth.  
Let no child unclip in turbulence.  

Yet beneath all this discipline there is a more secret quality: longing, but not for escape. They are building upward inside the mountain as if answering a summons from the crack of sky. New platforms rise before old ones are fully named. Scaffolds become markets, then homes, then machine rooms, then foundations for further scaffolds. They do not expand outward because there is no outward available. They elaborate. They intensify. They fold more life into the cleft.

Perhaps they were sent there for a project whose original purpose has been forgotten. Perhaps they mine something in the summit winds, some rare charge or element born where the planet’s new wound meets the upper atmosphere. Perhaps they are refugees from wetter worlds below, people who chose impossibility because impossibility could be engineered. Perhaps the mountain itself is still forming around an event, and the city is less a settlement than an instrument inserted into the wound to listen.

Whatever their origin, they now belong to the cleft.

They are not conquerors of the mountain. They are tenants of a vertical machine made of stone, animal, dust, and habit. They live in the seam between geology and industry, maintaining a city too young to be ancient and too crowded to be temporary. They have made companions of vermin, streets of scaffolds, weather of pressure failure, food of cultured foam, and ceremony of fastening themselves to rails.

What lives there is a civilization that has accepted narrowness without becoming small.

It lives clipped to rings in the dark, listening for the next tremor. It has pale animals under its stairs and children who know the smell of ozone before rain. It sleeps in stacked cells while the mountain cools around it. It builds with one hand while holding on with the other. It fears water, reveres dust, harvests wind, and measures time by bells, lift chains, shedding cycles, and cracks marked in white paint.

And when the lamps flicker and dust rises in a soft veil from every walkway, all of it—the workers, the haulers, the fluttering colonies, the mites in the junction boxes, the elders touching the rail-grooves, the children clipped into their cradle-nets—pauses together.

Not in panic.

In recognition.

Then the dust settles, the chains take weight again, the animals resume their scraping, and the inhabitants of the hidden city continue their patient, crowded ascent inside the mountain.
