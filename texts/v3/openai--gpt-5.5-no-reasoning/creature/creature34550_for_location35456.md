The inhabitants are the keepers of the wet.

Not quite gardeners, not quite engineers, not quite sewer-workers, though every one of those names has been used for them by people above who like clean categories. They are tenders, mostly. Flow-tenders, rootwrights, valve nurses, moss-cutters, bed-keepers, heat men, lamp women, sump readers, canal hands. The town’s underside has made a people shaped to its pressures: patient, damp-skinned, practical, with the quiet intimacy of those who live among systems that do not forgive negligence but reward attention.

They are not a separate species, though at first sight they might seem bred by the place. They are human, or near enough to human that the distinction matters only to outsiders. Shorter on average than the people above, perhaps, or appearing so because they move half-stooped through low vaults and softened arches. Their shoulders are narrow from slipping through leaf-choked passages, but their forearms are strong from turning wet handwheels, dragging silt screens, hauling vine bundles, and lifting water-swollen planks. Their hands are marked with pale scars from cane edges, copper burrs, root-saws, and steam kisses. Fingernails are kept blunt. Hair is worn tied tight, wrapped, cropped, or tucked into waxed caps, because loose hair tangles in belts and drip-lines and living walls.

Their clothing is layered for heat and wet rather than cold. Canvas, oilcloth, linen gone soft from repeated washing, rubber aprons patched at the knees, sleeveless wool vests in cooler side chambers, belts hung with pruners, chalk, rag, gauge keys, wax tablets, little brushes for pollination, coil knives for cutting roots without splitting them, and tin whistles for signaling through pump-noise. Many wear boots whose soles are studded with dull ceramic teeth to grip algae-slick brick. Others go in split-toed rubber sandals through the beds and root rooms, feeling by touch when the planks are unsafe or the mud beneath them has gone hollow.

They smell faintly of the place even when they climb into town: green water, hot metal, bruised fern, soap, smoke, and a mineral damp no laundering quite removes. Town children know them by that smell and by the way they pause under eaves during rain, listening not to the weather itself but to what it will mean below.

They have eyes accustomed to partial light. Not better eyes, necessarily, but more selective ones. A tender can notice a gauge needle’s tremor through mist, the change in leaf shine that means a lamp is too near, the bulge in a brick joint before mortar fails, the slight yellowing that means the reed filters are overloaded, the different rhythm of a pump beginning to draw air. They read surfaces the way others read notices. A sweating pipe is language. A dry corner is alarm. Duckweed pressed against the upstream side of a gate is a warning. Pale roots hanging too thick over a service panel are future trouble. A smell of tannin in one canal before a storm may tell them which sluices to crack open first.

Their faces tend toward stillness, not because they are solemn by nature but because they work where listening matters. They do not waste expression in rooms already crowded with signal. Underground, gestures are small and exact: two fingers down for lower the gate; palm circling for start the wheel; fist to chest for pressure held; knuckle tapped against pipe for blockage suspected; chalk mark across a stem to say cut here after flowering, not before. Voices are used, but sparingly in the loud chambers. Many of their calls are pitched to carry between pump-beat and falling water: short, hard syllables, family names worn down into working sounds, warnings that can be shouted through leaves.

They are born above and below both. Some families sleep in ordinary houses over the district, in rooms where the floor is always warm and condensation rims the windowpanes in winter. Others have quarters tucked into dry-ish side galleries: brick cells with raised bunks, cabinets sealed in wax, stove niches vented into old flues, small shrines or notice boards protected behind glass. No one sleeps in the wettest rooms if they can help it. The place is useful, beloved even, but it is not romantic to those who keep it. They know what mildew does to lungs, what steam can do to skin, what a hidden sump can swallow.

Children are brought down early but not casually. At first they sit on the moss-worn bench in the far corner, or in the central room near the pump assembly where an elder can keep one eye on them. They are given harmless work: wiping fog from gauge glass, sorting cut vine, brushing pollen from one marked flower to another, carrying chalk, rinsing grit from valve tags, counting drip intervals aloud until rhythm enters the body. They learn the map not as a drawing but as a chain of sensations: the warm alcove, the metallic-smelling canal, the left-hand turn where roots brush the ear, the low pipe that has cracked three heads this year, the pale moss stair, the handrail that vanishes into fern and returns colder than before.

Their education is apprenticeship and civic duty mingled. Every child in the town knows, in theory, that the underworks keep the foundations from drowning and the air from souring, but the children of tenders know it bodily. They know that rain above is not over when the clouds pass. They know that a festival crowd in the market means grease and washwater in the drains by midnight. They know which baker’s chimney warms a ceiling vent, which factory sends bitter runoff if its filters are neglected, which new street paving changed the rate at which stormwater reaches the brick stair.

The old among them are revered less for age than for memory. An elder may remember the flood that lifted the planks to the handrails, or the season the heat exchangers failed and white fungus took three rooms, or the year a root buttress cracked the west retaining wall and had to be braided into a new support instead of removed. They carry remembered calibrations no board can fully hold. “This gate sticks after the second autumn storm.” “The third bed drinks more when the tramlines freeze.” “Do not trust that dry patch; it lies.” They sit with their backs against warm brick and correct younger workers by sound alone.

Their society is organized around rotations, not clocks alone. Aboveground time exists, of course—market days, rent days, holidays, elections, school bells—but below, the more important units are rain, drawdown, flush, misting, pruning, harvest, sediment clearing, lamp shift, boiler rest. A person’s status comes from reliability in these cycles. Someone who can be trusted to close a gate in the dark by wrist-depth and wheel-resistance is valued more than someone with a title. Records matter greatly, but so does judgment. The handwritten boards near the central pump are not decoration; they are civic memory in damp ink.

They keep a bureaucracy, though it is more tactile than paper-heavy. Tags hang from stems: copper for structural growth, ceramic for food beds, blue enamel for medicinal stock, red glass for dangerous obstruction, white chalk for temporary cuts. Valves have names as well as numbers. Beds have lineages. Some root buttresses are older than any living tender and are referred to almost as retired workers: stubborn, dependable, greedy, forgiving. A newly trained vine is spoken of with cautious optimism; an old pump with affectionate irritation.

They eat what the place gives, though not only that. Their meals are green, warm, mineral, and practical: steamed leaf bundles, cresslike mats cut fresh from shallow trays, pale tubers grown in heated silt, mushrooms from managed rot chambers, sour pickles, pressed seedcakes, broth flavored with resin and salt brought from above, flatbread warmed on pipe housings. There are no underground animals to trap, no eggs tucked in rafters, no fish in the canals unless brought ceremonially from elsewhere and eaten quickly above. The absence of animals has shaped their foodways into something careful and vegetal. Protein is cultivated in pulses, fungi, pressed pastes, and trade.

They do not dislike animals, but they distrust their unpredictability underground. A rat is not merely a rat here; it is chewed insulation, contaminated beds, fouled channels, damaged seedlings. Insects are not charming; they are imbalance, infestation, a failure of managed pollination. If a moth appears near a lamp, work pauses until it is caught, examined, recorded, and removed. Children may stare at beetles aboveground with wonder, but below they learn to cup them gently and carry them up in sealed jars. The underworks are alive enough without appetites no one scheduled.

Their rituals are maintenance rituals because maintenance is how the place remains habitable. Before the wet season, they hold the Opening of Gradients: not a grand public festival, but a long day of testing gates, clearing handspan canals, sounding channels with poles, reading storm marks, and repainting level lines. At the end, they share hot bitter tea in the central room while the pumps run at full draw for a measured hour. In the dry lull, such as it is, they celebrate the First Condensation, when the walls begin to sweat evenly again after dangerous thinness. A dry wall may be touched with wet palms by the youngest apprentices, a gesture half superstition and half inspection.

Deaths are marked by water, not burial below. The dead are taken above, because the underdistrict does not spare space for graves and because decay there is work, not remembrance. But a tender’s name may be written on a valve tag, a bench brick, a gauge board margin, or a pruning knife handed down. Sometimes a favored alcove is planted with a cutting from a bed they tended. To outsiders it looks like nothing: another fern, another glossy mat of leaves. To those who know, it is exact.

Their beliefs, if one can call them beliefs, are mostly rules made tender by repetition. Nothing must dry completely. No flow is innocent. Growth is not goodness unless it holds where it should and yields where it must. A leak is a message before it is a failure. A machine that complains early is a loyal machine. Cut cleanly. Label what you change. Never trust still water that has no smell. Never curse rain where a child can hear you. Never praise a pump while standing beside it. Feed the moss before it feeds on mortar. Leave one hand on the rail.

They are wary of people from above who come down for novelty. Visitors duck at the wrong places, step on root lips, put hands on hot pipes, lean against living supports, exclaim over “wildness,” and ask if it is frightening to live beneath the town. The tenders answer politely if they must. They know the underdistrict is not wild. Wildness is unmanaged arrival, appetite without schedule, water where no channel waits for it. This place is dense, difficult, alive, but it is also civic. It is as deliberate as a courthouse and as vulnerable as a lung.

There are conflicts among them, but these too take the shape of the place. Young tenders argue for new tubing, brighter lamps, automated mist nozzles, plastic liners that roots cannot breach. Older ones distrust materials that have not yet endured thirty wet seasons. Bed-keepers want more warmth for yield; foundation crews want roots slowed near load-bearing walls. Pump hands resent gardeners who let leaves blind gauges. Gardeners resent pump hands who cut too severely around intakes. Aboveground officials demand numbers: output, flood mitigation, maintenance cost, cubic flow, fuel savings. Belowground workers reply with stains, histories, risks, and repairs no ledger sees until they fail.

They are not miserable. The place is hard, but it gives them competence, continuity, and a strange form of abundance. There is pleasure in a sluice set just right, in a bed flushing clean after weeks of trouble, in the green surge after controlled rain, in the first breath of filtered air moving through moss panels, in the heavy quiet satisfaction when a storm passes and the foundations hold. There is beauty, too, though they may not call it that often: lampblack leaves shining after mist, copper gone blue-green beside new shoots, steam rising from warm channels, a root curtain combed by dripping water, the muscular patience of a belt turning through shadow.

At rest, they gather in the few widened places: workyards under low vaults, the central pump room, the moss bench in the far corner, a dry ledge near the pale stair where air from above carries a faint taste of weather. They drink tea kept hot in pipe-warmed kettles. They mend gloves. They sharpen blades. They argue over flow predictions. Someone may sing, but softly, in songs with repetitive lines fitted to work rhythms. Their music avoids high trills and delicate pauses; the room would eat those. It favors hum, pulse, call, response, the low tones that can live beside machinery.

Their bodies know the town inverted. They know where a market square becomes a ceiling grate, where a church drain feeds a medicinal bed, where a tramline’s vibration shakes pollen loose, where a rich house’s heated cellar keeps a fern wall thriving through winter. They know the hypocrisy of those above who wrinkle their noses at damp and then rely on it for clean air, flood control, winter greens, medicines, and dry foundations. Yet many tenders feel no resentment, or not only resentment. The town above is the roof of their work. The underdistrict is not beneath it in rank. It is the other half of the same organism.

And there are a few among them who seem less like workers in the place than expressions of it.

The oldest rootwright, perhaps, whose skin has the brown softness of wet brick and whose breath always sounds faintly mossed. The twins who can hear a clogged reed filter from two rooms away. The woman who tends the medicinal beds and paints cut marks so beautifully that apprentices preserve her chalk stubs. The heat man with one clouded eye who sleeps beside the exchangers during cold weeks and wakes before any gauge shows trouble. The child who is not supposed to be in the canals but knows every floating mat by name. The quiet apprentice who spends rest hours at the far bench, watching water seep through the root-wall as though waiting for instruction.

If the place has a true resident beyond them, it is not an animal or spirit but the maintained condition itself: warmth held in brick, water persuaded to move slowly, growth cut back and invited forward, machinery kept from rusting into silence, roots allowed to grip but not devour. The tenders live inside that condition and serve it, but they also create it. Without them, the green would choke the iron, the water would sour or tear free, the foundations would crack, and the town would discover that its underside had never been passive.

So what lives there is a human craft made almost ecological. A damp-handed citizenry of listeners and cutters. People who measure weather in wheel-turns, who understand plants as colleagues and hazards, who trust moss more than paint but records more than memory alone. They move through the cellar-city with rags, blades, keys, brushes, and lamps, negotiating every day between flood and drought, root and brick, heat and rot, usefulness and overgrowth.

They are the ones who keep the town breathing.
