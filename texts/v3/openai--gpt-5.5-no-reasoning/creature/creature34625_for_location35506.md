What lives there is not a species so much as a distributed body: an industrial fauna that has taken the shape of maintenance.

At first it seems to be many kinds of creatures. Small pale things with jointed shells. Canal larvae. Heat-drinking discs. Steam-bellied squat forms. Long submerged animals that are known only by the displacement they make in black water. Rail-bed grazers with enamel scraping plates. Wire-clinging signal feeders. Pipe-mouth nesters. Drain-things, valve-things, condenser-things, creatures of brick seams and cable sag and mineral crust.

But after a while the divisions become less convincing.

They do not behave like wildlife introduced into an abandoned works. They behave like functions that learned to move.

The smallest ones live in surfaces. They gather where condensation beads under rails, where mortar darkens, where ceramic meets iron and produces some minute electrical taste. They travel in sheets, not quite swarms and not quite currents, their mica shells clicking softly against brick. Their bodies are flattened and hinged, built for cracks, undersides, ledges, the backs of signs, the hollows inside capped smokestacks. They have no obvious eyes because vision would be an unnecessary extravagance here. The world is legible to them through vibration, wetness, mineral gradient, pressure, tone.

They feed by abrading.

Not destructively. They do not strip the district; they polish it. They remove salts, films, residues, thin metallic blooms, old grease, fungal slick too fine to become visible growth. Their mouths are files and valves and soft rasping pads. Their passage leaves stone satin-smooth, iron blackened and clean, ceramic dulled by use. They are the reason no moss takes hold, no weed roots in a crack, no organic mess accumulates long enough to become soil. They are the district’s immune skin.

Other creatures are condensation specialists. They cling beneath pipes and railings in black soft clusters, each body a flexible disc with a trembling rim. When machinery cycles, they shiver in unison, drinking warmth from metal and dissolved matter from water droplets. Their undersides are patterned with radial grooves like gaskets. When full, they release a clear bead that rolls into channels cut by generations of the same act. They are neither parasites nor cleaners exactly. They are regulators of damp.

The canal holds slower, more private animals.

Some are eyeless skimmers, narrow as strips of wet leather, that skate along the oily surface when the water lies still. They vanish when the current reverses. Others are translucent and immature-looking no matter their age, carried in pulses between sluices, grates, and hidden basins under the pump houses. There are larger forms below them: long, muscular bodies that turn without breaking the surface, their presence visible only as a traveling pressure, a soft heaving of reflected smokestack silhouettes. They do not hunt in bursts. They align themselves with the canal’s intermittent flow and open ribbed throats to strain whatever the district gives them: shed shell, oil skin, mineral flake, dead mite, larval slick, softened tar.

Nothing is wasted because waste is simply another phase of operation.

The rail grazers are among the most visible of the larger inhabitants. They are tall only by comparison, many-legged and narrow-bodied, built to move along tracks without disturbing freight carts. Their limbs are jointed like folding calipers. Their heads hang low, and beneath them are rows of enamel plates that scrape iron, stone, and compacted ash-clay. They move patiently, often at dawn or during pressure lulls, following polished routes along tram lines and curb edges. Where their bodies pass, railings show a smooth wear at nonhuman height. They are not grazing plants, because there are none. They graze the mineral consequence of industry: salt, oxide, conductive bloom, the edible memory of current.

Near heat, the barrel-bodied steam feeders gather.

They wedge themselves beside condensers and under turbine housings, skins tightening and loosening with a slow mechanical rhythm. They inhale through vertical slits and exhale through pores that bead with warm moisture. Their hides are thick, grey, and slightly translucent at the seams. When vents open, their bodies swell; when the tone from the central yard deepens during the district’s inhalation, they brace all their short limbs and become still as bolted equipment. Smaller creatures shelter beneath them, feeding on their runoff. They are living accumulators, storing heat where the town refuses to become warm.

There are cable animals too.

They arrange themselves along insulated wires at regular intervals, facing the same way not from discipline but from sensation. Their bodies are small and heavy, with hooked limbs and flattened faces pressed to the cable sheath. They feed on signal, vibration, induction, or some taste of electricity too faint to light a bulb. At junction boxes larger ones squat like old black padlocks, several limbs spread against the housing. Their shells are patterned with pale ceramic nodules, perhaps grown from what they consume. When messages move through the wires—if messages still move—they receive them bodily.

Whether they understand is the wrong question.

Understanding here is not separate from conducting.

There are nesters in pipe junctions, especially where warm and cool flows meet. They build not from twigs or fiber but from shed plates, mineral crust, tar flakes, old labels, and hardened secretion. Their nests resemble repairs. A person might mistake one for intentional insulation, a gasketed collar, a crusted brace around a leaking seam. This confusion is part of their success. They do not hide in the infrastructure; they complete its grammar.

The dead do not remain visible.

A crushed shell on a threshold will be gone by the next pressure cycle. A drowned skimmer becomes canal food before it sinks. A steam feeder that fails is opened from beneath by needle-faced scavengers, reduced first to membrane, then to softened mineral plates, then to nothing one could call remains. Some part goes into drains. Some part goes into furnace mouths. Some becomes raised glossy markings on brick. Some is carried in the bodies of larvae toward sluices. Death is not an interruption in the district. It is a routing decision.

Their reproduction is likewise infrastructural.

Eggs, if they are eggs, appear as beads in seams, sealed into black corrosion or glued beneath ceramic insulators. Larvae develop in bowls beneath dripping pipes, in warm washroom vents, in the dark backs of humming crates. Some creatures bud from colonies like fittings added to a line. Others shed shells that are occupied by smaller forms until the shell itself is eaten thin. No nursery looks separate from a pump, a drain, a cracked wall, a capped chimney. The young are born already inside a system of use.

They are numerous beyond counting, but their abundance has no frenzy in it.

They do not erupt. They circulate. They do not conquer space. They assign themselves to it. Their density is astonishing, yet the streets remain broad, the yards bare, the walls readable. A ledge may carry hundreds of small shell-bodies, but they align with the ledge’s function. A warm casing may be black with adhering discs, but no animal presses beyond the temperature boundary it has learned. At the central factory yard, where the low tone rises from six vents, they gather in immense numbers and do not cross. Their restraint is as much a part of them as appetite.

This is perhaps the most important thing about them: they obey.

Not commands in any simple sense. Not laws written by vanished engineers. They obey pressure, tone, damp, vibration, conductive gradients, canal pulses, heat pockets, mineral deposits, the opening of valves, the silence before machinery moves. They obey the district’s continuance, and by obeying it they create it.

Their bodies are full of industrial analogies because the district has made those analogies literal. Shells like mica and ceramic. Mouths like files. Limbs like clamps. Skins like gaskets. Breathing like bellows. Herding like current. Nesting like repair. Communication like bearing noise. They are animals, but animal life here has no pastoral memory. It has never needed leaves, loam, blossom, nest-fiber, sunlight filtered through trees. It knows brick, black water, iron, tar, ash-clay, condensate, insulator, pipe.

If there were once people, these creatures did not merely outlast them. They inherited the routines more completely than any citizen could have. They occupy housing blocks as underside dwellers. They use schoolhouse diagrams as textured surfaces. They pass through human-sized doors without acknowledging human scale as primary. They have worn grooves where hands would not reach and polished rails no handrail was meant to be. Their marks have merged with signs, their secretions with labels, their trails with maintenance routes.

The district reads them and they read it.

The central tone may be their governor, their predator, their weather, their god, or simply the deepest organ of the shared body. Whatever it is, every creature is shaped around it. When the whole district inhales, they pause. Wall-creatures flatten. Cable feeders stiffen. Canal bodies turn. Steam feeders seal their slits. The small shell-things hold one leg lifted, arrested mid-click. Then the exhalation passes through vents and gutters and grilles and opened mouths, and the creatures resume—not as if released from fear, but as if a measure has been completed.

They are not invasive. They are not decorative. They are not vermin.

They are continuity given legs, shells, throats, mandibles, membranes.

They keep the place from rotting, but also from changing. They consume excess. They erase beginnings. They prevent endings from leaving evidence. They maintain the hardness of edges, the absence of green, the damp equilibrium, the mineral legibility of walls. They are the caretakers of a world that no longer distinguishes between care and corrosion.

What lives there is an ecosystem that has accepted industry as its climate and machinery as its geology.

Its creatures do not dream of escape into the grey flats beyond the walls. Beyond the district they thin, becoming routes rather than crowds, single-file lines between drainage towers, exploratory feelers of the larger system. Their true habitat is not land but operation. They need the thump under paving, the canal’s reversal, the sour taste of old electricity, the steady rinse of condensation, the unvarying prohibition at the central yard.

They live in continuance.

And if one tried to name them collectively, perhaps the nearest word would be not fauna, not workforce, not colony, but metabolism.
