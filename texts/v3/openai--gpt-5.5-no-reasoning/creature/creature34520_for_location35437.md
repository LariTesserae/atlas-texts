What lives there is not a population, but a civic condition of plant life.

It is a thing with no face and many districts. Not one organism exactly, not many organisms exactly, but a vegetal metropolis behaving like a single unfinished body: a city-body, a basin-brain of sap and fiber, a municipal forest without animals to interrupt its self-attention. It lives there by occupying every possible form of residence at once. Wall, weed, weather, foundation, street, memory, appetite, and architecture are all phases of the same life.

It has no citizens because it has not separated dwelling from dweller.

The inhabitants are the buildings.

The avenues are not made for traversal by feet; they are the long grooves left by growth arguing with itself. They are pressure-ways, recurrence-channels, habitual absences preserved by tension. The walls lean over them because walls here are not inert structures but slow muscular intentions made of bundled stem and root. Doorways appear because moisture and shadow and repeated vacancy persuade tissue to arch. Windows open because membranes thin where light and heat negotiate. Balconies are leaf-thick protrusions testing air. Towers are sap columns under ambition. Bridges are vines that have learned tension before they have learned destination.

Everything alive here is architectural because there is nothing else to become.

At the smallest scale live the sealers and scribes: mosses, liverworts, creeping red mats, pale root-hairs, films of algae, velvet bruises of low growth. They are not decorative. They are the skin’s intelligence. They close wounds. They drink the city’s breath. They record where water has lingered, where pressure passed, where shadow lay long enough to matter. Their patterns are writing, though not written for eyes. They make maps for roots, not travelers. They remember in dampness and discoloration. They are the first responders to any rupture and the last witnesses before a surface is overgrown into something else.

Above them live the struts, ribs, columns, buttresses, and braided trunks: the structural caste, if caste can be used for a life without hierarchy. These are the weight-bearers, the street-makers, the civic bones. They do not stand still. Their labor is constant correction. They thicken when sound calls them. They bend when humidity asks. They crack, rejoin, peel, and thrust new supports into newly discovered loads. A wall may be born from a hundred stems making the same mistake in the same direction. A stairway may be a buttress root repeating a spiral because torsion pleased it. These forms do not imitate human construction; human construction is merely one narrow way of describing what support looks like when it becomes visible.

Then there are the occupants of the architecture, though they too are architecture: vines, curtains, pitcher-forms, suspended gardens, translucent membranes, storage knots, fruiting shelves, awnings of clustered leaves. They live in ledges, hollows, seams, shaded ribs, and rain pockets. They crowd every available margin, not parasitically so much as participatorily. They furnish the city with interiors. They make rooms inside rooms, climates inside climates, pockets of stillness inside the roar. A pitcher full of green water is a chamber. A hanging mat of epiphytes is a neighborhood. A curtain of vine is both resident and wall-hanging and door.

Higher still lives the canopy-weather: immense leaves, aerial roots, inflated sacs, flowering platforms, suspended pools, umbrella arrays, heat bladders, pressure gourds. This is the city’s sky-organ. It manufactures local climates. It rains over courtyards by decisionless condensation. It drops petals like debris from celebration or collapse. It sighs heat. It strains the sun into incompatible hours. It sends shadows that do not always obey the leaves that cast them. It is not a roof, because roofs separate inside from outside. This canopy makes inside and outside continuously renegotiate.

At the center, or where the idea of center keeps growing back, lives the largest thing: the not-tree, the not-cathedral, the not-hill. It may be the parent organism. It may be only the most successful knot in the system. It may be a pressure-heart, a growth parliament, a vegetative memory palace. It is too large to be understood from any one avenue. Seen between nearer walls, it seems always under construction: spires thrusting upward, softening, bowing; leaf-sheets falling like banners; flanks pulsing faintly in daylight. When it pulses, the city answers. Roots tighten. Plazas clear. Flowers open. Pods burst. Streets alter by small degrees. It is not a ruler in any animal or political sense, but a rhythm-source. A center of recurrence. The nearest thing this place has to a heart.

Yet even that may be misleading. The true inhabitant may be the whole basin’s tendency to grow cityward.

It lives as a set of urges:

toward edge,  
toward moisture,  
toward support,  
toward repetition,  
toward light,  
toward sound,  
toward available volume,  
toward the preservation of useful absence.

It does not think, but it arranges. It does not intend, but it formalizes. It does not remember as an animal remembers, carrying images inside a nervous chamber; it remembers by regrowing the spatial consequences of what has collapsed. A vanished avenue returns as bark grain. A courtyard reappears in the branching of a vine. A tower’s fall becomes the curve of a future street. Its memory is compostable, edible, structural. Nothing is archived; everything is digested into tendency.

Its senses are not eyes, ears, mouths, or skin, though skin is everywhere. It senses through gradients.

Moisture is grammar.  
Pressure is touch.  
Light is hunger.  
Sound is instruction.  
Heat is permission.  
Shadow is invitation.  
Emptiness is alarm.

The roar of the city is therefore not mere noise. It is civic process. It is ordinance, traffic, weather report, structural calculation, birth cry, demolition, and song without singer. Reed-whistles, pod detonations, root-thunder, leaf-surf, sap-drums, the papery hiss of unfurling bracts: these are not communications in the animal sense, not messages sent from one subject to another. They are forces that growth can read. A low vibration thickens a wall. A shriek shreds a leaf-curtain. Repeated pulses teach a bridge to tighten. The city builds by listening to itself.

It has no mouths, so it cannot devour in the usual way. But it includes.

That is its mode of life: inclusion.

A fallen chamber is not abandoned; it becomes substrate. A collapsed bridge becomes food and pattern. A dry plaza becomes contested territory. A visitor’s breath becomes humidity. A footprint becomes engineering advice. A gaze, if held long enough, may become a formal pressure. To remain there is to be entered into negotiation. The place does not attack because attack implies an outside. It has almost no concept of outside. It recognizes only surfaces not yet recruited, hollows not yet thickened, warmth not yet used, stillness not yet grown around.

It is innocent in the way weather is innocent and engulfing in the way mold is engulfing and magnificent in the way cities are magnificent when no single mind controls them.

The flowers that live there are not advertisements for insects. They have never needed to seduce bees or birds. Their extravagance is for other exchanges: pressure, condensation, resonance, distance, perhaps memory. Some open when air is disturbed. Some bloom toward a sound. Some release pollen into architectural drafts. Some may pollinate by shadow, by vibration, by the touch of mist, by the brief impossible contact between a near tower and a far one when distance loses confidence. Their colors are not signals to animal eyes, though eyes can be wounded by their beauty. They are local decisions made visible.

The fruits are stranger still. With no mouths to feed, fruit has become a vessel for non-animal futures. Some contain water. Some soil. Some folded plans. Some contain a note that sounds for days when the rind splits. Some may contain weather. Some contain the memory of an avenue not yet grown. They are seed, archive, instrument, district, and prophecy.

The city’s life is young. That matters. It has not settled into a stable ecology, a mature forest, a recognizable swamp, or a ruin. Its ground is still forming beneath it. Its laws are provisional. Its categories are wet. It is exuberant because it has not yet learned economy. It spends growth lavishly. It builds towers that fold by evening. It opens plazas only to suppress them again and again. It invents windows without inhabitants, stairways without walkers, streets without traffic, chambers without sleep. It is practicing form. Urbanism here is not a solution but a phase of botanical experimentation.

If it dreams, it dreams in gradients and recurrence.

It dreams of a path, and stems withdraw just enough to make one.  
It dreams of a wall, and trunks braid themselves into resistance.  
It dreams of a square, and the ground must be beaten open by subterranean pulses.  
It dreams of height, and towers rise until their own sap-stress tears them apart.  
It dreams of rain, and the canopy sweats.  
It dreams of memory, and old layouts return as veins in leaves.

There may be no mind in it. But there is mentality’s raw material: responsiveness, preference, correction, inheritance, anticipation without image. It does not know what it is doing, yet what it is doing accumulates into a city. It has no plan, but it has planning. No will, but insistence. No citizens, but neighborhoods. No language, but grammar. No history, but compost.

What lives there is a root-dream of civilization before animals.

A green intelligence too distributed to become a person.  
A forest that has discovered corridors.  
A swamp that has learned facades.  
A seed thinking in streets.  
A basin-wide body whose organs are districts and whose breath is mist.  
A living place so alive that nothing inside it can remain merely a place.
