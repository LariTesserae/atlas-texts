What lives there is not a people, exactly, though the place has citizens.

It is inhabited by a city-organism: a basin-wide arrangement of plants, fungi, animals, water, sound, and softened distance that has become something like a mind without needing one center. The walls live. The streets live. The cisterns breed their own brief populations. The predators and browsers and insects and birds are not merely residents inside it, but moving organs, pollinators of architecture, editors of route and memory. The place is lived in by everything at once, and the “everything” has learned civic behavior.

At first the most obvious inhabitants are the plants, because they are the buildings.

They are huge and old and various, but they share a grammar. Ribbed wall-leaves, root-causeways, bladderlike water cups, fungus balconies, leaf roofs, spouts, sealed scars, hanging traps, listening flowers. They grow as if obeying inherited street plans. Not commanded, not designed in the human sense, but trained by repetition: hoof traffic, rainfall, shade, mineral seep, blood, pollen, the pressure of bodies passing through the same gaps for generations. Their intelligence is distributed through sap pressure and root tension and chemical bargains. A passage kept open is an appetite postponed. A doorway is a truce. A plaza is a digestive pause.

The plant-structures are not passive habitat. They bargain, herd, mislead, reward. They sweeten certain corridors so browsers keep them clear. They narrow others toward mud that does not release what enters. They raise nurseries above the reach of ground-hunters. They arrange rain into pools. They grow ear-shaped flowers that wait for sufficient noise before opening, as if sound itself were sunlight. They remember water better than stone would. They remember feet, too.

Under and through them lives the fungal citizenry: pale threads, velvet mats, hidden shelves, soft white mouths in cracks. These are the clerks and undertakers of the place. They erase droppings, feathers, fruit pulp, blood, drowned insects, shed skins. They translate accident into soil. They stitch old wounds in living walls and digest the places where the city has overgrown itself. If the plants are the architecture, the fungi are the bureaucracy—quiet, tireless, impossible to separate from the law.

There are small waters everywhere, and each water has its own population.

In the leaf-cups and cistern hollows live fish that should not be able to live there: silver-and-black, abrupt, appearing with the rain as if precipitated from memory. Some are no longer entirely fish in the ordinary sense. They pass dry intervals packed into wet plant-flesh, gills held open by root-hairs, their bodies slowed almost to seedhood. Tadpoles graze beside them. Transparent snails write pale crescents through algae. Red mites mass like powdered mineral. Long-legged insects patrol the surface. Blue flies walk on reflections rather than water, belonging half to the pool and half to whatever the pool is reflecting.

These little reservoirs are nurseries, but also embassies. Every cup of water connects the whole basin in hidden ways: by overflow, by eggs stuck to feet, by larvae carried in bird feathers, by roots that drink from one hollow and sweat into another. Nothing wet is isolated for long.

The insects are everywhere, not as a background but as weather with legs.

Cicadas or cicada-like things drive the day to a metallic boil. Beetles move in lacquered processions into slits that open for them and shut afterward. Ants strip fruit to lace and carry it into the undercity. Mites gather where water retreats. Flies arrive at death before death has fully settled. Some insects are pollinators, some are alarms, some are keys: their weight, sound, scent, or chewing pattern causing the plant-buildings to loosen, open, fold, exude, or seal.

The frogs are among the loudest inhabitants. They live unseen in gutters, cups, chambers behind leaves, hollows beneath roof-spouts. Their calls are not decoration. They are part of the town’s structural pressure. When enough throats call at once, flowers open, predators hesitate, birds shift roosts, water trembles in its basins. The frogs may not know they help operate the city, but the city knows how to use them.

The birds occupy the upper levels.

They live in the ribs of the walls, in folded green alcoves and fruit-shadowed galleries. They are small, sharp-voiced, social, and sudden. They leave in coordinated bursts, wheel once through the basin’s hollow air, and return by different apertures, as if testing which doors are still true. Their calls are bright enough to hurt. They eat insects, spread seeds, alarm browsers, fertilize wall-gardens, and perhaps navigate by the basin’s unreliable acoustics better than by sight. To them, the city’s rearrangements may be ordinary: an opening is not a fact, only a current opportunity.

There are lizards on the root roads, brilliant and quick, with throat-sails they flare at intersections. They are readers of the living pavement. They taste chemical messages left by roots, prey, rain, and one another. They know which causeways are swollen with water and which are drying into traps. They bask on warm leaf-sheaths, vanish beneath bark-shelves, and lay eggs in compost chambers where the heat is steady as animal breath.

The browsers are larger, and they make the plazas feel inhabited in a more recognizable way.

They are low, narrow-faced, soft-footed animals, striped in algae colors, with huge translucent ears that are constantly turning. They move cautiously because the city itself may collaborate with hunger. They lick mineral films from clay and browse the sweet growths offered to them. Their repeated paths keep avenues open. Their dung feeds wall-roots. Their bodies carry seeds and mites and spores. They are citizens and livestock and gardeners and prey, all at once.

They understand, perhaps more than most animals here, that an opening is not a promise.

The predators understand it too.

One of them is long-bodied and root-colored, with shoulders that move like slow waves. It hunts not only through stealth but through alliance with the place. It waits for vines to lower, for passages to narrow, for a wall to offer one animal escape and deny another. It does not merely chase through the city; it hunts with the city. Whether this is learned behavior, instinct, or chemical pact hardly matters. Predator and architecture complete the same sentence.

There are other hunters at night: bat-like things orbiting the upper chambers; vine-patient shapes near the ground; perhaps frogs large enough to take birds; perhaps plants that have crossed the threshold between trap and animal intention. In this basin, categories are not abolished, but they are frequently negotiated.

The central pool is inhabited too, though not in the same way.

Whatever lives there may not be a creature. It may be the city’s navel, spring, eye, archive, wound, or mouth. It reflects weather that has not arrived, stars at noon, birds before they enter the sky. It gathers all rain-routes. It makes the court larger than it can be. When struck by runoff from the walls, it rings the whole basin until outlines loosen and doorways multiply.

If there is a governing presence, it is there—not ruling, exactly, but synchronizing. The pool may be where water remembers all routes at once. It may be where the basin digests time. The blue flies that walk on its reflection might be its only true attendants.

And beneath all visible residents lives the basin’s strangest inhabitant: a communal editing of reality.

The animals accept it because they have no other world. The plants exploit it because growth already bends around invisible pressures. Sound arrives before motion. Doorways briefly contain memories instead of rooms. Roots persuade distance to shorten or lengthen. Shadows detach from leaves. A stair may lead down in the morning and up in the afternoon. This is not illusion imposed on life; it is one more ecological process. Reality has become a medium, like water or soil, and too many organisms have learned to root in it.

So what lives there?

A living city lives there.

A civic ecology.

A warm, roaring, plant-built organism whose citizens include fish in leaves, frogs in cisterns, birds in wall-ribs, lizards on root-roads, browsers in mud courts, root-colored predators, blue flies on impossible reflections, fungi in the cracks, seeds that decide quickly, vines that hunt, flowers that listen, and water that carries messages between all of them.

No single species owns the basin. Ownership would be too simple. The place is inhabited by arrangements: bargains between appetite and architecture, between movement and growth, between rain and memory, between death and reuse. It is full of creatures, but the largest creature is the pattern they make together.

It is a settlement whose residents are also its streets.

A forest that learned urban planning.

A town that became alive enough to forget it had ever been built.
