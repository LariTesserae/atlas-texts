What lives there is not a single species, nor even a simple people among animals, but a compact society of listeners.

They are valley-edge folk: small-voiced, weatherwise, narrow-handed, accustomed to damp sleeves and cold breath. They move as if the air has weight. Their bodies are likely lean rather than large, with strong calves from the stairways and low centers of balance from generations on wet stone. They have the practiced stillness of people who share streets with animals that can crush a roof by accident. Their eyes are quick but not restless. They look first at surfaces: whether a wall is sweating, whether a lashing has tightened, whether moss has changed color, whether a path is still the path it was yesterday.

They are not masters of their town. They are its custodians, tenants, interpreters, and sometimes its guests.

Their lives are arranged around condensation. Water shapes their economy, etiquette, architecture, memory, and law. A wealthy household is not one with treasure, but one whose roof catches well, whose drip nets are properly angled, whose jars remain sweet, whose cistern lizards are healthy, whose gutters do not offend the young stone. Children probably learn water before numbers: how to hear the fullness of a covered jar by tapping it, how to clean clay channels without wasting a drop, how to distinguish sky-rain from leaf-rain from wall-sweat from the bitter water that comes before births.

They do not waste gestures. In a town where counting animals aloud is forbidden and whistling into mist nets is dangerous, language itself has become careful. Speech is close, almost tactile. A person speaks into another’s shoulder, sleeve, or cupped hands. Public dealings happen through placement: a bundle moved from one mat to another, a token turned face-down, a dried fern frond laid across a threshold, a bowl left empty by the lower boundary. They are not silent because they lack feeling. They are silent because sound is one more material that must be handled correctly.

Their tools are intimate and worn smooth: bone needles, glass blades, stone adzes, antler awls, pitch-sealed baskets, hide-wrapped mallets, woven strainers, ladder-hooks, rain-combs, split-cane lattices. They value repair over replacement. A good object may have several makers across several generations: the first who carved it, the second who lashed it, the third who fitted it with a bronze hook, the fourth who mended the grip with mushroom leather. Ownership is probably recorded in knots, stains, notches, songs, and obligations rather than written marks.

They are agricultural, but only in negotiation with wildness. They farm terraces no larger than rooms and tend orchards that must also feed moths, roof grazers, beetles, fungi, and unseen agreements. Their crops are likely sharp, cold-loving, and concentrated: bitter plums, sour pears, berries, roots, moss-fed mushrooms, oil seeds, medicinal ferns, tough greens that shine with waxy leaves. They ferment because the air is damp and cold. They smoke food because rot is always near. Their cuisine would taste of peat, acid fruit, mushroom, rendered fat, mineral salt, cold herbs, and smoke.

Their homes are inhabited by more than families. Every house has its tolerated creatures: door moths, cistern frogs, condensation lizards, roof grazers, beetle colonies, spiders, perhaps a particular ash-bird under the eaves. A household without animals would be suspect, unlucky, maybe unwell. Children might be assigned not pets but co-residents: “mind the third cistern frog,” “do not wake the seam moths,” “leave peelings for the compost beetles,” “bow to the roof meadow before cutting thatch.”

Their religion, if it is religion, is practical and distributed. They do not seem to separate sacredness from maintenance. Ancestors are kept in sharp cliff niches, not far away in some remote heaven. The mountain must be reminded to hold. The lower forest must be crossed politely. The dry basin at the center is not worshiped noisily but acknowledged by lowered eyes. The people know there are larger arrangements in force, and that survival depends on behaving as though everything has standing: stone, mist, animal, roof, path, unborn child, stored water, old lie.

They have specialists, but not grand rulers.

There are likely cistern-keepers who know the taste and temper of each jar. Route-readers who wake before dawn to determine which lanes have shifted after mist. Roof-tenders who understand moss weight and the grazing habits of the tiny white animals. Quiet market arbiters who settle disputes by moving tokens rather than speaking accusations aloud. Black-fern cutters who must be innocent of the lie that grew the plant. Animal keepers who leave salt and bruised fruit on the high stair, though the cliff antlers belong to no one. Ancestor carvers whose fresh cuts in the young cliff are still too angular to look venerable. Birth-watchers who inspect sweating walls. Children’s teachers who train laughter into sleeves.

Their politics are probably hydrological. Drainage rights matter. So do fog paths, cistern access, terrace soil, roof runoff, orchard pollination, and the maintenance of shared runnels. Disputes may arise over a roof that sheds too much water into a neighbor’s wall, over a family that lets black ferns spread by repeating a useful lie, over someone who accepts a roof fox’s returned theft incorrectly, over a careless traveler who crosses the lower boundary full-handed and brings parasites back into the town.

They are adapted to impermanence without being nomads. Their continuity lies not in unchanging buildings, but in the knowledge of how to rebuild them. A child inherits songs for tightening lashings in wet weather, taboos for unstable paths, the order of animals at forest pools, the correct way to restack a retaining wall after frost, the names of dry routes, wet routes, and below-mist routes. Property is less a line than a responsibility. To inherit a place is to inherit its leaks, its resident animals, its obligations to ancestors, and its moods.

They are cautious, but not timid. It takes courage to live where paths move, predators pass as pressure, and stone may wake if watered carelessly. Their courage is not heroic in the loud sense. It is the courage of remaining still while a cliff antler breathes against the door. Of crossing into the lower forest with an empty bowl. Of not filling the dry basin. Of rebuilding a wall that the mountain shrugged loose. Of teaching a child that the town is safe only because everyone remembers how unsafe it is.

Among them live the cliff antlers, who are almost citizens without citizenship: tall, moss-colored, sensory-horned herbivores whose evening visits rearrange human behavior. They lick mineral salts from posts and taste walls as if reading the town with their tongues. The people do not own them, hunt them, or command them. The town’s lanes are partly theirs.

The roof foxes live there too, though “live” may be too simple. They inhabit thresholds, rooftops, wrong turns, misplaced objects, the little gaps between memory and route. Their thefts are material and cognitive: eggs, combs, cordage, insects, directions. They are trickster animals, but treated with procedural seriousness rather than folklore’s indulgent amusement. One does not curse a roof fox. One turns the coat inside out. One uses the correct hand.

The smaller lives are perhaps the true majority: moths, frogs, lizards, beetles, spiders, snails, ash-colored birds, translucent flies, seed-insects, folded-leaf pollinators, luminous fungi, roof grazers no larger than cupped hands. The town is built for humans but also perforated for these others. Its seams, eaves, cistern lips, compost undersides, roof meadows, and mortar cracks are all dwellings. The settlement is less a human habitation than a layered organism in which humans are one careful tissue.

Even the plants seem to live there with intention. Moss chooses walls but spares veins of black rock. Ferns crowd thresholds and then stop abruptly. Orchards lean over miniature terraces. Vines curtain without smothering. Black ferns mark repeated falsehood. Fungi illuminate cracks and shrine shelves. Roots pulse beneath paving stones in late afternoon, lifting the town slightly as if it breathes.

And beneath all this, or behind it, lives the place itself.

The young stone is not inert. The mist is not merely weather. The dry basin is not an object. The paths are not fully built things. The town is alive in a civic, animal, fungal, geological sense. It remembers through route changes, sweated walls, forbidden counts, reflected leaf-undersides, and a basin that refuses water. Whatever lives there includes something older than the fresh mountain and larger than the settlement’s rules, something the people do not name directly because naming may be too close to summoning, counting, claiming.

So the inhabitants are best described as a quiet, damp-adapted people sharing a young ledge with animals, plants, stones, paths, waters, ancestors, and unspoken agencies. They live by etiquette because etiquette is ecology made conscious. They survive by restraint. They build as if every structure is a question posed to the mist: may this stand here for another season?

And each morning, when silver drops gather at the cane joints and begin falling one by one into the clay channels, the whole town answers yes, softly, again.
