What lives there is not a creature so much as a pressure of living things, a whole impatient population clinging to a place that has not finished deciding what shelter means.

The most numerous inhabitants are small and hard-shelled.

They move in rivers over the warm stone: fist-sized, pebble-backed, clicking animals with many jointed legs hidden under overlapping plates. At rest they are almost indistinguishable from rubble or mineral nodules, except that they breathe through little seams that open and shut along their sides. When rain falls from the ceiling, they wake all at once. A dry basin that seemed empty becomes crowded with them, each shell rising on pale legs, each blunt head unfolding a pair of scraping mouthparts.

They feed on what the stone exudes. Thin films. Salt blooms. Greasy mineral skins. Crusts that form overnight and vanish by noon under their rasping. They travel in dense processions, not quite herds and not quite floods, following dampness, heat, and chemical tastes in the floor. Their bodies make a constant porcelain clatter. When frightened, they stop together, and the silence of thousands of small shells is more alarming than their noise.

Among them move the grazers.

These are pale, many-legged animals, low to the ground, with translucent hides through which a slow gray digestion can be seen. Their mouths are wide oval pads lined with black rasps. They press themselves to walls and floors and scrape, scrape, scrape, leaving clean swaths through mineral bloom. Some carry young in jelly pockets along their backs. Some have symbiotic mites in the folds behind their heads, grooming away harmful crystal growth. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are buried under skin. They do not look up. They know the world by contact: warmth through their feet, moisture through their bellies, vibration along the stone.

The soft-bodied pool things live where the warm seepage collects.

They resemble walking bladders or organs that escaped a larger animal before learning independence. Their bodies swell and slacken with water. They stand on six or eight boneless legs that lengthen like dripping wax, and they drink by lowering rootlike tongues into mineral pools. When disturbed, they deflate and flatten against the ground, becoming slick, almost impossible to grip. Some are harmless. Others exude a numbing slime that makes predators forget the shape of their own mouths.

Eggs gather around them. Not always their own.

The pools are nurseries for half the underland. Gelatinous strings. Pearly clutches glued beneath shelves. Dust-fine eggs that lie dormant in dry crust until rain wakes them. Larger eggs carried in baskets of saliva and mineral fiber. Eggs protected by parents, eggs abandoned, eggs stolen, eggs laid inside old shells, inside porous stone, inside the packed clay of shelter walls. The place is full of beginnings. Many will become nothing but food.

In the shallow channels, blind fish flicker like scraps of muscle.

They are not permanent residents of water, because water itself is not permanent here. They arrive through cracks, as if squeezed from the stone. Some survive in films thinner than a handspan, lying sideways and breathing through skin until the next trickle deepens their world. Their skulls are soft, their bodies pale or transparent, their fins long and sensitive. They follow currents that may last only an hour. When channels dry, they bury themselves in mud or are taken by beaks, claws, tongues, hooks, and hands.

In the gutters of the ceiling live the eels.

They are glassy, narrow creatures with sucker mouths and ribbon bodies, almost invisible when pressed into condensation. They travel upside down along wet seams, grazing on microbial slicks and the larvae of winged things. When vapor thickens, they loosen. Some fall accidentally. Some drop deliberately onto passing animals, fastening to a warm back and riding until shaken off. In mating season they knot together in ropes, translucent bodies braided and pulsing. Wise inhabitants do not stand beneath those ropes. A falling knot can smother a person, a grazer, or a whole basket of eggs.

The air is occupied by membrane-fliers.

They are not birds. Their wings are damp skins stretched over many knuckled fingers, shining where the cavern light passes through them. Their bodies are small, furred in pale bristles, with blunt faces and mouthparts like fine tools. They roost in black glass amphitheaters and under glowing seams, hanging by hooked toes in thousands. Their voices are metallic: shrills, pings, brittle whistles that bounce from the mineral roof.

They drink during indoor rain. They skim pools. They snatch soft larvae from wet stone. Sometimes they descend on a fresh mineral bloom in such numbers that a wall seems to tremble with wings. Their droppings nourish films that feed grazers, which feed scavengers, which feed larger things. In a world without grass, even waste is pasture.

The heavy animals belong to heat, mud, and sudden construction.

Barrel-bodied diggers shove through the youngest ridges. Their hides are thick, gray-white, and armored not by scales but by adhered debris: pebbles, shell fragments, flakes of glassy stone, old crusts cemented to their backs by secretions. They have blunt wedge heads and side vents that snort steam when they exert themselves. Their forelimbs are short and immensely strong. They do not dig tunnels in the patient way of moles; they bulldoze, slump, churn, and open brief opportunities for everything else.

Behind a digger comes a crowd: shell-creatures harvesting exposed salts, membrane-fliers taking stunned larvae, long-jawed scavengers waiting for crushed bodies, people collecting loosened stone. A digger’s path may become a streambed by evening, a nursery by morning, a sealed crystal ridge by the next week.

The long-jawed scavengers live on shelves.

They are patient, ugly, and clever in the way hunger makes a thing clever. Their bodies are low and corded, with long forelimbs ending in splayed gripping claws. Their jaws project far beyond the face, narrow and hinged, suited for pulling meat from cracks or plucking fallen eels from the backs of larger animals. Their skin is mottled mineral-gray, and they can remain still for hours among stones.

They seldom chase. They wait for the underland to make mistakes. A slipping grazer. A drained pool. A nest caught over a new vent. A person too tired to hear the silence change. They understand turbulence. They are students of interruption.

The tallest creatures are the jointed feeders.

From a distance they look like walking scaffolds or pale, unfinished machines. Their legs are high and thin, with too many hinges. Their bodies hang between them like suspended baskets of hide and cartilage. From the underside descend tendrils that open into fine branching nets. They wade slowly through crowds, patient beyond reason, lowering those tendril-baskets into pools where larvae boil in warm water.

They are not violent unless obstructed. Smaller animals pass between their legs. Membrane-fliers perch briefly on their joints. People shelter in their shadows during rain, though never directly beneath the feeding basket. A jointed feeder can stand in one place for a whole day, adjusting only by finger-widths, harvesting what the pool offers until the water drains away. Then, with the same solemn care, it folds its tendrils and moves on.

There are predators overhead, underfoot, and disguised as surfaces.

Some are like stretched sheets of flesh that cling to warm ceilings and release threads when vibration passes below. Some resemble mineral shelves until touched, then fold around a hand or snout. Some live in sucking hollows under clear-skinned pools. Some are nearly microscopic, entering cuts and turning flesh hot and sour unless treated with ash, salt, or bitter scrapings from certain stones.

Not everything that looks alive is an animal. Not everything that looks mineral is safe to stand on.

There are films that pulse away from touch. Crusts that bleed brine. Scalloped plates that retract into rock. White combs that grow so quickly they can trap sleeping tools, sleeping eggs, or the leg of a careless child. The inhabitants classify things less by kingdom than by behavior: what can be eaten, what eats back, what wakes in rain, what wakes in heat, what sings before splitting, what poisons only after hatching.

And there are people.

They are not masters of the hollow. They are another shore species living at the edge of forces larger than themselves. They occupy shelves, ledges, stable-for-now terraces, and the margins of channels. They build low and repair often. Their shelters squat under mineral overhangs or cluster beside warm pools, made of stacked stone, rib-bone, shell plates, hide, tendon, and clay. Nothing is built as if forever were a sensible idea.

They are probably pale, but not colorless. Their skin would carry the tones of mineral light: warm gray, clay, faint ocher, the bruised translucence of things that live without sun. Their eyes may be large, but not necessarily blind; this is not darkness. More important than sight are hearing, touch, and smell. They listen constantly. They know the difference between ordinary clatter and the pause before a vent-burst. They smell copper wind and salt wind. They feel through bare soles or thin sandals whether a floor is only warm or dangerously awake.

Their clothing is practical and repeatedly mended: scraped hides, woven sinew, shell guards over shins and forearms, chitin plates tied at shoulders, belts heavy with hooks, wedges, cord, and small containers of salt, ash, grease, and dried food. Children wear rattles or shell tags so adults can hear where they are among the larger noises. Elders carry staffs not as symbols but as listening tools, tapping floors, testing crusts, reading hollowness through the wrist.

Their tools are simple because complicated things fail too quickly here.

Hammerstones. Glass blades. Bone awls. Gut nets. Shell baskets. Claw hooks. Wedges for splitting new stone. Scrapers for edible film. Clay jars for water that may not remain water long. Fire is precious but awkward in the damp, so smoke marks active dwellings. They dry strips of pale meat near vents, ferment mineral scrapings and animal films in sealed jars, roast eggs when there are enough to take without angering whatever laid them.

They do not farm, not in any ordinary sense. They tend opportunities.

They maintain channels only until the floor revises them. They mark breeding pools so no one steps into a valuable frenzy. They move eggs away from vents when the parent creatures tolerate it, and sometimes when they do not. They break fast-growing mineral combs to keep passages open. They seed safe basins with edible larvae after rain. They hang shell strings where pressure changes first. They sweep thresholds not because cleanliness can win, but because noticing new dust, new slime, or new cracks can save lives.

Their knowledge is rhythmic and conditional.

This pool is good after second rain, but not if the ceiling eels have dropped. This film can be scraped before it pearls, never after. This larva is edible while white, bitter when gray, deadly when it begins to shine. This route is safe if the shell-creatures are noisy. If they are silent, wait. If the diggers face uphill, climb. If the wall sings, leave everything.

Their maps cannot be fixed drawings. They are songs, stories, strings of shells, hand signs, scars on posts, arrangements of stones, and remembered sequences of smell and sound. A route is not “left at the pillar” but “left at the pillar if the pillar still sweats, otherwise climb above the cracked basin and listen for the black-wing roost.”

They live scattered because density is dangerous.

Too much stored food brings scavengers. Too much waste changes the films. Too many fires stain the roof and alter the dripping. Too many feet weaken a shelf. Families, bands, or small clans occupy separate ledges and pool margins, trading when paths allow: dried fish for glass blades, shell plates for fermented paste, tendon cord for vent-stone, stories for warnings. News travels with migrants, hunters, gatherers, and children sent along known routes with rattling message strings.

They likely have ceremonies, but ceremonies here must also be useful.

A child’s first lesson may be silence: standing with an elder while the shell-creatures click, learning which pause means shadow overhead and which means pressure below. The dead may be placed where mineral growth will seal them, not buried in soil that does not exist. Handprints mark passages, but also become memorials when the passage slumps away. A good threshold, swept clean and rebuilt after each rain, is an act of devotion. To maintain a crossing for one more season may be as sacred as prayer.

They do not worship stability. They respect timing.

Their myths, if spoken aloud over smoke and dripping stone, would not imagine creation as something completed in the past. Creation is obviously still happening. The ground rises. Walls bloom. Pools open throats. Animals arrive from cracks. The world is unfinished, and therefore one must behave carefully around it. A careless footstep is not merely foolish; it is rude to a place still forming its skin.

Around the people live commensals.

Small translucent insects nest in their walls and eat mites from drying hides. Shell-creatures gather beneath shelters for scraps and mineral-rich ash. Thin white worms inhabit damp rope if it is not smoked. Ceiling eels learn the warmth of cooking fires and sometimes drop near them. A kind of palm-sized scavenger with a face like folded leather steals eggs, tools, and shiny mineral flakes, then hides them in vents too hot for human hands. Children trap these thieves with baited shell cages and sometimes keep the young as alarm animals.

Other creatures are feared but tolerated.

The scavengers clean what would rot. The diggers open new routes. The membrane-fliers warn of pressure shifts by leaving their roosts early. Even predators have meanings. A place with no predators may be poisoned, unstable, or about to vent. Absence is a warning.

The inhabitants all share one quality: readiness.

Every body here seems designed around triggers. Moisture unfurls it. Heat drives it upward. Vibration wakes it. Salt summons it. Light makes it retreat. Silence makes it strike. Dormant eggs wait in dust for rain. Fish wait in cracks for channels. Shells wait under crust. People sleep lightly. Tools are hung where they can be grabbed. Bridges are made to be lifted. Huts are built to be abandoned without grief.

Nothing lives here by owning the place.

They live by reading it, by yielding before it breaks, by returning after it has broken, by eating what the newest change exposes. The underland’s creatures are not primitive in any simple sense. They are exact. They belong to a world where categories are luxuries and survival depends on noticing whether a shine is water, skin, salt, glass, egg, poison, or hunger.

What lives there is a crowded, pale, mineral-fed multitude: shell rivers and blind fish, bladder-drinkers and ceiling eels, steam-snorting diggers, patient towering feeders, membrane-winged roosts, long-jawed opportunists, retracting not-plants, hatching crusts, and small human settlements tucked among them with handmade caution.

They are all temporary residents of a temporary landscape.

And yet, for now, they flourish.
