What lives there is not an animal, not a people, and only barely a flora.

The chief inhabitant is the ocean itself—if “inhabitant” can include a thing large enough to be its own world and restless enough to behave almost like a metabolism. It does not live as flesh lives. It has no skin except its density boundaries, no organs except its vents and pressure lenses, no intention except what can be inferred from recurrent tendencies. But it is the most animate presence there. Everything else is secondary: mineral, film, electrical weather, young stone, and rules moving invisibly through solution.

The high ocean lives by continuously revising its own body.

Its currents are its limbs, though they do not reach for anything. They fold through one another in cold, glassy immensities, carrying heat sideways, lifting sediment upward, holding voids open where voids should not persist. It grows temporary structures in itself: rain-walls, brine cliffs, bubble thunderheads, transparent faults. These are not organs in any stable sense, but for a few hours or a few months they perform functions. A falling river inside the sea cuts a shaft through murk and clears a light-path. A lens of still water preserves minerals in suspension until some later pressure wave turns them into a seeding storm. A density wall holds two incompatible chemistries apart long enough for something delicate to assemble along the boundary.

The ocean’s life is procedural. It makes conditions, watches them fail, makes others.

Within it, the closest things to ordinary living beings are the sparse photosynthetic films that cling to new stone. They are humble, thin, and widely scattered, yet in such emptiness they feel startlingly intimate. A green stain on a pale mineral shelf can seem almost like a village.

They grow as skins, veils, threads, and tarnishes. Some are dark and velvety where the water is kind and the light steady. Some are rusty, copper-green, or almost black where iron and metallic salts seep from vents. On warm seams they lengthen into fine hairlike streamers, not rooted deeply but adhered molecule by molecule to soft young rock. These strands all lean with the current, combed in one direction for months until a shear reverses and lays them flat the other way. In still pockets they form transparent membranes over stone, lifting and settling with pressure pulses like sleeping breath.

They do not compete much, because there is almost nothing to compete with. There are no grazers to crop them, no larvae to settle among them, no shells, worms, fins, mouths, or eyes. Their enemies are temperature, chemistry, force, and accident. A mineral bloom can smother them under white precipitate. A cold upwelling can stop their growth for a season that has no name. A pressure avalanche can peel them from the stone in sheets and carry them upward, still faintly alive for a time, until they dissolve into green smoke. A vent can sterilize a shelf in moments. A lightning pulse can rupture their cells across square kilometers. They are not hunted. They are erased.

And yet they return.

Their propagules—spores, cells, seedlike chemical packets, or something simpler than all of these—drift through the immense water in almost invisible numbers. They are not plankton in the lively, teeming sense. There is no glittering broth of appetite. Instead they are rare motes of possibility, patient in cold suspension, sometimes dormant for years as they travel through blue distances large enough to contain nations. When one touches a mineral face with the right light, the right ions, the right tolerable pressure, it wakes. A stain begins. The world acquires one more small claim of green.

Some of these films may not be wholly biological by familiar standards. Their cells may carry mineral lattices through themselves like scaffolding. Their surfaces may conduct charge during violet storms, using diffuse electricity the way other plants use sunlight. They may photosynthesize, chemosynthesize, and crystallize all at once. In some places, green membranes appear to encourage carbonate deposition beneath them, thickening the shelves they occupy; elsewhere they soften stone, extracting elements and leaving pitted lace. They are not engineers deliberately. They simply live, and their living slightly biases the newborn geology around them.

They are the first negotiators between water and stone.

Beneath and around them live other almost-lives: chemical colonies too primitive to call organisms with confidence. Films of catalytic mineral spread along vent lips and arrange molecules into repeating chains. Metallic salts form temporary conductive meshes that pulse during storms, break apart, and later reassemble elsewhere with nearly the same geometry. Carbonate membranes grow in kilometer-wide hexagons, then dissolve before they harden into cliffs. These things reproduce only if repetition is reproduction. They do not inherit bodies so much as recur whenever the conditions remember how to make them.

There are also the unseen assemblers.

Whether they are machines, microbes, laws, or the degraded reflexes of some ancient planetary technology, they pervade the water as a hidden population of instructions. Nothing about them resembles creatures. They have no visible colonies, no nests, no tools, no factories. But their presence is evident in the unreasonable precision of events. Crystals grow where they should not. Heat refuses to disperse. Buoyancy contradicts mass. Mineral membranes hold impossible tension. Voids persist inside water. Superconductive sludge briefly aligns into continental circuits and then forgets itself.

If they live, they live as preferences inside matter.

They may be innumerable: nanoscopic agents drifting through every stratum, reading pressure and salinity, nudging ions into place, repairing the conditions that keep the ocean raised above the world. Or they may not be agents at all, but the operating principles of the place—technology so old or so complete that it has ceased to be separable from geology. In either case, they are the deep custodians of the high ocean’s impossibility. They do not build monuments. They maintain likelihoods. They keep the world capable of continuing.

They are most visible when something almost architectural appears.

A lattice rises from a vent: white, flexible, cathedral-sized, veined with black dust and pale fire. For several hours it seems impossible that no intelligence designed it. Its struts meet at clean angles. Its membranes curve with mathematical grace. Electrical light inhabits it. Green cells settle on it as if on a reef. Then the internal currents shift, and the structure fails. It rings once, cracks, twists, and loosens into fragments. The fragments drift away through blue water, each one briefly trailed by threads of algae and mineral powder.

Nothing mourns it. Nothing meant it to last.

This is true of almost everything that lives there. Permanence has not yet been granted as a property of the world.

The young stone lives in its own slow way. Basalt ribs rise as if testing a shape. Mineral shelves extrude and soften. Arches inflate from vents and collapse before erosion can claim them. Trenches begin but do not yet know whether they are wounds, valleys, or failed hollows. The geology is embryonic, and because it is being constantly suspended, inverted, lifted, and rewritten, it behaves less like foundation than like tissue. It swells. It seals. It opens. It sheds unfinished forms into the water.

The stones are newborn and undecided. Some hang in the water, porous and dark, caught in edited gravity. Some precipitate as pale fans that tremble like gills but do not breathe. Some form needles, isolated black spires standing alone in hundred-kilometer clarity, so solitary that they become presences. A traveler might feel watched by them, though they have no awareness. In that empty blue, mere endurance begins to resemble character.

There may be microbial life in the vents, but it would be austere and deeply local: heat-loving films in black chimneys, sulfur-fed mats lining cracks, colonies that flare chemically for a few days after a seam opens and then die when the chemistry shifts. These would not form reefs or gardens. They would be stains of metabolism on the lips of catastrophe. Their world would be measured in gradients: warmth here, poison there, dissolved metal, acidity, charge. A whole civilization of chemistry might bloom in a fissure no wider than a hand and vanish before the next pressure wave.

But there are no animals.

That absence defines the place almost as strongly as water does. No eyes adapt to the folded light. No fins learn the shear zones. No translucent predators hang in the blue. No scavengers wait beneath mineral snow. No whale-song crosses the pressure strata. No barnacles harden on basalt needles. No worms aerate the silt. No reefs accumulate skeletons. Death, when it occurs, is mostly dissolution, stripping, chemical undoing. Organic matter does not pass through stomachs. It drifts, settles, lifts again, breaks apart in cold reactions, or becomes part of a mineral film.

Because there are no animals, there is no fear in the biological sense. Nothing flees. Nothing stalks. Nothing hides from being eaten.

Yet the place is not peaceful. It is full of danger without malice.

The inhabitants, such as they are, live by tolerating vast impersonal violence. A green mat survives because the shear misses it. A vent colony survives because the heat remains just below destruction. A suspended chemical membrane persists because the next lightning bloom is distant. A propagule survives because it can wait longer than instability. Here, endurance is not heroic; it is statistical. Life exists as a thin scattering of successful delays.

The most advanced “organism” may be the entire system: the high ocean, its edited physics, its mineralizing crust, its sparse photosynthetic attempts, its invisible assemblers, and its weather of pressure and light. Taken together, they resemble a single vast pre-ecology before the arrival of hunger. Not a biosphere yet, but a protobiosphere. Not an ecosystem, but the machinery from which ecosystems might one day condense.

It lives in phases.

In its quiet phase, it is almost empty: blue light, cold suspension, distant thunder, a few mineral flakes turning slowly in water so clear that distance becomes abstract. A green veil clings to a shelf. A black needle stands alone. Internal rain hisses in the far strata. Life is reduced to patience.

In its formative phase, vents open and the water darkens with powdered metal. Chemical membranes bloom. Lightning diffuses through superconductive salts. Heat climbs in braided channels. New stone swells from fissures, pale and raw. Photosynthetic cells arrive almost immediately, as if the ocean has seeded itself with anticipation. For a while, creation looks deliberate.

In its destructive phase, pressure changes direction. Still plains fold. Silt races sideways. Void-lenses burst. A shelf cracks and sheds its living skin. Mineral scaffolds collapse. Warmth is stolen by the surrounding cold. The unfinished geography is returned to suspension.

Then clarity comes again.

What lives there, then, are beginnings.

Green beginnings on stone. Mineral beginnings in water. Technological beginnings hidden as law. Geological beginnings rising before they become mountains. Weather systems beginning to act like organs. A continent beginning under an ocean that should not be there, held aloft by processes that may be ancient intelligence, planetary repair, failed terraforming, or simply a physics not yet domesticated by history.

If anything there possesses awareness, it is not concentrated enough to speak. It would not think in words or images. It would think, if thinking is the word, in gradients: warmer, colder, denser, charged, soluble, stable, unstable, lift, fold, precipitate, dissolve. It would remember by leaving tendencies in matter. It would desire only by repeating certain formations more often than chance. It would answer injury by routing currents elsewhere. It would nurture accidentally and destroy without noticing.

A traveler looking for inhabitants would first report none. Then, after long exposure, they would begin to feel surrounded.

Not by creatures.

By processes with habits. By water with a constitution. By young stone trying on shapes. By films of life so sparse that each patch seems precious. By invisible agencies persuading matter at scales too small to see and too large to comprehend. By an ocean that is less a habitat than a living precondition for habitats, a world not yet populated but already occupied by the labor of becoming one.
