What lives there is not a people, not a kingdom, not even a set of easily named species, but an overfull congress of cold-adapted life: a whole altitude ecology so dense and interdependent that individual creatures seem less like inhabitants than temporary organs of the place.

The largest beings are the ones first mistaken for terrain.

They move slowly across the white shelves and dark moss plains, broad as hillocks, their backs ridged with plates and weathered fur, their shoulders crusted in frost. From a distance they resemble broken moraine or low volcanic domes until one lifts its head and exhales two white columns into the air. They are grazers, but not gentle in any pastoral sense. Their mouths are industrial instruments: mineralized jaw-plates, rasping tongues, horn-edged lips capable of planing lichens and moss from bare rock. Where they feed, they leave long parallel grooves in the stone, clean pale scars that other organisms immediately begin to claim.

Their horns are not smooth antlers but splintered structures like frost-burst wood or basalt columns, grown in layers, shed in slabs, often alive with red lichen before the animal is done using them. Their hides are ecosystems: mats of algae in the fur, white mites in the folds, hook-legged cleaners moving along their flanks, small birds or birdlike things clinging near the steaming vents of their nostrils to drink salt and warmth. Some carry entire gardens on their backs—moss, fungus, bladder-grasses rooted in old scars—so that a grazing animal may also be a wandering pasture.

They do not hurry. Their migrations are geologic in mood. A herd may take years to cross a plateau, generations to complete a circuit. Calves are born dark and wet in fog hollows among clicking reeds, already breathing steam, already attended by swarms. When one dies, the herd does not remain long. The body itself becomes a temporary valley: wings descend, shell-backed scavengers arrive, filaments root into the eyes and mouth, bone-raspers gather in rows, and within days the carcass is less a corpse than a warm, crowded worksite.

Around and beneath these giants live the quick, small multitudes.

Six-legged runners pour over stone in braided streams, armored and coordinated, their bodies black, blue, or coppery depending on what minerals they have eaten. They travel in dense lanes over exposed ridges, stopping all together when shadow crosses them, then resuming with a dry rattling like seeds in a gourd. Some polish fresh grazing scars. Some harvest fungal crust. Some carry spores in grooves along their shells. Their colonies occupy the undersides of stones, the hollows beneath cushion plants, the soft margins of old horn and bone.

There are burrowers that move through moss as fish move through weed. Their presence is visible as trembling, then as small collapses, then as neat chimney-holes that steam faintly in the morning. They aerate the deep mats, redistribute mineral dust, and cultivate underground films on which other creatures feed. Some are blind and soft-bodied, with rings of sensory hairs along their flanks. Others are plated, wedge-headed, and patient, built to shove through frozen root and spongey peat. Their tunnels are not random. Over time, instinct produces repeating fields of vents, drainage lines, chambers, and warm nests—settlements without intention.

The cliffs belong to the nesters.

Every ledge is occupied. There are feathered animals with compact bodies and long hooked toes, pale bellies, dark backs, and eyes adapted to blinding glare. Some resemble birds only until they open their mouths and reveal filamented tongues used to strain fog for suspended organisms. Others carry ice crystals back to warm vent-mouth nests, cooling eggs that would otherwise cook in geothermal heat. Their colonies shriek, clatter, and boom from the cliff walls, staining the rock with guano that feeds vertical gardens of fungus and lichen. Their young are raised among steam, frost, and falling mineral dust.

Above them ride the high scavengers: immense, membranous-winged beings, slate-black and silver-veined, more like gliders than birds. Their wings shine as if oiled. They have long skulls, narrow torsos, and sensory crests that read heat columns rising from fissures. They do not flap often. They wait in the updrafts, circling for hours over herds, avalanches, vent-fields, births, and deaths. Their calls fall in metallic notes, descending and then vanishing into the cold air. When they land, it is awkwardly, folding themselves like broken tents over carrion, their beaks or mouth-parts precise enough to open frozen hide.

In the fog basins live the reed-thickets and the creatures hidden inside them.

The reeds are not passive plants. They click and pump, store water in beadlike organs, twist in light, unwind in cold, and grind their own seeds open with internal plates. Their hollow stems sing in wind. Some have crowns that hold black pools of water; in those pools swarm larvae, ciliated threads, tiny translucent crustaceans, eggs, spores, and minute predators like living punctuation marks. A single leaf-basin may contain a complete world: grazers, hunters, filterers, decomposers, mineral bacteria, drifting embryos waiting for the next hard gust to carry them elsewhere.

Among the reeds move thicket-animals: slow, many-limbed bodies disguised by growths of twig, moss, and fur. At rest they appear to be shrubs bent by wind. Then the whole shrub lifts and walks. Their limbs unfold in sections. Their mouths are hidden low, beneath curtains of rootlike whiskers. They browse on reed sap, fungi, insects, eggs, and mineral crust. Some are solitary. Some gather in loose groups during fog-heavy seasons and make low knocking sounds through hollow chambers in their bodies.

There are also the pump-colonies along the lower cliffs.

They are among the strangest inhabitants because they are neither one creature nor many in any familiar way. Each individual is shell-backed, fixed or slow-moving, with translucent tissue extending into tubes. Together they web the rock with shining conduits, carrying meltwater uphill, sideways, and across exposed faces. Their bodies pulse. Their valves pop softly. In exchange for water, sunlit mats feed them sugars; vent-bacteria feed them salts; fungi house their young. A cliff colonized by them looks veined with glass. At dawn, when ice grips the tubes, the whole face ticks and strains as if full of tiny clocks.

Near the mineral chimneys live grazers of a different scale: low, broad-mouthed, heat-tolerant animals that harvest bacterial mats in careful arcs. They return again and again to the same vent-rings, cropping one color band but leaving another to regrow. Their lips are insulated. Their eyes are small, protected from steam by shutters. Some carry symbiotic heat-bacteria in chambers along their bellies. Others lay eggs in warm mineral mud, where the shells harden with metallic salts.

The chimneys themselves are alive in the communal sense. Bacteria build them, clog them, poison them, repair them, abandon them. Around each vent are rings of color—rust, green, violet, sulfur-yellow, milky white—each ring a different metabolism, each metabolism feeding or excluding another. Tiny animals graze the rings. Larger animals graze the grazers. Fungi lace through the cooled outer crusts. When a chimney bursts, a neighborhood dies and another begins inches away.

The lichens are everywhere and may be the true rulers.

They bloom in colors too saturated for the cold: red like pigment, yellow like powdered sulfur, violet like bruised skin, white like exposed bone. They grow on stone, horn, shell, old ice, living bark, dead feathers, and sometimes on the slow backs of animals that tolerate them. Some are brittle crusts. Some are velvet mats. Some form glassy plates that focus light onto their own tissues. Some darken at noon and glow faintly under frost after nightfall. On certain cliff faces, colonial lichens advance through mineral veins in branching fronts so vast they look like weather systems mapped onto rock.

Mosses and cushion plants fill the rest.

They are armored, low, dense, and social. They remember pressure. A footstep does not simply crush them; it leaves a pale ghost, a chemical afterimage, a signal that spreads through neighboring growth. Burrowers use these signals. Grazers learn them. Predators wait near old paths where the moss still remembers repeated weight. The mosses drink fog, melt rime, trap mineral dust, shelter eggs, feed mites, insulate roots, and slowly manufacture the first real soil in cracks where no soil should yet exist.

The air is inhabited as densely as the ground.

Spores drift like weather. Winged crustacean-things flicker in bright swarms. Translucent ribbons rotate through updrafts, feeding on suspended droplets and microscopic life. Small feathered bodies dive between cliffs and reed-fields. Seed-vanes release spiraling embryos that stay aloft for days, collecting frost before anchoring in a wet crevice. There are organisms that look like snowflakes until they turn against the wind. There are floating larvae that spend their youth in clouds and descend only when heavy with condensed water.

Even the falling snow is occupied: crystals bearing spores, dormant eggs, mineral dust, bacteria, tiny encysted lives waiting for stone, fur, pool, mouth, or wound.

The predators here are not always dramatic. Many are traps, films, waiting surfaces.

Some pools grow skins overnight that wrinkle away from breath, then close over insects that land on them. Certain fungi form amber shelves that look solid until touched, then soften and hold. Reed basins contain transparent hunters with springing jaws. Moss mats harbor rootlike nets that tighten around burrowing larvae. Cliff lichens exude salts attractive to grazers, then poison them slowly enough that scavenger colonies benefit nearby. In such a packed world, predation is often indistinguishable from hospitality until too late.

But there are also hunters with limbs and eyes.

Lean ridge-runners travel the black animal causeways, low to the ground, furred in gray-white, with heat pits along their faces and claws made for ice-glazed stone. They hunt the six-legged streams, cliff chicks, burrowers, and the weak young of larger animals. In fog, larger predators move almost silently: jointed, antlered, reed-colored things that stand still for hours until prey forgets which thickets are rooted and which are not. Some hunt by vibration, tapping stone with forelimbs and listening through their feet.

Nothing lives alone.

Every creature carries other creatures. Every mouth feeds a chain of mouths. Every trail becomes a habitat. Every exhalation condenses into water for someone. Every patch of warmth is occupied, contested, rented, exchanged. The inhabitants do not build cities, but colonies become citylike through accumulation: cliff-tube networks, vent neighborhoods, reed basins, grazer roads, nesting walls, burrow fields, corpse festivals, glacier-edge pastures red with algae and black with feeding bodies.

If there is a governing intelligence here, it is not a mind but a pressure: the compulsion to occupy.

Life has entered every crack in the young stone before the stone has finished becoming landscape. It has learned to pump water uphill, grind seeds with wind, farm heat, harvest frost, polish rock, digest minerals, remember footsteps, and turn death into weather. The creatures are not invaders of the mountain-continent. They are how the mountain-continent expresses itself while it is still raw.

So what lives there?

Herds that look like ridges. Birds that nest in steam and carry ice. Scavengers that sail on volcanic breath. Mosses that remember. Lichens that mine cliff faces. Reeds that turn like mills. Shell-colonies that pump meltwater through glassy veins. Burrowers that ventilate the living ground. Swarms that move like poured metal. Pools with mouths. Stones furred with hunger. Snow seeded with unborn things.

A cold abundance lives there.

A factory of bodies.

A mountain pretending to be a wilderness, and a wilderness already busily turning the mountain into itself.
