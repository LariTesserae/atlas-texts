What lives there is not a species so much as a pressure of life—a whole secret fauna pressed thin by heat, mineral, and tightness until it has become almost indistinguishable from the place that shelters it.

They are the inhabitants of a newborn wound in the mountain: animals of condensation, pore, seam, and film. They have no forests, no soil, no sky, no seasons except wet and less wet, hot and hotter, pressure rising and pressure slackening. Their world is measured in droplets. A basin the size of a thumbprint is a lake. A basalt spur is a ridge. A salt crust is a temporary plain. A fissure too narrow for a fingernail is a migration corridor, a refuge, a hunting ground, a border between climates.

Most of them are nearly colorless. Pale, amber, gray, smoky-white, black only when they borrow the color of the rock beneath them. Transparency is common here, perhaps because there is nowhere to hide except by becoming part of water or glass. Many of them show their organs through their skins: dim pulses, threadlike guts, slow-contracting sacs, little mineral grains held inside the body like swallowed stars. Under the strange light, they seem unfinished, as if life here has not decided whether bodies should be opaque.

They are small because the mountain allows no largeness. Their forms are flattened, folded, coiled, jointed into impossible economy. A creature may spend its life in the wet shadow beneath one flake of basalt. Another may migrate daily between two droplets, a distance that to it is a dangerous exposed crossing. Some are narrow enough to live inside the meniscus where water touches stone. Some are shaped to wedge themselves into vesicles when steam thickens. Some seal shut so completely that they resemble seeds, mineral beads, or old bubbles in the lava.

There are grazers, and they are the most numerous.

They move over the slick skins that grow on rock: microbial films, mineral curds, fungal veils, colonies too simple or too strange to name. The grazers have soft mouths, scraping plates, rasping cilia, or rings of tiny hooked teeth. They polish trails into the black glass by passing the same way again and again. A feeding lane may be only the width of a hair, but generations know it. Along these lanes the rock becomes smoother than the rest of the chamber, faintly lustrous, an animal road made without intention.

Some grazers are disks: soft, low, almost featureless, sliding on films of their own mucus. They leave trails that crystallize after them, so every journey becomes a temporary line of salt or silica. Some are translucent slugs reduced to a rim and a stomach. Some are mite-like, with thread legs that move delicately over mineral lace without breaking it. These mites gather in disciplined knots at warm seep-lines, feeding shoulder to shoulder while the water lasts, then scattering upward when a heat pulse clouds the pool below.

Others are swimmers.

They live in the hand-sized basins, the thumb-sized basins, the bead-sized basins. They are pale and jointless, more gesture than anatomy, coiling and uncoiling with a slow precision. They avoid the boiling centers and the sulfur-yellow vents, grazing instead on the invisible bloom that forms near the cooler rims. In deeper droplets they spiral as if following lines no eye can see. Their bodies catch the faint light and vanish in it. When disturbed, they do not dart away so much as become thinner, aligning themselves with the brightness until they disappear.

Some pools hold entire lineages that have never crossed into another pool, though the distance may be no more than the width of a hand. One basin’s swimmers are ribbon-like and blind. Another’s are bead-jointed, able to contract into pale seeds when the water warms too much. A third has creatures with faint internal lamps, not true light perhaps, but a pearly disturbance in their flesh that makes the pool seem deeper than it is.

The shelled ones live at the edges.

They are no bigger than seeds, and their shells are not true shells in the old sea sense, but mineral agreements: silica, salt, iron, and secreted cement layered into curved shields. When steam grows harsh, they clamp themselves to the stone and become pebbles. When cooler seepage returns, hairlike limbs unfold from beneath the casing, and they begin to feed, scrape, drag, arrange. They leave discarded shells behind, and these accumulate in little barricades along rivulets. Over time, their dead make dams. The dams slow water. Slowed water grows film. Film feeds the living. So the dead shelled ones become part of the architecture of hunger.

They are among the closest things the place has to builders.

Not builders in thought. Builders by repetition. By mouthpart. By the insistence of need. They push flakes. They stack casings. They seal cracks with waste and mineral paste. In some corners their low walls divide one wet trickle into three, making terraces small enough that a human breath could destroy them. These arrangements look deliberate. Perhaps they are, in the way a wasp’s nest is deliberate, or a coral reef, or a scab.

There are predators.

They are quiet, patient, and extremely near.

An amber flat-bodied hunter waits in the skin of a pool, indistinguishable from the meniscus until prey rises into it. Then it folds shut. The movement is too fast for the eye to follow clearly; one moment the swimmer is there, the next the lens of water has thickened, and something inside the amber body darkens.

Hair-thin hunters span cracks. They are not spiders, though a visitor would be forgiven for thinking of webs. Their lines are secreted filaments that stiffen in dry heat and soften in steam. When dampness returns, the lines become adhesive. Anything crossing a warm seam may find itself touched, slowed, then drawn toward a mouth hidden in a pore. The hunter itself may be only a knot, a pale hinge, a cluster of gripping limbs surrounding a dissolving gut.

Other predators are chemical. They live inside droplets with others and wait until the volume is enclosed enough. Then they release a faint cloud—an anesthetic, an acid, a hunger made soluble. The victims stop moving before they are touched. In a bead of water no larger than a tear, a whole massacre can occur without ripple.

Some predators hunt the sealed.

They patrol drying planes where stranded animals have curled into protective beads. With slender drills, they pierce the seals and drink what waits inside. Their mouthparts are often reinforced with mineral needles stolen from the very crust they cross. These hunters are patient because the mountain is patient in them. A sealed grazer may survive heat for days, only to be opened by something smaller than a splinter.

There are also animals that seem almost not to be animals.

Chains of mouths hanging where condensation gathers. Salt fuzz that retracts from breath. Cloudy stains that creep against the direction of water flow. Gelatinous beads that divide when warmed and crawl when cooled. A film may graze another film. A stain may lay eggs. A patch of gray may suddenly wrinkle, expose feeding pores, and become a colony of soft bodies sharing one edge.

Here, the boundary between creature and surface is unreliable. Life borrows mineral habits. Mineral deposits imitate flesh. The young rock, still close to its molten memory, seems to invite such confusion. Some inhabitants incorporate black glass chips into their backs until they look like broken stone. Some grow sulfur-yellow nodules that may be armor, organs, or cultivated microbes. Some carry heat-loving colonies on their bodies and feed them with excreted mineral salts, becoming little moving gardens in a place without plants.

Eggs are everywhere once one learns how to see them.

They appear as stippling on overhangs, rings around warm pores, tiny pearls embedded in silica crescents, orange dust along iron films. Some are laid in clusters under flakes, roofed over with flat mineral shards. Some are strung in gelatin threads between droplets, swelling only when the humidity reaches a certain thickness. Some hatch almost immediately, as if the embryo had been waiting elsewhere and merely arrived through the shell. Others remain dormant through heat pulses, salt blooms, droughts of minutes or days, until one precise combination of condensation and temperature opens them all at once.

The young are often unlike the adults. A shelled crawler begins as a swimming comma. A pool-coil begins as a creeping bead. A thread hunter begins as something like salt fuzz, feeding on films until it finds a crack to span. Many lives here are reversible in appearance if not in fact: swimmer to crawler, crawler to sealed grain, grain to unfolding animal. The chamber rewards those who can become several things without leaving one body.

Their senses must be intimate and strange.

Eyes, where present, are simple dark flecks, useful only for brightness and shadow. More important are heat, pressure, wetness, mineral taste, vibration. They feel the approach of a steam sigh before it arrives. They know the cooling of a stone face a body-length away. They read chemical traces left in condensation. A predator striking in the pool sends a message through the meniscus; grazers feeding along a seam pause, though they cannot have seen it. A pressure change in an unseen fissure draws animals upward or down long before droplets begin to move.

They must also sense the chamber’s small distortions, because they live inside them without hesitation.

If a droplet shows an event before it happens, some creatures alter course. If a crack lengthens while entered, the thread-legged migrants continue, measuring with limbs rather than distance. If a falling drop takes too long to fall, something climbs out of its way. If a previous posture lingers faintly behind a moving body, hunters sometimes strike at the afterimage and miss; others have learned to wait for the second outline, the truer one, or perhaps the hungrier one.

None of this makes them mystical to themselves. It is weather. It is terrain. It is simply how the mountain pocket behaves.

Their lives are urgent but not frantic. Urgency has been built into their bodies so deeply that it no longer resembles panic. They feed while wet. Seal while dry. Climb when heat rises. Descend when warmth slackens. Lay eggs where warmth will hasten but not kill. Avoid the vents unless they are among the few that need the vents. Cross open glass only when condensation thickens. Retreat when stone ticks. Freeze when steam sighs. Resume when the first mite moves.

A human visitor would first think them innumerable. Then fragile. Then less fragile than anything.

They survive boiling margins, sudden drought, mineral encrustation, poisonous vents, thin air, unstable stone, and each other. They can be cooked, dissolved, sealed over, starved, eaten. Yet as a collective they are stubborn as lichen, though there is no lichen there. Let one pool vanish and another beads into being. Let a mineral crust crack and film blooms in the fracture. Let a shard fall and crush a hundred bodies, and within the wet scar eggs swell, grazers gather, predators follow. The place kills constantly, but it also makes openings constantly.

Their colors, when gathered into a whole, are the colors of the chamber: wet black, iron orange, silica white, sulfur yellow, cloudy gray, translucent amber. Their movements are the movements of the chamber: dripping, seeping, clinging, clouding, cracking, sealing, reopening. They do not occupy the mountain as tenants occupy a house. They are a behavior of the mountain. A living phase of its cooling.

Perhaps they came from outside: windblown cysts drawn through a hairline fissure, microbial films riding seepage, ancestral mites or larvae washed down from snowmelt and altered by heat over generations. Perhaps they rose from deeper mineral waters, descendants of subterranean things that had never seen the sky. Or perhaps the pocket, being new and undecided, found life latent in the contact between hot stone, water, salt, and light, and coaxed it into bodies.

Whatever their origin, they now belong wholly to this hidden seam.

They are the mountain’s damp thought made animate.  
They are droplets that learned to hunger.  
They are mineral edges with mouths.  
They are a crowded, silent civilization of instinct, surviving in a place where the world is still cooling into certainty.
