What lives there is not a creature in the ordinary sense, though it has learned to occupy a city’s worth of rooms.

It is a population of slow procedures.

At first one would call them caretakers, because care is visible everywhere: the skimmed basins, the reset plugs, the unblocked teeth of overflow, the renewed chalk marks at water height, the fiber wicks combed clean of silt. But if caretakers exist here, they are too continuous with their tasks to stand apart from them. They do not seem to live in the basin as people live in houses. They live as the basin’s habits.

They are small, or they have made themselves small. Not necessarily in body, if body is the right word, but in their manner of presence. They inhabit apertures, seams, plugged jars, the shaded backs of sluice stones, the water-dark hollows beneath ceramic lips. A stranger would not see them walking across a terrace. They do not cross open brightness unless disguised as maintenance: a stone settling, a wick tightening, a drop redirected from one groove to another.

Their most visible signs are corrections.

A channel that was crusted with white salt in the afternoon runs clear by dawn. A loose tile is found weighted true with a pebble of fired slag. Sediment in a settling pan appears combed into parallel ridges, each ridge exactly the width of a fingertip, though no fingertip has touched it. A basin filled too high at evening has been lowered in the night by one notch, no more. The work is minute, disciplined, almost devotional. Nothing is repaired grandly. Nothing is transformed. The inhabitants intervene only enough to keep the circulation from becoming event.

They may have once been human, or something near enough to humans to have inherited hands.

There are too many hand-shaped decisions in the place for the origin to be wholly alien: plugs that fit the grip, steps worn in the rhythm of knees and ankles, cup-marks placed at crouching height, scratch marks beside basins made by a tool dragged through damp plaster by a living wrist. The earliest builders were probably upright, warm, impatient, dependent on water in the usual desperate way. They must have arrived on this high exposed stone with no soil to trust and no river to follow. So they made a watershed by hand. They taught water to linger where bodies could drink.

But over generations the distinction between people and system thinned.

A community founded on catching mist would become attentive to smallness. Its children would learn not seasons first, but surfaces: which wall weeps at noon, which gutter lies, which cup fills before the others, which mineral bloom means sour water, which shining seam means a hidden channel is backing up. Speech would become measuring. Law would become gradient. Memory would be stored in the way a drop traveled across stone.

They would come to distrust haste. Haste spills. Haste erodes. Haste breaks the lip of the basin and wastes what took all night to gather.

So they changed, or were changed.

The present inhabitants are pale, if seen directly, but not with the pallor of sickness. Their color belongs to wet clay, limewash, old ceramic, the underside of stone that never sees sun. They are narrow and jointed, built for alleys and shafts rather than open rooms. Their limbs fold close. Their fingers are long and blunt, padded not for grasping weapons or fruit but for feeling flow. They can tell by touch whether a wall is sweating from rain stored behind it, condensation forming upon it, or pressure pushing through a failing seam.

Their skin holds moisture differently from animal skin. It is not slimy, not scaled, not furred. It has the matte smoothness of plaster before it dries, faintly granular, able to darken when wet and whiten when exposed. In bright light they are difficult to distinguish from the walls until they move. In shade they look like bundled cloth stiffened with lime.

They do not wear much, because cloth rots poorly here only by not rotting at all; it mineralizes, stiffens, becomes more apparatus than garment. What they use as clothing is wrapping, filter, wick, tool-belt, insulation. Strips of reed-fiber and lime-cloth cross their torsos. Small stone gauges hang from their shoulders. Clay plugs ride in loops against their ribs. They carry no metal blades unless metal is needed for scraping blue-green corrosion from hinges or cutting the mineral-stiff cords that regulate certain drip frames.

Their faces are seldom fully visible. Not because they hide in fear, but because faces are not how they identify one another. They work by posture, rhythm, moisture trace, the slight pattern of salts left by the body on pale stone. Their eyes, when seen, are large and filmed against glare. The basin is too bright; anything that lives here must learn to look indirectly. They glance through reflections, through water skins, through the cloudy mineral panes set into walls. To stare straight across a noon terrace would be a kind of blindness.

Their mouths are small. Their voices, if they speak, are not made for carrying.

They converse by taps, by controlled dripping, by opening and closing holes in the capped jars mortared into walls. A row of tiny apertures can whisper across a district when the wind passes over them. Water falling through a shaft can be interrupted with a reed pin to produce spaced notes. A message may take all night to arrive as a sequence of drops collected in cups below a ledge. Urgency exists, but it is expressed by deviation: a basin emptied out of turn, three cups inverted instead of two, a condensation screen left dry in fog.

Their language is probably not divided cleanly between word, number, prayer, and instruction. A mark beside a cistern can mean “full,” “remember the year of overflow,” “do not drink before settling,” and “the dead are beneath this level,” depending on when water reaches it. Meaning is completed by wetness.

They do not farm. There is almost nothing green to farm.

They live from what the basin permits: mineral water, microbial skins skimmed from certain cisterns, pale films cultivated in shaded tanks, residues settled and dried into edible cakes, perhaps the bodies of organisms too small to announce themselves as life. Their nourishment may seem impossibly meager to an outsider, but they have followed the basin into low expenditure. They are cool-blooded in habit if not in physiology. They rest often. They wait with perfect seriousness. A day of watching a cup fill is not wasted if that cup determines the opening of a downstream sluice.

Birth among them is likely rare and heavily ritualized, because the place has no abundance for careless multiplication. Infants, if they have them, are kept in the warmest wet chambers near kiln remnants, wrapped among condensation cloths, fed with clarified skins and measured salts. Children would learn the basin by crawling it: first the safe dry paths, then the low channels, then the parapets where dew forms even under stars. A child’s first duty might be to keep one palm-sized depression clean. Later, a row of depressions. Later, a wall.

The old do not retire. They become more suited to the place.

An elder may spend years in charge of a single drop-field, knowing each strand of mineral-stiff lattice by its sound. Another may tend a sealed well never opened except in drought, sleeping beside the stone plug with one ear against it. Some elders are eventually installed rather than buried: wrapped in lime-cloth, placed in niches, their bones or desiccated bodies becoming gauges, witnesses, or mnemonic weights. The bundled forms tucked into the buttresses may be ancestors, but not in the sentimental sense. They are incorporated into the hydraulics of memory. The dead remain where water can pass near them.

This may be why the place feels crowded.

The inhabitants are not only the living maintenance-workers who move at night. They are also the mortared jars containing old breath, the tablets underfoot, the capped chambers holding sediment from vanished rains, the standing stones with cup-marks, the wrapped bundles hidden in niches. Each retained thing has a task. Nothing is merely commemorative. Memory must function, or it is waste.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is not worship of water as a god. It is more severe than worship. They do not praise water; they negotiate with it. They delay it, divide it, cool it, clarify it, give it surfaces upon which to decide itself. Water is not benevolent. It is not alive. It is the condition by which anything here continues.

The central pool is their deepest civic place.

They do not gather around it in crowds. Crowds would warm the air, disturb condensation, shed dust and breath. Instead they approach singly or in ordered intervals. Each low pillar with its shallow cup may belong to a line, a duty, a chamber, a dead person, a measure of stored time. A full cup is not simply full. It says something has completed its passage: mist has become bead, bead has become trickle, trickle has endured all its stone delays and arrived without contamination.

When a drop falls into the central pool, they listen.

The ring widening over the silver surface is an announcement larger than speech. Its clarity tells them about tension, purity, depth, maybe weather beyond the ridges. One ripple crossing another may be read as warning or assent. A basin culture would naturally make divination out of hydrodynamics, though to them it would not be divination. It would be literacy.

They are frightened by growth.

Not because growth is evil, but because roots break masonry, leaves clog teeth, moss hides cracks, insects breed in standing water, animals foul cisterns, soil holds histories too chaotic to measure. The absence of vegetation is not failure alone; it is policy. They scrape green away before it can soften the edges. If a seed ever blows in and sprouts in a seam, it is removed with something like pity and alarm. A plant is a leak pretending to be alive.

They also fear deep earth.

The mountain, if it exists beneath the sealed platform, is an old danger. Bare ground drinks unpredictably. Springs emerge where they choose. Soil collapses. Roots and burrows make private channels outside communal measure. The builders covered the mountain not only to build upon it but to silence it. The cold through the soles is the buried world still insisting, still trying to enter the designed one.

So the inhabitants live above geology but below weather, between two untrustworthy immensities.

They have made a third thing: a hand-built climate.

Their society is probably not organized by houses, since houses are almost irrelevant. It is organized by catchments. By flowsheds. By circuits of responsibility. One group tends the morning condensers on the eastern terraces. Another keeps the settling pans free of fine grit. Another listens to hidden shafts. Another recuts cup-marks when mineral crust dulls them. Another fires replacement tiles in the black-mouthed kilns, using fuel so scarce or strange that smoke is never smelled.

Perhaps they burn dried microbial cakes, old wrappings, distilled oils made from the basin’s pale skins, or imported fuel brought from lower worlds no one speaks about. Or perhaps the kilns are mostly memory now, opened only when a district must be remade.

They sleep in dry cells within wet walls.

Their beds are shelves above floor channels, not for comfort but for regulation. Too much dryness harms them; too much wet invites mineral crust on the joints. They keep their sleeping places at exact degrees of damp. A family cell might be recognized by the pattern of suspended cups, the smell of wet clay warmed by bodies, the particular arrangement of plugs and cord. There are no hearths in most rooms. Warmth would be disruptive. They value cool steadiness over comfort.

Their tools are simple and exquisite: reed brushes hardened with lime, shell-thin ceramic scoops, cord-wrapped stone stoppers, calibrated wedges, absorbent cloths for drawing water from cracks, small hammers with heads of vitrified slag, finger gauges worn smooth by generations. A child inherits not a sword, not a field, not a name-house, but perhaps a set of plugs fitted to a particular cistern mouth.

They do not hurry even in catastrophe.

If a wall fails, the response is distributed: upper sluices closed, side channels opened, delay terraces made thirsty, emergency basins uncapped, people taking positions at known points. The basin itself absorbs panic by design. Disaster is not met by shouting but by procedures older than memory. Someone will stand knee-deep in cold water holding a slab in place for hours. Someone else will count drops from a relief spout until the pressure falls. Someone will mark the new crack, not dramatically, but accurately.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts would be too human, too attached to former rooms.

They are not machines. Machines would impose motion from elsewhere.

They are more like civic lichen without greenness, like monks of seepage, like the remaining nerves of a culture that transferred itself from flesh into maintenance and then never entirely died. Each individual may have a life, a preference, a private tenderness for a certain ledge at dawn, a dislike of one kind of copper stain, a memory of being carried as a child under condensation frames in fog. But the basin does not emphasize individuals. It makes identity porous. To live there is to become one careful part of a larger circulation.

At night, when the white glare leaves and the air thickens, they come out more plainly.

They move along the narrow causeways in low processions, not ceremonial exactly, but ordered by need. Their pale bodies bend over trays. Long fingers lift the skins from cisterns and fold them into shallow bowls. One kneels beside a wall and places an ear to the plaster. Another scrapes orange film from a stagnant corner into a cup marked with dots. Two work together to lower a slab gate, their motions slow because a sudden drop would disturb three terraces below. Far off, a row of apertures begins to click softly as someone sends a message through jars in the wall.

Before dawn they retreat again, leaving only corrected water.

A visitor might never meet them. The basin protects them by making all events ambiguous. Was that a figure in the alley or a pale hanging cloth? Did a hand withdraw into the cistern mouth, or did a shadow break when the droplet fell? Did those cups under the ledge rearrange themselves? Did the sluice scrape because of pressure, or because someone unseen decided it was time?

They prefer it that way. Visibility is wasteful. To be seen is to interrupt work.

And yet the whole place is their body in another sense. The bright terraces are their exposed skin. The hidden shafts are their throats. The cisterns are stomachs, lungs, wombs, archives. The central hollow is not a heart, because it does not pump; it receives. Perhaps it is an eye, silver and upward-looking, watching the sky through the evidence of every drop.

What lives there is a people who have become nearly indistinguishable from stewardship.

They do not grow crops, raise animals, build monuments, wage visible wars, or sing into open air. They keep water from leaving too quickly. They keep memory wet enough not to crack. They keep the sealed mountain sealed. They keep weather returning.

They shine only when wet. They speak only when necessary. They count by filling. They mourn by overflow. They are born into catchments and given to niches when finished.

They are not absent.

They are simply living at the speed of condensation.
