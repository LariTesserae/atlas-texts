What lives there is a civic species of weathered patience: people, animals, plants, machines, minerals, fungi, and half-born landforms all crowded into one metabolism.

The human inhabitants are not settlers in the triumphant sense. They are maintainers, negotiators, interpreters of a place that will not stay put. They live with tools close to hand and weather in their bones. Their clothes are layered and practical: felt coats stiff with damp, rubber boots patched at the calf, wool cuffs darkened by grease, oilcloth hoods, leather gloves rubbed with tallow so they do not freeze to railings. Many carry chalk, wire, a folding probe, tokens, ration cards, little maps printed that morning and already suspect. They know how to test a paving stone before stepping on it. They know which moss means “safe,” which means “sinkhole,” which means “do not breathe too deeply here.” Children learn street names alongside soil moods.

They are not loud people. The basin has trained them out of wasteful sound. They bargain with nods and thumb pressure, whistle codes, chalk marks on crate lids, taps on pipework. Even arguments tend to happen close to the body, muttered under awnings, hands tucked into sleeves, eyes moving toward cracks, steam, birds, the behavior of dogs. They have the watchfulness of dockworkers, miners, gardeners, and surgeons. They do not trust surfaces, but they are not fearful. Fear would be too exhausting. Instead they possess an intimate caution, a civic reflex.

Their trades have adapted to a world where geology is not background but event. There are ordinary masons, but also threshold fitters who rehang doors each week because the walls have climbed or sunk. There are rootwrights who breed anchoring trees for brickwork. There are crack listeners who lie with one ear to the street and diagnose upward flows by tone. There are municipal botanists in soot-stained aprons, cultivating mosses that eat smoke and vines that warm glasshouses. There are morning lifters, crews licensed to open hinged paving slabs and harvest whatever the dark has produced overnight: basalt knuckles, ceramic worms, shale fans, mineral teeth.

Cartographers are essential and slightly pitied. They work in shifts, issuing maps like newspapers, knowing each edition is already becoming false. Surveyors are treated with the respect given elsewhere to pilots or midwives, because they walk first into districts that may not yet have decided whether they are streets, marshes, factories, or hills. Engineers here are less like conquerors than animal handlers. They collar newborn rises with iron hoops, coax water sideways into reedbeds, lash riverbanks with timber and cable, install pumps not to defeat the ground but to delay its next opinion.

The poor live close to warmth: near conduit lines, under boiler vents, beside bakeries, around fungal vats, in terraces sunk halfway into sedge where the second floor has become the front door. The better-off live not higher—there is not much higher to live—but more securely braced, with adjustable foundations, private cisterns, licensed roof gardens, trained dogs, and walls threaded with approved rootwork. Status is measured partly in dryness, partly in predictability, partly in how recently your building has had to be renamed.

No one wastes space. A windowsill grows herbs. A tram roof carries seedlings. A courtyard becomes a rabbit pen, then a reed filter, then a temporary market, then a clay vent field, then cabbage beds. Furniture is hinged, stacked, suspended. Beds fold into walls. Ladders are more common than parlors. A respectable household owns buckets, pulleys, tarps, fungus knives, a coal ration, a rain ledger, and at least one charm or instrument meant to persuade the ground to behave.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is practical and distributed. There are shrines built around pipes because the pipes were there first and could not be moved. There are saints of drainage, patron figures of foundations, household offerings left in cracks not out of superstition alone but because some cracks close more gently when fed. Prayer is a kind of maintenance. Maintenance is a kind of prayer. The difference is mostly municipal.

The dead are troublesome. Ordinary burial is unreliable; the ground may warm, shift, remember, reject, or transport. Cemeteries are slate-paved and watched. Families visit with flowers, oil, and weights. Some dead are cremated in civic furnaces, some sealed in ceramic grown from dawn-harvested worms, some interred in cold vaults braided with roots instructed not to seek heat. There are stories—half denied, half planned around—of coffins turning slowly underground toward boiler districts, of names appearing in damp plaster, of old footprints resurfacing after rain.

The children are quick, narrow, and competent. They move along pipes and walkways with the confidence of cats. They collect brass pigeon rings, moth scales, mineral beads, discarded tram tickets, small fossils from walls that were brick last week. They play games involving maps no adult trusts. They know how to freeze still when a street begins humming. School lessons include sums, reading, civic ordinances, plant identification, sinkhole signs, and the ethics of not following an unmapped extension alone. Their playgrounds are fenced not to keep them in, exactly, but to keep new ground out until inspected.

The animals live there not as decoration but as workers, citizens, scavengers, prophets.

The dogs are among the most respected. Shaggy, low, broad-pawed, bred for cold mud and patience, they can smell hollows before the cobbles sag. A good sinkhole dog may save a tram crew, a market, a wedding procession. They wear bells, brass tags, sometimes municipal colors. People make room for them in crowds.

The rats are disciplined and nearly institutional. Silver-backed, heavy, intelligent in a collective way, they move along warm conduits like maintenance crews on another shift. People dislike them but also read them. If rats abandon a wall, the wall is suspect. If they gather quietly near a pipe, heat or food or failure is coming. There are rat-catchers, but there are also rat-observers, and the two professions argue bitterly.

The foxes belong to the tram depots and warehouse roofs. They slip through steam and cable nests, red-gray and narrow-faced, taking pigeons, beetles, scraps of eel pie. They den under machinery that has not moved in years but remains warm. Workers leave them bones when winter is hard, then curse them when they chew signal wires.

The birds are everywhere. Corvids own the crane arms, clicking and croaking above the fog, black punctuation against the pale sky. Gulls patrol the sluices and slaughterhouse roofs. Small birds live in alder crowns, reed thickets, glasshouses, and the warm hollows of engine-house trees. Pigeons are official messengers, legs ringed in brass, carrying inventory slips between towers when wires fail or maps disagree. Certain birds are protected not out of tenderness but because without them beetles would eat the city’s poles from within.

The insects are cold, glossy, persistent. They bead under bridges, sleep in cracks, drill slag, pollinate roof orchards, aerate cabbage heaps. Some are pests until the hour they become necessary. Some are necessary until they breed too well and become a civic emergency.

The less classifiable creatures are not mythical to the inhabitants; they are inconvenient facts. Translucent frogs pulse blue in filtration chambers, singing silently in patterns that technicians record but do not understand. Mineral worms grow between rails and must be harvested at dawn, before they harden in place and derail the morning tram. Root-antlered animals graze fresh concrete in the eastern quarter and leave behind patches of antiquity, aging pavement by centuries. People argue whether they are beasts, geological symptoms, municipal property, or trespassers.

The plants are citizens too, though quieter and more aggressive.

Moss is the city’s skin. It covers brick, filters smoke, signals subsidence, insulates walls, absorbs soot, falls in damp sheets when overloaded. People harvest it for felt, fuel, packing, bedding, poultices. Children are warned not to sleep too near the fast-growing varieties, which may decide a coat is masonry.

Ferns and lichens occupy cracks with the tenacity of squatters. Lichens drink metals and bloom in toxic colors: copper, yellow, black-green, gray-white like old bone. Their patterns are read by workers the way sailors read cloud. Vines trained along steam pipes bear translucent fruit, faintly warm inside, eaten in winter markets with salt or fermented into a sharp municipal liquor.

Roof trees are engineered but never entirely obedient. Dwarf willow, alder, crowberry, soot-leaved shrubs, dark broad leaves rimmed with frost: all root through brick, around pipe, into mortar, into boiler warmth. Municipal botanists speak of “persuading” roots, never commanding them. A good root system can keep a tenement standing through three ground swells. A bad one can pull a bathhouse into a new cellar.

Fungi perform much of the hidden labor. Fungal looms turn textile waste into building felt. Vats digest pulp, leather scraps, reed mash, spoiled grain. Some fungi are food; some are insulation; some are prohibited because they remember shapes too well and begin reproducing rooms, handles, sleeping faces. In the markets, mushroom sellers are as closely regulated as pharmacists.

And then there are the machines.

The machines live there in a subdued, animal way. They thud behind walls, sigh under floors, breathe steam through vents, tick as they cool. They are not sleek. They are patched, riveted, damp, insulated with felt and moss, rooted around by vines, nested in by birds. Cranes move like patient herons over the roofs. Trams complain through warped rails. Pumps cough through the night. Pneumatic tubes spit damp messages. Generators whine at the edge of hearing like insects trapped in glass.

The inhabitants treat machines as powerful domestic beasts: feed them, warm them, listen to them, do not startle them, do not ignore a new sound. Some machines have been built around trees. Some trees have grown around machines. Some old engines are no longer used but remain connected because disconnecting them caused bad weather once, or subsidence, or a week of black water in the wells.

Below all this lives the basin itself.

It is not alive exactly, not in a way that can be named without error. But it behaves. It produces. It reacts. It swells, extrudes, accepts impressions, grows corners, breathes clay, births stone, ages pavement, extends streets, reclassifies rooms. It is young ground, undecided ground, ground still rehearsing its forms. The people live on it the way others might live beside an unpredictable sea.

They do not love it sentimentally. They curse it daily. It ruins thresholds, swallows survey poles, floods cellars, makes maps liars, moves the dead, breeds regulations, breeds work. But they also depend on it. It gives ceramic worms, fertile clay, mineral water, strange fuels, useful mosses, new land, warnings, harvests, employment, identity. Their city is not built against the basin so much as built in conversation with its appetite.

So what lives there is not one race or monster, not a single dominant civilization, but a dense, cold, industrious compact:

people who have learned to be provisional without being fragile;  
animals folded into infrastructure;  
plants bred into masonry and rebellion;  
fungi that manufacture shelter from waste;  
machines that breathe like livestock;  
minerals still being born;  
streets with unreliable intentions;  
a city that is less inhabited than continuously negotiated.

Everyone and everything there is useful, used, using, or becoming useful. Nothing remains separate for long. A wall becomes a cliff, a roof becomes a thicket, a pipe becomes a shrine, a market becomes a marsh, a dog becomes an early-warning instrument, a moss patch becomes law. The inhabitants are those who can endure that intimacy without demanding final forms.

They live there because they can keep working while the world changes underfoot.
