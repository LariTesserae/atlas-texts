What lives there is not one creature, or even one kingdom of creatures, but a whole highland society of bodies that build by living.

It is inhabited by makers without plans: insects, birds, mammals, plants, fungi, mosses, roots, and all the smaller eaters and cleaners that move among them. None of them owns the place completely. Together they are the place.

The oldest and most numerous inhabitants are the small colonial builders hidden in the walls. Termites live in the warm cores, in russet sheaths and clay partitions, in towers buried inside older towers. They travel through covered runnels, blind or nearly blind, reading dampness and scent. They repair cracks as soon as rain opens them. They farm fungus in sealed chambers. They chew dead wood, leaf fiber, dung, and old nest material, turning collapse into structure. Much of the mountain’s inner stability belongs to them.

Ants hold the roads. Their colonies run like nervous systems through the constructed highland: black, red, amber, minute, soldier-headed, leaf-cutting, seed-harvesting, stinging, scavenging. They occupy seams, hollow stems, abandoned combs, knots of root, galleries beneath bark. Some tend scale insects on shadow leaves. Some raid the broods of others. Some clean carcasses to shining bone before fungus can take them. Their paths are older than many branches, roofed with soil pellets and protected from rain, crossing beams, ledges, and vertical walls as confidently as rivers cross plains.

Bees and wasps live wherever warmth gathers and an opening can be defended. Bees fill cracks with wax-dark honeycomb, resin, pollen, and larvae. Their chambers smell sweet, hot, and animal. Stingless bees seal small entrances with propolis and hover in wavering clouds around them. Larger bees boom through the dimness like thrown pebbles. Paper wasps fasten grey-brown cities under protected eaves. Mud daubers and potter wasps plaster cells beneath overhangs, stocking them with stunned prey. Hornets own some of the outer hollows, and the air near them has a tense, metallic authority.

Birds possess the margins of light. Weaverbirds hang their clustered nests from flexible branch-ends and outer awnings, hundreds together, their entrances facing away from snakes and rain. Parakeets and fruit pigeons arrive shrieking at the fruiting trees, scattering rinds and seeds that will later root in walls. Mountain crows and other clever dark birds patrol the exposed ridges, prying grubs from old thatch and stealing eggs when they can. Swifts and swallows stitch the open ravines, vanishing under eaves into mud cups glued to cliff-like faces of animal construction. Nightjars lie flat on dry ledges, indistinguishable from bark and fiber until their eyes open.

There are bats by the thousands in the ceiling shadows. Some hang in tight leather clusters from dry chambers, waking at dusk to pour through narrow slots into the valley air. Fruit bats roost in the higher woven courts, wrapped in their wings, smelling of musk and fermenting figs. Small insect bats sleep in cracks behind sheets of root and clay. At night they become part of the sound of the place: clicks, dry wingbeats, faint quarrels, the quick absence of moths.

Monkeys and other climbing mammals move through the upper galleries. They know the strong branches, the safe vine-bridges, the roofs that will hold weight and those that will not. They bark across gaps, cough alarm at snakes and cats, groom in warm pockets of filtered light, and sleep on old platforms built atop older platforms. Their hands polish routes into roots and clay. They drop fruit, dung, hair, seeds, and broken twigs; without meaning to, they plant and pave.

Smaller mammals fill the middle and lower passages. Rodents tunnel through old thatch and guano soil, nesting in dry cavities lined with stolen feathers. Shrews and insectivores hunt under loose flakes of bark, listening for larvae in walls. Civet-like night hunters pass silently along beams, pausing at bee hollows and bird colonies. Little hoofed animals, no larger than dogs or hares perhaps, browse in dim gardens growing from compost pockets. They pick fern tips, fallen fruit, fungi, and the soft leaves sprouting in dung-rich seams. Their hooves drum in hollow corridors, and their droppings become future masonry.

Reptiles take the heat where they can find it. Geckos cling to vertical surfaces and click in the night, hunting moths near fungal glows and pale flowers. Skinks slide through leaf litter gathered in gutters. Tree snakes lie along warm seams in the clay, waiting near bird routes, frog cups, or rodent holes. Larger constrictors may occupy the older, steadier platforms, living as patient shadows under woven roofs. Lizards bask on rare sunlit balconies, flattening themselves to drink every shard of warmth before the canopy closes again.

Amphibians live in the gathered water. Frogs sit in bromeliad tanks, split seedpods, hoofprints hardened into clay, root bowls, cistern chambers, and forgotten bird baths lined with algae. Some are bright as poisonous flowers; others are the color of wet bark. They call from droplets and cups, each species keeping to a height, a season, a kind of container. Their eggs cling to leaves over water pockets or float in films of rain caught in living basins. Salamanders or newt-like creatures may move deeper in the damp seams, pale-bellied and secretive.

Beetles are everywhere: under shells of bark, in dung, in flowers, in carrion, in termite refuse, in old bee comb, in fungal gardens, in the softening hearts of beams. Dung beetles roll pellets through miniature highways. Longhorn larvae tunnel through deadwood that has already become wall. Scarabs shine briefly in sun gaps like dropped jewels. Rove beetles hunt in ant trails. Click beetles, weevils, bark beetles, fungus beetles, and carrion beetles divide the decaying architecture into edible provinces.

Spiders bind the place. Orb weavers hang nets across corridors where gnats drift in moist air. Funnel spiders occupy cracks between clay and root. Jumping spiders hunt on sunlit ledges and bird nests. Sheet webs lie under eaves, sparkling with dawn condensation. Some spiders live among ants by mimicry or stealth. Others wait beside frog pools for midges. Their silk is not decoration; it is stitching, warning, trap, nursery, and weather-catcher.

Moths, butterflies, flies, mosquitoes, midges, lacewings, mantises, crickets, katydids, cicadas, cockroaches, millipedes, centipedes, worms, mites, springtails, and nematodes form the living grain of the place. They are the background metabolism. They chew, bore, sing, pierce, pollinate, parasitize, clean, soften, and aerate. Cicadas are hidden engines in trunks. Crickets and katydids take over the dark with saw and rasp. Mosquitoes rise from tiny cisterns. Midges halo every damp hollow. Centipedes run the underside routes like jointed knives.

The plants live there too, but not as passive scenery. Figs are among the great powers. Their roots enter cracks and widen them, then become pillars, ladders, gutters, walls. Strangler growths swallow old trunks and abandoned nests alike, converting weakness into scaffold. Lianas hang as ropes, bridges, curtains, and snares. Ferns spill from shaded pockets. Mosses hold moisture on buttresses. Orchids and bromeliads colonize the brighter exteriors, offering flowers, water, nectar, and nurseries. Algae line cisterns. Fungi lace through compost layers, deadwood, dung, abandoned brood chambers, and the inner dark, digesting the old city into food for the next one.

Fungi may be as important as animals here. They glow faintly under some overhangs. They rot beams just enough for beetles to enter. They bind soil. They feed termites. They bloom after wet weeks in shelves, cups, veils, threads, and soft knobs. Some are eaten by mammals; some poison the careless; some live inside leaves and roots, helping the whole damp architecture hold together.

The inhabitants do not merely occupy shelters. Their bodies are the tools by which shelter exists.

Birds weave roofs. Termites plaster walls. Ants maintain roads. Bees seal chambers. Roots brace terraces. Moss pads and preserves shade. Beetles hollow beams. Mammals compact paths. Frogs claim the water stores. Snakes claim the warmth. Fungi turn death into adhesive, soil, and bloom. Every species inherits the unfinished work of others.

There are predators, but no sovereign predator. A snake rules one ledge until a hawk finds it exposed. Ants overrun a nest until rain breaks their bridge. A civet empties a bird colony’s lower chambers until hornets drive it off. Monkeys scatter at the cry of a raptor. Frogs vanish under gecko tongues. Parasitoid wasps lay eggs in caterpillars feeding on leaves rooted in compost made from old wasp paper. Authority is local, temporary, and constantly revised.

The true resident, if one must name a single one, is the accumulated colony of colonies: a mountain-skinned superhabitat, warm and dark, made from instinct repeated for centuries. It is a city without citizens in the human sense, yet full of districts, routes, nurseries, refuse heaps, granaries, cisterns, hunting grounds, temples of sound, and inherited laws.

What lives there is life that has forgotten the difference between dwelling and body.

It is a warm-blooded forest without one bloodstream, an insect-built mountain with feathers for shingles, roots for bones, clay for skin, fungus for memory, and animals for weather.
