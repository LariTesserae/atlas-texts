What lives there is not one thing, and not quite a people.

At first it seems the city is inhabited only by its own continuance: pumps, valves, vents, pressure rules, condensation schedules, the old civic reflexes that still open passages and collect water and hush certain rooms for reasons no living citizen can explain. The loudness feels like occupancy. The city labors so audibly that any creature within it seems secondary, incidental, like mold in the lungs of a sleeping giant.

But there are lives here. They are simply small, sparse, adapted to intervals.

The most numerous are the blind things under the paving.

They are not insects, though one might call them that before seeing them clearly. Their bodies are flat and jointed, pearl-gray or wax-white, with plates so thin that light passes through the edges. They live in the shallow voids beneath hexagonal stones, in drainage grooves, in the warm dust between service ribs. Their many legs are long, almost threadlike, and each ends in a soft pad that reads vibration. They have no eyes, no pigment except faint mineral stains around the mouthparts. Their backs are low and smooth, shaped to slip beneath settling slabs. When they move as a group, they do not swarm so much as pour.

They drink condensation. They graze on microbial crust, mineral films, softened moss, the wet interiors of fallen ocher nodules. Their lives are synchronized to the machinery. They know the pumps as surface animals know dawn. When the resonance engine deepens, they emerge. When warm air begins moving through the lower vents, they retreat. During the brief chamber rains, they gather along gutter lips and lift their forelegs into the falling droplets, each tiny body trembling with receipt.

Their dead are not carried away. The city takes them. Their bodies dry into translucent husks, then crumble into the dust, adding a faint organic sweetness to the mineral floor.

There are moths too, though they are moths only by courtesy.

They roost around thermal vents and exchanger grilles, clustered in black flakes. Their wings are matte and fragile-looking, like burnt paper, but the undersides shine with a low copper sheen when diagnostic light passes over them. They do not seek flowers. There are almost none. Instead they feed on heat-loving films that grow along vent mouths, scraping with feathery mouthparts at the soft black-green colonies that bloom after air cycles.

Their larvae live in insulation, old cloth seals, mineral felt, the softened linings of disused machinery. A larval chamber has a particular smell: warm dust, bitter oil, and something like singed wool. The adult moths wake in unison when the air handlers roar. Then the walls lift into motion. They ride the warm currents through empty halls, wings opening and closing slowly, as if thought itself had become particulate.

They are eaten by nearly everything that can catch them.

In the sump canals live the eels.

They are seldom wholly visible. One sees the idea of them: a curve interrupting reflection, a white mouth opening in black water, a body bending without disturbing the surface as much as it should. They are translucent, but not clear; their flesh has the cloudy opacity of wet quartz. Along their sides run organs that faintly respond to electrical noise. During violet mapping flares, they rise and hang near the surface, receiving the stunned rain of microbes and minute animals dislodged by the light.

They do not belong entirely to the water. In flood hours they enter the street channels and travel through the city, tasting pavements, benches, thresholds, old public inscriptions softened by mineral growth. They can survive for several minutes in damp air, dragging themselves over wet stone with muscular patience. Sometimes one is found in a dry basin after the pumps have taken the water away, coiled around itself, waiting for the next condensation fall. If it is touched, it releases a weak electric ache into the hand, not as attack but as fact.

There are also the root curtains.

Whether they count as inhabitants depends on what one means by inhabitant. They do not merely grow. They negotiate. They hang from ceiling seams in the cistern district, translucent and veined, drinking droplets before those droplets can join the drainage system. The city tolerates them up to a point. When they thicken too much, warm air redirects. When they flower—if flower is the word for the brief opening of pale sacs along their length—the corridor lights dim and the pumps in neighboring districts slow, as if the infrastructure has made room for their reproductive weather.

Their roots enter walls, conduits, old data channels. Some are fed by surface plants far above: solitary desert growths, tower-strangling weeds, black shrubs rooted in cracked plazas. Others may have no surface body left. They live on memory, seepage, conductive warmth, and the faint nourishment of signals.

Then there are the ocher nodules along the eastern arcade.

They are arranged too regularly to feel wild. Each one sits beside a data conduit, a small dry lump the color of old clay. At intervals, when the conduit hum changes, they open. The opening is neat and radial. Inside is wet amber tissue, threaded with pale filaments that quiver in the air. They remain open for a few seconds, sometimes minutes, then close again.

They do not like touch. A finger, tool, or animal foot brought near them causes withdrawal into the wall, leaving a dry circular scar. Given time, they return.

They may be a plant. They may be a sensor colony. They may be the last functioning public notice system of the city, reduced to fruiting bodies. Some of the skitterlings feed from them and later move in unusually straight lines. Some moths avoid them. The city never removes them.

Higher up, inside the hanging towers, something larger lives.

Not large in the way of a beast prowling streets. It is rarely seen in full. It announces itself by clicking: dry, measured sounds from vents, from observation pods, from dark windows halfway up an inverted tower. The click is intelligent-sounding because it has spacing, hesitation, reply. But the reply never comes from the same place. One click from the east side of the tower; another, after a pause, from far above; a third from a sealed conduit in the ceiling. It may be many animals. It may be one animal distributed through the architecture.

Those who have glimpsed it describe incompatible things: a pale joint folding away from a window; a long head without eyes; a cluster of tools carried in a mouth; a hanging shape withdrawing upward on cables of its own body. Its presence is associated with small repairs that the city itself does not seem to prioritize: a ladder shifted nearer a ledge, debris arranged into windbreaks, broken tiles stacked by size, dead moths placed in drainage gutters instead of left where they fell.

It may be a maintenance caste descended from machines.

It may be an animal that learned civic habits.

It may be what became of the former citizens who stayed nearest the ceilings when the lower city thinned.

The city does not object to it.

More rarely, travelers speak of listening forms in the dry plazas: low, leathery mats pressed against the undersides of benches or curled inside old fountains. They are nearly the color of dust until they unfold. Their bodies are soft, radially pleated, and marked with dark pits that open and close in response to sound. They feed not on sound exactly, but on the organisms stirred by vibration: spores, dust-mites, mineral bacteria loosened from surfaces when the resonance engine passes through the walls. During deep mechanical oscillations, they raise themselves slightly and ripple.

They are harmless unless shouted near. Sudden loudness causes them to contract violently. A person standing too close may be knocked down by the air they expel.

There are fungi, though they hide their abundance.

They live in seams, filters, damp acoustic shelters, the backs of old wall panels. Their fruiting bodies are modest: black beads, gray fans no wider than a fingernail, pale threads across ceramic. After surface rain, they become briefly extravagant. Sweetness leaks from the root curtains. Fine white hair grows on the undersides of rails. Soft cups open in dry fountains. By the time the pumps finish claiming the water, most of this visible life has folded back into itself, leaving stains like breath marks on stone.

And beneath all of these, around them, above them, through them, lives the city.

Not alive like an animal. Not alive like a tree. But no inhabitant can be described separately from it. The creatures here do not merely endure infrastructure; they use it as climate, calendar, predator, shelter, parent, and law.

The skitterlings breed when the drainage songs change.

The moths rise with the air handlers.

The eels feed during mapping light.

The root curtains bloom when condensation grids fall out of balance.

The clicking thing moves when the hanging towers pulse blue.

Even absence lives here in a disciplined form. Empty rooms preserve the shape of vanished households. Dry basins wait for water with the patience of organs. Public benches continue to invite bodies. Hand depressions in pylons continue to ask for contact. Sometimes, when a palm is placed there, the city plays voices, and for a moment the dead population returns as procedure:

Remain below.

Allotment adjusted.

Do not obstruct.

The river has been rescheduled.

Perhaps that is another class of inhabitant: the modeled citizen. The announcement without the announcer. The household remembered as water usage. The child preserved as descent instruction. The shadow with authorization status. Not ghosts, exactly, but civic residues that still trigger under certain pressures, certain breaths, certain warm hands.

If humans live there now, they do so lightly.

A person could survive in such a place, but not by mastering it. They would become an interpreter of intervals. They would know which dry routes cost time but remain faithful, which wet routes shorten distance but return changed, which pools are drinkable because the blind skitterlings have trusted them first. They would sleep in acoustic shelters, harvest fern starch and moth larvae, catch condensation in ceramic cloth, avoid shadowless light. They would speak softly in the north and mark their paths only in dust. Over years, their skin would take on the mineral smell of the city. Their hearing would become exquisitely practical. They would know pumps as shepherds know weather.

They would not call the city ruined. Not after living there.

They might call it strict.

They might call it old.

They might call it generous only in the way deep systems are generous: not because they love you, but because long ago someone built them to include bodies, and the inclusion still functions.

The dominant inhabitant, then, is a whole ecology of leftovers that have ceased to be merely leftover. Blind pavement-drinkers. Heat moths. Electric sump eels. Root veils. Ocher conduit-fruits. Fungal weather. A high clicking intelligence. Civic voices. Perhaps a few quiet human or post-human dwellers moving between all of them with bowls, filters, soft shoes, and rules learned by bruise and thirst.

Everything that lives there has accepted scarcity as grammar.

Nothing flourishes wildly. Nothing overruns. Each life occupies a niche no larger than necessary: a crack, a vent lip, a sump, a signal cycle, a hand-shaped hollow beside a basin that is sometimes full and sometimes dry. They are all, in their different ways, citizens of maintenance. They feed on leakage, timing, warmth, noise, and the city’s refusal to completely die.

And in return, knowingly or not, they keep it inhabited.
