What lives there is not one species, not cleanly.

It is tempting, from the surface, to call the inhabitants a people. They have shelters, tools, paths, rules, children, caches, songs perhaps. They tie markers to stalks. They know which flower-cups can be trusted and which warmth is a mouth. They cross the white spans when the ceiling light is steady. They wait out growth-surges under root-buttresses. They leave offerings or warnings, depending on how one chooses to read a loop of cord, a polished seed, a shard of black glass tucked into a living fork.

But whoever lives there has had to become less like settlers and more like a weather pattern that remembers itself.

They are slight, mostly. Not frail, but narrow and economical, made for conserving warmth and moving over yielding ground. Long feet, or broad foot-bindings, spread their weight across the plant-mats. Their steps are placed with the thoughtfulness of herons. Even the children learn early not to run unless something is already closing behind them. Their bodies carry the cold in their shapes: compact torsos wrapped in layered plant-hide, high shoulders, hands kept gloved except when reading stems, faces shielded with masks of woven fiber, bone, and translucent leaf.

Their skin, where visible, tends toward the colors of the place after long generations: ash-brown, gray-olive, wax-pale, violet in the lips and knuckles when the air is hard. Some have large dark eyes, not because the undercontinent is dark, but because its light is unstable. Their pupils contract to pinpricks under ceiling flare and widen quickly when the world falls blue. Many wear slitted visors made from dried membrane or polished shell to protect against the painful glare of a full bloom-vault.

Their hair is often braided tight and sealed with resin, not for ornament alone but to keep it from catching on hooks, burrs, filament curtains, and curious tendrils. Loose hair is for enclosed shelters, mourning, or rites in which one intentionally lets the land touch what is usually guarded.

They smell faintly of smoke they rarely use, resin, old frost, and the bitter oils rubbed into their clothing to discourage rooting.

They are not numerous. Nothing in that hollow favors multitudes. A band may be twelve people, or twenty, or five families braided by marriage, oath, and mutual rescue. Larger gatherings happen only in stable districts, under old arches whose behavior has been known for generations. Even then, a “village” is less a permanent settlement than a recurring negotiation with a favorable piece of vegetation.

Their homes are nests of permission.

A dwelling begins by finding a plant that will tolerate interruption: a root-buttress slow to seal wounds, a ribbed stem that thickens without engulfing, a hedge responsive to trimming but not angered by it. The builders do not clear a site. Clearing invites response. Instead they persuade space open. They prop leaves rather than cut them. They bind young shoots into rafters while they are flexible. They teach pod-vessels to swell in rows. They train curtain-vines to shed condensation into basins. A good house is not dead material arranged against the world; it is living material convinced, continually, to remain useful.

This makes their architecture seasonal in a strange way. Not summer and winter—those words mean little beneath the crust—but pulse and lull, flare and dim, wet event and dry event, surge and sleep. A wall may thicken during bright weeks and have to be shaved thin again. A doorway may pucker and narrow if no one passes through it daily. A sleeping platform may lift slowly, becoming first inconvenient, then sacred, then inaccessible. When a house chooses upwardness, families sometimes follow it for a while by adding ladders. More often they remove their goods and let the structure become plant again.

They do not own land as surface people might. They hold routes, intervals, names, permissions, debts to shelters, memories of safe timings. A family may say, “We have the third hollow after the blue rain for six dimmings,” or, “Our grandmother taught the white mat to let us cross.” Their inheritance is partly botanical etiquette.

Their maps are sung, tapped, knotted, and embodied.

A flat map lies. The ground does not remain where ink puts it. So they carry maps in cords with knots for delays, hard beads for stone exposures, soft beads for mats, hollow beads for spans over void. Children learn routes as sequences of actions: duck under the sour vine, strike the sleeping stem twice, wait for the answering knock, cross only after the frost-ferns turn their pale sides. A journey is not “north by east for seven miles.” It is “from the old ladder-flower, follow the dry ticking until the air tastes of pear, then descend before the third flare.”

Some elders are valued because they remember what the plants remember. They can touch a hardened path and know whether it has been walked recently, welcomed, or merely endured. They can tell from the tension in a bridge whether it will hold a body, a group, or no one after the next ceiling pulse. They can smell a growth-surge before the younger hunters hear the silence that precedes it.

They are not masters of the place. Their wisdom is mostly humility organized into habit.

They eat carefully.

Food is abundant only to the ignorant. Most of the green world is indigestible, poisonous, narcotic, adhesive, parasitic, or simply too costly to harvest. The people know edible timings rather than edible species. A pod safe before frost may sour afterward. A translucent root may nourish when cut from a shaded side and sicken when taken from a vein facing ceiling-glow. Pale grazers are trapped in breathing holes with loops of cord and thorn triggers, but never too many from one district, because their nibbling keeps certain mats from thickening dangerously. Blind gliders are caught in high nets during thermal weather, dried in cold air, and powdered with bitter lichen.

They cultivate, but not in fields.

They cultivate tendencies. They encourage cup-plants near camps. They seed tough cord-vines along known routes. They feed ash to hearth-stones and let rootlets turn old fire into mineral sweetness. They train bowl-pods. They maintain “quiet patches” where sleeping is possible because the ground has been taught, by repeated pressure, oils, ash, and body heat, not to fold suddenly. Their gardens look wild to strangers. To them, a garden is a set of living agreements distributed through miles of undercontinent.

Children learn the dangerous blooms like surface children learn fire.

Do not crawl into warmth unless an adult has struck the outer petals and listened.

Do not sleep in sweetness.

Do not laugh into hollow stems during spore-weather.

Do not pull a filament that hums back.

Do not step on a mat that has stopped collecting dew.

And above all, do not believe emptiness is empty. The great bright spaces between towers, bridges, and membranes feel vacant, but vacancy there is often waiting. A smooth white plain may be the exposed back of a single slow organism. A silent arch may be measuring vibration. A curtain may be drifting not with wind but toward salt, warmth, breath.

Their dead are difficult.

Burial in soil is impossible where soil is rare, and leaving bodies exposed invites incorporation too quickly, too impersonally. Some groups place their dead in seedpods high above the floor, sealed with resin, letting them dry in cold brightness until only bone and memory remain. Others give bodies to particular digesting mats with ceremony, believing that to be taken by a known plant is better than to be swallowed by accident. There are ancestor-groves in sheltered places: pale trunks swollen around bones, masks, tools, and hair-cords, each tree carrying a family line not as metaphor but as nutrient, shape, and sometimes faint bioluminescent pattern.

An ancestor may be visited as a tree whose leaves brighten when touched by descendants. Whether this is chemistry, belief, or something more intimate is not a distinction they insist upon.

They are quiet people, but not silent.

Sound travels strangely through the undercontinent. A shout can vanish into open air, while a tap on the right stem can travel for miles. Their music is percussive and careful: knuckle-clicks on pod-shell, bone rods against hollow vine, breath through split reeds, low throat tones meant to vibrate in the chest more than fill the air. Loud music is reserved for places where they want response: pollination festivals, bridge-wakings, mourning, warning. Some songs are deliberately incomplete unless the plants answer with knocks, creaks, or falling seeds.

They speak softly in ordinary life, with many signs made by hand, shoulder, and lamp-cover. A lifted palm may mean wind-change. Two fingers drawn across the throat may mean a closing leaf nearby, not death but possible death. Touch has grammar: a warning squeeze, a permission tap, a stay-still pressure at the back of a child’s neck.

Their myths are not of creation from nothing, but of opening.

They say the world above is the shell. They say the hollow breathed once through a wound, and the first plants entered as light. They say stone is what has not yet learned to bend. They say fire is a hungry surface-sun and must be kept small so it does not call its lost sky. They say the ceiling blooms are not above them but watching in the direction of above.

Some groups believe the undercontinent is still becoming an organism, and that all who live there are temporary organs: people as nerves, grazers as teeth, gliders as thoughts, vents as fevers, luminous mats as eyes. Others believe the plants are many nations in endless negotiation, and humanlike people survive by diplomacy. These two beliefs often coexist. It matters less what the whole place is than whether today’s bridge will accept weight.

Their clothing is ingenious and constantly repaired.

Outer garments are made from layered leaf-hide cured in cold resin, flexible when warmed by the body, stiff at the edges. Cloaks are pale outside to reflect ceiling glare, dark inside to hold heat. Many wear mantles strung with dried seed-shells and small bone chimes, not decorative merely: the faint clicking announces motion to certain plants that dislike sudden contact. In thorn-fog districts they wrap themselves in smooth membrane sheets so hooks slide away. In dry papery regions they wear spark-guards, because even static from friction can wake smolder in old stems.

Their masks are among their most personal possessions. A mask filters spores, dims glare, holds warmth over the mouth, and displays lineage. Some are plain slitted ovals of polished pod. Others have antler-like feelers imitating the great browsing beasts, not as disguise but as a request: let me pass as one who feeds lightly. Children receive temporary masks that are expected to root, crack, or be outgrown. An adult mask may last decades, repaired with resin, shell, and ancestor-fiber.

They fear warmth as much as cold.

Cold is ordinary hardship. Warmth is event, invitation, danger. A warm hollow may save a freezing traveler, but it may also be the throat of a flower. A geothermal vent may nourish a whole camp for a season, then trigger such violent growth that the camp is lifted, split, and consumed by its own walls. Lovers seek warmth, the sick require it, infants must be kept within it, and so warmth is always surrounded by rules. No sleeping alone near a vent. No sealed shelter heated through a full ceiling flare. No bringing untested seeds inside a body-warmed dwelling.

Their relationship with animals is sparse and respectful.

The pale grazers are tricksters in children’s tales, vanishing into holes just before the land folds. Blind gliders are messengers or thieves, depending on whether they foul the nets or lead hunters to thermal columns. The great slow browsers are treated almost as moving shrines. When one passes, people stop work. Its pollen-crusted hide carries districts in its fur. Seeds sprout in its wake. Its wide hooves press temporary roads into the mats. Some bands follow such a beast for days, harvesting what its passage reveals: crushed safe tubers, opened water-cups, exposed mineral shards.

Predators, when they are animals, are spoken of obliquely. But the true predator is usually mistaken shelter. The undercontinent kills most often by hospitality.

There are other inhabitants too, less human in their arrangements.

There are the roof-colonies: pale, long-limbed climbers who live almost entirely in the hanging ceiling forests, if the stories are true. Ground people find their dropped tools embedded in fallen ice—hooks made from black spine, nets woven finer than hair, little carved weights shaped like closed leaves. Sometimes, during dim periods, figures can be seen moving upside down across luminous mats miles overhead, silhouetted against bluish radiance. Whether they are kin, animals, or something the plants have made to tend them is debated.

There are vent-dwellers, broader and darker, who live near warmth and pay for it with shortened lives, fungal lung, and abundant food. They trade dried protein, resin, and heat-cured pod-shell for obsidian, glider membrane, and cold-region medicines. They laugh louder than most and distrust any shelter that does not grow visibly by morning.

There are solitary listeners who travel without permanent band, carrying frames of hollow stems on their backs. They sleep little. They press their ears to roots and record, in knots and scars, the long-distance messages of the plant systems. A listener may arrive at a camp and say, “Do not go to the open white span; it is being remembered from below,” and no one will ask for clearer explanation until after the danger has passed.

And beneath even these peoples are the almost-people: plant arrangements that have learned habits from human use.

A former campsite may grow sleeping hollows before anyone arrives. A path may lift stones aside in anticipation of feet. A ladder rooted in a favorable place may continue producing rung-like branches, though no hand has shaped it for generations. There are groves that mimic shelters, complete with low entrances and dry interiors, some safe, some fatal, some perhaps simply trying to understand the animals that once bent them into usefulness.

So what lives there?

A civilization of caution lives there, if civilization can be made of temporary paths, edible timings, sung maps, and shelters that must be persuaded not to digest their occupants.

Small pale animals live there, quick as thoughts across frost.

Huge uncertain browsers live there, half beast and half wandering orchard.

Blind gliders live in the upper drafts, carrying spores through the luminous weather.

Predatory flowers live there, and digesting mats, and bridges that remember pressure, and towers that release pollen when distant footsteps knock the correct rhythm through the roots.

Communities live there who have never known a true sky, only a ceiling so alive and bright it serves the function of heaven. They do not think of themselves as underground. To them, the surface—if they believe in it—is the hard outer rind of the world, a dead direction beyond stone. Their world has horizons, weather, migrations, ruins, trade, hunger, beauty, and burial. It has too much light and not enough fire. It has no permanent roads, but many ways.

They are not conquerors of the hollow. They are its brief, clever, warm-blooded negotiations.

And the greatest living thing there may be the place itself: not a single mind, not yet, but a vast unfinished botanical intelligence spread through ceiling, floor, bridge, pod, frost, scent, and vibration. It has not decided what people are. It shelters them sometimes, eats them sometimes, learns them slowly. They, in turn, learn how to be small enough to continue.

Beneath the crust of the world, life has not filled the emptiness.

It has taught the emptiness to breathe.
