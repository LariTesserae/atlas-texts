What lives there is not one creature but a maintained assembly of creatures, each of them natural only up to the point where nature would ordinarily begin to improvise.

They are the basin’s inhabitants, but also its moving parts.

Not servants of the hidden engine exactly, and not products of it in the ordinary sense. They have blood, hunger, eggs, fur, shells, gills, tongues, mites in their creases, rain caught in their lashes. They breathe. They feed. They startle sometimes, though never all the way. They sleep, or enter postures close enough to sleep to satisfy whatever law here requires rest. But they do not seem to belong to a lineage that extends beyond the rim. They are not mountain animals that wandered in and adapted. They are what life looks like when it is allowed to occupy a fixed instant forever.

The smallest of them are everywhere first.

In the rain cups live the black newts. They are thumb-long, slick as ink, with bellies transparent enough to show the pale loops within them and the steady bead of a heart working like a tiny pump. Each newt holds itself around a bubble of air, not clutching it, but curved to it, as if the bubble were an egg or a thought. When they move, they do so with astonishing deliberateness. One foot opens. One foot closes. The tail flexes once, and the whole body turns in a clear cup of water that is older than rainfall and younger than yesterday.

They do not leave their cups, though sometimes they are found in different ones. There is no journey between. A newt vanishes from a basin of stone near the north wall and later appears among three others in a pool beside the central outcrop, with the same silver bubble intact under its throat. Their eyes are lidless, black, and slightly faceted, as though they are not looking outward but measuring pressure. When the underground throb passes through the rock, their bodies quiver in exact sympathy.

They breed without increase. Gelatinous strings of eggs appear on moss filaments under shallow water, each egg containing a dark comma. The commas turn. Some hatch. Some do not. The number of newts in the basin remains the same, though counting them produces different answers depending on which pools are included.

Among the moss grains live insects with the manners of instruments.

The bronze beetles are the most visible. They are not brightly colored until water touches them, and water is always touching them. Then their shells bloom into tarnished copper, oil-green, smoked gold. Their legs are fine, jointed, absurdly precise. A beetle will climb a wet stone, pause halfway, turn left by the width of its own antenna, clean one foreleg, proceed, pause again, descend by another route, and begin. Not repeat. Begin.

They feed on lichen without reducing it. Their mandibles scrape microscopic color from the rock, yet the pale blue and sulfur-yellow patches remain unthinned. If one is turned over, its underside is not soft but patterned with pale plates like the diagrams of a watch. It will right itself after three pulses of the engine. Always three.

There are moths too, colder than moths should be. They cling beneath shelves of basalt, wings folded into damp triangles. Their bodies are furred in white and gray, but the fur never mats. When they open their wings, there are markings on them like water rings in a pool: concentric, interrupted, slightly misaligned. They do not fly often. When they do, their flight is brief and almost vertical, a pale flutter between one underside and another, as if they are punctuation marks being moved by an unseen hand.

Their caterpillars are rarely seen, but sometimes a fern frond bears a row of small translucent grazes, each one identical in length. Somewhere nearby, under the curve of a stone, something pale is eating with the patience of a mechanism.

The frog-things are heard more than seen.

They sit deep in the moss, pressed into wet green hollows, their skins the color of unlit pewter. Their throats inflate not into round sacs but into angular little bladders, flat-sided, like tiny bellows. Their calls are bursts of static: short, dry, electrical sounds that do not suit the wetness at all.

Krrt. Krrt-krrt. Krrt.

The pattern is stable for hours. Then, without transition, it changes to another stable pattern. No courtship frenzy follows. No rival answers with heat or urgency. The call seems to exist because the basin requires intermittent signal. If one manages to part the moss and see the frog, it looks back with golden eyes crossed by horizontal black bars. Then it is elsewhere in the same clump, or the moss has closed, or the viewer has forgotten exactly where to look.

The crabs are harder to accept.

They should not be there, and yet they are among the most settled inhabitants of the basin. They move through the moss islands and gravel flats on long, pale legs jointed like measuring tools. Their shells are flat and dark, marked with thin mineral lines: branching white veins, hexagonal patches, little black dots set at regular distances. They resemble diagrams for faults, pressure systems, cutting plans. Their claws are small and dexterous, less like weapons than calipers.

They lift things constantly. Moss fragments, pebbles, beetle husks, feathers, blue berries fallen from wiry shrubs. Each object is raised, turned once or twice, inspected by mouthparts that click softly in the pressure-silence near vents, then placed back almost exactly where it was. Almost. Perhaps that is their labor: not change, but tolerable variance. Keeping the basin from becoming too exact.

They are not aquatic, though they enter water without difficulty. A crab may step into a pool, vanish below a depth too shallow to cover it, and emerge dry-legged from a seam of fern ten paces away, bearing a clean white bone in one claw. The bone will be laid in a gravel hollow with other bones, none of them broken, none misplaced.

The birds occupy the walls.

They are small mountain birds at first glance: compact bodies, sharp tails, dark wet feathers, pale throats. But their beaks are too straight, too hard-looking, with a bluish sheen like tempered steel. Their claws grip the fresh basalt without scraping it. They nest singly in alcoves spaced with the same strange courtesy that governs the plants: one pair here, a long interval of bare black rock, one pair there.

Their nests are made of moss, down, hair from the ungulates, and thin threads of something that looks like root but glints when the dim light catches it. There is no metal in the basin, but the nests sometimes shine as though wired.

Their calls are rare. A bird will cock its head, open its beak, and release one note:

Tink.

Or:

Klang.

Or a high, thin chime like a glass pin struck against stone.

After a call, the whole basin attends. Beetles pause. Crabs hold their claws motionless. Newts stop turning. Even the ungulates chew more slowly. Then the engine fills the space again, and life resumes its permitted motions.

The birds lay eggs that look like wet stones: black, gray, blue-white, speckled with rust-red dots too regular to be speckles. Chicks hatch naked and enormous-eyed. They beg without sound. Food goes in. Feathers come. The number of nesting pairs does not increase. No young bird is ever seen crossing the rim. No old bird is ever seen dying. Yet feathers accumulate beneath the alcoves, damp and whole, tucked gradually into moss as if being archived.

The ungulates are the basin’s largest obvious residents.

They resemble goats, or chamois, or small deer from a country where geometry is a kind of weather. Their coats are dense and always wet, lying dark along the spine and paler at the belly, colored like basalt seen through mist. Water beads on them but does not soak them through. Their legs are narrow, strong, and black below the knee. Their hooves divide cleanly and leave prints in the mud that close behind them without healing, simply becoming unprinted.

Their faces are long and reserved. They do not have the nervous quickness of prey animals. Each one grazes within its own envelope of stillness. They may stand close, but they do not huddle. When they lift their heads, they do so at different times, never as a herd.

Their horns are the strangest part of them. They rise from the skull in angular branches, not sweeping curves. The horns fork at hard angles, flatten, cross, ladder upward, and terminate in squared or beveled tips. No two sets are identical, but all appear designed under the same constraint. They look grown from bone by an intelligence that had studied cooling lava, circuit boards, and winter trees, then refused to distinguish among them.

Moss grows in tiny seams of the older horns. Droplets hang from the lower bars. Birds sometimes perch there, and the ungulate will continue chewing, eyes half-lidded, while a bird delivers a single metallic note from its head.

They drink from the central pool. Their muzzles touch the dark surface. Ripples pass outward in perfect rings. The water level does not fall. When they graze, the dwarf shrubs bend and lose leaves, yet remain dense. When they bear young, the young are already wet, already steady-legged, already part of the count. Something else, somewhere, has ceased to be visible.

There is also the otter-like one, though calling it one may be wrong.

It appears alone, always in motion between pools that are not connected. Its body is long, dark, and fluid, with a sleek pelt that holds the dim light in silver streaks. Its whiskers are white and stiff. Its paws are clever. Its eyes are round, black, and amused in a way nothing else in the basin is amused.

It dives into a pool the size of a bowl and surfaces from another across a stretch of gravel. It rolls in water too shallow to wet its back completely and disappears under it anyway. When it shakes itself, droplets fly off and briefly ignite into tiny white sparks before becoming water again midair. The sparks make no heat. They leave no smell except a faint sharpness, like struck flint or ozone.

It eats frogs, beetles, newts, perhaps fish if the pools contain fish, though no fish are ever plainly seen. Nothing is depleted by this. A frog taken in its jaws continues calling somewhere in the moss. A newt swallowed whole is later coiled around its silver bubble in a rain cup. The otter-thing is not exempt from hunger, but hunger here does not subtract. It circulates.

Sometimes, near the central outcrop, it rises halfway from the dark water and rests its forepaws on the rim. Its wet head turns toward the unseen sound below. It listens like an engineer.

There are other lives less easily separated into species.

Transparent worms live between stone and water, visible only when bubbles pass through them. Their bodies form loops and knots that resemble script no one has written. Tiny snails with glass shells graze on the undersides of leaves, their organs coiled in visible spirals, always at the same stage of fullness. Midges hover in vertical columns, each column fixed in place no matter how the cold breeze silvers the leaves. The midges do not drift because drifting would imply weather.

Mites live in the ungulates’ fur. Pale larvae live in the moss cushions. Blind white things move in cracks in the black rock, withdrawing when looked at directly, though their withdrawal is not fast; rather, the visible crack seems to decide it contains only darkness.

And beneath all of these lives the chief inhabitant, if inhabitant is the word.

The engine.

Not a machine buried in the mountain, not literally. Or not only literally. There are no tunnels, no manufacture, no metal transported up impossible slopes and hidden under moss. Yet something below has the persistence of machinery and the authority of geology. It turns without turning. It pumps without emptying a reservoir. It cycles the present through itself and returns it polished, wet, alive, unchanged.

The creatures are not its workers. They are its gauges, valves, indicator lights, cooling fins, pressure releases, oscillators, counters, alarms. The newts hold bubbles of permitted air. The beetles keep intervals. The frogs issue signals. The crabs adjust small displacements. The birds strike calibration notes. The ungulates maintain mass, appetite, warmth, breath. The otter carries passage between unconnected waters, proving connection is unnecessary if recurrence is exact.

But this makes them sound less alive than they are.

They are alive in a way that precedes story. They do not need development. They do not need escape. Their lives do not point toward migration, ruin, succession, abundance, famine, adaptation, extinction. Each creature is complete inside its action. The moth opening its wings is not preparing to fly; it is fulfilling mothness. The ungulate lowering its mouth to water is not preventing thirst in a future hour; it is making drinking present. The bird’s metallic note does not communicate a message through time; it fastens the moment to itself.

They are, all of them, animals of maintenance.

They inhabit a place where life has been separated from change but not from motion, from hunger but not from feeding, from birth but not from increase, from death but not from remains. Bones exist there. Feathers fall. Eggs hatch. Something is eaten. Something sleeps. But consequence is carefully limited, as though the basin accepts the gestures of time while refusing its arithmetic.

If one stood very still near the central pool and let the throb enter the ribs, the inhabitants would not gather around, not flee, not judge. They would make room by continuing exactly as they are. A beetle would stop at its required interval on a lichen-lit stone. A crab would lift and replace a pebble. A bird would watch from a crack with bright black eyes. An ungulate would chew a wet leaf beside a pool that never lowers. The otter-shape might surface where there had been no depth and vanish with a sound like a hand drawn once across silk.

And perhaps, after long enough, one would understand that the basin does not contain wildlife in the ordinary sense.

It contains a living diagram of permanence.

Wet bodies. Cold breath. Shell, horn, feather, moss. Every creature slick with the rain that has just happened forever. Every movement coupled to the hidden rotation below. Every life allowed to be vivid, abundant, precise, and real, so long as it does not ask to become otherwise.
