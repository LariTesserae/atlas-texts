What lives there is not one creature, at first. It is a way of being alive under pressure.

Life in the drowned chamber has the look of things made half from water and half from mineral weather. Nothing here appears fully separate from the basin that sustains it. Bodies are clear, veiled, armored, filamented, jointed, flattened against stone, suspended like flakes, folded into seams. They do not seem to have entered the place so much as thickened out of its gradients: cold and warmer, salt and fresh, iron and quartz, falling melt and rising vent-breath.

The first inhabitants are almost beneath the idea of an inhabitant. They are skins.

They varnish the young rock in faint, uneven gleams: smoky films, pale orange smears, black reticulations in iron seams, milky translucent slicks spread over basalt shelves. In clear water they are easy to mistake for wetness itself, as if the stones have simply acquired a sheen. But where the light strikes low, these films show texture. They pucker, web, bead, and clot. They thicken around warm cracks into soft halos, each halo a slow city of organisms feeding on chemistry rather than sunlight. Around some vents the mats are the color of old apricot flesh; elsewhere they are bruised gray, rust-red, oily black, pearl-white. They make the chamber habitable by making stone edible.

Everything larger comes afterward.

The grazers are numerous and exact. Many are no bigger than fingernails, with low, flexible shells that fit tightly against the rock when the water shudders. They resemble beetles pressed flat by depth, though they are not beetles: oval plates, jointed undersides, many small legs that move with disciplined fussiness. Their mouths scrape constantly. In the quieter alcoves the rasping of thousands of them becomes part of the chamber’s machine-noise, a fine granular labor under the hammering falls and groaning plates. Some carry their own translucence poorly, their stomachs visible as dark commas full of living film. Others are mineral-colored, patterned with gray and amber so perfectly that only their slow advance gives them away.

Among them live worms like blown glass threads. They knot themselves deep in cracks and extend soft crowns into the current: pale fans, feathered circles, branching nets finer than frost. At the first tremor they vanish. The retraction is so quick it seems the stone has swallowed a thought. When the chamber settles again, they reopen one by one, not like flowers but like instruments resuming a task. Their bodies remain hidden, looped in mineral-dark crevices, anchored among young sharp edges that would slice softer life apart. Only the feeding crowns show, combing suspended dust, bacteria, eggs, fragments of bodies, whatever the water is carrying that hour.

There are shrimp-like things everywhere in the middle brightness. Some are nearly invisible except for their black pinpoint eyes or, in the blind kinds, for the faint blue line of the gut and nerve. They flick and vanish, then reappear in clouds when a plume rises or the ice roof sheds powder. Their shells have the clarity of broken ice. Some are over-eyed, bearing broad dark lenses that gather the filtered glacier light; others have no eyes at all and move by vibration, antennae held wide like divining rods. They are the basin’s quick punctuation, though even their quickness is restrained by cold. They do not dart wildly. They correct themselves in short, economical strokes.

The soft stars live on vertical stone and under shelves where the currents bring food without tearing them loose. They are strange, elaborate animals: central knots with arms that divide and divide again until each creature resembles a frost pattern that has learned hunger. Their surfaces are matte and faintly granular. When still, they are almost mineral. When feeding, their branched arms lift into the flow and gather particles with patient, sticky precision. A startled one contracts into a pale fist. A calm one may span a broad patch of wall, not by size of body but by extravagant subdivision, making itself into a living sieve.

Near the roof gather the bell animals.

They are the most visible citizens of the upper chamber, suspended beneath the translucent ice where cold droplets descend in endless broken strings. Each bell is an inverted cup of clear cartilage, sometimes no larger than a thumb, sometimes wide as a bowl. Their rims flex open and shut with delicate insistence. From within trail cables of nerve-blue tissue, thin as threads, occasionally branching into finer lines that glow only when the angle of light permits. They are not bright creatures, but they catch brightness. A colony under blue ice looks like a chandelier made by an animal that has never seen fire.

When meltwater strikes them they click.

One alone makes almost nothing: a glassy tick, a tiny impact translated into body-sound. Hundreds together become sleet, static, brittle applause. The rain wall belongs to them because the rain wall feeds them. They open under the falling droplets and strain from the water whatever the mountain has ground fine enough to be swallowed: mineral flour, bacterial chains, powdered shell, glacier-borne detritus, the unborn and the dead reduced to drifting richness. Their larvae are small luminous specks, attracted upward. They rise toward the milky roof and fasten to ice or stone, hanging in clusters like cold stars waiting to grow mouths.

Below them, the fish move in disciplined circuits.

They are narrow, silver, blunt-headed animals, more sensitive than intelligent in any familiar sense. Their flanks carry scalloped ridges, enlarged organs for reading pressure and vibration. These ridges make them look embossed, as if stamped by the same machinery whose pulses they obey. Their eyes are present but not dominant. They turn before sound fully arrives. They gather before vents exhale. They widen their circles before a concussion rolls up from the floor. To watch them is to see the basin’s hidden timing made visible.

They school slowly, often in rings or tilted ellipses around central currents. When a warm plume begins, they tighten—not frantically, but with the precision of filings answering a magnet. The first ones arrive at a fissure seconds before the cloudy water blooms. Then the school opens like a silver gate and feeds on stunned shrimp, microbial flakes, vent-loosened particles. If danger appears, they do not scatter in panic so much as change geometry. A band becomes a sheet; a sheet becomes a twisting cord; a cord vanishes behind a buttress all at once.

Their predators have learned slowness as a craft.

One kind waits against the down-current side of dark fins and broken shelves. It is broad and winged, a flattened animal with the texture of torn rubber or wet basalt skin. Its back is so dark it seems less colored than lightless. Around its rim grows a crown of pressure-sensitive hairs, fine enough to tremble when a shrimp passes too near, fine enough to read the approach of fish through water already loud with stone. The mouth is underneath. It does not chase. It lifts, tilts, and becomes suddenly larger than expected, folding the water around prey. Then it settles back into looking like damage.

Another predator lives in cracks.

Most of its body is hidden in vertical seams where young rock has split but not widened. What shows is pale, jointed, and hook-ended: a limb, then another, then perhaps a portion of ridged carapace withdrawing into blackness. It is a creature of ambush and arithmetic. It knows the reach of its own joints, the rhythm of passing schools, the hesitation of grazers crossing an exposed blade of stone. It extends one appendage at a time. A hook opens, pauses, closes. A transparent shrimp disappears. A fish with careless timing is seized sideways and folded into the fissure, where smaller mouths and inner limbs finish the work unseen.

In the warm vents live the eels.

They are pale, almost water-colored, with blood-dark organs pulsing through their skin. Their bodies coil in hollows where the surrounding cold is interrupted by a wavering exhalation from below. They do not like heat exactly; there is no true heat here. They like the difference. Their heads rest at the margins of the flow, tasting chemical changes, waiting for edible turbulence. Some have reduced eyes under milky skin. Some carry sensory pits along the jaw and neck. Their mouths open softly, without drama, taking in larvae, loosened mats, small grazers dislodged by the vent’s breath.

They are among the chamber’s more intimate animals, not because they are gentle but because they occupy recognizable shelter. A vent hollow with three pale eels wound inside it looks almost domestic: bodies braided in the wavering light, tails anchored between crusted stones, heads facing outward toward the cold. When the deep pulse comes, they stir first from the tail, then the whole coil loosens, and for a few minutes the hollow becomes a pale knot of feeding.

There are also the drifters: eggs, husks, transparent juveniles, shed skins, larval forms with temporary shapes unsuited to adulthood. Some resemble droplets with cilia. Some are little rods of cartilage and hunger. Some are brief umbrellas or threads or beads with eyes. The chamber is full of unfinished bodies. Many will be eaten before they settle into a final form. Many are designed to be eaten, in the larger accounting of the place. Reproduction here is not lush in the green sense; it is particulate. Life casts itself into the water as dust, trusting layers and currents and improbable attachment.

The creatures of the bottom are heavier, slower, more secretive. In the dark syrup of dense cold water, among brine trenches and talus cones, live animals shaped by contact rather than swimming. Limpet-like grazers with flexible mineralized backs polish vent crusts. Pale segmented scavengers enter carcasses through the softest places and remain there until the body collapses around them. Tiny jointed things crawl between crystal teeth with such care that each step seems negotiated. Some animals are known mostly by tracks in bacterial film, or by the clean-edged wounds they leave on mats, or by the way nodules are turned over in certain hollows and not others.

Death has its own residents.

When a large body falls—a winged predator, a mature eel, a school fish killed by pressure shock—it does not vanish quickly. The cold preserves it, but preservation only lengthens the feast. First the invisible skins brighten over it. Then shell-grazers gather at the soft seams. Worms enter. Shrimp pick at eyes, gills, membranes. Hooked crack-dwellers reach farther than usual. The fish may circle and take fragments lifted by current. Over weeks and months the body becomes a temporary district, a warm idea in a cold place, dense with visitors. At last only pale cartilage, bone, shell-plates, or tougher fibers remain, lodged in black gravel and slowly varnished by the same life that consumes stone.

No animal here owns much in the way a surface animal might own a nest, a burrow, a territory. What they possess is tolerance.

The bell animals possess falling melt because they can endure its cold impact and eat its dust. The orange mats possess warm fissure mouths because they can build themselves from seepage. The black lace organisms possess iron seams. The eels possess gradients. The flattened grazers possess film wherever film is thin enough to scrape and currents are not strong enough to peel them away. The fish possess vibration. The predators possess waiting.

Even their senses belong to the chamber more than to themselves. Sight matters, because light does come through the ice, but sight is unreliable in layered water. A thing crossing brine may double or stretch; a predator may be bent into harmlessness by refraction until it is already near. So the inhabitants listen with skin, flank, hair, limb, gut. They read pressure. They read the long preparation before a boom. They know the difference between the roof’s rain and a settling plate, between gravel shifting under current and gravel shifting under a moving body. To live here is to be partly an ear.

Their colors are not colors of display but of substance: clear, white, iron, smoke, amber, bruise, dull silver, basalt black, nerve-blue. There is very little red except where an animal is opened or where organs show through translucent tissue. There is no green at all. Beauty here is not floral, not soft, not inviting. It is the beauty of exact adaptation: a shell curved to resist pressure waves, a bell rim tuned to droplets, a worm crown retracting before the tremor reaches it, a fish school bending around sound.

Their lives are slow, but not empty.

A day in the basin, if day can be spoken of under ice, is full of minor decisions. Bells open wider when the rain strengthens. Shrimp rise into clearer layers when silt falls out. Worms withdraw, return, withdraw. Fish alter their circuits by a body-length and keep that alteration for generations if a mineral curtain has changed the current. Predators wait through abundance because the angle is wrong, then take one animal when the water delivers it perfectly. Grazers leave pale clean trails across dark microbial varnish, and behind them the film begins again.

When the great pulse comes, every inhabitant becomes more itself.

The bells close to endure impact, then reopen into plenty. The fish tighten and feed. The eels uncoil. Shrimp bloom upward in glittering confusion. Hidden predators extend from cracks. Microbial mats tear and spread, colonizing new stone. Larvae are carried to places they could never have reached by swimming. The basin’s violence is also distribution. A boom from below rearranges opportunity.

What lives there, then, is a whole cold industry of bodies without machines: grazers, strainers, listeners, ambushers, varnishes, drifters, coils, bells. None is master of the chamber. None stands apart from the mineral engine. They are not decorations added to a harsh place; they are the place continuing by other means.

They are the mountain’s submerged metabolism.

They eat its dust, polish its wounds, thicken its seams, read its settling bones, and turn its slow accidents into flesh.
