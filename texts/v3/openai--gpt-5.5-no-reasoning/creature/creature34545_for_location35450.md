What lives there is not a people, not exactly, though the place gives the impression of habitation too strongly for that word to be avoided. It is inhabited by arrangements: by sealed bodies, mineral appetites, patient colonies, animals that behave like moving fragments of the terrain, and plants that have accepted stone as a climate.

The dominant life is neither plant nor fungus in any familiar sense, though it borrows from both. It is lithic, dry-bodied, photosensitive, and chemical. Its tissues are built with salts, silicates, resins, translucent horn, mica-like laminae, and brittle membranes that open and close without ever appearing moist. It does not drink water so much as intercept briefness: dew that forms and vanishes before dawn, vapor passing through stone, bound moisture locked in minerals, hydrogen teased from vent-gases, trace condensation hidden inside pores too small for a drop to form. Water here is not a substance lying around. It is a rumor caught molecule by molecule.

The oldest inhabitants, though “old” means perhaps decades rather than centuries, are the black and rust mats that skin the sunward stone. They are the first citizens of exposed surfaces. A new rockfall lies pale and bare for a short while, then a gray veil appears on it, then soot-colored islands, then a continuous pelt of breathing velvet. Each mat is made of millions of upright filaments, very short and very dense, each with a hard mineral sheath and a dark photosynthetic core. They feed on light, heat, dust, salts, and exhalations from the cracks below. They are slow, territorial, and exacting. Their borders rise into ridges where competing colonies meet, and those ridges are the nearest thing the basin has to fortifications.

They make soil without making softness. Dust sticks to them. Mineral grains lodge between their filaments. The bodies of dead insects, shed skins, cracked eggshells, crystal droppings, and powdered husks are held in place until acids and alkalis draw them apart. Underneath the mats, the pale stone becomes stained, glazed, and chemically opened. Into these prepared surfaces come the taller forms.

The glass orchards live where the first sun strikes.

Their stems are opaque white and crusted at the base, as if the stone has foamed upward around them. Their fans are clear, stiff, and veined with nearly invisible channels. They turn through the day in movements too slight to see directly, each pane angling itself to gather light without overheating. They are not leaves but lenses and shutters. Inside the fan tissue are arrays of mineral cells that split light, trap certain wavelengths, and pass excess heat down through the stem into buried storage knots.

At dawn, tiny animals gather among them for safety. The orchard light is hard on hunters. It confuses depth, erases edges, and throws small glittering ghosts into the air. A predator trying to strike there misjudges distances. Even the thread-birds cross the orchards carefully, sideways and low, their filament feathers drawn tight.

The amber columns are another kind of resident: warm, ribbed, hollow in places, and half-animal in their presence despite being rooted. Each column grows from a deep crack. It feeds on upward warmth and gas, processing what rises from the young mountain’s interior. Its dry outer wall is tough and translucent, but the inside is chambered, lined with membranes that expand during the day and contract at night. When the sun is high, a grove of them may give off faint clicking sounds as internal valves adjust. At evening they cloud over, becoming opaque from the inside outward.

Many things live in them. Mites no larger than grains of sand browse inside the ribs. Pale larvae sleep in sealed chambers. Smoke-colored predators occupy abandoned hollows and wait with their bodies folded into vertical seams. Certain thread-birds wedge their eggs in the upper flutes, where heat remains steady after sunset. The columns tolerate these tenants up to a point. Too much chewing, too much scraping, too much waste in one chamber, and the column seals that section with a resin plug, entombing whatever failed to leave.

The blade-thickets are the basin’s hedges, walls, and closed neighborhoods. They are low, overlapping plates, each blade shaped like a shard of shell or hammered metal, colored copper, ivory, ash-blue, or violet according to its mineral diet and exposure. Their edges are fine enough to cut soft skin. They grow in interlocking rosettes, not to catch rain, but to make shade gradients. Beneath them the air is cooler, stiller, and fractionally less dry. They create the microclimates on which smaller life depends.

When evening comes, they close. The outer plates tilt inward, then the inner ones, each touching the next with a small dry whisper. Anything beneath them must already know whether it is welcome. Some animals shelter safely in these closing chambers; others are crushed, pierced, or held until their fluids—what little there are—can be absorbed through root-mouths in the stone.

The vine-chains are perhaps the strangest of the large growths. They are not flexible like true vines. They are articulated: hollow segments joined by tight collars, each segment grown, hardened, and then partly abandoned as the chain extends. They bridge cracks, drape between ledges, and lace the whole basin together. Young segments are amber-gray and faintly warm. Old ones bleach nearly white and become resonant in the heat.

They carry spores, animals, shadows, and signals. A vibration at one end travels down a chain faster than any scent could move through the dry air. Thread-birds use them as roads. Leaf-horn scavengers scrape mineral bloom from their undersides. At times the chains tighten across an alley over several days, narrowing it; at other times they sag and crack, leaving new passages where grazers begin to travel. In this way the city’s streets alter without any creature deciding to alter them.

The most visible animals are the ceramic-backed grazers.

They are six-limbed and low, with triangular shoulders, long delicate feet, and backs armored in pale plates that resemble chips of the surrounding stone. Their bellies are recessed and dark. Their heads are narrow wedges fitted with scraping mouths. They eat the black mats, but never randomly. A herd will advance across a surface in a slow front, removing the upper layer while leaving enough living base for regrowth. They crop borders, reopen clogged corridors, and keep certain plazas from sealing over.

They are more important than they seem. Without them, the mats would smother too much exposed stone. Without the mats, the grazers would starve. Without the grazers’ crystal droppings, certain glass fans would lack salts. Their herds are therefore part livestock, part street-maintenance, part weather pattern.

They are timid because fear conserves them. When alarmed, they freeze utterly. Their eyes withdraw beneath ridges. Their limbs tuck into angles. Their pale backs align with chips and flakes around them. A whole herd can vanish in plain sight, becoming a scatter of broken stone. They remain that way for minutes, sometimes hours, until one at the edge resumes scraping and the others remember movement.

The thread-birds are light-boned climbers, all length and tension. They have wings, but flight is not their main art. Their feathers are dry filaments, each branching into finer hairs that trap dust and static. When they leap, they do not soar so much as fall with intentions. They spread themselves, drift, hook a vine-chain, swing under a ledge, and climb again.

Their calls are felt more than heard. The basin’s quiet changes around them: a tightening in the ear, a pressure behind the teeth. They use these high calls in the hollows of the spire and among the glass fans, mapping passageways by returning tremors. Their nests are bundles of shed filaments, mineral fibers, cracked seed-cases, and thin strips peeled from dead vine segments. They bind everything with a dry saliva that hardens almost instantly into amber threads.

They are thieves of capsules. Many of the rooted inhabitants produce seed-pods, spore-stones, or brittle capsules that must be carried before they open. Thread-birds steal them, cache them, forget them, or wedge them into unsuitable places where some nevertheless germinate. Much of the basin’s accidental architecture may be due to their bad memory.

Under the overhangs live the flat horn-leaf animals. They are scavengers and gardeners. Their bodies are broad, segmented, and flexible at the edges, with many small legs hidden beneath. From above they look like fallen plates of brown horn or dead blade-thicket leaves. They move mostly at twilight, sliding along the undersides of ledges and through powder-filled cracks. They eat husks, shed skins, salt crusts, resin crumbs, dead spores, and the thin exhausted tissues left when larger plants seal themselves.

On their backs they carry little gardens: colonies of spores, mineral lichens, and dust-binding threads. These are not camouflage, or not only camouflage. They are agreements. The animal carries the colony to new surfaces. The colony regulates heat, disguises scent, and sometimes produces bitter compounds that discourage predators. When a horn-leaf rests for several days in one place, a new patch of life may begin beneath it. When it dies, its back becomes a prepared field.

There are many smaller grazers too: needle-mouthed mites that feed in the glass orchards; pale wedge-headed insects that drill salt from column bases; jointed worms that live entirely inside vine-chains; dust-colored hoppers that jump only once or twice in their lives, each leap made to escape a closing plate or falling flake. Most have sealed skins, lacquered shells, or crystalline waste organs. None can afford leakage. Even their eggs are glazed.

The predators are not rulers here. They are specialists, rare and economical.

The smoke-creature in the amber columns is a patient folding thing. At rest it resembles a shadow caught behind translucent resin. Its body is ribboned, jointless to the eye, with small gripping hooks along its inner surface. It senses vibration through the column wall. When a grazer noses too close to the base, or a thread-bird descends into the wrong hollow, the predator unfolds. It does not spring. It pours outward with dreadful restraint, lays itself where escape will pass, and tightens. Its mouth is small. Feeding takes a long time.

The cord-knot predator is even less animal-like. It lies in paths as a twisted length of dry fiber, indistinguishable from fallen vine. In truth it is a colony of linked bodies, or one body divided into cords. When stepped on, it loops upward and closes. Its strength is not speed but geometry. A victim struggling against it only pulls the knots tighter. After feeding, the cord dries again, stiffens, and resumes its imitation of debris.

There are also hunters no larger than fingernails that live among the blade-thickets, transparent scissor-mouthed things that take mites and larvae; and pale burrowers beneath the hexagonal plates that feed on anything trapped in the seams. But open violence is rare. Predation is an ambush of pressures, closures, and delayed consequences.

Inside the central spire lives the basin’s densest community.

The spire itself is stone, young and still flaking, but it has been claimed so thoroughly that it is also a vertical city. Dry vines clasp it in bands. Shell-plants form balconies. Amber chambers cling to its warmer face. Glass fans grow where morning and evening light strike at angles. The shaded side is blackened by mats and populated by horn-leaf scavengers. Tiny corridors thread through the scaffold, just wide enough for grazers’ young, thread-birds, mites, and smoke predators.

The knocks heard from within are made by several things. Some are thermal cracks answered by living tension. Some are vine-chain joints slipping as they tighten. Some are the signals of heavy-shelled animals that live deep in the scaffold and rarely emerge.

These spire-dwellers are squat, slow, and almost never seen whole. They resemble walking seed-cases or small tortoises made of bark and porcelain. Their forelimbs are blunt hammers. They knock on hollow chambers of column and vine, not loudly, but with rhythm: three taps, a long silence, two taps, a pause that might last minutes. Other individuals answer from elsewhere in the spire. Whether this is courtship, territory, navigation, or the coordination of feeding is difficult to say. In a place where sound is expensive, even a knock must serve several purposes.

They may be the closest thing the basin has to deliberate citizens. They maintain certain passages by wearing them smooth with their shells. They drag fallen capsules into crevices. They break dead amber columns open, allowing scavengers access. Their droppings are dense pellets of mineral and organic residue, prized by glass seedlings and black mats alike. Yet they build nothing. They do what their bodies require, and the city results.

The basin’s life shares a common discipline: closure.

Everything can seal. The mats crust over during cold nights. The fans fold. The blades overlap. The columns plug chambers. Animals tuck limbs beneath armor. Eggs harden. Wounds dry into lacquer. Mouths are hidden when not in use. Breathing happens through valved pores that open only when heat, chemistry, and risk permit. Even death closes down into whiteness and hardness before being reopened by patient mouths.

Rot, in the wet sense, hardly exists. A dead grazer does not bloat. It stiffens, lightens, and cracks along plate seams. Its eyes sink into dark pits. Its softer inner tissues dry into a dense resinous mass, which scavengers rasp away in layers. Horn-leaf animals arrive first. Then mites. Then mat-filaments creep over the remaining shell. Eventually the bones, plates, and mineralized organs become part of a salt patch or a growth base. The dead are not returned to soil, because there is little soil. They are incorporated into surfaces.

The newborn are equally dry. Birth happens in capsules, husks, detachable chambers, stone-like eggs, or folded larval cases. Many young hatch only when warmth rises through a specific crack or when dust chemistry changes after a rockfall. Some may wait for years in the sealed dark of a crevice, not asleep exactly, but suspended as a complete intention.

Because the place is young, many inhabitants are opportunists of first contact. They specialize in beginnings: the first film on naked stone, the first root in a fracture, the first bridge over a chasm, the first grazer to test a fresh terrace. There are pioneer mats that tolerate raw mineral sharpness. There are glass seedlings that only germinate in fresh breaks before the black colonies arrive. There are animals that follow collapses, attracted by exposed salts and unclaimed corridors. A new crack is not a wound in the city. It is a street being born.

No single creature governs the basin, yet the whole has behavior.

In the morning, the east brightens first. Glass fans open. The smallest mites retreat from the exposed faces. Grazers emerge from darker shelves and begin scraping softened mat-tips while the stone is still cool enough for movement. Thread-birds climb upward to warm themselves, filament feathers spread into halos. The black mats dilate their pores.

By midday, motion nearly ceases. The city stands open but guarded. Vents are aligned. Plates are angled. Shadows are occupied. Predators wait where heat and hunger will send prey. The spire ticks and occasionally knocks. Dust rises only in brief flashes and is immediately caught by hairs, pores, static filaments, and rough mineral skins.

In the afternoon, traffic resumes. Grazers shift terrace by terrace, following temperatures rather than destinations. Horn-leaf scavengers begin to unseal themselves beneath overhangs. Thread-birds make their falling crossings from balcony to balcony. Vine-chains tense in the cooling air. Amber columns adjust their internal chambers for the night.

At evening, the basin performs its most visible ritual. The glass orchards fold first, becoming a field of closed transparent knives. Blade-thickets shut in overlapping sequences, whispering from outer districts toward the center. Mats seal their pores. Grazers withdraw into cracks and under plate roofs. Thread-birds hang upside down beneath the lattice, heads tucked into filament breasts. Smoke predators retreat into hollow stems unless hunting has succeeded. The amber columns cloud. The spire keeps light longest, and then loses it.

What lives there, finally, is a dry congregation.

A mineral garden that behaves like a town. A town whose citizens are mats, fans, columns, blades, chains, grazers, climbers, scavengers, sleepers, ambushes, capsules, spores, and shells. Nothing there is casual. Nothing is lush by softness. Its abundance is made of restraint: sealed moisture, slow breath, hard skins, careful paths, alliances of shade and salt and heat.

It is young, but already full of customs.

It wastes nothing.

It hurries almost never.

It has no builders, but it has streets.

It has no speech, but sometimes the spire knocks softly into the afternoon silence, and from somewhere inside the dry living scaffold, after a long delay, something knocks back.
