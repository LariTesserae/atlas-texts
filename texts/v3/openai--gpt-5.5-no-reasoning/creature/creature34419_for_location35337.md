What lives there is not a crowd, not in the ordinary sense. The city is occupied by a civilization that has receded from visibility, or by the remnants of one that learned to become infrastructure.

They are few now, if they were ever many. They do not gather in streets or look from windows. They do not fill markets, sing from balconies, hang cloth to dry, or leave bright litter in gutters. The plateau would make such habits seem vulgar, even dangerous. Whoever lives there has taken on the temperament of pressure systems: restrained, patient, distributed, difficult to locate from any single point.

They live behind sealed doors, below pavement, within thermal walls, in chambers where condensation is measured before breath. They inhabit the city the way a nervous system inhabits a body.

At first one might think the place is ruled by machines alone: carriers moving according to old schedules, sluices answering changes in cloud, gauges ticking to themselves, lamps shifting color after a horn note no one hears. But the machines are not mindless enough. Their hesitations have judgment in them. A carrier pauses beneath a dripping conduit and waits for the mineral flow to thin before passing through. A pressure gate opens halfway, closes, opens again by two measured handspans, as though listening. Warm vents are shuttered ahead of a weather surge not yet visible. Somewhere, something anticipates.

The inhabitants are present as corrections.

A valve cleaned where no automated brush could reach. A strip of new ceramic set into a worn channel. Notches added to old bridge rails in a pattern too deliberate for erosion. A warning mark changed overnight from yellow to black. A small sealed bowl left beneath a sweating pipe to collect a particular mineral bloom. A lamp hood turned by hand toward a path no one is seen walking.

They are maintainers, but not merely workers. Maintenance is their culture, their economy, their ethics, perhaps their religion. They do not build monuments separate from function because function is monument enough. A pump kept alive for four centuries is an ancestor. A channel that has never boiled over is a treaty. A pressure bell with a particular tone may be known by name. Their histories are probably not written in books—paper could not endure here—but in flow records, pipe scars, valve settings, bridge notches, mineral layers, and the changing taste of water at district mouths.

Their bodies, if bodies is still the right word, have adapted to heat and damp and mineral air.

They are likely pale in the way cave things are pale, but not fragile. Their skin may be thickened, sealed by oils, gray-white or amber-brown with deposits at the creases. Their hands would be broad, insulated, clever: hands for hot metal, wet stone, calibration screws, ceramic seals, old wheels stiff with mineral crust. Their nails might be dark, hornlike, or replaced by fitted caps of noncorroding alloy. The air has made delicacy expensive, so they carry themselves carefully, conserving motion.

They wear layered coverings not for cold but for contact: waxed fabric, rubberized cloth, felted mineral fiber, ceramic-scaled aprons, breathing collars, masks that can be lowered over the mouth when a street begins to vent. Their garments are probably dark at first glance, though closer inspection would show coded stains: blue salts at the cuffs, white carbonate along hems, rust-red handprints near tool belts. Rank, trade, district, and recent labor might all be readable from these accumulations.

Their eyes would need protection. Thick amber lenses, perhaps, like the old round windows. Some may have natural films over their eyes, or lids that close sideways against steam and dust. In the dim brown light they would not look quickly from object to object. They would watch gauges, surfaces, vapor, the behavior of droplets. They would notice whether water beads or sheets; whether a wall sweats evenly; whether a rail hums at the wrong pitch.

They are people of omen, but their omens are physical.

A sweeter smell in the vapor means a buried line is thinning. A tremor in a metal plaza means a lower chamber has changed load. A greenish condensation under a black pipe means the fluid inside has found a hairline path outward. To outsiders this would seem superstition because it is expressed in ritual language. To them, ritual and engineering have never separated. You bow before opening a steam door because the old bow places your ear near the listening notch. You mark your wrist before crossing a bridge because the chemical paste will discolor if the air is wrong. You wait after the horn because the first quiet after pressure change is when stone speaks most clearly.

They do not hurry unless something has already gone terribly wrong.

Their children, if there are children, are raised away from the broad streets. The young would live in warm inner courts or low amber-lit rooms where air is washed through mineral baffles. They would learn first not words but prohibitions: do not touch the black handrail; do not step on plates that sing; do not breathe white vapor; do not drink clear water unless it has passed the blue mark; do not trust silence after a gate opens. Their games might involve routing trickles through carved boards, balancing heat stones, identifying metals by smell, memorizing pipe signs. A child’s first true civic act might be to open a minor sluice under supervision.

The old would be valued intensely. In such a place, memory is infrastructure. The elderly would know which repairs were official and which were desperate, which tunnels were abandoned because they collapsed and which because something below began answering knocks. They would remember when a district still required human crews, when a tower still smoked, when the lower cloud came higher one year and did not recede for months. Their bodies may be ruined by the city—lungs lined with mineral dust, joints swollen by damp, burns hardened into shiny maps—but they would carry charts no archive could replace.

They likely speak softly. Loud voices are useless in vapor and dangerous near listening systems. Their language may favor pressure, gradient, valve, seep, temper, load. They may describe moods thermally: a person is “overheld,” “venting,” “below measure,” “near flash.” Promises might be phrased as maintenance vows. Love might be less often declared than demonstrated through calibration: cleaning another’s mask filters, checking the seal of their gloves, waiting at a pressure door until they pass through safely.

Their music, if they have music, is not song in the open air. Nothing sings there. But they may make tonal records from pipes and bells: low notes struck through metal to test hidden voids, chimes tuned to condensation rates, horn calls that are both warning and liturgy. In enclosed rooms they might listen to the city’s organs amplified through ceramic horns—the thud of deep engines, the sigh of reservoirs, the ticking of gauges—and hear in them genealogies.

They eat what can be grown without soil or sun. Pale cultures from warm tanks. Protein mats from condensation chambers. Blind sump-creatures harvested from regulated channels. Mineral-fed fungi scraped from safe brick. Perhaps small soft-shelled things raised under pipes in stacked frames, fed on bacterial film. Food would be dense, salty, metallic, served warm because everything is warm. Flavor may be measured by trace elements rather than sweetness. A feast might be a rare cool dish, or water from an old uncontaminated cistern, or something brought up from below that flashes silver when cut.

There may also be another class of inhabitant: the delegated ones.

The automated carriers are not merely carts. Some contain decision-engines, old and slow, sealed in black iron and ceramic against heat. They know routes better than any living worker. They remember weights, delays, gradients, accident sites. When empty, they are not purposeless; they are rehearsing the city’s circulation, keeping rails clear, proving that passages remain passable. A carrier that stops appearing would be treated like a missing citizen.

The tall waders in the vapor marshes may be related to these. Perhaps they are maintenance machines designed so long ago that mineral, heat, and microbial film have softened their outlines into animal ambiguity. They stand in basins reading chemistry through their feet. They dip narrow heads to sample water, then transmit or remember the result. Their gray membrane may be grown, not manufactured: a living sheath over articulated struts. If approached, they would not flee at once. They would turn with grave slowness, evaluating heat, vibration, salinity, intention.

And there are the smaller lives: translucent channel-sliders, pipe-clingers, film-grazers, vent worms, blind things in cisterns. They are not background. They are part of the city’s metabolism. Some clean mineral film from essential vents. Some indicate contamination by dying in visible clusters. Some are cultivated as sensors. A pale patch on brick that pulses in warm vapor may be watched more carefully than a gauge, because gauges lie when corroded but living stains react.

The inhabitants may not draw a firm boundary between citizen, tool, animal, and process. A human valve-keeper, a self-moving carrier, a bacterial skin, a pressure bell, and an old hot spring may all be members of the same civic body, each with duties, tolerances, and signs of distress.

The city’s true ruler, if it has one, is probably below.

Not a king beneath the pavement, but the feverish substrate itself: the mountain’s heat, the pressured waters, the chemical veins, the old volcanic cavities, the deep thudding engines that may have been built to serve the mountain or to negotiate with it. The inhabitants do not conquer this force. They attend it. They have made an agreement with something too vast to defeat and too useful to abandon.

In the sealed towers with listening dishes aimed down into stone, specialists may sit for hours in harnesses, palms resting on warm plates, translating tremors into instruction. They may be engineers, priests, doctors, miners, or diplomats. Their faces would be damp behind amber masks. Before them, needles would tremble over smoked glass. Condensation might run upward along ribbed ceilings because the chamber is shaped around some inversion of heat or charge. They would listen not for words but for intention-like patterns: rising pressure, blocked vent, new fracture, old chamber filling, a warning knock from a depth no human has entered in generations.

The circular plazas paved with trembling metal plates may be communal instruments. When the plates shiver without engines nearby, inhabitants might gather at the edges in silence, counting intervals. A tremor from below could announce a seasonal shift, a successful release, a death in the deep works, or the approach of a dangerous boiling event. Outsiders would see empty metal trembling in fog. The residents would see the city speaking plainly.

Why are so few visible? Perhaps because visibility is exposure. The open streets are not where life happens; they are circulatory channels, thermal gradients, work surfaces. To linger there without purpose would be as strange as lingering inside an artery. Domestic life is likely inward: behind amber portholes, in low rooms carved into insulated rock, along galleries reached by sealed lifts, in communal chambers where walls are warm and dry enough for memory objects to survive.

Their dwellings would be spare but not joyless. Objects would be made to withstand damp: ceramic cups, metal tablets, mineral-glass beads, etched tools, woven heat-fiber mats, waterproof scrolls, polished stones from particular basins. Decoration might consist of controlled deposits—panels where water has been guided for years to lay down blue-green fans or white scalloped ridges. A household might cultivate a wall of mineral growth the way another culture tends a garden.

They have no trees, so ancestry cannot be imagined as roots and branches. They may imagine it as channels: descent by flow, kinship by convergence, obligation by return. The dead may not be buried in soil. Perhaps bodies are given to hot water in sealed dissolution chambers, their minerals separated and added to civic works. A person might become a trace in a bridge rail, a pale line in a cistern wall, a calibrated weight in a pressure bell. Mourning would not involve preservation of the body but correct redistribution.

Their faces, when seen unexpectedly through vapor, would seem severe. Not cruel, but sealed. The city has trained expression inward. Smiles may be brief because exposed teeth collect mineral grit; laughter may be saved for dry rooms. They would look at a stranger first as a contamination risk, then as a pressure problem, then perhaps as a guest.

Hospitality would be procedural. They would not embrace. They would take your outer garments, scrape your boots, test the water on your skin, give you a mask, mark the safe route on the back of your glove, and instruct you in horn signals. Only after you survived the first passage would they ask your name.

They are not miserable. That would be too simple. They live inside hardship, but hardship made coherent by practice. There is pride here: in keeping channels distinct, in knowing the old routes, in hearing a failing pump before it fails, in passing through a whiteout cloud without losing count of steps. There is beauty too, though it is austere: copper light under wet pavement, amber windows in fog, mineral terraces at the edge of lower cloud, the slow bloom of color in basins cut by ancestors.

Their festivals may coincide not with harvest but with successful venting. The day a lower reservoir is cleared. The hour when seasonal cloud first climbs the plateau. The yearly opening of a deep sluice that stains the stepped basins blue. People might emerge then in greater numbers, not cheering, but standing along galleries while horns sound in sequence and lamps change from red to green to white. Children would be lifted to see vapor rise from the old channels. Elders would touch bridge notches and recite the years when the water ran too hot, too dark, too thin.

And beneath all this, something stranger may live as well.

The city may have begun as a human industry, but its long delegation has given rise to an inhabitant larger than its makers. Pumps, condensers, conduits, bacterial films, mineral precipitates, signal lamps, heat gradients, decision-carts, listening towers, pressure bells, and the remaining people form a single slow organism. No one part commands. The city regulates itself through them all.

It senses weather through sweating walls. It remembers through deposits. It digests through refineries. It circulates through channels and carriers. It dreams in gauges, tremors, and lights behind fog. Its immune system is maintenance. Its speech is horn, valve, drip, hum, and rerouted water. Its skin is brick, slag-stone, ceramic tile, oxide, wet iron, mineral bloom. Its blood is hot water. Its bones are the black volcanic terraces. Its fever is the mountain.

The visible inhabitants, then, are caretakers of a being they also belong to.

They live there by becoming less like owners and more like organs. They do not occupy the city; they participate in it. They have surrendered bustle, spectacle, and perhaps ambition in exchange for continuity. They endure because the city endures, and the city endures because they keep answering its small demands.

If one waited long enough in a broad wet avenue, hidden under an overhang while cloud moved through, one might finally see them.

A lamp would change color. A sealed door would unseat itself with a sigh. Three figures would emerge in dark heat-cloth, short and broad against the vapor, each carrying a tool-case shaped to the hip. Their masks would have amber eye-plates. One would kneel by a gutter and touch the mineral film with two gloved fingers. Another would stand listening to a wall. The third would look down the empty street, not afraid, only measuring.

They would speak little. They would open a plate in the pavement. Steam would rise around them and bead on their shoulders. One would descend a ladder into orange light. One would remain above, counting under their breath. The last would scrape a new mark beside many older marks on the rail.

Then the plate would close. The figures would return through the sealed door. The avenue would be empty again.

But not uninhabited. Never that.
