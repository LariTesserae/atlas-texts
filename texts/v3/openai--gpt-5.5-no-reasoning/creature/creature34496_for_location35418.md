What lives there is not an animal, though it has learned the advantages of posture. It is not a plant, though it occupies surfaces and returns after weather. It is not a spirit, exactly, unless spirit is what a place becomes when it repeats an action for long enough.

The inhabitants are passages.

They live as deliberate gaps, as wet continuities, as habits of drainage preserved in stone and gesture. The plateau’s true residents are the things that cannot remain where they are: water, echo, reflected sky, cold, pressure, memory, and the pale, half-made intelligences that have grown around the need to let everything pass through.

They are easiest to notice where the old makers left their work.

A low shelter under an overhang will appear empty at first: only stacked stone, a sloped floor, a broken slab-roof that admits a patient drip. But if one watches through several changes of light, something crouches there—not in the center, where a body would have rested, but along the edges of leakage. It gathers around the roof-drips and the shallow channel cut through the threshold. It is present in the way each bead of water hesitates before falling, in the way the runoff divides around the entrance and rejoins beyond it. A listener might think the shelter still remembers a person inside. That is nearly true. What remains is the portion of personhood that consisted of yielding: the bent back, the cupped hand, the learned tilt of a bowl, the instinct not to block the outflow.

The locals, if that word can still be used, are thin. Not thin like starving bodies, but thin like poured metal beaten into leaf, like cloud reflected on a blade. They have been worn down by altitude and brightness until only their functions remain.

Some are almost human when glimpsed sideways in wet stone. A slant of shoulders appears in a wall of seepage. Jointed hands form briefly in frost needles. A face leans up from a pool, not drowning, not emerging, only arranged by light and depth into the suggestion of regard. These figures do not stand apart from the water. They are made of its behavior. When runoff quickens, they become intent. When mist thickens, they become numerous. When the sun is at its fiercest and every shallow basin burns white, they nearly disappear, reduced to dark seams and the soft ticking inside rock.

They have no skins. Their boundaries are rules.

One begins where water is being guided and ends where water is stopped. One occupies a stairway during rain, moving step by step as little streams find the pecked hollows and descend. Another lives in a row of overflowing bowls, inhabiting the curved exchange between one lip and the next. Another keeps to the carved thumb-width channels and is no wider than those channels permit, but may run for miles if the grade holds. In the amphitheater, where suspended droplets fill the air, there are multitudes of them—small, precise presences hanging inside each bright bead, each one inverted, each one listening.

They do not speak in voices. They indicate.

A warning may come as a drip striking twice where it should strike once. A welcome may be the sudden clearing of silt from a shallow groove. Refusal is a basin going mirror-smooth and showing nothing but blinding sky. Grief is heavier: a long subterranean boom, followed by water emerging gray from a seam that had run clear for years.

They know the terrain the way blood knows vessels. They know which ridges are reliable only before noon. They know which pools have forgotten their bottoms. They know the basins that reflect future weather and the basins that reflect abandoned interiors. They know the hollows where sealed water has become dense with elsewhere, and they avoid them with the caution of creatures avoiding sickness.

Their bodies are temporary arrangements of wetness, mineral, and attention. A being may gather during a storm as dozens of separate trickles combine across a tilted shelf. For an hour it will be broad-backed and many-limbed, moving down through the channels the vanished makers cut for it. It may finger the notches of a spillway, taste salt from a seam, leave delicate fans of white crust behind it. Then sunlight opens, the streams separate, and the being becomes a family of smaller intentions: a bead under an overhang, a tremor in a pool, a darkening fissure, a whisper traveling down a hollow pipe of stone.

They remember by depositing.

Not stories, but crusts. Not names, but stains. Their memory is mineral and cumulative: iron rust along a preferred descent, salt blooms at a former resting place, polished stone where countless flows have passed, blackened channels where glass has been touched by water until it resembles worn skin. What they know, they lay down. What they fear, they route around. What they revere, they keep open.

The old makers may have been their ancestors, or their hosts, or their larval form.

Perhaps once there were bodies here: small, crouching, practical people with reddened fingers and careful feet, people who carried perforated bowls and slept under leaking roofs, people who gathered in the amphitheater to listen to the pitch of runoff and judge the condition of the world. They may have built the channels to survive the plateau’s distortions. They may have learned that a sealed vessel invites another place into it. They may have taught their children the circle with the gap at the bottom before they taught them any animal’s name.

Over generations, their customs may have thinned them. A people who never stopped water, never held too tightly, never trusted enclosure, never allowed a roof to keep out all weather, might gradually become less distinct from the passage they honored. Their dead would not be buried, because a grave is too close to a sealed basin. Their names would not be fixed, because names dam the self. Their houses would leak. Their tools would be left in niches where rain could reach them. Their bowls would overflow by design.

Eventually, perhaps, they passed through themselves.

What remains lives by maintaining the old law: water must be led, but never stopped.

They tend the gaps in stone rings. They keep the drainage grooves clear. They widen blocked channels grain by grain. They tilt fallen slabs just enough that runoff can escape beneath them. Their care is not gentle in a human sense; it is erosive, patient, exact. If a wall settles too tightly across a gully, they enter it during frost and pry it apart from within. If a bowl becomes clogged with silt, they call rain into it from clear air. If an unwary traveler were to plug a break in a circle, the inhabitants would first murmur, then click, then darken every stone around the mistake. If the blockage remained, the water behind it would begin to reflect impossible interiors, and the locals would retreat from the place as from a wound.

They are afraid of stillness.

Not quiet, not rest, but sealed stillness: water denied exit, sound without escape, light caught between surfaces, a path counted too many times. In such places something else may live, or begin to live. Deep pools with no overflow develop a kind of inward appetite. They show rooms that were never built, corridors under the plateau, faces without weather on them. They grow blue beyond depth. They become heavy enough to bend the ledges around them. The passage-beings avoid these pools, but they also feed them reluctantly, because all water must pass somewhere, and even danger must drain.

Their young, if they have young, are born as hesitations.

A bead of water hanging too long from an overhang may acquire a preference about when to fall. A trickle may choose the left side of a pebble rather than the right for no visible reason, and continue making that choice through many rains. A patch of condensation may gather each dawn in the shape of a hand before losing itself at noon. These are infant forms: not yet beings, but tendencies. Most vanish with sunlight. Some persist, find channels, acquire mineral taste, learn the difference between limestone and basalt, between safe depth and false depth, between reflection and warning.

The oldest inhabitants are less visible because they are larger than sight.

One may occupy an entire basin system, distributed through hundreds of shallow pools. Its thoughts move as ripples that do not need contact. Its moods alter perspective. When it attends, shadows fall with different delays. When it withdraws, empty basins reflect anyway. Another lives inside the cliff that murmurs. It is made of pressure, seepage, and old fractures rounded into familiarity. It adjusts itself with grave complaints, not because it is collapsing, but because it is remembering how to bear weight.

The amphitheater houses something older still.

Not a king, not a god, not a mother. A listening.

It lives among the suspended droplets, in the deferred fall that fills the circular hollow. The converging channels feed it drop by drop, but it does not drink. It hears. Every bead carries a tiny inverted weather, a possible ridge, a basin elsewhere, a storm not yet arrived. The listening compares them. It receives the state of all passages: which channels are open, which pools are dangerous, where frost has sealed a seam, where a dry hollow has begun reflecting, where a carved gap has narrowed with mineral growth. The whole plateau reports to it in impacts that have not quite happened.

If the old makers gathered there, they did so to consult this listening. They sat or crouched along the terraces while the wind moved through stone flutes and the suspended rain trembled. No one needed to speak. The answer would arrive as pitch: a rise in the runoff tone, a resonance in a hollow ledge, a sudden alignment of droplets showing an upside-down mountain beneath a pool. From that they would know whether to open a wall, abandon a shelter, deepen a channel, or leave a certain basin uncounted for another season.

Now the listening remains, and the lesser inhabitants come to it without bodies. A thousand tiny flows report at once. A mist-column bends toward it. A frost seam cracks in a rhythm like syllables. Empty bowls along the rim fill with brightness but not water. The place confers with itself.

These beings do not hunt, mate, graze, nest, or sleep. They recur.

Their hunger is gradient. Their pleasure is release. Their labor is arrangement. They want passage the way animals want warmth. They are distressed by blockage, soothed by overflow, sharpened by cold, expanded by rain, thinned by glare. They do not possess territory, but they have routes of fidelity. Certain channels are used again and again not because they are easiest, but because they have become meaningful through repetition. Certain basins are avoided because they remember being sealed. Certain stones are touched by every flow that can reach them, polished to a softness almost like devotion.

They have an ethics of permeability.

To keep everything would be monstrous. To spill everything at once would be waste. To guide without imprisoning is the proper act. A good vessel is incomplete. A good shelter admits weather. A good body can be left behind.

This is why they seem both absent and crowded. They do not stand in the landscape; they happen through it. They are the plateau’s old intelligence dispersed into wet mineral action. They live in the unfinishedness of every structure, in the deliberate leak, in the broken circle, in the bowl with a hole drilled through it. They are what became of life after it accepted that remaining whole was less important than continuing to move.

If one walked there, the inhabitants would not approach like animals. They would test.

A shallow stream might cross the path repeatedly, though no source appears. A reflected figure might lag half a step behind. Water would bead on dry skin and refuse to evaporate. A bowl left upright would fill too quickly; a bowl set upside down would ring. The traveler’s shadow might enter a basin and fail to leave until the body turned back to release it. If the traveler cut a new groove in stone to let trapped water escape, the place would brighten around the act. If the traveler sealed a crack, even accidentally, the wind would acquire a reedy, almost human displeasure.

To live there, one would have to become somewhat like them: careful, permeable, suspicious of closure. One would learn to sleep under leaks, to carry containers that cannot truly contain, to read weather in wrong reflections, to trust the open gap at the bottom of the circle. One would stop asking where water comes from and ask instead where it is trying to go.

The inhabitants are not dead people, but they may be what people look like after the plateau has taught them its first law completely.

They are seepage with memory.

They are drainage given custom.

They are the old hands of the place, loosened from wrists, continuing to guide the rain.
