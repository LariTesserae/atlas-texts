What lives there is not one creature, though one may sometimes seem to be implied by the chamber’s breathing. The place is inhabited by a whole small civilization of things that have never known anything unmade, and so do not distinguish between pipe and cliff, vent and spring, light panel and sun.

They are the descendants of accidents, maintenance cultures, escaped stock, cleaning organisms, repair machines, and whatever stubborn life first survived the warm industrial water. None of them are pure anymore. Every lineage has been edited by pressure, copper, heat, darkness, minerals, current, and the patience of machinery.

They are creatures of the tank that no longer know it is a tank.

The smallest are the true owners of the place: films, threads, blooms, skins. Green-gold algae slick the old alloys and make the first softness over the hard made walls. Blue-black bacterial pads grow in pressure shadows where the current slackens. Rust-colored mats feed on iron and manganese, turning corrosion into velvet. Clear mucus veils span the corners of grates, trembling when pumps change rhythm. These beings read the chamber more precisely than any animal. They know which bronze mouth will exhale cold water, which crack brings warmth, which hairline seam leaks the faint sweetness of nutrient gel from some ruptured cartridge deep in the wall.

Over them graze the little mouths.

There are coin-sized wall-creepers, soft and pale underneath, armored above with flakes of mineral crust stolen from the chamber itself. They move so slowly that they seem like stains until they lift and reveal the clean crescent they have scraped behind them. Their mouths are circular rasps. Their bodies pulse with the pump beat. When pressure rings through the room, they flatten until they are nearly indistinguishable from the deposits around them.

There are glass-jointed shrimp-things, almost invisible except for their black pinhead eyes and the green flecks showing through their bellies. They move in nervous commas, plucking at filaments, grooming the leaves of ribbon plants, vanishing backward into screw holes when shadows pass over them. Some have picked up metallic habits: a faint preference for charged surfaces, a tendency to gather near exposed wires, a twitching response to the dimming cycle of the lights. At night they glitter briefly, not with their own light, but with reflected panel-glow along their limbs.

Tiny silver fish live in loose, flashing schools, though “fish” is only partly correct. Their bodies are flattened like filings, narrow and flexible, made for slots and sudden reversals. They graze, scatter, gather, and scatter again. They sleep in the mouths of old vents with their heads pointed outward into the current, each one held in place by minute fin adjustments. To them the chamber is vast: a world of trenches, overhangs, forests, caverns, hot rivers, cold storms, and forbidden ducts.

Longer things move between these smaller lives.

The translucent eels use the cable trays as roads. They are ribbon-bodied, blunt-headed, and almost boneless in motion. Their inner organs show as faint shadows; their spines sometimes pulse blue-white when they pass near frayed wiring or rest against a live conduit. They are not electric in the ancestral sense. They borrow. They accumulate little charges and spend them in startled flashes, enough to stun a shrimp, confuse a silver school, or warn another eel away from a favored hollow.

They know the reversal schedule of the pumps. Before the pull begins, they coil through anchor points and loop themselves around roots, conduit brackets, corroded rungs. The young that fail to learn are taken sideways into the ducts. Some ducts return them days later, scraped and blind. Some do not return them at all.

The plants are not passive scenery. They are inhabitants too, with old maintenance ancestry folded into them.

The ribbon plants are the most visible: long olive leaves rooted in fastener holes, inspection sockets, gasket seams. Their veins carry copper dust, and in strong light they look like thin hammered metal held inside living tissue. They comb the water. They close during dim cycles. When cold rain enters, they fold inward with the disciplined quickness of engineered equipment shutting down.

The miniature mangrove-forms are slower, stranger. Their pale tendrils grip cable bundles and broken housings, exploring not for soil but for residue: salts, cartridge crumbs, insulation rot, warm condensation along the edges of pipework. Some still show obsolete serial numbers embedded near their bases, nearly hidden by barklike swelling. They may once have been filtration stock, designed to stabilize water chemistry. Now they are little trees of the machine-sea, forming groves in alcoves where no mud has ever existed.

Bladder-leaves drift near the ceiling, tethered to hooks, wires, or their own old root fibers. They rise and sink by adjusting gases inside pearl-colored sacs. During storm input they are driven downward in pale clusters, then ascend again when warmth returns, bruised but intact. Small animals shelter among them as if among clouds.

The predators are few, because the world is small.

Needlefish wait in the pump-shadow: narrow, warm-water hunters with black eyes and bodies the color of tarnished glass. They hold so still that silt settles on their backs. Then, when a school loosens or a shrimp strays too far from cover, they strike with a speed that seems mechanical, a straight-line thrust like something released by a spring. Afterward the vegetation continues waving, and the remaining silver fish reform around the absence.

There are also flat ambush eels, paler than the cable-runners, who bury themselves in mats of loose fiber near the central trench. They hunt by pressure. When rain-input blinds the chamber with bubbles, they become bold, lifting from the substrate and taking prey that cannot see them coming.

But the most important individual creature, or the one that most resembles a keeper, lives in the grated trench at the center.

It is crablike, but not entirely an animal.

Its shell plates resemble oxidized steel because some of them may be oxidized steel, overgrown, replaced, incorporated, laminated with chitin or ceramic or mineral secretion. It has eight delicate legs tipped for gripping mesh, and one asymmetrical manipulator-claw, pale at the point, still bearing the smooth hardness of manufactured ceramic. Its eyes are small and milky. It does not need them much. It feels vibration through the grate and reads the bubble columns with its limbs.

It tends the vents.

When fibrous algae clog the upflow, it climbs into the white churn and pries the mats loose. It eats part of what it removes and releases the rest, feeding the waiting fish. When mineral crust narrows a jet, it chips at the rim. When a dead eel lodges across a bubble mouth, the crab-thing dismembers it with patient, janitorial precision. It may be descended from a repair unit given soft parts by time, or from an animal that learned to wear machinery, or from both through some long confusion of design and adaptation. In this place, that distinction has lost authority.

The other creatures treat it less as a predator than as weather. They avoid its claws but gather around its work. Its maintenance creates feeding storms. Its mistakes change currents. When it dies — if it dies before another replaces it — a vent may close, a warm column may fail, and one quarter of the chamber may become a different climate.

There are also things that live half-outside the visible room.

Pale fish press sometimes against the observation window from the dark cavity beyond. They are flattened by generations in narrow passages, their faces broad and soft, their eyes reduced to silver dimples. They feed on what the pressure surges push through cracks. They may never enter the green chamber, but they know its light as a glow beyond glass, a false moon in the wall.

Cables drift there too, and sheets of loosened insulation like shed skin. Among them move rootlike colonies, blind and listening, flattened against the window by pressure changes. The animals inside avoid that alcove because something about it is too large. Not dangerous in the simple sense, perhaps, but connected to volumes the chamber’s creatures cannot survive. The window sings, and their bodies remember to stay away.

Some inhabitants are not alive in the usual way.

Old sensors still wake at intervals, tasting water they no longer report to anyone. A maintenance lens turns a fraction once every few days, its view completely obscured by algae. Exposed wires snap and whisper. Valves open with arthritic force. Pumps reverse. Heat rises from below on a nineteen-minute memory. These mechanisms are not separate from the ecosystem; they are its elder fauna, immense and embedded, too large to see whole. Their behaviors are seasons. Their malfunctions are migrations, famines, blooms.

The living creatures have evolved around these rhythms until instinct and schedule are the same thing.

They know the first tremor before a cold pulse enters from the upper bronze mouths. They know to cling when the pump-note drops into reversal. They know the dimming of the panels as evening, though no sun has ever set for them. They know exterior thunder as a command to contract, flatten, hide, seal, or hunt. They know mineral dust as feast. They know waste heat as summer.

They do not know stone.

They do not know sky.

They do not know that water can have a surface open to air.

The chamber’s inhabitants are therefore innocent of nature, but not unnatural. Their world is made of second purposes. A bolt is a cave. A gauge is a reef. A warning stripe is camouflage. A leak is a spring. A corroded ladder is a cliff face furred with edible green. The old artificial mountain has become their geology so completely that to remove the machinery would be to destroy the wilderness.

What lives there is a warm-water ecology of maintenance and forgetting: shrimp with glass limbs, silver slot-fish, charged eels, rasping wall-grazers, needle predators, manufactured mangroves, copper-veined ribbons, bacterial enamels, and a crablike vent-keeper with a ceramic claw.

But more than any single species, what lives there is adaptation itself.

A small, old, green-lit society has made a sea out of a room.
