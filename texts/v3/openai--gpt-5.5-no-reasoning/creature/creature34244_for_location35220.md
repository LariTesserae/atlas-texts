They are people of ledges, gauges, and second thoughts.

Not small people, exactly, though distance often makes them seem so: dark figures crossing suspended walks in twos and threes, lanterns at their hips, hoods up against the wet, one hand always near a rail whether or not they trust it. From another ridge they look like soot-marks moving across the flank of a machine. Up close they are sturdy in the particular way of those who live where falling is a civic fact. Their bodies learn early to lean into slopes, to sleep through vibration, to pause before entering a fog, to test a stair with the foot before giving it weight.

They are not a single folk so much as an accumulation, like the city itself. Quarry families whose grandparents followed the first black stone upward. Condenser crews from colder plateaus. Foundry dynasties with names stamped into iron lintels. Garden keepers descended from refugees who arrived carrying soil wrapped in cloth. Cable operators, glasswrights, boiler-priests, map-menders, goat herders, echo archivists, rail children, anomaly surveyors, moss landlords, fog-bottlers, roof farmers, bird catchers, municipal clerks, bathhouse attendants, and the patient mechanics of things that should not work but do.

Their faces tend toward weather: chapped cheeks, pale lips, red knuckles, eyelashes damp with mist. Many have a permanent squint from watching through cloud and steam. Hair is worn tied back, braided close, wrapped in scarves, clipped under caps, or cropped short to avoid machinery. Beards are common among older workers but kept divided by brass pins when near belts and gears. Children go about with runny noses, scratched shins, and a terrifying ease on narrow places, hopping drainage channels and balance-walking parapets while adults shout without expecting obedience.

Their clothes are layered, patched, and practical to the point of ornament. Wool under oilcloth, leather aprons over quilted jackets, gloves hung from cords, boots hobnailed or magnet-soled depending on district custom and family wealth. Most coats have extra fastenings for sudden wind. Many carry small brass instruments the way others might carry knives: pocket levels, pressure whistles, fog compasses, echo tokens, little plumb bobs on chains. A person from the Boiler Terraces can be recognized by heat-scorched cuffs and eyebrows singed thin. Someone from the Moss Courts smells faintly of leaf mold. Crown Works laborers come home with feathers in their collars and a glaze of condensed cloud on their sleeves.

They are not superstitious in the simple sense. They are too technical for that, and too intimate with danger. But they are courteous to uncertainty.

They greet certain staircases before descending. They knock on retired doors. They leave broth in the drainage galleries, not because they can explain why the pale foundry salamanders matter, but because the week they forgot, three rollers jammed, a furnace throat cracked, and an apprentice heard his own death spoken from a culvert in his mother’s voice. They do not whistle in the echo vaults. They cover mirrors during large recalibrations. They mark new cracks in red, old cracks in blue, cracks that have moved twice in green. They spit—not rudely, but formally—before crossing bridges whose far ends are uncertain in bad weather.

Their relationship with the city is less ownership than negotiation. A house is not something one has; it is something one maintains a treaty with. Walls are shimmed, roofs chained, foundations adjusted by seasonal custom. Families inherit not only rooms but correction schedules: which beam must be tightened after frost, which window opens only during low pressure, which cellar corner grows mushrooms and which cellar corner occasionally contains a different cellar.

A respectable household keeps three maps: the official cloth map, the family map, and the map no one shows visitors. The official map bears district stamps and approved routes. The family map has annotations in many hands: “safe in dry fog,” “Aunt Rena lost two hours here,” “goat path, not for funerals,” “stairs shorter if carrying bread.” The private map records grudges, favors, warm vents, hidden cistern taps, doors that answer politely, places where one can cry without being overheard until next winter.

They value maintenance almost as a moral category. To repair is honorable; to neglect is shameful; to build without leaving room for future adjustment is considered vulgar, reckless, nearly obscene. Their proverbs are full of this.

“Leave slack for the mountain.”

“Trust a rivet after its third winter.”

“Measure twice, descend once.”

“No bridge is finished until it has forgiven a storm.”

A child’s education begins with counting steps, identifying bells, reading water seals, and knowing which animals belong to whom. Arithmetic is practical, severe, and full of exceptions. Six crates minus two crates may be four crates unless the pulley returns before the shipment leaves, in which case weight must be recalculated. A corridor may have twelve doors going east and nine coming back. In the Unmeasured Ward, children learn to multiply by smell and divide by stairwell. They become quick-minded, but not abstract in the clean academy sense. Their intelligence has calluses.

Schoolrooms are built low and warm when possible. Lessons are frequently interrupted by announcements from the Department of Gradient and Anomaly. Students practice evacuation drills for ordinary fires, reverse rain, echo return, stair retirement, animal incursion, and “sudden map invalidity.” They keep personal slates, chalk, lunch tins, and emergency wool socks. Older students apprentice early: half-days in glasshouses, cable sheds, cistern offices, aviary markets, or municipal survey crews. The brightest may become engineers. The most careful may become water clerks. The ones who can hear the difference between a normal echo and a returning one are taken aside quietly and trained for the archive.

They are a bureaucratic people, though not passionless. The city breeds paperwork because paperwork is a kind of railing. Water allotments, bridge tolerances, fog harvests, goat ownership, cistern purity, chimney permissions, anomalous incident reports, echo licenses, temporary hallway extension permits: all are recorded in ledgers guarded against damp by waxed covers and tin cabinets. A clerk with a dry pen can command more awe than a foreman with a crane.

Yet bureaucracy here is intimate. The clerk knows whose grandmother needs extra hot water for her joints. The surveyor knows which tenement has been leaning bravely for thirty years. The cable inspector remembers the names of workers lost to fog, misstep, and distance behaving badly. Official stamps bear human hesitation. Rules are enforced firmly, except when they are not, and everyone knows the difference between corruption and mercy.

Their food is dense, smoky, sour, and ingenious. They eat what can be carried up, grown in boxes, cultured in warmth, trapped on ledges, or coaxed from damp. Fungal loaves, black-crusted and chewy. Goat cheese wrapped in fern leaves. Pickled cabbage dark as lacquer. Beetle flour cakes fried in rendered fat. Tubers mashed with lamp-warmed herbs. Cliff eggs, speckled and strong-tasting. Soups thickened with moss starch. Rare fish sold in slivers for feast days. Children chew dried berries from rooftop gardens until their tongues turn blue. Factory workers drink mineral tea from dented tins, bitter enough to wake the dead or at least recall a delayed echo.

Water is never casual. It is poured with attention. Visitors who leave cups unfinished are judged silently and remembered for years. Weddings involve shared water from both families’ cistern shares, mixed in a glass tube and held up to the light. Funerals use a damp cloth across the brow, then the remaining moisture is returned to moss or root. A newborn’s first public blessing is not by flame but by condensation: the child is carried at dawn to a cold pipe or condenser fin, and the first bead that gathers is touched to the tongue.

Religion, where it exists, is practical, polyphonic, and local. There are official chapels under green glass, with hymn pipes tuned to the wind and altars of basalt, brass, and collected water. There are saints of safe descent, patron dead of boiler rooms, household spirits that may once have been ancestors or echoes or misremembered maintenance instructions. The Crown Works has its own rites involving fog, pressure readings, and the cleaning of condenser fins. Quarry crews speak to the unborn mountain as if to a difficult child. Gardeners bury fingernail parings in imported soil and ask the leaves to keep drinking.

They do not imagine heaven as a place above. Above is wet, cold, thin, dangerous, and full of municipal infrastructure. If they imagine rest, it is level ground, perhaps. A room that remains the same size. A bed not shimmed. A basin of water one need not account for.

But many would not leave.

They complain constantly, beautifully, with the craft of generations. They complain about damp socks, tax valves, migratory gulls, municipal paint, spoiled fog, shrieking belts, ambitious goats, new cracks, old cracks, inspectors, lack of inspectors, children on roofs, old people blocking trams, young people trusting trams, the price of lamp oil, the unreliability of east-facing doors, and the fact that nothing in the city stays put except debts. Yet when lowland visitors speak pityingly, the citizens grow cold. This is not a failed plain. It is itself.

Their humor is dry because everything else is wet. It favors understatement and timing. If a bridge vanishes into fog, someone will say, “Shorter today.” If a delayed alarm returns three months late, someone will mutter, “Typical administration.” If rain falls upward, children run bowls beneath balconies while adults pretend not to be impressed. The city’s impossibilities are frightening, but also domestic; people joke with what they cannot defeat.

They are good neighbors by necessity and quarrelsome for the same reason. Privacy is cherished but porous. Sound travels strangely; smells pass through walls; danger rarely respects property lines. If a beam cracks, six households come running before the owner can be proud. If a cistern seal fails, an entire court forms a bucket line in silence. If a goat births twins on someone else’s roof, ownership arguments begin only after the kids are warm. Festivals are loud because ordinary life is already loud, and so celebration must add bells, horns, drums, cable gongs, raven calls imitated badly, and children beating spoons on railings.

Their festivals follow weather, water, and maintenance rather than clear seasons. The First Heavy Condensation is celebrated with moss cakes and green lanterns. The Regrading brings processions of rail workers carrying polished levels. On Echo Night, safe recorded echoes are released in public squares: old songs, historic speeches, market jokes, the laughter of people dead a century. Sometimes an unauthorized echo slips through and the crowd falls silent, listening as some forgotten worker curses a jammed valve, or a child calls for a mother, or rain from twenty years ago passes audibly overhead.

The animals are not pets in the lowland sense. They are citizens of a less accountable kind.

The goats are thieves, climbers, omens, nuisances, milk-givers, insulation-chewers, weather predictors, and unofficial inspectors of structural confidence. A goat standing calmly on a bridge reassures everyone. A goat refusing a stair can close a route faster than a municipal sign. Some families mark their goats with colored horn bands. Others swear the goats alter their own markings to avoid fines.

Ravens are treated with respect bordering on exasperation. The white-throated ones learn whistles, names, warnings, and insults. A raven that mimics a pressure alarm can empty a workshop in seconds and will then eat unattended lunches. Certain old ravens haunt the Crown Works and know more about shift changes than management does. Pigeons carry messages, though not always faithfully. Mothbirds gather in lamps and are considered unlucky to kill, partly from tenderness and partly because their wings sometimes show tiny moving images of streets not yet built.

The amphibians are given the particular reverence reserved for things that should not exist but have persisted longer than argument. Cistern frogs are counted during water audits. Copper-flecked newts in condensation trays are signs of clean metal. The long pale salamanders below the foundries are spoken of indirectly: “the underkeepers,” “the soft ones,” “those below heat.” Children dare each other to glimpse them through drain grates. Adults leave broth.

There are also other inhabitants, less easily named.

Some may be human only by courtesy. In the Unmeasured Ward live families who have adapted to repetition and spatial error so thoroughly that their homes seem to fold around them. They appear in doorways one could swear were not there a moment before. Their children count on extra fingers that are not always visible. Their elders can walk a thirteen-third-floor tenement without hesitation and become impatient with outsiders who insist on ordinal consistency.

The echo archivists change over time. No one knows whether the vaults alter them or merely reveal a necessary temperament. They speak softly, with pauses after every sentence, as if leaving room for future returns. Their hearing is trained to distinguish fresh speech from aged speech, local resonance from traveled sound, harmless memory from dangerous recurrence. They wear padded collars and carry tuning forks. Some older archivists are followed by their own voices half a breath late.

The anomaly surveyors are regarded with admiration, annoyance, and pity. They are the city’s professional doubters. They test staircases, measure gradient drift, confirm whether plazas remain themselves, and retire public infrastructure when necessary. Their gray coats are streaked with rain and mineral dust; their boots have replaceable soles; their instruments are polished obsessively. They are invited to weddings because they bring luck, but seated near exits because they may be called away at any moment to determine whether an alley has become tomorrow.

Then there are the Crown Workers.

They live near the top, among condensers, algae curtains, and birds. They are quieter than most. Their lungs are strong, their skin often raw from wet cold, their hands scarred by fins and ice. They move through cloud as others move through rooms. Their lamps are hooded amber. They know the moods of metal forests: when the fins will sing, when to expect rime, when a bird-clogged gutter will flood a district below, when the cloud has gone strange and voices will not carry properly. They sleep in narrow bunks with boots ready beside them, because water is the city’s blessing and burden, and the Crown Works must not stop.

The dead remain present but not morbidly so. Cemeteries are difficult on young stone and valuable land; bodies are cremated in municipal furnaces, interred in wall niches, composted into sanctioned garden soil, or, for certain guilds, sealed into counterweights and foundation masses with nameplates attached. Ancestors are not imagined as distant. Their adjustments remain in beams, their handwriting in maps, their warnings in margins, their voices sometimes literally returning in damp weather.

The people have an unusual sense of time. They live in increments: the turn of a flywheel, the drip of cistern water, the years required for moss to cover a wall, the generation needed for a district to settle into its name. They are not impatient with slow change, but they are intensely alert to small change. A new sound in a cable. A doorframe pinched by a finger’s width. A familiar alley smelling of ozone instead of cabbage. The absence of frogs. The arrival of condensation where none was scheduled.

To outsiders they may seem fatalistic. They are not. Fatalism would be too lazy. They believe in intervention, shoring, bracing, rerouting, petitioning, recalculating, feeding the boilers, feeding the goats, feeding whatever lives below the foundry, and showing up with a wrench before dawn. They know disaster intimately enough not to romanticize it. The heroic ideal is not the person who conquers the mountain. It is the person who notices the third bolt loosening before the bridge learns to scream.

They are also makers of beauty, though they distrust useless delicacy. Their beauty clings, hangs, glows, endures damp. Brass railings polished by generations of hands. Green glass roofs patched in deliberate patterns. Doorframes carved with plumb lines, fern spirals, ravens, pipes, and saints holding tools. Children’s mittens knitted in district colors. Water tanks painted with fierce municipal pride. Lamps shaded blue in mourning, amber for safe return, red for pressure hazard, green for feast. Moss walls clipped into soft geometric reliefs. Factory whistles tuned to chords so shift change becomes, accidentally and then officially, music.

Music travels oddly there. Songs are built for call and delay. Work chants leave spaces where echoes may answer. Brass bands play slowly across ravines, letting the far district return the melody in fragments. Lullabies include instructions: sleep, little one, the west wall holds; sleep, little one, the rain is falling downward; sleep, little one, the bell has not rung thrice. Children learn to sing in rounds not because it is quaint, but because the city itself insists on participating late.

Love is conducted under difficult logistics. Courtship involves walking someone home by a reliable route, sharing dry gloves, bringing water without being asked, remembering which stair their aunt refuses, or helping regrade a family threshold. Lovers carve initials not into trees—too precious—but into replaceable shims and hidden metal plates. Marriage contracts include cistern rights, goat liabilities, room drift clauses, and mutual obligations in the event that one spouse becomes temporally inconvenient.

Old age is honored when it comes with memory. Elders sit in warm market corners and tell younger people where streets used to be, which bridges once sagged before they rose, what the Third Cistern sounded like before the lining was replaced, when the ravens first learned the noon whistle wrong. Their stories are practical archives, but also myth. Every district has at least one old person who insists that in childhood the sky was higher, the stone softer, the city quieter, and the anomalies better behaved.

Whether this is true is impossible to prove.

Beneath all of them, around all of them, within their customs and tools and lungs, lives the sense that the mountain is not scenery but neighbor, adversary, infant, god, patient, and unfinished machine. They do not worship it uniformly, but they address it constantly. Every drilled bolt, drainage channel, retaining wall, staircase, cistern, and warning sign is a sentence spoken to the still-forming world:

Hold here.

Drain there.

Do not fall today.

Let us pass.

Give us water.

Take this weight.

Wait until morning.

And sometimes the mountain answers badly. Sometimes it answers generously. More often it shifts by a hair’s breadth and requires a committee.

So they live alert and damp, ingenious and stubborn, tender toward small green things, severe about structural negligence, amused by impossibility, respectful of animals, suspicious of straight lines, loyal to districts that may not occupy the same coordinates next decade. They are citizens of a city that is never quite finished becoming accessible to itself.

Each morning, when the mist pales and bells begin their careful sequence, they wake and listen before rising. Not out of fear only, but out of manners. A drip has moved. A beam has settled. A raven is imitating an alarm two streets over. Somewhere a surveyor is already painting a new crack red. Somewhere a goat has reached a roof no goat should have reached. Somewhere a delayed word is coming back through a drain, older than when it left.

Then boots are pulled on. Kettles are lit. Gauges are checked. Broth is set out beneath the foundry. Children are buttoned into coats. The city, having altered slightly in the night, is met again by those who know how to live with alteration.

They are its inhabitants: not masters of the high black unfinished place, not victims of it, but its maintenance crew, its witnesses, its negotiators, its parasites and gardeners, its clerks and singers, its warm-blooded instruments.

They live there because the city lives by being lived in.
