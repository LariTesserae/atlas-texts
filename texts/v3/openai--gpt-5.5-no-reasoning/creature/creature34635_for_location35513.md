What lives there is not a single species, exactly, but a custodial ecology: a population of makers, tenders, half-animals, ritual gardeners, and things that are only alive because the place keeps choosing them.

The true inhabitants are probably the keepers.

They are rarely visible in the open grove, not because they are absent, but because they have learned not to appear whole where the light can make decisions about them. They live in the thickness of the walls, in the unmade rooms beyond painted doors, in crawlspaces behind plaster membranes and beneath lifted floor-plates. They are the source of the dry brushing sounds, the fresh powder in the basins, the peeled sheaths, the sharpened reed-tubes, the corrected labels on jars. They are the ones who know which roots may be combed and which must be apologized to, which fruits are to be rolled into shadow, which animals must not be counted twice.

They are small, or they make themselves small.

Not dwarfish in a human sense, not childish. More like people compressed by custom and architecture. Their bodies suit the low paths, the under-shelf chambers, the tight service hollows behind ceramic walls. They move with folded knees and narrow shoulders, wearing mantles that look at first like bundles of dried leaves, cut fiber, shed skins, shell tiles, and carefully tied scraps of stained plaster. When crouched among the plants, they resemble work left unfinished: a heap of tools, a rack of drying husks, a stack of barklike plates. Then a hand emerges, three long fingers and a thumb set low, and adjusts the angle of a root.

Their skin is matte and pale-brown, or ash-grey, or the color of unfired clay depending on which powders they have handled that week. It holds marks easily. Not scars so much as registrations: thumbprints, dust-lines, old burns, mineral blooms, pale bands where masks have rested. Their faces are difficult to remember. Some wear actual masks, but even without them their features seem arranged for practical intimacy rather than expression. Narrow eyes under heavy lids. Nostrils that open and close delicately to sort scents into place. Small mouths kept covered with woven veils, partly from discipline and partly because breath itself is a tool here and must not be wasted.

They do not speak often into the air. Air carries sound badly, and besides, spoken words weigh things down.

They communicate by tapping trellises, scraping bone pins along fired ridges, knotting cords, shifting powders into tiny heaps, touching wrists, blinking from behind masks, setting stones in niches that may later revise themselves. A message passed through the lattice can cross the entire grove as a tremor too faint for a stranger to notice. A reprimand may be no more than one dry click from a ceramic ring. A warning may be a change in smell: pepper resin opened, bitter pollen disturbed, ash-seed cracked underfoot.

When they must speak, they speak softly and concretely. Their language seems built from prohibitions, measurements, relations, seasons of opening, degrees of dryness, and the moral condition of materials. It has many words for “almost cracked,” “not to be named yet,” “fed with former brightness,” and “safe if no one remembers water.” It has few words for rain. Those that exist are spoken only through masks.

They are not primitive, though nothing around them is mechanical. Their knowledge is exact, old in method even if the world itself is young. They practice a technology of touch: pressure, heat, timing, abrasion, scent, patience. A keeper can tell by pressing one knuckle to a cartridge whether the salts within have begun to sour. They can read the angle of a shadow and know which wall will accept a painted doorway. They taste powders with the back of a fingernail. They listen to roots through hollow sticks. They warm resin eggs under their sleeves and know which ones contain fruit, which contain insects, and which contain memories that should be buried in ash.

They cultivate life the way others might maintain law.

The plants are not wild to them, but neither are they tame. Each installed root, each dry vessel, each braided tendril participates in an agreement. The keepers do not command the grove; they negotiate with it through repetition. Every morning, if mornings can be trusted, they sift mineral milk over the grey ribbon-plants. They polish glass-thread fronds with stiff brushes until the fronds remember to quiver. They scrape dead leaf-matter into shallow trays, burn it without flame under black ceramic lids, and feed the shining residue back into slots beside the parent stems. They rotate porous loaves for the sleeping fox-sized animals. They remove old signs when the wall has revised too far for the rule to remain safe.

They are frightened of water with a fear deeper than superstition. It is theological, chemical, historical. Their oldest masks show swollen cheeks and running eyes. Their deepest carvings are the water-laws. Children, if they have children, are taught first not to pour, not to spit carelessly, not to cry openly. Grief is caught in cloth and dried near the alkali troughs. Illness is handled with powders, fumes, heat, and binding. A cup is a dangerous shape. A bowl must prove itself by holding ash.

Yet they are not joyless.

Their pleasures are dry pleasures: the clean snap of a sheath removed whole; the scent of a fruit blister at perfect ripeness; the successful opening of a flower after forty days of waiting; the subtle rearrangement of a niche that confirms the grove has accepted a change. They decorate tools with embedded teeth, paint the undersides of shelves where only crawling animals can see, braid shed plant-fibers into wrist cords, and carve small laughing mouths on pumice idols hidden among the roots. They make jokes by relabeling harmless powders with terrifying knots. They celebrate by releasing moths into stored light.

The small animals belong to them and do not belong to them.

The mouthless parchment lizards are sun-calibrators. They lie flat against warm tiles and turn their translucent bodies toward whatever hour the light is pretending to be. The keepers read their posture to decide when to open powder jars.

The ceramic beetles are not quite animals and not quite tools. They click when a seam shifts, when a root-cartridge dries unevenly, when a painted door becomes too nearly real. Some are born from eggs. Some are repaired. Some are found already dead inside walls that did not exist the day before.

The fox-sized sleepers are resin-dreamers. Their tails wrap around mineral loaves because the loaves absorb whatever would otherwise become dampness in sleep. Their dreams thicken the hanging pockets where certain plants germinate. They are treated gently, even reverently. A keeper waking one without cause must wear a bitter mask for a full cycle.

The jointed seed-carriers are the grove’s pollinators and archivists. They carry dust, yes, but also tiny instructions: what color a flower should attempt, what smell belongs near which basin, which plant came from which burnt ancestor. The baskets on their backs are grown, not made, but the keepers trim and lacquer them. Some seed-carriers become vain and collect useless glittering flakes. These are tolerated unless they begin making maps.

The moths are less trusted. They drift by loopholes in gravity and sometimes pass through painted outlines before the door itself has arrived. A moth returning with outside dust is caught under a shell cup and questioned by scent. If it smells of openness, it is released into the central basin. If it smells of moisture, it is sealed in red resin.

There are also inhabitants that are not animals.

The walls live slowly. Not metaphorically. They digest touch, retain instructions, and alter their contents according to rules the keepers only partly understand. A wall may decide that a niche has always contained a sleeping animal. It may accept a painted ladder as a way to change the sky. It may reject a door by letting the pigment flake off in the shape of teeth. The keepers feed walls with plaster, bone dust, stories tapped into lattices, and occasionally obsolete tools. A tool given to a wall is not lost; it becomes precedent.

The shadows live there too. They are small domestic presences, attached to leaves, tiles, trellises, masks, but not always obediently. Some shadows are older than the objects casting them. Some arrive early and wait. Some braid together in corners and exchange hours. The keepers avoid stepping across braided shadows unless carrying ash-seeds. If a leaf casts a person’s shadow, the person stands still because the leaf is borrowing their outline to remember how weight works.

The central black basin may be the oldest inhabitant, though it looks like architecture.

It is warm, petaled, circular, and closed. The keepers do not stand inside it. They approach its rim with covered faces and empty hands. Resin eggs are sometimes placed along its petals; cracked tools are laid there; dead seed-carriers are arranged head outward as if listening. Nothing is burned there, and yet afterward things are reduced, clarified, made feedable. It may be a hearth that learned restraint. It may be a mouth that gave up speech to keep the place dry. It may be where the grove negotiates with whatever lies beneath the fitted floor.

Something does lie beneath.

The sagging plates suggest a body, or a memory of a body, broad and patient under the constructed ground. The keepers know it. They avoid saying it. When a floor seam opens wider than a fingernail, they press powdered bone into it and tap three times. When the granular settling grows louder, they lower their masks and move the smallest animals to higher shelves. The body beneath is not dead, but it is not awake in any ordinary way. It may be the first room. It may be the thing the grove was built to cover. It may be the source of the rule against water, because water would soften the floor and let the underneath remember its shape.

The keepers may have made the grove, but they may also have been made by it.

Their young are perhaps not born wet, as human children are. Perhaps they are assembled from dry inheritances: a scale from a parent’s wrist, breath caught in a fiber bag, powdered tooth, warmed clay, a seed carried too long by one of the jointed things. Perhaps they emerge from wall-niches after being painted there for several seasons. Perhaps the masks come first, and faces grow behind them.

However they begin, they are taught slowness before skill. A young keeper learns to wait beside a cracking tile until it finishes. Learns to pass through odor chambers without mixing them. Learns that a plant named too soon becomes heavy. Learns that abundance is not the same as excess. Learns that dryness is not lack, but covenant.

They maintain the grove because the grove is a pocket of reality still under construction. Outside is blank warm grit, a field waiting to be made. Inside, things have names, weights, scents, shadows, habits, prohibitions. The keepers are not merely gardeners. They are editors of existence. They prevent the place from becoming too fluid, too planetary, too remembered by the wrong laws. They preserve the local rules by performing them.

And now, behind the newly appearing wall, there are more of them.

The soft dry brushing is their preparation. They are sweeping dust into the shape of future paths. Hanging empty pockets. Pressing thumb marks into fresh plaster so it will know it was made by hands. Painting a doorway before cutting it open. Setting out low stools for workers not yet remembered. Filling shallow basins with ash and salt, raking them into maps of a room that will become true only if approached correctly.

One of them pauses when the ceramic beetle turns its head.

It knows the grove has noticed.

So it lowers its brush, presses two fingers to the wall from the other side, and waits for the painted outline to deepen into a gap.
