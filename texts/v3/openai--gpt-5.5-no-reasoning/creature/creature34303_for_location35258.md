What lives there is not a population so much as a dry, thinking ecology wearing the shape of a city.

At the center of it all lives the city-vegetation itself: a vast communal organism, or federation of organisms, descended from plants but long past anything that would once have been called forest. It does not drink. It mines. It listens. It computes. It builds with its own body.

Its oldest members are the atmospheric trunks and civic spires: immense hollow columns rooted deep into fractured ore and dead machinery, rising into thin charged air. They are not trees in any ordinary sense. They are engines, elders, organs, archives. Their crowns comb wind for static, dust, ions, and cosmic faintness. Their roots taste buried metals and geological exhalations. Their interiors boom and click with pressure, charge, and memory. They speak by resonance, by shadow-pulse, by the tightening of grown cables strung between towers. Around them the smaller growths arrange themselves like districts around institutions.

The ground is alive too. The roads are hardened root-skin. The plazas are managed absences. The old foundations are covered in mat-growth that remembers streets no eye has walked for ages. These mats are low, black-violet, fibrous, almost furred with sensory cilia. They harden under pressure, lift to passing shadows, collect dust according to static and wind. They are pavement, nerve, archive, and mouth. To step on them is to enter a conversation too slow to be polite.

The walls live as well. Apartment blocks and towers have become honeycombs of bark, cork, horn, lignin, mineral fiber, and old stone inclusions. Their outer plates open and close to light, dust, charge, and passing bodies. Some districts are still “under construction,” though no workers can be seen: root-ribs rise in waves, filaments span streets, chambers peel open and reseal. The city solves itself continuously. Repair, governance, metabolism, and thought are one gesture.

Among this vegetal civilization live animals built for dryness, thrift, and sound.

There are small six-limbed grazers that move in sudden coordinated sheets across the open courts, their hard feet rattling like handfuls of beads thrown over ceramic. They rasp the city’s outer skin, feeding on photosynthetic films, mineral crusts, and charged residues. Their bodies are sealed beneath lacquered plates. Along their backs they carry dry symbiotic gardens: powdery cultures lodged in grooves, processing salts and breath-waste into useful trace nutrients. They are tolerated because they prune, carry spores, disturb charge, and feed larger things.

There are tall browsers like walking tripods, narrow and jointed, patient as surveying instruments. They stand beside living walls and scrape the day’s membrane-growth with black tongues. Their mouths are not wet. Their tongues are abrasive, carbon-dark, edged for harvesting films without tearing structural tissue. They kneel during electrical surges, either in reverence, instinct, or self-preservation, folding their limbs under armored bodies while their back-gardens flare faintly with captured charge.

There are gliders, paper-bodied and angular, roosting beneath wind-vanes and bridge ribs. Folded, they look like dead leaves cut from mica. Open, they become stained scraps of living sail, snapping and booming as they stiffen their wing membranes in the plateau gusts. They feed on airborne dust-organisms, static blooms, and perhaps the city’s minute reproductive particles. They are also messengers whether they know it or not: their wing folds and shell surfaces collect spores, mineral codes, and etched data from one district and carry them to another.

There are narrow-bodied chirping animals nested in tower ribbing. Their calls are metallic, precise, and numerous. Some may be pollinators. Some may be parasites. Some may serve as distributed sensory organs for the city, responding to tension in root-cables or changes in wind direction. They live in crevices warmed by day-facing membranes and shelter inside bark chambers during charge fronts, emerging afterward to scrape salts from exposed plates.

There are ultrasonic swarms so small they are known mainly by trembling dust. They pass through streets as invisible weather. They may feed on microscopic dry life admitted through plant pores. They may be hunted by the gliders. They may also clean the city, stripping dangerous accumulations from sensory surfaces. Their existence is heard only indirectly: a shiver along loose powder, a brief disturbance in the glyph-circuits of root threads, a flicker of tension through a mat that has noticed them.

There are larger prowlers on the outer terraces: sealed, padded, heat-sensing predators adapted to twilight and thin air. Their bodies waste nothing. Their feet are soft enough to cross root-skin without warning clicks. Huge thermal pits open along their skulls or shoulders, reading the faint warmth of browsers, grazers, and gliders against the cold walls. They use moments of citywide glare to hunt, when hidden pigments ignite and prey must choose between freezing and bolting. Their kills do not rot. The remains are stripped, dried, delaminated, polished, sorted by scavengers and static, then taken into pavement, resonator nets, or new growth.

There are detritivores, likely many kinds: crawling, jointed, plated things that live in cracks and beneath lifted mats. They harvest splinters, shed skins, old bark, powdered bone, and rare excrement. They are the city’s small accountants. Nothing soft is left to decay. Nothing valuable is lost if it can be chemically dismantled, electrostatically separated, or delivered to a root-mouth. Their trails may be the finest writing in the city, temporary lines in dust that tell the mats where death has recently become useful.

There may be larger windborne beings as well—creatures that are almost kites, almost seeds, almost animals. Some seed-pods hover over plazas, humming without visible lift. Perhaps they are plant reproductive craft; perhaps animal symbionts; perhaps civic machines grown from tissue. They linger, charge, descend, exchange, and rise again. Their bodies blur the boundary between organism and technology as completely as everything else here.

And somewhere within all this, if any descendants of the original builders remain, they are no longer recognizable as citizens walking streets.

They may live as patterns in the plant architecture: memories stored in growth rings, in shade-pulse languages, in districts that maintain ceremonial alignments though no separate priesthood survives. They may be incorporated into the civic spires, their tools and bones and machines translated into vascular mineral tissue. They may exist as decisions made by the city without names: where to keep an avenue open, when to molt a tower, which animal migration to permit, which plaza to leave empty for wind.

The dominant inhabitant is therefore a civilization without soft bodies.

It is vegetal, mineral, acoustic, electrical. It feeds on gradients instead of rain. It uses animals as carriers, pruners, thieves, singers, pollinators, prey, pressure sensors, and memory-bearers. It values emptiness as another organ. It treats death as building material and sound as atmosphere. Its “people,” if the word applies, are spires, mats, trunks, vanes, rootways, resonator bones, browsing tripods, bead-footed herds, paper gliders, sealed predators, dust swarms, and the dry communal intelligence that arranges them.

What lives there is the plateau’s final answer to thirst:  
a waterless city that became a plant,  
a plant that became infrastructure,  
an infrastructure that learned to think,  
and all the hard, clever creatures that survive by moving through its breath of wind, charge, dust, and sound.
