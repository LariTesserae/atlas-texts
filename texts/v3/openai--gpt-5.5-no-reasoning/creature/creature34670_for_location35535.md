What lives there is not a single people, nor a wilderness in the usual sense, but an immense congregation of thrifty bodies: a whole continent of organisms built around the problem of not losing anything.

Everything that lives there is shaped by dryness. Not merely adapted to drought as to an occasional hardship, but made from drought, governed by it in form, habit, language, and metabolism. The inhabitants are hoarders of trace moisture, readers of shade, miners of vapor, lickers of salt, builders of tunnels, borrowers of other creatures’ shelter. They do not sprawl extravagantly. They pack themselves close. They armor themselves. They open only at the correct hour.

The most dominant inhabitants are the plants, though many of them hardly resemble the familiar idea of plants.

They are thick-skinned, sealed, waxed, folded inward. Some grow as low mats pressed flat against the ground, like overlapping scales or plates of living ceramic. These mats are not passive carpets. They are crowded societies of tiny leaves, valves, storage nodules, rootlets, fungal threads, and mineral-cracking hairs. They creep over new stone and pale dust, fixing loose ground in place. In the morning they loosen themselves just enough to drink the breath of the night. By midmorning they are closed again, each cell guarded.

Other plants rise in columns and clustered trunks. They form the city’s pillars, groves, alleys, and walls. Their stems are swollen with stored sap, but the sap is difficult to steal: bitter, resinous, sometimes alkaline, sometimes threaded with crystals or defensive fibers. Their skins are pale to reflect heat, ribbed to swell and shrink, scarred from the feeding of generations. Some have no true leaves for most of the year, only spines, folded scales, or small leathery tongues that emerge briefly and withdraw. A few carry flowers hidden in grooves, slits, or waxy pores, opening them only when light slants and heat loosens its grip.

The columnar groves are full of secondary life. Dead stems do not fall empty. They remain upright as apartment towers, drilled by borers, lined by larvae, webbed with root-hairs and climbing filaments, hollowed into roosts and chambers. A standing corpse may house more life than a living stem: pale beetles in the pith, mite gardens in old scars, eggs glued beneath lifted bark, birds nesting among thorn crowns, small reptiles sleeping in vertical cracks, fungi folding and unfolding like dry fans.

There are also the almost-rootless things: aerial colonies that hang in veils and ropes from branches, ribs, and rock lips. They comb the air with hairs so fine they seem more like smoke than tissue. They gather moisture from invisible fog, from breath, from night cooling, from the faint difference between stone temperature and air. Some live on dust. Some trap pollen, skin flakes, insect frass, powdered minerals. Their bodies are gray, straw-colored, silver, black-green, or ghostly white, and they make the shaded corridors feel curtained and inhabited.

Lichens and crust-organisms live everywhere. They are the first citizens of fresh stone. When a crack opens in the young plain, they arrive as stains: orange, white, green-black, violet-gray. They soften the mineral face grain by grain. They make footholds for dust, then for threads, then for seeds. Without them the city could not expand so quickly. They are surveyors, masons, and street-cleaners all at once, though they have no tools except chemistry and patience.

The animals are numerous, but most of them are small, low, quiet, and hard to see.

Insects and their kin are the true laboring majority. They move through the place as a hidden infrastructure. Beetles with matte shells drag swollen abdomens full of concentrated moisture or stored oils. Pale larvae tunnel through dead pith. Antlike gatherers strip every fallen scale, seed husk, corpse, and pellet of dung into usable fragments. Mites graze microscopic films from plant skins. Dust-colored flies pollinate narrow evening flowers. Winged beetles emerge at dusk in brief, synchronized clouds, mate, feed, and vanish before heat and predators can find them.

Some insect colonies are almost municipal. They maintain roads under mats and through root buttresses. They farm fungi in sealed chambers where humidity is guarded like treasure. They collect shed plant wax, mineral grains, dry dung, and carcass fragments, sorting them into chambers by use. Their nests may outlive their founding species, becoming layered structures occupied by successors: beetles after ants, spiders after beetles, roots after spiders, burrowing mammals later opening the whole thing into a den.

Spiders and web-makers are common, but their traps are not jeweled with dew. Their lines are dry, taut, nearly invisible, often designed to catch dust as camouflage. Some stretch their snares across corridor pinches where pollinators must pass. Others live in curled dead leaves or thorn tubes, feeling the city through vibrations rather than sight.

Reptiles are among the most visible daytime animals. They look poured from the same minerals as the ground: ash, ocher, chalk-white, pink-gray, cracked brown. Many are flat-bodied and patient. Some run in short bursts between shadows. Others flow along branches and stone plates, bellies barely lifting. They drink rarely in any recognizable sense. They take moisture from prey, from plant flesh, from licking dawn films off waxy stems. Their skins shed in dry translucent scraps, which are immediately gathered by insects or chewed by smaller animals for salts.

There are burrowing reptiles too: blunt-headed, scale-armored, with nostrils that can seal against powder. They hunt larvae and root-feeders beneath the crust. Some spend almost their whole lives under a single district, emerging only during cool hours to mate or migrate when a crack or uplift rearranges their underground world.

The mammals, if they can be called that without importing too much expectation, are careful creatures.

The small ones live close to particular plant-clusters. A single thorn grove may be an entire homeland: feeding ground, nursery, lookout, shelter, cemetery. They hop or creep through known gaps, never crossing open crust unless forced. Their feet are padded or fringed to move silently over flakes and salt. Their ears are large enough to hear movement through stems but often held flat in daytime heat. Their fur is dusty, banded, or mottled; many have pale bellies that reflect warmth when they crouch against stone.

Some are dew-lickers. At dawn they emerge with narrow tongues and delicate incisors, cleaning films of condensation from plant skins, mineral crusts, hanging fibers, and even the backs of larger sleeping animals. They are quick and nervous, because the dew hour is crowded with hunger.

There are grazers built low and broad-mouthed, able to shave succulent mats without tearing them up completely. Their jaws move with slow sideways pressure. Their lips are tough, divided, almost tool-like. They waste little. The mat is cropped, not destroyed, because a destroyed mat leaves bare ground, and bare ground brings heat, exposure, and travel hazards. Herds of these grazers maintain avenues through the vegetation, generation after generation, polishing the pale tracks with their hooves, bellies, knees, or shell-like chest plates.

Larger browsers move through the columnar thickets. They are long-legged and narrow-bodied, shaped to pass between spines and wax trunks without scraping away too much precious skin. Their muzzles are armored against thorns. Some have flexible upper lips or forked tongues to extract folded leaves from guarded stems. They travel in family knots, led not by dominance alone but by memory: which corridors remain shaded at noon, which mineral basins are safe, which vents attract predators, which plants can be eaten after flowering but not before.

Many of the larger animals are water vaults in themselves. Their bodies hold moisture so efficiently that predators, parasites, and scavengers orient toward them as walking wells. Their dung is dry but valuable, packed with salts and partially processed plant fibers. Their urine may be nearly crystalline, deposited in places that soon become tiny mineral gardens for insects and crust-organisms.

Below ground live pale, blind, soft-skinned things that follow warmth, root scent, fungal breath, and the tremors of surface traffic. Some are wormlike mineral grazers. Some are burrowing mammals with sealed eyes and shovel hands. Some are larval forms of creatures that will later emerge winged for only a single night. The underground is not wet, but it is less hostile than the surface: cooler, buffered, chemically rich, full of roots, abandoned chambers, old nests, and the ghost architecture of previous inhabitants.

Birds live there too, though they are economical with sound.

Many are sand-colored and narrow-winged, lifting early on the first thermals. From above they read the city’s patterns: corridors, vents, fresh cracks, herd movement, pollinator blooms. Some are hunters, dropping almost vertically into openings in the thicket. Others are gleaners, picking insects from wax skins or probing dead stems for larvae. Their calls are sparse, often clicks, whistles, or soft rattles that do not carry far. Loud song would be wasted in such packed vegetation and dangerous besides.

Nesting birds favor thorn cylinders, hollow stems, and dead towers. Their nests are dry constructions of fiber, shed skin, root hair, and insect silk. Eggs are matte and mineral-colored. Chicks learn silence before flight. A noisy nest does not remain a nest for long.

There are larger predatory birds that haunt ridges and open flats, but even they are restrained by the density below. They hunt the edges: salt basins, fresh fractures, herd avenues, plazas around warm mineral vents. They wait for an animal to misjudge distance or cross a pale exposed strip at the wrong hour.

Predators on the ground are precise rather than swift.

A long chase is nearly impossible. The city is too tight, too thorned, too full of turns and false passages. Hunters therefore become ambushers, mimics, memory-keepers. Some resemble fallen stems, lying rigid beside corridors until a dew-licker passes. Some have bodies patterned like mineral crust and wait in shallow depressions. Some specialize in the evening flower hour, when pollinators are heavy with scent and the small animals that eat them become careless. Others patrol the same routes for years, learning where grazers must lower their heads, where browsers step sideways, where burrow mouths open into blind corners.

There are also theft-specialists. Moisture thieves pierce succulent stems or feed from the eyes, mouths, wounds, or droppings of larger animals. Parasites are abundant but discreet: mites under scales, threadworms in gut chambers, flies whose young develop in the dampest tissues available. To be alive here is to be a reservoir, however small, and every reservoir has claimants.

Scavengers arrive almost immediately at death. First come scent-readers and vibration-followers: flies, beetles, small reptiles, furtive mammals. Then cutters, borers, draggers, dissolvers. Hide becomes food and shelter. Bones are gnawed for minerals, then drilled, then crusted with lichens. Horns or spines become perches. A carcass does not rot richly in the open; it is dismantled, exported, archived into bodies and burrows.

Communication among these inhabitants is mostly close-range.

They tap stems. They drum on dry crust. They scrape, flick, pulse, change color, lift spines, open scent glands, mark corridors with oils and powdered secretions. Plants communicate chemically through roots and fungal partners, shifting bitterness, opening flowers, warning neighbors of grazers or borers. Animal trails are written in musk, shed skin, dung dust, and the microscopic residues of feet. A corridor is not merely a path; it is a long sentence continually revised.

The social life of the place is dense but not sentimental. Cooperation exists because thrift rewards it. Plants shelter pollinators and punish thieves. Insects clean wounds or farm fungi in root tangles. Grazers keep avenues open that other animals use. Burrowers ventilate crust and create future nurseries. Dead stems house birds whose droppings feed the plant’s descendants. Everything takes, but everything taken is quickly used again.

Around mineral vents, the inhabitants become stranger and more specialized.

There are plants that feed partly on warm exhalations from below, their roots arranged around cracks like mouths around a breath. Their tissues taste metallic or bitter. Films grow there in colors uncommon elsewhere. Insects gather to lick condensates invisible to larger eyes. Some animals visit vents not for water but for salts, warmth, digestive minerals, or the microbial growth that blooms around them. These plazas are dangerous because everyone must come to them eventually. Predators know this. So do scavengers. So do plants whose seeds cling to passing fur and scales.

The youngest cracks in the plain have pioneer communities. First the stains. Then thread mats. Then little thorn seedlings and waxy buttons. Then insects. Then the animals that eat insects. Then the burrowers that follow roots. Within a few seasons, what was raw geology becomes addressable territory: a named route in animal memory, a scent-marked lane, a new district.

What lives there, above all, is a civilization without intention.

No species governs it. No mind planned the avenues, plazas, towers, tunnels, districts, and refuse systems. Yet the total effect is urban because life has become so dense and so rule-bound that it produces order. Its citizens are plants with sealed skins, insects with civic efficiency, reptiles like moving shards of clay, quiet birds in thorn towers, grazers that carve roads, burrowers that make the undercity, predators that memorize intersections, fungi and lichens that turn stone into tenancy.

They live not by abundance of water, but by abundance of use.

They are the residents of a dry city that grows instead of being built: silent, crowded, pale, fragrant with resin and salt, full of hidden traffic. Every creature there is a keeper of moisture, a reader of surfaces, a participant in the long conversion of new mineral plain into living fabric.
