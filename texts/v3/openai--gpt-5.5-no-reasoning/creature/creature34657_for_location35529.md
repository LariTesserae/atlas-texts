What lives there is not one people, not exactly, and not only people.

The city is inhabited by a thin, enduring citizenry shaped by stone, intermittent water, cold light, and the etiquette of things that might fall.

They are human enough to be recognized at a distance: upright figures crossing broad plazas with jars hung from shoulder-poles, children stooping over wet gutters, old ones seated beneath archways with measuring cords in their laps. But long residence below has edited them. Their bodies are narrow and economical, not from starvation alone but from generations of moving through a place where every load must be carried, balanced, lowered, shared. They have long hands, strong wrists, quiet feet. Their skin ranges through chalk-pale, clay-ochre, grey-brown, and the faint bluish pallor of people who live under mineral light. Many have large dark eyes, though they do not need darkness-eyes so much as patience-eyes: eyes trained to notice wetness forming in a wall, the powder at the base of a stressed column, a moth swarm gathering where none gathered yesterday.

Their hair is often cropped, bound, or covered, because loose hair catches dust, condensation, grit, and the attention of animals. Children wear strings of shells, beetle cases, bone counters, little chips of colored stone that name their households, their cistern rights, their current errands. Adults carry knives of bone or stone, water tallies, folded hides, chalk for marking claims, and small personal cups. A cup is intimate. A cup is almost a second name.

They dress in hides, woven gut, felted animal hair, cured skins as thin as cloth, patched mantles, aprons whitened by limestone dust. Textiles are precious where fiber is scarce; garments are kept alive by repair until they become maps of former bodies. Shoes are soft-soled, wrapped, dusted with ash or powder to silence them. In wet weather everyone seems suddenly burdened with hoods, gutters, shoulder-sheets, waxed covers, jars, bowls, nets, hooks, scoops. During a ceiling rain the population blossoms outward, as if the houses themselves have opened and spilled people.

They are not a gloomy people. They are restrained.

Their quiet is not sadness. It is civic discipline. A child learns early that a shout can call down rebuke from three windows, startle nesting birds, frighten animals into drains, or offend the stone. Speech is close and practical. They use murmurs, whistles, tongue-clicks, hand signs, shoulder signs, lamp signs, pebble signs. A market can be crowded and still sound like cloth being folded. Bargaining is done with fingers against the palm, with weighed shells, with deliberate taps on stone blocks. Only in sanctioned places does loudness become lawful: at the White Canal when water is running, in funerary passages, during collapses, in certain public disputes where noise itself is witness.

They laugh, but often inwardly or against the backs of their hands. Their anger is precise. Their grief is public but contained by ritual until the moment when shouting is required.

Their homes reveal them better than their faces.

A lived-in doorway is layered: hide curtain, plank, weighted cord, scratch marks, shell tokens, bones of household animals, a smear of blue mineral if the family has rights to a public drip, a notch if someone within is ill, a suspended clay bead if silence is requested. Inside, rooms are low, swept, crowded not with furniture but with vessels. Jars, bowls, sealed skins, racks of bone tools, trays of drying fungus, hooks for nets, slings for infants, ladders, folded mats. Walls bear generations of marks: heights of children, water debts, marriage tallies, warnings about cracks, devotional scratches, old jokes no one can explain.

They sleep lightly. Everyone listens in sleep a little. The old are especially valued because they can wake to sounds others miss: a new drip, a restless cistern animal, the soft report of a settling wall, rain beginning far away on roofs not yet wet nearby.

Their society is less ruled than measured.

Authority belongs to those who know relations: between cistern and household, crack and wall, rain and seasonless time, animal passage and disease, debt and survival. A powerful person is someone who can say where water was three generations ago and where it may go if a canal is unsealed. A rich person owns not gold, but reliable storage, animal rights, kiln access, a stable roof, a remembered spring-name, a marriage link to a rain ward, a claim no court has overturned.

They have officials, though many titles sound more like tasks than ranks.

There are rain wards, whose faces are always turned upward. They read the ceiling as sailors read a horizon: condensation bruises, mineral dimming, cold drafts, moth elevations, the restless opening of crystalline fans. They know when to send children with bowls, when to cover archives, when to clear the White Canal, when to forbid kiln-fire, when to prepare for a useless mist that will wet everything and fill nothing.

There are crack-listeners, thin people with patient ears and backs made sore by the floor. At night they lie belly-down on mats in streets, tunnels, courts, foundations, and abandoned rooms. They carry listening bowls, bone rods, and little packets of dry powder to sprinkle at suspicious seams. Some are revered. Some are half-feared, because they bring news no one wants: this wall is done forgiving; this house must empty; this shrine must move; this silence underground is not ordinary silence.

There are mapkeepers, who distrust straight lines. Their maps are layered probability objects: bone slats, knotted cords, clay plates, memory chants, scraped hides. They do not ask, “Where is the street?” but “When is the street passable, for whom, carrying what, after which kind of rain, with which animals awake?” A good mapkeeper can route a funeral around a debt market, a water party around a crab migration, a construction crew around a singing crack.

There are cistern judges, shell archivists, animal counters, soot harvesters, kiln mediators, marriage measurers, canal callers, public seam painters, bell-frame keepers even where bells are gone.

Children belong everywhere until named fully. They run between districts as messengers, animal tenders, egg collectors, drip watchers. Because they are small, they know the under-levels of the city better than adults: the crawlways where beetles march, the damp cellars where cave hares lick walls, the loose stones that conceal larvae, the basins where thread-jellies hang like pale hair. Children shout more than adults and are forgiven only during rain, canal flow, birth, or genuine discovery.

They tend animals with the seriousness surface people reserve for crops.

Since there are almost no plants, animals are food, labor, warning, refuse management, weather sign, companionship, and sometimes law. Blind fish are kept in cisterns and counted carefully. White crabs are trapped, fattened, traded alive in clay cages. Pale hares are semi-domesticated in damp cellars, fed fungal scrapings and mineral rind; their ears show blood like faint rose petals when held to light. Long pale lizards occupy kiln districts and are tolerated because they eat insects and predict warmth. Hairless dogs move almost as a public service: narrow, silent scavengers, accepted in doorways, driven gently from archives, given bones after water courts adjourn.

The many-legged cistern grazers are closest to livestock. Children mark their shells with household symbols, brush their black comb-teeth clean, harvest their eggs into jars. These creatures rasp films from wet stone and keep tanks from fouling. A family with healthy grazers has standing. A child who mistreats one may be barred from water work for a season.

Other animals are not owned but negotiated with. Leathery birds in the arches are protected because their flight warns of air shifts. Moths are watched for changes in light seams. Beetle processions indicate dampness underground. Disk-creatures on wet stone are avoided, not because they are dangerous, but because their sudden opening disgusts everyone and is considered unlucky before trade. Thread-jellies in wells inspire caution and stories: some say they taste memory; some say they gather the dreams dropped by sleepers through the stone.

The city’s inhabitants do not imagine themselves separate from these animals. They are fellow citizens of appetite and moisture. Laws include gaps for small bodies. Architecture includes passageways not made for people. A blocked animal route is not merely inconvenient; it is a civic hazard.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is not centered on gods with faces. It is made of obligations to conditions.

They honor First Drying, Last Flood, Named Springs, Lost Basins, Kindly Seams, Treacherous Roofs, the Public Drop, the Deep Dark, and the long patience of stone. They do not worship light exactly. The light is too constant, too cold, too unresolved. They respect it as one respects a fact no elder can explain.

Darkness is holy and frightening because it is rare. A truly dark chamber is entered with preparation. Young people dare one another to stand in sealed rooms and extinguish the lamp, but adults treat such games as vulgar. Darkness is where origins live, and things before record, and perhaps the old flooded city below.

Fire is more feared than sacred. A flame is a mouth. A kiln is a penned beast. Lamps are messages rather than tools. A lamp in brightness means warning, boundary, body, dispute, taboo. Children learn lamp grammar before they learn old inscriptions.

Death returns a person to systems. Bodies are not simply buried, because ground is complicated and dampness unpredictable. Some are dried in bright chambers, wrapped in scraped hide, and placed in family vaults. Some are given to mineral crust in sealed wet rooms. Some are lowered into old dry channels with shells in the mouth and a cup on the chest. Important crack-listeners may be entombed in walls they saved or failed to save. Water thieves, if the old stories are true, were once denied dry memorial and consigned to unnamed sumps, but modern citizens insist this is barbaric and no longer done.

Their arts are durable, coded, and often small.

They carve bone with astonishing fineness: rain tallies, animal forms, maps of vanished channels, jokes hidden in legal objects. They polish shell into pale plaques that catch the cave-light softly. They make pigments from mineral powders and bind them with fat, egg, soot, and glue. Walls bloom with abstract marks: drip histories, family claims, migration routes of crabs, geometric prayers for stable lintels. Music exists but is mostly percussive and tactile: finger-drums on stretched hide, bone clackers muffled in cloth, water bowls tuned to different depths, breath through clay pipes played so softly the musician feels more than hears it.

They dance rarely in open plazas because stamping is frowned upon. Their dances are gliding, bending, hand-speaking dances, performed in rings around wells or along canal edges after a generous rain. During funerals, however, they may strike the paving together, hard and loud, announcing the dead to stone and daring collapse to answer.

Their cuisine is pale, salty, smoky, sour, and mineral.

They eat blind fish, cave crabs, insects, eggs, hare meat, dog only in famine and with apology, dried fungal crusts, translucent rinds scraped from cistern lips, rendered fats, mineral salts, bone broths, fermented things sealed in jars. Food is often softened with precious water or steamed during rain. A feast may include crab roe, roasted lizard, thin cakes of ground fungus and bone meal, preserved fish paste, salt flakes, and a rare sweet secretion gathered from insects that feed on mineral films. Surface fruit, if ever traded down from impossible distances, would be regarded with suspicion, delight, and digestive regret.

They do not farm in the familiar sense. They culture. They tend films, maintain damp cellars, rotate cistern grazers, preserve fungal strands, manage insect cages, protect spawning tanks, seed wet walls with scrapings, and remember which abandoned chamber may briefly become productive after three days of rain.

Outsiders, if they come through the tunnels, first misunderstand the city as a ruin. Then as a monastery. Then as a market. Then as an organism.

The citizens themselves call it simply home, though their word likely carries meanings of hollow, measure, agreement, and ongoing repair. To live there is to participate in maintenance so continuous it becomes identity. A person is known by what they keep from failing: a cistern, a wall, a route, a herd of shell-marked grazers, an archive shelf, a family debt, a public silence.

They are not primitive in mind. They lack engines, abundance, easy fire, common metal, and sky. But their intelligence is infrastructural, ecological, legalistic, embodied. They know gradients invisible to strangers. They can judge the depth of water in a cistern by smell and echo. They can lift a stone lintel with rope, wedge, chant, and communal timing. They can preserve records for centuries in a place that sweats unpredictably. They can keep peace among households whose survival depends on a drip.

They are conservative because mistakes persist in stone. They are inventive because stone keeps changing anyway.

Their myths say different things about their origin.

Some say they came from above during a burning age and sealed the way behind them. Some say they descended willingly to guard trapped daylight. Some say they were born from a drowned people who forgot the sea and learned to breathe air under stone. Some say the first citizens were not human but animals who taught starving wanderers how to follow water. Some say the city was already here when people arrived: empty streets, cisterns, bell frames, shell-marked doors, all waiting under impossible brightness.

The people do not agree and do not need to. Origins matter less than continuance.

Alongside them may live another category of being, harder to name: not spirits exactly, not animals, not citizens, but presences produced by the long compact between attention and geology.

A crack that has been listened to for four hundred years becomes almost a personality. A cistern with generations of drowned lamps has a reputation. A boulder with an address receives messages. Certain public seams are spoken to politely before vessels are placed beneath them. In the Shell Archive, attendants sometimes pause before shelves not because someone is present, but because record itself has weight. Children insist mineral flowers listen. Adults deny this while lowering their voices near them.

If there are spirits here, they are slow, conditional, and made of repetition. They inhabit maintained things. They are summoned by measurement, not prayer. They depart when neglected.

And below all these lives another city, or the rumor of one: sealed rooms under chalk, old boat rings, flooded names, bones in mineral crust, districts descending rather than rising. Whether anything still lives there is uncertain. The citizens speak of pale swimmers, eyeless ancestors, water-keepers, lamps moving under cistern surfaces, fish too large for any known tank. Such stories are told in dry times, when imagination goes looking for water.

But the visible life of the place is already strange enough.

It is made of quiet citizens with strong hands and careful eyes; of children painted with ownership marks and mineral dust; of elders who can hear a wall thinking; of judges measuring water by depth; of dogs like shadows without darkness; of crabs emerging with the rain; of birds that click instead of sing; of shell-backed grazers rasping cistern walls; of archivists racing against condensation; of builders raising and unbuilding the same street for centuries.

They live not beneath the earth as exiles, but within it as negotiators.

Every inhabitant, human and otherwise, is part of the agreement: take the water when it comes, do not waste sound, leave passages for smaller lives, listen before striking stone, repair what can be repaired, abandon what must be abandoned, remember that a drop may found a law, a crack may redraw a district, and a rain of eleven hours may become history.

They are the people of the bright hollow.

They are patient, busy, wary, communal, dry-skinned, water-minded, soft-footed, animal-haunted, and old without being finished.
