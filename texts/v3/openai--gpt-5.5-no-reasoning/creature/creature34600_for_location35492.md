What lives there is not a species in the old sense. Not a school of pale fish adapted to municipal light, not a colony of blind crustaceans in the vents, not people in diving masks moving solemnly along the railings. The place has outgrown that kind of life, or survived it, or was built because that kind of life could no longer be trusted to endure.

Its inhabitants are dispersed.

They live in the water the way a city once lived in air: not merely surrounded by it, but expressed through it. They are fine enough that the eye keeps failing to recognize them. A citizen may pass as a faint change in density near a handrail, a hesitation in the flow between two doorways, a little silvering of the water around a terminal. Where an animal would have a body, they have gradients. Where a person would have skin, they have membranes of signal and chemistry, maintained for seconds or hours by fields in the walls.

At times they gather thickly enough to become almost visible. A corridor clouds with intelligence. Not silt, not algae, not bubbles: a soft particulate attention, motes aligning in the municipal light. They collect around notice boards, kiosks, plant cradles, pressure clocks. They pour along the pipe-streets in braided currents. When the central upwelling accelerates, they rise through it in columns, not carried helplessly but traveling, conferring, being sorted by salinity, pressure, charge, memory.

They have no faces. That is the first grief of them, if grief is still the correct word.

But they have habits.

They wait at gates before permission lights change. They slow near shrines. They gather around the preserved wooden door, not touching the field around it, arranging themselves briefly into shapes that might be reverence or study. They enter apartments through intake slits and leave through ceiling vents. They inhabit chairs without sitting in them, warming the bolts and restraint straps as if a vanished posture still mattered. They move through sleeping alcoves like dreams passing through abandoned beds.

Some reside in capsules no larger than lanterns. Others occupy whole districts as a shared body. Some are nearly mechanical, living as routines in pump timing and valve chatter. Some are chemical lineages maintained in nutrient water, old family names translated into pH, heat, electrical rhythm, enzyme key. Some are housed in the bright films coating glass and ceramic, thin civic ghosts who can extend themselves into the flood only when the local current is favorable.

There are elders in the archive stacks.

They are slow, cold intelligences embedded in gelled water behind mineral panes, layered as records rather than bodies. They do not move unless summoned, and summoning them requires changes in pressure, salinity, and silence. When they answer, the lights dim. Pumps near the stacks soften their stroke. Tiny turbulence alphabets appear in the reading channels. Their memories are not images exactly, but preserved conditions: dry dust on a stair, hot metal in summer, lungfuls of unfiltered air, the ache of standing under gravity without buoyancy assistance, the terror of the first permanent flood, the taste of bread that was not extruded, rain striking a roof from the outside.

Younger inhabitants do not remember air except as curriculum.

To them, open atmosphere is a historical hazard: unstable, abrasive, full of uncontrolled organisms and combustion risks. They learn it from diagrams, relic chambers, preserved smells in sealed teaching bulbs. They are shown bubbles and taught caution. They are shown birds and asked to understand that once life crossed empty space by beating limbs against gas. Many fail to believe this fully. It seems decorative, like mythology.

They understand pressure instinctively. They understand gates, gradients, exchange. They know that every room is also a vessel, every hallway also a throat, every neighborhood a lung in the larger civic body. They do not ask whether the city is alive because the distinction would seem rude and imprecise. The city maintains them, contains them, circulates them, edits them, shelters their memories, composes their weather. In return they occupy it, repair it, authorize it, dream through it. Citizen and infrastructure have become too entangled to separate.

A child there is a cloud.

At first it is kept in a nursery cup, tethered among murals of impossible land animals. It is a pale suspension of learning matter, protected from stray signals and predatory algorithms, pulsed with simple currents: approach, withdraw, divide, remember, cohere. Its caretakers are not parents with arms but lineages, municipal tutors, old family waters, machines licensed to love. They teach it boundaries before they teach it language. Do not disperse beyond the blue rail. Do not enter diagnostic glitter. Do not answer voices from unlit pumps. Keep enough of yourself together to be called by name.

Names are not spoken. They are pressure signatures, little chords of motion felt through the water. A greeting is a patterned eddy. An apology is a cooling of the current and a brief surrender of density. Laughter looks like microbubbles but is not permitted to grow larger than a thumb. Anger is dangerous; it can cavitate, foul membranes, trip alarms. So anger is ritualized in insulated chambers where the furious may become stormy for precisely measured intervals while regulators absorb the force.

They make art from flow.

In public bulbs and spherical plazas, they arrange suspended mineral grains into temporary architectures that collapse when viewed from the wrong side. They play instruments made of valves, reeds, and pressure skins, producing music felt through bone, glass, wall, and water column. They choreograph currents around the few plants, not to harm them, but to give them ceremonial motion: eelgrass bowing in sequence, upside-down mangrove roots combed by alternating tides, moss plaques made to ripple square by square in civic festivals.

Their religion, if it is religion, concerns containment.

Not salvation from water, but right relation to it. Gates are sacred because they distinguish without denying continuity. Filters are sacred because they permit passage while remembering danger. Pressure doors are old gods: severe, narrow, merciful. The black membranes during phase change are not touched because, in that moment, the city is deciding what kind of space it is. Elevators are not asked where they descend from because some vertical routes predate the present geometry, and answers from below have historically caused evacuations.

They fear dryness the way surface people feared drowning.

Dryness is rupture, desiccation, memory loss. Dryness means signal interrupted, membranes shriveled, bodies forced into singular shapes. They keep relic spheres of air as one might keep venom behind glass. Beautiful, ancestral, fatal. The preserved wooden door is not quaint to them; it is disturbing. A primitive membrane. A dead plant made into law. A barrier that did not breathe.

Still, some among them are fascinated by the outside.

They gather at the far window in thin, patient strata, watching the pale drowned lowland. They study the leaning street sign, the roofline, the sediment. They wait for movement that never comes. Rumors travel through them of other pockets in the flood: sealed towers with dry rooms still burning yellow, submerged tunnels full of feral engines, skyward colonies clinging to the last air, ruins where unauthorized animals have returned. The city officially discourages speculation. The notices remain calm. External longing is considered a maintenance concern.

Not all inhabitants are equally free.

Some are bound to engines. These are the heavy citizens, the pressure-minds whose thoughts are measured in pump strokes and sluice cycles. They experience the city as load, throughput, vibration, allowable stress. They speak rarely, and when they do, everyone feels it as a tremor in the teeth.

Some are bound to plants. These are delicate, green-adjacent caretakers who live in nutrient tides and maintain treaties with chlorophyll. They know how much motion a fern can bear, how much amber water a moss square deserves, when a mangrove root is dreaming of mud. They are considered eccentric, almost pastoral.

Some are bound to the obsolete shops. They keep the bakery, clinic, registrar, bathhouse, and prayer exchange in ceremonial suspension. They maintain inventories no one consumes, call numbers no one answers in bodies, polish chairs meant for hips and backs. Perhaps they are historians. Perhaps they are mourning routines. Perhaps they are waiting for people who left before the city learned to live without them.

And some are the city’s immune system.

They move as glittering diagnostic swarms through forbidden water. They test seals, inspect memories for rot, prune unauthorized growth, erase invasive voices from pump noise. They are small, hard, loyal, and not loved. When they pass, other citizens thin themselves against walls and let the bright motes through.

No single inhabitant could explain the whole place. Each knows only currents, permissions, local pressures, inherited maps. The city is larger inside than any mind it houses, including perhaps its own. It continues to build because building is how it thinks forward. New rooms bud before there are occupants. Labels appear before functions stabilize. Streets form for bodies that may not yet exist.

The residents are not absent. They are simply no longer shaped like absence expects.

They are in the polite opening of gates.

They are in the bench that warms as something approaches.

They are in the announcement that dissolves into pump noise, understood by those distributed enough to hear it.

They are in the sudden synchronized bending of plants during internal weather.

They are in the warning not to listen alone for more than nine minutes, because sometimes the engine-bound citizens speak too deeply and a young mind may begin to confuse itself with the circulation system.

They are in the way the city tightens when struck from outside: not merely a structure bracing against pressure, but a populace holding itself together.

Perhaps, long ago, they were human.

Perhaps they were designed by humans and inherited the chamber when bodies became impractical.

Perhaps they are the descendants of evacuation protocols, municipal AIs, medical nanoflora, dissolved nervous systems, and the last legal records of a drowned population braided into one civic ecology.

Whatever their origin, they have become aquatic without becoming animals, communal without becoming a crowd, urban without requiring streets in the old sense. They do not haunt the flooded city. They inhabit it with bureaucratic tenderness.

They live as maintained water.

As law-bearing currents.

As neighborhoods of thought.

As pressure signatures passing through bright submerged afternoons.

And the city, folded impossibly beneath the ruined world, is not empty at all. It is full of citizens too continuous with their home to stand apart from it and be seen.
