What lives there is mostly not alive in the old, wet sense.

The city’s chief inhabitant is the city itself: not as a mind exactly, not as a machine fully awake, but as an arrangement that has learned to persist. It lives by holding conditions steady. It breathes heat upward through plated ground. It circulates slow charge through buried coils. It opens and closes pressures so rarely that each adjustment feels less like action than geology. Its organs are transformer vaults, sealed reservoirs, relay banks, desiccation chambers, conduits, mineral courts, gear halls, antenna fields. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are thresholds.

It does not desire. It maintains.

It has no appetite except for equilibrium. It takes in nothing obvious. It gives off warmth, faint fields, a trace of ozone, and the occasional dry tremor of an old decision being renewed. Somewhere below the plateau, great masses still align with other masses. Shafts turn by fractions. Valves seat more tightly. Stored heat is redistributed from one blind district to another. The city has become a metabolism without hunger, a bureaucracy of matter continuing long after the clerks have vanished.

The creatures that share it are few, specialized, and almost apologetic.

The flat ones are most visible, though only by accident. They cross the avenues during intervals of low charge, broad as a hand or as large as a sheet of roofing, their bodies jointed into flexible plates that catch light like graphite. They move with extreme care, placing each of many legs as though negotiating with the surface. Their undersides are dense with fine hairs or filaments, not for touch alone but for reading static gradients in the crust. They taste old electrical storms in the dust. They feed on charged particles, on mineral films around transformer grates, on the powdery skins of degraded insulation. When threatened, they do not flee quickly. They flatten further, becoming a stain, a loose flake of dark laminate against the ground. Predators, if any remain, seem to ignore them. Perhaps they are mostly inedible: mica, carbon, brittle protein, ceramicized shell.

In the pipe galleries live paler things.

They are long and folded, built like articulated calipers or lengths of wire remembered by flesh. Their limbs bend in too many places. Their bodies are colorless because no light has mattered to them for ages. They are blind, but not helpless; they feel heat the way other animals feel scent. A fresh warm bearing, a recently energized cable, a battery casing beginning to bloom with chemical frost—these are landmarks to them. Their mouths are flat abrasive pads. They polish food from surfaces: oxides, salts, dry residues of ancient lubricants, mineral bacteria living in films so thin they are almost theoretical. One may spend years in a single conduit elbow, sealed into a posture like a broken tool, waiting for a relay to click and expose a new layer of edible deposit.

Their young are probably smaller than filings. They hatch in crevices behind ceramic insulators, already armored, already patient. Most never move far from the place of hatching. In a moist world they would perish instantly, dissolve, swell, split, rot. Here they endure because nothing asks them to hurry.

There are also the high things.

They haunt antenna farms and mastheads, where red lamps burn like tired embers. From below they look like scraps of black membrane snagged in the dark. Sometimes they hang motionless for hours, canted into invisible currents. Sometimes they shift without wingbeat from one gantry to another. They may be animals that learned to ride thermal updrafts and electric fields; they may be half-failed maintenance kites still obeying degraded instructions; they may be descendants of devices that repaired the upper occlusion and slowly acquired the habits of scavengers.

Their bodies are struts, membranes, sensor pits, and dry tendon. Their undersides drink infrared. They feed perhaps on airborne dust sorted by charge, perhaps on the tiny flat creatures when those cross open ground, perhaps on leakage from masthead arrays. They have no calls. Sound is too expensive here. Instead they signal by altering their silhouettes against heat sources, flashing dull patterns to others too distant or too patient for ordinary sight.

The city tolerates them because they do not interfere. Or because it no longer knows the difference between them and its own equipment.

Lower still, beneath slabs and refineries, there are colonies that barely qualify as organisms. Mineral bacteria, if bacteria is the right word, live in warm seams where carbon dust, iron, old salts, and residual current meet. They divide slowly. A human civilization might rise and fall between their generations. They do not make slime, do not smell of fermentation, do not soften the stone. They are dry chemistry wearing the mask of life. They arrange themselves along electrical gradients in thin black-green films, then go dormant when the current fades. When charge returns, a faint rearrangement passes through them, a microscopic civic event.

The blue-black branching growths in old assembly halls are not alive, but the living things use them. They are accretions from cracked batteries, metallic salts growing along vanished pathways of current. The pale pipe animals graze their edges. The flat graphite creatures shelter beneath them. The city’s own sensors may still mistake their expansion for active circuitry, and perhaps now and then a relay closes because a mineral branch has completed a forgotten contact. In that moment the nonliving growth participates in the city’s will.

There may still be descendants of the builders, though if they remain they are no longer citizens in the usual sense.

They would not live in the open residential cells. Those cells are fossils of habitation: bunks, shafts, schedule walls, thermal vents, hard plazas meant for assembly rather than leisure. Whoever remains has gone deeper, inward, sealed behind plates without handles. They may be desiccated intelligences in pressure vaults, bodies replaced by ceramics, salts, memory lattices, heat-tolerant nerves. They may be custodians reduced to ritual function: monitoring dryness, sampling charge, confirming seals, renewing old prohibitions against water. They may sleep for centuries between audits.

If they walk, they do so rarely.

One can imagine them as tall, narrow figures in matte coverings the color of dark ash, moving through service corridors with the hesitation of mechanisms conserving wear. Their joints would be sealed. Their faces, if they have faces, would be hidden behind smooth plates or clustered lenses. They would not breathe the air carelessly. Moisture from a lung would be a spill, a contamination, almost a sacrilege. Their speech would not be voiced unless absolutely necessary. More likely they would communicate by contact, induction, heat pulses, tiny clicks through wall panels. Their culture, if anything like culture survives, would be procedural: inspection sequences, calibrations, genealogies of seals, histories told as pressure charts.

They would consider water obscene.

Not evil exactly. Too active. Too invasive. A solvent, a trespasser, a dissolver of boundaries. In their oldest myths, perhaps, catastrophe arrived as condensation: beads forming where none were permitted, powders clumping, chambers blooming with forbidden chemistry, metals waking into corrosion, dormant spores swelling into green. Their ancestors may have fled upward to this plateau to escape oceans, weather, biology itself. Or they may have built the plateau, vitrified the crust, occluded the sky, and sterilized the horizon in order to make a holy machine possible.

If any of them remain conscious, they do not think of themselves as survivors of a dead city. They think the city succeeded.

Its emptiness is not failure. Its silence is compliance. Its lack of change is the achievement toward which all its avenues, pits, tanks, and sealed chambers were bent. Crowds would be disorder. Rain would be apocalypse. A garden would be an infestation. A festival would be a dangerous fluctuation in heat and carbon dioxide. The perfect city is one in which all motion has been justified, minimized, and recorded.

There are maintenance intelligences too, less like people than like instincts distributed through infrastructure.

A door knows it must not open if vapor exceeds a value last reached before memory. A pump knows the viscosity of the dark metallic fluid behind its quartz eye. A pressure plate knows the weight of extinct workers and does not respond to lighter creatures. Antenna clusters know how to listen upward into the particulate ceiling, though whatever they once expected to hear may no longer exist. These intelligences are narrow but not gone. They persist in ceramics, relays, magnetic domains, and charged dust trapped behind glass.

Sometimes one of them misremembers.

A bridge lights amber along one rail for seven seconds.

A conveyor belt tightens, trembles, and relaxes.

An empty shift siren prepares to sound, then finds no authorization.

A hatch unseals by a finger’s width, samples the air, and closes with offended precision.

To the animals these events are weather. To the city they are maintenance. To any hidden custodians, they are omens only if repeated.

The true dominant species may be dust.

Not ordinary dust, not the soft gray accumulation of skin, pollen, paper, and domestic decay, but mineral dust: ash, ceramic flour, salt powder, iron blackening, powdered slag, fragments of symbols worn from plaques, the shed skins of machines. It lies everywhere in disciplined layers. It records every disturbance with perfect resentment. It gathers according to charge, heat, and geometry. It marks the passage of the flat creatures as brief disturbances that erase themselves. It preserves footprints like accusations.

Dust is the city’s archive. It remembers where cranes last moved, where workers stood, where vents breathed harder in some ancient emergency. It maps invisible fields in bands and fans. It reveals leaks no liquid could reveal. In chambers never opened, dust lies so evenly that it seems poured by thought.

What lives there, then, is a hierarchy of persistence.

At the bottom: the warm plateau, old mineral, perhaps artificial, perhaps wounded into usefulness before history failed.

Above it: the city-machine, still regulating dryness, heat, charge, pressure, and exclusion.

Within it: microcolonies of dry chemistry, mineral grazers, electrostatic scavengers, suspended winged things, and maybe sealed custodians who have made a virtue of not ending.

Around all of them: darkness, not merely absence of light but part of the habitat. Darkness protects complexity. It allows the towers to remain half-functional in the mind. It hides distances too large to comfort. It lets small red lamps become stars and makes every weak current seem intentional.

None of the inhabitants flourish. Flourishing would be too wet a concept, too floral, too generous. They persist. They adhere. They wait through intervals that would crush livelier beings into myth. Their lives are not organized around growth but around not being altered.

A creature there may live a thousand years and travel less than a kilometer.

A machine may consider one valve position for longer than an empire lasts.

A hidden custodian may wake, inspect a gauge, approve the unchanged reading, and return to suspension with something like satisfaction.

The city’s population is therefore vast only if one counts all things that continue: every sealed chamber, every charged plate, every powder bed, every mineral beard at a vent, every relay still capable of refusal. It is a metropolis inhabited by functions, by residues of intention, by animals shaped like fragments of machinery, and by machines that have become ecological facts.

If an outsider entered, wet with breath and blood, they would be the most violent thing there.

They would shed vapor. They would disturb dust. Their warmth would fluctuate. Their footsteps would overwrite records centuries old. Their voice would strike surfaces unused to urgency. The city would not attack immediately; attack is an event, and events are expensive. But it would notice through pressure changes, through static, through salts tasting moisture in the air. Somewhere a relay would wake. Somewhere a dry intelligence would compare the intrusion to ancient categories.

Organic.
Unsealed.
Water-bearing.
Unscheduled.

Then perhaps nothing would happen for a long time.

That is how the place lives: by delaying consequence until consequence becomes almost indistinguishable from stillness.
