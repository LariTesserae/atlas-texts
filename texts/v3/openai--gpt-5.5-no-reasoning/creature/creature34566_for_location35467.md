What lives there is not one species, or even one society, so much as a whole pressure-adapted ecology of maintenance.

The inhabitants are creatures of brightness, condensation, ledges, handrails, shade, and routes. They live with the sun as with a powerful neighbor: respected, used, screened, redirected, never ignored. Their bodies and habits belong to height and heat. They squint without seeming to. Their skin is often sun-browned, freckled, coppered, weather-dark, or permanently lined by glare. Their hair is wrapped, braided, oiled, netted, or cut short for work near gears and vines. They dress in layers meant less for cold than for light: loose pale cloth, broad woven visors, shoulder cloths, veils, tinted goggles, reed hats, aprons patched with resin, gloves polished dark at the palms. Even children know how to cross a mirror street with one hand shielding the eyes and the other trailing along a rail.

They are not hurried people, but they are never idle in the empty sense. Their tempo matches the machinery: slow, constant, recursive. A person may pause for a long time under an awning, watching droplets gather on a copper pipe, then step forward at exactly the right moment to tilt a tray beneath the line. A water inspector may walk the same forty-seven stairways every morning for thirty years, tapping grates with a little brass rod and knowing by sound whether a lower cistern is swallowing cleanly. A vine cutter may spend all afternoon deciding which tendril is a threat and which has earned permission to become part of the wall.

They are a people who have learned that attention is survival.

Their homes are inserted into the city rather than set apart from it. They live behind shutters in cliff walls, above workshops, beneath glass roofs, beside fan housings, inside the thickened ribs of retaining structures, along catwalk neighborhoods where the floor is perforated metal and one can smell breakfast rising from three levels below. Rooms are narrow and tall, often arranged around shafts of borrowed light. A kitchen may have one wall of tile permanently damp from the cistern behind it, green at the grout no matter how often it is scrubbed. A sleeping loft may look directly into the leaves of a fruit tree trained along an exterior rail. A nursery may share a wall with a warm flue where pale moths gather at dusk.

Windows are precious but managed. Many are screened by plantings, slatted shades, water curtains, or patterned cloth. Interiors are bright by reflection rather than exposure: white limewash, hammered tin panels, glazed ceramic, glass bricks, mirror ducts angled down from roofs. A household’s wealth may be measured not by spaciousness but by reliable shade, clean drainage, and a condensation line that does not fail in the dry weeks.

They sleep to the city’s breath. Pumps below. Fans above. Chain lifts in the distance. Birds in pipes. Frogs after rain. A child raised there may be unable to rest in true silence.

Their food is grown close and vertically. They eat terrace grains, legumes from trough fields, mushrooms from lower damp works, goat milk, eggs, cistern fish in some quarters, pressed oils, sour fruits, rooftop herbs, fermented leaf pastes, dried peppers, algae cakes, honey from numbered wall hives, and little glossy fruits coaxed from espaliered trees against heat-retaining walls. Meals taste of sun and stored water: tart, mineral, smoky, green, salted carefully. Nothing wet is wasted. Cooking water cools, then feeds herbs. Fruit peels go to compost basins. Bones become powder. Ash goes where ash is needed. Children learn the difference between refuse, feed, filter matter, and forbidden contamination before they learn letters.

They keep animals, but seldom as simple pets. Animals are workers, companions, pests, omens, guild-property, neighborhood citizens, or tolerated thieves.

The cats are lean, bright-eyed, and socially important. They move through grain depots and conveyor alleys with official indifference, wearing bead tags or old stamped plates at the neck. Some belong to families; some belong to routes; some belong to no one but are nevertheless counted during district tallies. It is considered bad luck, and bad sanitation, to obstruct a grain cat.

Goats live on roof pastures and tray-lawns, sure-footed as rumors. Their hooves click along ramps in the morning while herders guide them with soft whistles and strips of dried fruit. Hens occupy stacked coops near kitchens and market backs. Bees occupy sun-warmed walls, entering through painted ceramic mouths. Their keepers mark swarm seasons on lintels and teach children which corridors to avoid when the air becomes gold and irritable.

The monkeys, or monkeylike creatures, are a constant treaty violation. They know latch types. They strip fruit trees before harvest if not watched. They steal bolts, spoons, mirror chips, prayer tokens, valve caps, buttons, and once in a while someone’s spectacles. People curse them, feed them, trap them, release them, and tell stories in which they are either ancestral engineers or the souls of careless apprentices. Some roofs hang decoy boxes full of polished scrap to lure them away from real tools.

Birds belong to every level. Tiny pipe-nesters flicker through brackets. Pigeons own the market rafters. Black-winged scavengers ride thermal columns above the foundry gardens. Long-tailed insect hunters flash between laundry lines. Certain old towers, no longer useful as machines, are maintained entirely because colonies nest there, and the birds keep insect populations in balance. The inhabitants understand that a rookery can be infrastructure.

There are also the engineered creatures, and people treat them with the practical affection given to old tools. Pale maintenance moths cluster near vents and are gently brushed into carrier boxes when filters are cleaned. Numbered snails graze algae from channel walls, their marked shells recorded by water clerks. Bees are bred for heat tolerance and steady temperament. Some districts raise small filtration eels or cistern frogs whose songs are interpreted as indicators of water quality. No one calls these things magical. They are husbandry, design, inheritance.

The people themselves are organized around upkeep more deeply than around rule.

There are water families who maintain rights and obligations attached to cisterns. Gutter guilds who know the roof slopes and overflow chains of entire districts. Shade committees who arbitrate whether a new awning steals light from a grow-wall or protects a public stair. Root negotiators who inspect invasive growth and decide when to cut, brace, redirect, or bless it as structural companion. Lift mechanics whose hands are permanently black at the nail beds. Condenser children apprenticed to read droplets. Mirror wardens who cover and uncover light arrays. Aviary keepers. Pump listeners. Moss scrapers. Rail polishers. Bee-wall keepers. Compost judges. Rain bell ringers.

Their politics are probably less ideological than hydrological. A blocked drain is a civic offense. A stolen water share can begin a feud. To build without accounting for shadow, runoff, root pressure, animal passage, and maintenance access is not merely rude but almost obscene. Every household knows itself part of systems older than its oldest ancestor. Independence is admired only when it does not leak onto someone below.

They have festivals, but the festivals are also procedures.

The first heavy rain after the long bright season brings children running barefoot onto safe terraces while adults pretend not to be just as relieved. Rain chains are garlanded. Cistern mouths are opened and watched. People stand under awnings listening not only with delight but with calculation: Is the north channel taking? Is the overflow early? Did the repaired seam hold? Songs rise with the rain, but so do inspection crews.

Tank-cleaning season has its own foods, sour and crisp and salted, eaten in courtyards while lower chambers are drained, scrubbed, and blessed. Pollinator days close certain streets beneath painted arches while bees and moths move freely through flowering corridors. High-sun weeks bring veils across mirror roads and public shade cloths unfurled like sails. At dusk on the longest day, old polished panels are tilted westward so amber light pours into the lower wet levels, and for a few minutes the fern courts shine as if lit from within.

Their myths are built from maintenance memories enlarged by time.

They tell of the hidden mountain below, not always as dead geology but as a sleeping mass whose patience holds them up. In some stories it is angry, buried beneath bolts and tanks. In others it is grateful for the green returning to its skin. Some say the oldest roots have found stone and speak to it in mineral thirst. Some say the deep engines imitate the mountain’s original breathing. Some say that if every pump stopped and every gutter clogged, the city would not simply fall—it would remember being rock and slowly close around them.

Children are warned about lower sealed levels where roots and machines have made alliances without human permission. They dare each other to press ears against old black iron and listen for underground rivers. Lovers tie threads on condensation pipes, asking for patience. Mechanics leave small offerings of oil and flowers at ancient lift housings before major repairs, not because they believe the machine is alive exactly, but because after several centuries the distinction feels impolite.

What lives there has adapted not by becoming wild, but by becoming inseparable.

The human inhabitants are not masters of the place in any clean sense. They are one of its maintenance species. Like the snails in the channels, the cats in the grain alleys, the bees in the hot walls, the vines on the facades, they keep the city alive by using it. Their footsteps polish routes. Their repairs become strata. Their mistakes become caution marks painted on tiles. Their dead become compost, ash, memorial plaques, family tools, names for bridges, stories attached to leaks.

They are intimate with failure. Not catastrophe, but the daily small betrayals of matter: a seal sweating, a moss patch spreading, a panel bowing, a root lifting tile, a pump losing rhythm, a mirror drifting out of alignment, a goat finding exactly the wrong planter. They do not expect permanence from anything untouched. Permanence is what is touched repeatedly and well.

Their faces, if you met them in a market court after rain, would likely seem open but assessing. They would notice where you stepped, whether you blocked the gutter, whether you wasted the cup rinse, whether you flinched at the frogs, whether you looked up before crossing under a dripping pipe. Hospitality would be generous but instructional. You would be given fruit, shade, perhaps a place to sit where the breeze rises through grating from the lower levels. You would also be told which rail not to lean on, which stair sweats, when to move for the goats, and why that vine with the blue thread is not to be cut.

They are practical, sun-marked, sensuous people, living among wet leaves and hot metal. They value clever hands, durable cloth, accurate memory, patience with systems, restraint in thirst, and the ability to hear a change in the machines beneath ordinary noise. Their art is likely patterned with channels, terraces, wings, leaf shadows, pipe rings, rain marks, and the repeated geometry of stairs. Their music may use drips, clacks, low drones, metal bowls, reed flutes, work chants timed to pumps. Their language probably has many words for shade, for kinds of damp, for useful versus dangerous roots, for the smell before rain on heated iron.

And around them lives the greater resident: the city itself.

Not sentient, perhaps, but animate in the way an old orchard, reef, monastery, ship, and machine can become animate through layered dependence. It sweats, catches, exhales, feeds, corrodes, heals, shines, leaks, and thickens. It has moods of glare and steam. It has organs below, leaf-lungs above, mineral bones, animal voices, human hands, insect shimmer, water memory. Every seam is inhabited. Every shadow has a function. Every exposed edge is bright enough to hurt.

So the answer is: a highland people of water-keepers, shade-makers, gardeners, mechanics, animal-tenders, roof-herders, pump-listeners, and bright-eyed children live there, alongside birds, goats, cats, bees, frogs, lizards, snails, moths, vines, ferns, fruit trees, mosses, and machines old enough to be treated like elders.

They live not on a mountain, but in a mountain that has been rebuilt out of labor.

And slowly, together, they are becoming its new geology.
