What lives there is not a people, exactly, and not a fauna in any clean sense. The inhabitants are adaptations to a world that will not hold still long enough for anatomy to become proud of itself.

They are low, patient, compressible things.

Most of them do not present themselves as bodies. They live as films, stains, skins, buried nets, breathings in the clay. A square handspan of wet mud contains more inhabitants than a plain full of grazing animals would elsewhere: mineral-eating slicks with bronze and green sheens; bead-chains of translucent cells strung through porewater; pale threads that grow downward until yesterday becomes accessible; shell-specks hinged like seeds; blind pulses of soft tissue that migrate along chemical gradients so slowly their movement is mistaken for discoloration. They do not move through the marsh so much as change which part of the marsh is them.

The dominant life is colonial and provisional. It has learned that to be a single creature is dangerous here. Singularity invites drowning, salting, burial, inversion, translation into another century. So life distributes itself. It keeps copies. It sends out spores, cysts, flakes, seedlike scales, detachable mouths, sleeping knots of nerve and hunger wrapped in mineral varnish. One individual may be a mat under a pool, a string of bladders on a reed stem, and a buried rind around a warm stone, all joined not by flesh but by timing. When rain falls upward from the basins, they release themselves into it. When the mud exhales, they enter the breath.

Many of the inhabitants are flat.

There are things like leaves that are not plants, spread over clay in overlapping lobes the color of old tea. Their upper surfaces are slick and light-drinking; their undersides are crowded with cilia and tiny mouths. They creep in the millimeter-deep water, grazing salts, bacteria, powdered shell, and the softer edges of footprints. When thunder from beneath passes through them, they contract into hard wrinkles and become indistinguishable from flakes of sediment. When the pressure eases, they unfold again, wider than before.

In the reed clusters live folded pale creatures, thin as wet paper, clasped to the undersides of stems. They have no evident heads until feeding, when one edge puckers open into a fringe of fine red threads. They drink condensation, spores, reed sap, and the faint electrical trembling that travels through the stalks before storm-states. Their young are transparent scales laid in rows inside reed joints. When the reeds drown, the scales do not die; they become part of the drifting scum, waiting for another vertical thing to exist.

The worms are among the more visible residents, though only during certain rains. They emerge from vertical cracks and stand half-upright, not because they aspire upward, but because the air is briefly richer than the mud. They are glassy, faintly banded, and full of slow dark particles moving within them. They taste with their whole skins. Some fork as they rise, splitting into three or four tasting ribbons, then merge again before retreating. Others harden if touched by dry mineral crust, becoming pale rods that may lie dormant for years until a new basin forms around them. They are not prey in the ordinary sense. Anything that eats them also becomes, for a time, part of their buried network.

There are shell-creatures no larger than lentils, living in mobile villages across the surfaces of shallow pools. Each carries a hinged mineral plate made from whatever epoch the pool bottom has exposed that day: rust-red, green-black, chalk-white, blue-gray. They graze on lightning blooms, those sudden microbial flushes that appear after electrical storms. When disturbed, they all close at once, and a pool that was alive becomes a bed of colored gravel. Hours later the gravel opens and resumes feeding, now arranged in a different pattern, as though a small geology has reconsidered itself.

Some creatures are almost entirely absence.

At night, if the dimming can be called night, the larger animals move as quiet hollows in the general roar. Their bodies muffle sound before they are seen. A traveler would first notice that rain no longer seems to strike a patch of water, that reed-clatter stops in a moving oval, that the mud’s sucking mutter becomes careful. Then a long black nostril may rise, bead-bright, followed by the faint ridge of a back traveling just below the surface.

These long-backed basin animals are ancient in their design, if design is the word. They resemble submerged otters, crocodiles, lungfish, and moving bars of peat without fully becoming any of them. Their bodies are low and muscular, coated in a hide that grows mineral crusts in changing plates. The plates are not armor so much as memory: salt from southern flats, iron from seep-stones, pale shell powder from drowned shoals, black fungal varnish from reed marshes. Their limbs are short and webbed, but they rarely swim openly. They press themselves through saturated mud, using water, silt, and pressure as one medium. Their eyes are sealed under translucent lids except during mating storms. They navigate by the tension of basins ahead of them, by bubbles rising from places not yet crossed.

They feed by passing slowly over living films and pressing them into grooves along their bellies. The grooves digest selectively. What is useful enters the body; what is not is rolled into pellets and left behind, each pellet a concentrated seed of ecology. Whole reedbeds sometimes begin from the droppings of one such animal.

The black turtle-like grazers are more steadfast. Each chooses, or is chosen by, a mineral seep. There it may remain for a lifetime longer than many landscapes elsewhere. Its shell is flexible, segmented, and soft-edged, like old leather soaked in tar. It can flatten until it is only a hump in the mud, or swell with stored water until its plates lift and expose mats of symbiotic growth beneath. Its mouth is broad, lipless, and patient. It scrapes green and silver films from stone, then waits days for them to regrow. Around old grazers the ground changes color in circles. Their scraping exposes earlier strata; their waste seals later ones. A seep with a grazer becomes an archive slowly eaten into legibility.

The tall waders are seldom seen whole because the marsh rarely permits wholes.

Their legs descend from the mist like jointed reeds, long and backward-bending, dark at the knees, pale at the tendons. Their feet are wide, many-toed, and soft-rimmed, spreading weight over treacherous crusts. Above, hidden in vapor, hang narrow bodies with shallow keels and skins beaded constantly with condensation. They feed from vents by lowering ribbon tongues, sometimes many yards long, into boiling mud. The tongues are blue-black, muscular, and fringed with sensory hairs that can sort heat, acidity, metal, rot, and time-exposed clay. They do not merely eat organisms from the vents. They drink the vent’s condition: minerals, microbes, warm gases, and the brief opportunities created when deep layers meet air.

Their heads, when lightning reveals them, are small and grave, with wide lateral eyes and no beaks. Their calls arrive at the wrong time. A cry heard now may belong to a wader that passed an hour ago, or one still coming through the gray chamber from the north. The creatures accept this. Their mating depends on it. They answer calls not by location but by delay.

Birds exist, though they are less like birds than decisions made by the vapor to carry bone for a while. Wide-winged and nearly silent, they drift low under the warm ceiling, feeding on gnat-clouds, spore knots, and the small upward rains that rise from disturbed basins. Their wings are broad, fingered, and translucent at the trailing edge. They seldom flap. Their bodies are dark in outline but pale when seen from beneath, as though filled with diluted sky. Nests, if they build them, are not fixed structures. They braid floating mats from reed fiber and microbial skin, lay eggs into them, and allow the mats to wander. Chicks hatch already able to cling flat and wait through submersion.

The eggs of these birds are famous among no one, but they should be. They do not contain embryos at first. They contain weather-sensitivities, small milky gradients that arrange themselves only after several storms have passed through. A clutch laid in a saline crust-hollow becomes one kind of chick; the same clutch drifting into warm reedwater becomes another. Species here are less inherited than negotiated.

There are insects, or gnat-like specks, though many are not insects in any strict lineage. They rise in trembling columns over warm pools, each body a dot of hunger with glass wings and a stomach full of spores. Some have larvae that live inside bubbles under mud skins, breathing the gases of decay. Others spend most of their lives as airborne cysts, only becoming winged when they pass through the refractive knots that hang in the air. They bite rarely. More often they sample: salt from skin, mineral from tears, fungus from cloth, memory from old stone basins if they settle there long enough.

The bladdered stalks have inhabitants of their own. Their translucent crowns collect moisture, and inside each bladder swim small comma-shaped animals with lampblack eyespots. They live suspended above the ground, enclosed in plant water, feeding on whatever the stalk condenses from the air. When the bladder overfills and drips, some are released. Most die in the root hollow. A few burrow into the stalk’s base and become cysts, waiting to climb into the next generation of bladders. The stalk and animal are not quite separate species. In dry places elsewhere they might have diverged. Here, wetness keeps their bargain unresolved.

Beneath all of this, in the deeper saturated beds, live the pressure animals.

They are rarely seen because emergence would injure them. Their bodies are broad, boneless, and layered, made to occupy seams between clay strata. They move by redistributing themselves, thinning in one place, thickening in another, squeezing water ahead of them. They feed on buried organics, old peat, mineral-rich seepage, and the dead compressed into useful softness. When they shift, pools leap. Mudbanks settle. Stone rings tilt another fraction. The largest of them may be mistaken for geological adjustment, and perhaps that is what they are: animals that have become events of subsidence.

They have no faces, but they have fronts. Their fronts are warmer, chemically awake, crowded with sensory folds that read weight and rain through yards of mud. They know the marsh from underneath as a slow map of pressure. They know where a basin will open before the surface knows. They know when a causeway is hollowing. They know the old rings by the cooler stones pressing down like memories in the wet.

Some of the ring-ruins are inhabited, though not by builders.

In the still basins carved into slabs live reflective colonies. They appear as dark water, brim-full and unrippled, but the surface is a membrane maintained by countless microscopic bodies arranged in tension. They do not reflect what is above them. They reflect what is chemically, temporally, or hydrologically adjacent: a reedbed miles away, lightning from a future storm, a clay bank under erosion, a submerged animal passing under another flat. The colonies feed on attention as much as on minerals. Looking into them changes the pitch of the sound nearby because the membrane tightens, listening back through the bones of the observer.

Anything that touches them is not wetted. It is indexed. A finger withdrawn from such a basin may carry no moisture, but later mud will remember its shape before it presses down. Small creatures drink from these basins only by extending disposable filaments and abandoning them afterward.

The upright slabs and dark rings also host slow lichens that are half mineral, half recollection. They grow in glyphlike fans along the leeward sides of stones, though there is no reliable wind. Their colors are muted—ash violet, black green, dull ocher—but after thunder they briefly brighten into complex maps. These lichens digest weathering. They draw nourishment from the difference between what a stone was and what rain is making of it. On very old stones they become thick enough to peel away in sheets, and under each sheet the stone is younger-looking, not restored exactly, but temporally abraded.

If anything like intelligence lives there, it is not housed in skulls alone.

The marsh favors distributed cognition. The reed clusters exchange pressure signals through rootwater. The microbial films alter flow to direct nutrients. The shell-creatures close in patterns that redirect sunlight into shallow pools. The basin membranes store reflected elsewhere. The pressure animals move beneath and change the drainage of entire districts. None of these alone is a mind like a human mind. Together, during storm-states, they approach something like deliberation.

When the organ-tones rise and the land begins making and unmaking itself, the inhabitants do not merely endure. They participate.

Flat grazers fasten down and release digestive acids that soften crusts in branching patterns. Reed creatures beat their pale bodies against stems, adding a dry rattling code to the thunder. Shell-creatures close in waves, altering the albedo of pools. Worms rise by the millions, opening vertical conduits for gas and warm rain. Bladder-stalks drip faster, dosing their roots with condensed minerals. Deep pressure animals shift away from or toward rising domes. The result is not planning, but neither is it accident. The storm thinks with them because they are part of the matter with which the storm thinks.

The faces that appear in water and clay during these states are not gods, but they are not illusions. They are temporary portraits of total participation: drainage, hunger, seepage, memory, pressure, spore release, vent heat, animal movement, microbial bloom. A brow of silt may include the migration of worms. A mouth of collapsing peat may be opened by a pressure animal turning below. Paired pool-eyes may be held reflective by colonies feeding on lightning from days away.

There may once have been people here.

If so, their descendants are not upright tool-users walking the flats with names for things. The place is older than the habit of standing upright, and it corrects such habits. But in some chambers of clay mounds, where erosion has opened interiors like wet lungs, there are hand-smoothed hollows that fit no current animal. There are stacked stones too evenly patient to be chance. There are basins that accept attention as if attention was part of their intended use.

Perhaps the builders entered the marsh slowly, not by death but by concession. Their houses sank. Their paths became causeways, then ridges, then suggestions. Their rituals became hydrology. Their memories entered reflective basins. Their bodies fed films, reeds, shell-beds, pressure seams. What remains of them may be distributed among the ring-ruins: a habit of arrangement in certain stones, a tonal preference in basin hums, a tendency for some reedbeds to lean toward watchers. If they live, they live as a culture dissolved into process.

There are also things that only intermittently qualify as alive.

In some saltier southern flats, red reeds grow from brine and produce hard black nodules at their roots. During dry-clicking thunder, the nodules loosen and roll over the crusts, tapping against one another. Where three or more touch, they unfold into jointed little walkers, root and animal at once, and travel toward fresher seepage. If rain catches them, they plant themselves instantly and become reeds again. If no water comes, they hollow out and fill with gnat eggs.

In the west, under wandering rainfields, drowned basins hold broad gelatinous rafts the color of smoke. They are almost invisible until rain strikes them; then each impact lights a ring inside their bodies. They feed on impact, on vibration, on the difference between falling water and standing water. Birds avoid them. Long-backed animals pass under them with care. A raft torn by storm does not die. Each piece rounds itself and begins listening separately.

In the northern gray chamber, among the aligned low mounds like sleeping backs, live mound-breathers. They are not the mounds, but inhabit them so completely that distinction fails. The mounds swell and subside with slow respiration. Their surfaces are clothed in silver abrasive moss, and beneath that moss are chambers full of warm vapor and blind white tendrils. Once in a long while a mound opens a seam and exhales a cloud of spores dense enough to dim the already dim light. Animals caught in the cloud become quiet and lie down. Later they rise furred in moss, carrying the mound’s offspring to other high ribs of clay.

The central shallow sea has its own inhabitants, adapted to water that is never deep and never merely surface. There are wake-readers there: small triangular fishlike things that swim ahead of travelers because the wake spreads before the footstep. They gather where a step will be, nibbling at compressed organisms stirred by a pressure that has not yet arrived. Their bodies are silver-brown and laterally flat; their eyes face forward and backward at once, divided by a vertical seam. To catch one by hand is nearly impossible, because it flees the intention before the movement.

Under the quilt of colored clays live epoch-feeders. They bore downward into one layer, digest its mineral-organic signature, then surface changed in color to match it. Green-black feeders are soft and cold. Rust feeders are sharp-edged and metallic-tasting. Bone-white feeders become brittle and shed flakes that seed microbial bloom. They are preyed upon by ribbon-tongued waders and by the mud itself, which closes over their tunnels when chronology shifts.

All these lives share certain principles.

They mistrust height. Height is exposure, leverage, future collapse. Even the tall waders keep their bodies in vapor, refusing full declaration. The reeds are jointed to break and root again. The bladdered stalks condense the sky only to drip it back into their own hollows. Nothing rises without a plan for becoming low again.

They mistrust hard outlines. Skin grades into slime, shell into sediment, root into seep, colony into weather. Bodies seal when touched. Mouths appear only where needed. Limbs are temporary assertions. Many animals can flatten, liquefy, cyst, mineralize, or become a stain until conditions pass.

They mistrust time. Eggs wait for storms to decide them. Pools remember stones before stones arrive. Larvae hatch into forms suited to extinct or future waters. Some creatures reproduce backward by leaving remains that later organize into ancestors. A dead grazer’s shell may become the nursery of the films it once ate, and those films may feed the young of its own line.

They do not seek permanence of form. They seek continuity of process.

The marsh’s inhabitants are therefore not beautiful in the clean, separate way of deer, hawks, flowers, forests. Their beauty is the beauty of endurance without monument. A worm tasting thunder with its whole body. A basin that reflects a storm days away because its surface is alive and listening. A black grazer spending a century widening a green ring around a seep. A bird egg waiting to learn what kind of weather it was born into. A pressure animal turning in the deep clay, altering the fate of ponds it will never see.

They live by yielding, but not passively. They are not victims of the saturated world. They are its methods made flesh, film, shell, vapor, and delay.

From far away, the place seems nearly empty: flat wet immensity under dim warm sky. Up close, it is overfull with lives that have abandoned the vanity of standing apart. They cling, seep, bloom, fold, hum, graze, listen, dissolve, and return. They are mud’s citizens, weather’s organs, geology’s last animals.

And when silence threatens—if ever the rain should pause too completely, if the mud should stop gulping, if the underground tones should fall away—they begin to move. Films tighten. Worms rise. Reed-creatures rattle. Basins hum harder. Deep pressure bodies shift in their seams. The whole marsh stirs to keep the sound going.

Because sound is circulation.

Because disturbance is breath.

Because in that low immense place, to live is not to remain oneself.

It is to take part.
