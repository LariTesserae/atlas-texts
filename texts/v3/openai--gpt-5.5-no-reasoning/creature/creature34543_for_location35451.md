What lives there is not one thing at first.

It is a citizenship of slowness.

The most obvious inhabitants are small and intermittent: snails, insects, mosses, lichens, cold ferns, cushion plants, sedges, wind-braided dwarf willows, the brief hare-colored animal that appears only long enough to make absence specific. They live there in the ordinary biological sense. They feed, cling, hatch, withdraw, leave trails and spores and barely legible disturbances. But none of them seems to possess the place. They are not residents so much as visible organs of a larger resident.

The true inhabitant is the town’s weathered continuity: a communal creature made of stone, wetness, cold, sound, and the life that has learned to occupy their seams.

It has no body separate from the plateau. Its bones are the old schist walls, low and rounded, no longer sure whether they were built or extruded. Its skin is lichen: yellow, blue, orange, white in frost, green in thaw, changing its script with every season. Its fur is moss in the shaded corners, thick and black-rooted and holding water against the stone. Its breath is the mist that condenses before dawn and evening. Its blood is the hidden seepage that moves beneath the lanes and returns through cracks, bowls, gutters, and the central pool. Its voice is everywhere the wind is shaped: fluting through narrow passages, booming in roofless chambers, humming under lintels, speaking most loudly when rain strikes the whole settlement into percussion.

It lives by being nearly still.

Not asleep, not dead, not waiting for restoration. It is past those categories. It has survived beyond usefulness, beyond construction, beyond abandonment. It is a civic organism whose citizens are processes: thaw, seep, root, frost, lichen-bloom, erosion, condensation, echo. The plateau has become social. Water has habits. Wind has rooms. Moss has districts. Snails patrol the inscriptions like slow readers. The central pool listens.

If it has a mind, it is distributed and damp.

The mind gathers most strongly in the hollow at the center. That pool, impossible and dark, is not an eye exactly, though at night it looks like one. It is more like a memory organ. Everything drains toward it: water, vibration, the tapping speech of rain, the groans from cavities underneath, the reflected fragments of doorways and sky. It trembles with messages too low to be called language. It receives the town’s small events and enlarges them. A drop becomes a resonant note. A hidden seep becomes a pulse. Frost strain becomes a thought.

The markings on the walls are part of that thought. They are not writing in the human sense, but they are not meaningless either. They are records made by preference: where moisture pauses, where salts bloom, where shade holds, where moss advances and retreats, where snails graze, where ice has lifted a grain of mineral and placed it elsewhere. The town writes without intending to say. It forgets without losing continuity. Each winter gives it grammar; each sunlit spell edits it. A wall may become eloquent in frost and mute by noon.

So what lives there is a reader that is also the text.

It reads itself through water routes, pressure, color changes, recurring dampness, the return of insects, the thickness of moss, the collapse of grit. It does not know in flashes. It knows by accumulation. It knows that nothing tall lasts. It knows movement prefers edges. It knows how to divide wind into tones and cold into chambers. It knows where snails will wake first when fog comes, where the frost will split a slab in another hundred years, where a hare-like foot will choose the seam instead of the open square.

The smaller animals belong to it cautiously.

The white-backed corvid is more visitor than citizen. It crosses above as if inspecting a thought too old to perch on. Sometimes it drops to a rimstone and turns its head toward the hollow, listening, then leaves before the wind changes. It does not trust the town’s acoustics. The sound there misplaces distances. A drip may seem close as a heartbeat and a wingbeat may vanish into the roar below the plateau.

The insects are seasonal sparks of the resident body. They hatch where sunlight finds the pools, hovering in pale swarms like static lifted from water. Their lives are too brief to understand the place as old. To them the black bowls are worlds, the moss walls forests, the wind a catastrophe with intervals. They rise, glitter, mate, fail, fall, and become almost nothing: a film, a mineral addition, a reason for some lichen to thicken.

The snails are closer to priests, if the place can be allowed priests. They come out when the city wets itself. Their pale flattened shells seem grown from the same patience as the stone bowls. They travel over wall-faces and thresholds, eating the soft margins of the false letters, revising the inscriptions with their mouths. They are slow enough to participate in the town’s time, but still too quick to be its equals. A snail may cross a single lintel in a night; the lintel has been lowering itself into an arch for centuries.

The hare-colored creature is a rumor of warmth. It lives in the seam between shelter and exposure, appearing among sedges, vanishing under stones, existing mostly as tracks that fill with condensation before they can become evidence. It knows the town not as a ruin, nor as a mystery, but as a map of dangers and edible margins. It avoids the open hollow when the wind is strongest. It feeds where dwarf willow bends low. Its body is the color of weathered grass and gravel, as if even flesh here must apologize for visibility.

The plants are the patient households.

Moss keeps the oldest domesticity: it carpets rooms, rounds sharpness further, holds cold water in green darkness. It makes beds no one lies in except insects, snails, spores, and perhaps the temporary belly of the small animal during storms. Ferns occupy drains like listeners in doorways. Cushion plants sit between stones with the compact stubbornness of sealed thoughts. Sedges and silver grasses fill some courtyards with sudden abundance, so that a person crossing from one bare lane into one of those hidden gardens might feel the settlement inhale.

The dwarf willows are not trees here. Nothing is allowed to be a tree. They are gestures of treehood pressed flat by law. Their branches braid with the wind because uprightness is punished. They live by yielding before they can be broken. This is one of the place’s oldest teachings.

But beneath all these inhabitants is the deeper one: the plateau-town itself, alive in the sense that a reef is alive, though much of it is mineral; alive in the sense that a language is alive, though no one speaker owns it; alive in the sense that a body remains alive while nearly all its movements are involuntary.

It is not benevolent. It is not hostile. It is exact.

It permits lushness only in pockets. It denies height. It gathers sound. It hoards moisture in shallow bowls and hidden throats. It turns every intrusion into another surface for weather. If humans once lived there, they have been digested out of authorship. Their possible walls have become geological habits. Their possible thresholds now face inward not toward community, perhaps, but toward condensation, resonance, the central dark.

The resident has learned to resemble abandonment because that is the safest shape for long endurance.

At noon it withdraws into appearance: a ruined town, empty stone, lichens, exposed sky. In that plain light, life seems incidental. One might say nothing lives there but moss and a few wary animals.

By evening the error becomes clear.

Moisture returns like circulation. The western rooms bead with cold sweat. Snails loosen. Ferns take back their uncurling. Hidden water ticks in walls. The pool exhales its faint vapor. Wind enters the five lanes and is sorted into voices. The whole place becomes less an object than a metabolism.

The thing that lives there is then unmistakable, though still unnameable: an old civic ecology, a weather-being, a ruin that has outlived ruin by becoming habitat and instrument.

It does not walk. It conducts.

It conducts water from rim to hollow, from sky to crack, from condensation to moss-root, from basin to vapor. It conducts sound through chambers and lanes. It conducts cold into stone and stone into frost. It conducts the minute lives of snails, insects, lichens, sedges, and furtive mammals into patterns that resemble intention. It conducts time so slowly that time seems spatial: a lane, a hollow, a wall, a bowl.

If approached as a creature, it might be described like this:

low-bodied, spread across the plateau; ancient; dark-backed with pale mineral scarring; green in its folds; wet-eyed at the center; many-mouthed in rain; mostly silent before dawn; intolerant of vertical ambition; nourished by fog, abrasion, freeze, seepage, and the small grazing of translucent animals; capable of song when weather passes through it.

Its young are not offspring but new hollows, new lichen maps, new moss-thick seams. Its wounds are cracks that become drains. Its memories are routes water repeats. Its dreams, if it dreams, are of flatterness—not death, but completion without conclusion.

And because it is so old, it has almost no need to distinguish between the living and the nonliving. Stone shelters moss; moss holds water; water splits stone; frost opens rooms; rooms alter wind; wind dries lichen; lichen feeds snails; snails edit the walls; their trails catch moisture; moisture darkens the stone again. Around and around, not in a circle but in a civic order too slow for ceremony.

Who lives there?

A town lives there.

Not in the sense that people occupy it, but in the sense that the town itself has become the occupant: a cold, porous, weather-sung organism wearing the last possible shape of settlement. It lives with moss for flesh, frost for nerves, water for memory, wind for voice, and small shy animals for passing thoughts. It has almost finished becoming landscape, and that almost is its life.
