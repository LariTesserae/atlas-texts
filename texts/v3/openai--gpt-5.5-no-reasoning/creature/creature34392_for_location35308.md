What lives there is not one people, nor one species, nor even a cleanly separable town. What lives there is a wet, negotiated society: human beings, yes, but also goats, ducks, moss, roof-snakes, beans, termites, fig roots, bees, drains, fungi, watercourses, and the particular habits that have grown between them.

The human inhabitants are mountain wetland people, though they would probably not call themselves that. They are compact in build more often than tall, not because the place demands it in any mystical way, but because generations of steep paths, low lintels, narrow bridges, loaded baskets, and constant climbing favor balance over reach. They move with the slight forward lean of people used to slopes. Their feet know before their eyes do whether a stone is slick, whether a plank has softened, whether moss will hold or shear away. Even the very old among them step with an intimate caution that is not fear. Children, by contrast, move like animals: barefoot, muddy-kneed, vanishing up ladders and through vine gaps, crossing chicken-bridges adults pretend not to use.

Their clothing is layered against damp rather than cold alone. Wool where it can be kept dry, bark-cloth and woven reed for work, short cloaks oiled with resin or rubbed with lanolin, skirts and trousers tied high when channels overflow. Sleeves are often rolled. Ankles are often bare. Hats are practical and various: broad leaf-lined rain hats, soft wool caps, woven hoods, patched shoulder capes stiffened with smoke. Almost everyone carries cord. Almost everyone has a knife, though not usually a weapon in the grand sense—more a beak, a claw, an extension of the hand for cutting vine, scraping fungus, gutting fish, trimming rot, slicing bread, freeing a goat from its own stupidity.

Their hands are never clean for long. There is earth under nails, green stain on fingers, small cuts from reeds, dark marks from tannin, resin, smoke, bruised herbs. Their palms are hard from basket handles and wet rope. They smell faintly of wool, woodsmoke, damp skin, crushed leaves, and whatever work they have done that morning: ducks, clay, mushrooms, fish, nettle, milk, ash.

They are not solemn forest mystics. They are busy, sharp-tongued, practical, loud when necessary, and often necessary is most of the day. A person calling from one terrace to another has to cut through frogs, water, birds, and the clatter of work, so speech here carries. Their voices are trained by distance and obstruction. They shout warnings before greetings: “The lower channel is backing!” “Your goat is on the bean roof!” “Do not step there!” Names are flung upward and downward like thrown stones.

They know one another too well. In a small place where a blocked gutter may flood three households below, privacy exists but independence does not. A lazy roof-gardener becomes everyone’s mosquito problem. A careless child with a sluice gate can sour a whole duck pond. A family that cuts too much living fence may loosen a path. So people watch one another, interfere with one another, rescue one another, resent one another, feed one another, and remember everything. Their social life is as terraced as the town: layered obligation, grievance, gratitude, inheritance, water-right, animal-right, pruning-right, roof-access, path-use.

They measure wealth less by coins than by reliable things: a spring that does not fail in dry weeks, a mature fruiting vine, a roof that sheds well, a dry sleeping platform, healthy goats, clean baskets, a smokehouse that does not mold, daughters and sons who return when drains clog, neighbors who answer when called, a living wall with deep roots. A family may be poor in metal and rich in water. Another may own knives, bells, and trade cloth but be mocked because their terrace slumps every second rain.

Their houses are not inert possessions. A household tends a dwelling almost as one tends an animal or orchard. Posts may still be alive, slowly thickening. A wall may need feeding with woven brush, mud, and cuttings. Roofs are weeded, harvested, patched, grazed, defended from monkeys, reseeded after storms. Children are scolded not for scratching furniture but for damaging root-bindings. Old people sit under eaves and notice things: the change in drip rhythm, the smell of rot, the angle of a beam, a new ant trail, a fern that has browned because water has shifted elsewhere.

The elders are repositories of weather memory. They know which year the west retaining wall sighed and had to be replanted. Which storm opened the cold spring below the fig house. Which family ignored horsetails and woke with water under their beds. Which vine bridge was once only a hand-rope. Which tree was trained by whose grandmother. Their authority comes not from abstraction but from having survived enough rain to recognize its intentions early.

Children are raised by relatives, neighbors, animals, and danger. Not dramatic danger, but constant small consequence. They learn that ducks bite, goats shove, bees dislike foolish hands, monkeys are thieves but not demons, snakes deserve warning, and water always takes the path it is given unless it finds a better one. They learn tasks young because there is no harmless work to invent for them; real work can be made small. A child may clear leaf cups, turn jars upside down, fetch dry kindling from a protected loft, sort edible snails from nuisance ones, chase chickens off seedlings, listen for a changed sound in a pipe. By adolescence they can cross the town by roof, root, bridge, wall, and drain edge without touching a formal path.

Their religion, if named religion, is probably inseparable from maintenance. The sacred is not remote. It is in first water, old roots, springs that should not be muddied, frogs that keep cisterns healthy, trees that hold terraces, stones that remember the original ledge. Shrines are small and numerous: tucked into roots, hung from central trees, placed beside sluice gates, built into retaining walls, sheltered under roof eaves where bees hum nearby. Offerings are practical, perishable, and quickly incorporated: first steam from cooked grain, a twist of wool, bright berries, a carved wooden frog, a shell from the lower river, a dab of honey, a little fermented drink poured where water begins.

They may have priests, but if so the priests carry hoes. Ritual specialists would know calendars of rain, grafting songs, the right words for opening a repaired channel, the taboo days for cutting living willow, the dreams that warn of slope movement, the old chants used while setting root-mats into a weakened wall. A blessing that does not help the water run clear is not much of a blessing.

Their dead likely remain close. In a wet mountain town, burial is complicated. Some may be placed in stone-lined family niches in drier caves, wrapped in smoked cloth with herbs against mold. Some may be given to root terraces, not as crude disposal but as incorporation into household ground, ancestors literally holding the ledge. Names might attach to trees, springs, bridges, and walls more than graves. “Grandmother’s fig” may be both botanical fact and genealogy. To inherit land here is to inherit the dead’s unfinished maintenance.

Their food is damp-country food: fermented roots, smoked goat meat, fresh herbs, sour greens, mushrooms carefully sorted, snails, fish from water baskets, duck eggs, goat milk, flatbreads cooked on clay griddles, beans from trellises, berries, pickled shoots, roasted insects, honey, nettle broth, pepper pastes, soft cheeses kept in cool caves, fruits that must be eaten quickly before mold, and stews that steam all day because the fire is small. Much is sour, smoked, salted when salt can be had, bitter with medicinal leaves, or bright with crushed mountain herbs. They know preservation intimately because rot is always waiting its turn.

They do not eat alone if they can avoid it, partly from sociability and partly because food spoils and labor is shared. Market days are not merely trade but redistribution of perishables: too many mushrooms after rain, too many eggs, a goat slaughtered because it broke a leg, a sudden flush of berries, fish trapped in a repaired channel. People haggle fiercely, but everyone knows when abundance must move fast.

The animals are not pets in the soft sense, though some are loved extravagantly. Dogs are wet, narrow, clever, and opinionated, sleeping under platforms and barking at civets. Cats are semi-domestic roof shadows, tolerated because grain exists. Chickens belong everywhere and nowhere. Ducks are respected workers, patrolling channels with busy seriousness. Goats are wealth, nuisance, milk, meat, and climbing disaster. Bees are neighbors with stings and rent paid in honey and pollination. Monkeys are cursed like rival children. Snakes are given civic status: not friends, not enemies, but participants in grain protection and rodent control, unless they violate sleeping space.

The nonhuman life has adapted to the humans as much as the humans to it. Birds time their raids to market noise. Frogs breed in forgotten containers despite all rules. Moss grows thickest where feet pass less often, but certain mosses tolerate the lanes and become living carpets. Fungi colonize storage mistakes with opportunistic genius. Civets know which roofs sag near smokehouses. Otters understand the lower waterworks better than any map. Bees choose eaves with morning light. Lizards haunt warm clay walls. Worms turn every fallen leaf, every scrap, every storm’s mess into future terrace.

There are probably specialist families or guild-like lineages, though none can afford only one skill. Some people are waterkeepers, able to hear a clog forming by the changed pitch of a gutter. Some are graft-tenders who shape trees across decades. Some breed goats sure-footed enough for wet stone. Some weave the strongest wall-mats from reed, willow, and vine. Some are mushroom-knowers, which is a dangerous and prestigious knowledge. Some smoke meat, tan hides, carve sluice gates, make clay pipe, keep bees, trade with lowland iron sellers, repair footbridges, train children in songs that encode paths and water rules.

Their art is likely everywhere but rarely framed as art. Patterns woven into baskets may indicate household, water-right, marriage line, or joke. Handrails are carved where hands rest. Bells on goats carry distinct tones so owners can identify them through mist. Rain hats may be dyed with fern and berry patterns. Children carve tiny animals and tuck them into wall moss. Clay jars are marked not merely with ownership but with warnings: clean water, souring fruit, sleeping frogs, do not seal, elder’s mushrooms. Songs imitate water rhythms, bird calls, frog pulses, work beats. Their music would be percussive but soft: loom clack, seed rattle, hollow-log drum, bronze bell, voices staggered across terraces.

They are probably suspicious of dryland people. Not hostile necessarily, but amused and cautious. Visitors from below would slip, complain of damp bedding, block drains through ignorance, mistake living walls for hedges, panic at snakes, and overvalue straight roads. Lowlanders bring iron, salt, cloth, stories, perhaps disease, perhaps officials who imagine property as lines on a flat surface. The mountain town understands property as responsibility in three dimensions: above, below, upstream, downstream, rooted, seasonal. A contract that ignores water is nonsense.

Strangers are watched most closely around channels. Hospitality exists, but instruction begins immediately: step there, not there; do not lean on that rail; cover your grain; shake your cloak outside; if you hear that bell at night, move uphill; if frogs stop all at once, listen; do not drink from that gutter no matter how clear it looks. A guest who learns quickly may be embraced. A guest who laughs at the rules will not be trusted with so much as a basket of wet laundry.

Their temperament is shaped by recurrence. They do not believe in final repairs. Every solution is temporary, but temporary may mean a hundred years if tended well. This gives them a particular patience, not passive but cyclical. They expect return: rain returns, moss returns, rot returns, monkeys return, leaks return, arguments return, children return from the lower market with unsuitable lovers, old walls return to needing hands. Disaster is not an exception to order but one of its ingredients.

At the same time, they are quick in emergency. When a terrace shifts, all ordinary quarrels pause. People arrive with stakes, mats, stones, ropes, children carrying pegs, elders pointing, dogs barking uselessly, goats removed with insults. They know how to make a failing place hold until morning. They know which plants root fastest. They know how to divert water in the dark by sound and touch. This competence is communal, and pride attaches to it. Cowardice would be less condemned than negligence.

Their bodies carry the town’s marks: leech scars at the ankles, smoke-reddened eyes, strong calves, bent backs in the elderly, hands nicked by knives and reeds, shoulders hardened by basket straps. Their hearing is finely tuned to water. Their sense of smell notices rot before sight confirms it. They may dislike silence. Total silence would mean something wrong: no frogs, no insects, no drip, no birds. A quiet house is suspicious; a quiet channel is urgent.

They sleep lightly during rain. A change in roof sound can wake an entire household. Lamps are kept ready in clay niches. Children learn not to cry out at every night noise, because the town is full of harmless movement, but they also learn which sounds bring adults upright at once: the deep wet thud of earth giving way, the crack of a living support splitting, the sudden rush of water where no rush should be, goats screaming in the wrong tone, dogs barking toward the outer walls before dawn.

What lives there, then, is a culture of permeability. The people do not imagine themselves outside nature, but neither do they vanish into it. They prune, divert, harvest, trap, smoke, argue, build, cut, and kill when necessary. They are not gentle in a sentimental way. They eat animals they have named. They tear out invasive growth. They drown pests, burn beetles from beams, curse monkeys, and sacrifice beautiful vines if the wall beneath is failing. But they understand that domination is too expensive and usually stupid. Better to recruit the world than fight it endlessly.

The town itself is almost a creature. Its bones are old stone terraces. Its veins are channels and hollow-log pipes. Its skin is moss, reed mat, clay, bark, leaf, and thatch. Its hair is grass and fern. Its breath is mist and woodsmoke. Its digestion is compost, duck pond, reed bed, worm soil. Its memory is root and path. Its voice is water striking stone, frogs in cisterns, people shouting through green light.

The humans are not masters of this creature. They are one of its organs: the quick-handed, fire-keeping, rule-making part that remembers storms, moves stones, trains trees, and tells children where not to step. They live inside something that would swallow them if they stopped working, and sustain them because they do not stop.

So whoever lives there is best described as a many-bodied household of the mountain: damp-footed people and their necessary beasts; ancestral trees and tolerated snakes; roof bees, drain ducks, wall moss, basket fungi, mist-fed gardens; all bound by water, obligation, and repair. They are a town that has learned to be habitat, and a habitat that has learned to keep people.
