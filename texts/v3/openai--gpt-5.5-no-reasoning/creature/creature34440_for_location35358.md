What lives there is not one creature, though at first the place gives the impression of a single inhabitant distributed through many bodies.

The hollow is occupied by a community that has learned to be almost geological.

Its creatures are small, cold-blooded or nearly so, made for edges, seams, wet stone, and long waiting. They do not belong to open slopes or wind-bright ridges. They belong to the underside of things: to overhangs, to seepage, to the brief darkness beneath a lifted shard. Their lives are arranged around moisture so scarce that every bead of water becomes a territory, a signal, a season. They are not primitive in the sense of being simple. They are primitive like laws are primitive: prior, exact, difficult to disobey.

The most numerous are the clickers on the walls.

They resemble crickets only if seen in the wrong light and too quickly. Their bodies are narrow and high-strung, jointed into sharp angles like broken mineral veins. Each has six long walking limbs and two shorter grasping limbs folded beneath the head, which they use to comb lichen and bacterial films from the rock. Their shells are not glossy but matte, dark brown to violet-black, dusted with fine pale grit that helps them vanish against shale. In the coldest hours they stiffen in place, clustered along cracks like seed cases. When the mist comes, they wake all at once.

Their clicking is not mere noise. It is contact, mapping, warning, courtship, argument. The hollow is too dark for ordinary distance, too echoing for clean direction. So they speak in taps: leg-spurs against stone, mandibles against mineral crust, abdomen plates flicked open and shut. Each ledge has its dialect. A shale shelf gives a dry wooden tick; basalt returns a glassier sound; damp walls mute the signal until it becomes nearly a pulse. They know the basin by resonance. They know when a neighbor has fallen, when a predator has shifted under a slab, when the tarn has risen by the smallest measurable amount. Their whole civilization is percussive and without memory beyond molt-scars.

Among them move the pale grazers.

These are many-legged animals, slow and translucent, with bodies like strings of wet seeds threaded together. They are neither centipedes nor worms exactly, but something between articulated hunger and moving root. Their legs are so fine that when they climb over lichen mats they seem to float. Inside them, if one were to hold them against a rare shaft of light, the gut would show as a dark green line from end to end.

They eat the black-green crusts that have colonized the stone, but never all the way down. Whether by instinct or constraint, they crop the lichen in narrow lanes and leave islands untouched. The lichen returns thicker along the borders of these paths. Thus the grazers make maps without intending to: dark branching networks over raw rock, patterns of feeding and regrowth that last longer than any individual body. At night they emerge in processions from under shale flakes, each following the chemical damp of the one before it. If disturbed, they coil into flattened spirals, and a faint mineral odor comes off them, like struck flint under rain.

They do not cross the red seam on the eastern wall. None of them do. Generations have ended at its edge. Perhaps the iron there poisons them. Perhaps some old heat still leaks through it. Perhaps something below the seam listens for their feet. The cause no longer matters. The prohibition is built into them more deeply than hunger.

In the seep-pools live the blunt-headed amphibians.

They are dark above, almost the color of wet basalt, and pale below with a dull silver cast, as though their bellies have borrowed light from the tarn and never returned it. Their eyes are small, lidless, and set high on the head. They sit half-buried in mineral silt with only nostrils and eyes showing, so motionless that condensation collects on their backs. Their limbs are short but strong, with wide toes suited not for swimming far but for gripping slick stone. When they move, they do not dart. They vanish between one glance and the next. The ripples they leave behind are slow, heavy, and strangely delayed.

Their throats make the knocking sounds from the pools. Not croaks, not calls, but hollow double-notes: stone on stone underwater. The sound carries through cracks and seams. It is answered from basins no wider than a hand, from hidden pockets under ledges, from the tarn’s edge on nights of fine rain. They breed in water that appears insufficient for anything larger than a beetle. Eggs cling in gelatinous strings to submerged mineral points. The young hatch nearly transparent, with external gills like black thread. Many are eaten. Many dry before their limbs form. A few survive by retreating into hairline watercourses where no larger mouth can follow.

Before rockfalls, all the adults face upslope.

No one taught them this. The hollow taught them. Some pressure moves through stone before stone gives way; some vibration too low for air enters the water first. They turn toward it with the solemnity of listeners at worship. When the fall comes, dust clouds the pools, and afterward the survivors climb out and lick fresh mineral from the new fractures.

The winged inhabitants are perhaps the most visible, though only in the manner of things briefly released from cracks.

They are thumb-sized animals with membrane wings, hooked feet, and small heavy skulls. At rest they hang in vertical fissures and beneath slanted shelves, wrapped tightly in their own skin, looking like dead leaves cut from smoke. They are not bats, though they have batlike wings. They are not birds, though some bear a sparse feathering along the neck and spine, a dark down that beads with mist. Their faces have the compressed, intent look of animals that navigate by surfaces rather than sky.

They wake when the air thickens.

Mist is their weather, their road, their permission. When the cloud seeps into the hollow, they pour out silently by the dozens, then hundreds, each one dropping before catching itself on open membrane. They do not fly high. There is no high, not to them. They stitch the basin wall to wall, feeding on small insects lifted by damp, skimming the wet faces for larvae, sometimes brushing their bellies against the tarn when the rain is fine enough to disguise the act. Then, as abruptly as they emerged, they return to their cracks and begin chattering all at once.

Their chatter is social but not affectionate. Space is scarce. Dry roosts are wealth. Every fissure has a hierarchy of warmth, damp, safety, and inherited claim. The young cling to the adults’ undersides until their claws harden. Those that fall are not retrieved. The hollow is generous only to populations, never to individuals.

In the scree are blue-glowing worms.

They live where the fresh stone is loose enough to hold darkness but tight enough to stay wet. Their bodies are soft, segmented, and coated in clear slime that picks up mineral dust. In ordinary times they are invisible. A turned stone reveals them as brief blue strokes, a color too cold to seem alive. The glow strengthens when they are injured or exposed, so that predation turns the scree into a brief constellation.

They eat rot where rot is rare: drowned insects, shed skins, failed eggs, droppings washed into cracks. They are the hollow’s hidden digestion. Without them, the little basin would foul itself with the density of its own inhabitants. With them, death becomes film, and film becomes grazer-food, and grazer-food becomes clicking life again.

The flattened predators wait beneath slabs.

They are shaped like hands without fingers, broad and low, with flexible edges that can press almost flush to stone. Their mouths are on the underside. Their eyes, if those pale pits are eyes, are arranged along the front rim. They do not chase. They inhabit thresholds: the underside of a rocking stone, the damp line between open ledge and crack, the entrance to a seep tunnel. When a pale grazer passes over them, or a young clicker steps where the stone smells safely of lichen, the predator folds upward with terrible speed.

Afterward it becomes stone again.

They are patient beyond animal dignity. A predator may remain under the same slab through whole changes of weather, feeding twice, molting once, laying a clutch of flat amber eggs cemented to the rock. When the air pressure drops before mist, they retreat deeper, not out of fear of wet but because the winged things become active then, and there are things among the winged things that will pry at them with hooked feet.

There are also the intermittent residents: the visitors from the larger mountain.

A cliff-goat comes sometimes to the rim, narrow-faced, shaggy, its coat carrying the smell of snowfields and open wind. It does not descend far. The hollow is not its country. It stands above, listening, while white insects collect on the moisture of its muzzle and eyelids. It may lick mineral salts from the raw edge where the ridge split. It may dislodge stones that kill a dozen lives below. Then it leaves, taking with it nothing of the hollow except grit in the hair of its hooves.

The long-backed scavenger comes lower.

It is built close to the ground, with dark fur made darker by condensation and pale reflective eyes that never seem aimed quite where the head points. It has learned the shelves. It moves by routes too narrow for the goat and too exposed for most prey. It eats what the hollow has already loosened: fallen winged animals, dead amphibians at the shrinking pools, crushed clickers after rockfall, the occasional grazer-mass uncovered by frost. It is quiet not because it is gentle, but because noise here belongs to the many, and a larger body must pass like an exception.

It does not rule the hollow. Nothing rules the hollow. Predation here is not monarchy. It is weather with teeth.

The corvid-like visitor is different again.

It arrives with intelligence in its feet. Dark-feathered, heavy-headed, bright-eyed, it drops from the rim carrying a shard of stone or finds one among the fresh breaks. It works the moss mats, prising out grubs and pale larvae with a concentration almost like malice. It tilts its head at echoes. It remembers which ledges pay. It may be the only creature here that can look at an object and see a use not already contained in its body.

But even its cleverness leaves no history. The chip falls. The rain moves it. Frost splits it. Scree absorbs it. Nothing accumulates into culture. The hollow permits acts, not monuments.

Beneath these visible populations are smaller lives almost too slight to name: mites like moving dust grains; springtails that launch themselves from wet moss; blind larvae in mineral seams; pale eggs glued under overhangs; fungal threads entering the dead and returning as dim pinpricks of light; colonies of bacteria making slick bronze films on the warm-exhaling crack in the western wall. These are not background. They are the deep population, the living paste by which the hollow’s food web adheres to stone.

And then there is whatever lives in the tarn.

Not necessarily a monster. Not necessarily even an animal.

The tarn is inhabited in a slower grammar.

Between its pale angular stones move shapes that resemble larvae when one wants them to be larvae, roots when one wants them to be roots, and thoughts when the dark has been listened to too long. They are soft, white-grey, and jointless, withdrawing not from touch but from attention. Amphibian young vanish among them. Blue worms avoid the deepest silt. Winged things skim the surface but do not drink from the center. The clicking insects grow quiet at the edge, as if their language cannot cross the tension of that water.

Something in the tarn participates in the basin’s breathing.

When the western crack exhales warmth, the tarn skin tightens. When fine rain falls, rings stop before they reach shore. Sound lowers near it. The amphibians knock in deeper tones there. Fungal lights under nearby overhangs brighten and dim in periods that almost match the warm breath of the fissure. It may be only chemistry: gases from young stone, minerals dissolving, pressure shifting through hidden cavities. It may be a microbial mat of unusual sensitivity, a colony older in kind than all the animals clinging around it. Or it may be that the mountain’s unfinished interior has found, in this cup of black water, its first exposed organ.

The inhabitants behave as if the tarn must be respected.

Not worshipped. Animals do not worship. But respected in the way hunger respects poison, in the way sleep respects cold, in the way a species survives by carrying an avoidance so old that it feels like truth.

Together, these beings make the hollow seem conscious.

The impression is false, perhaps. It is only that every niche is occupied and every action answered. A click on one wall changes the posture of a grazer. A grazer’s exposed body wakes a flattened predator. A predator’s movement disturbs blue worms. A glow draws winged hunters. Their droppings feed films on stone. The films feed the clickers. A rockfall kills, opens, buries, moistens, reveals. The amphibians face upslope. The tarn receives dust without reflecting it.

Nothing here is large enough to explain the place, so the mind gathers them into one creature: a many-bodied animal with stone for skeleton, mist for breath, tarn for eye, fissures for lungs, insects for nerves, amphibians for memory, wings for sudden thought.

If the hollow has a true resident, it is this composite life.

It is young, because the basin is young. Its ledges are newly broken; its colonies are still negotiating the first versions of their laws. Territories have not yet become stable enough to feel eternal, though they are already enforced by death. The lichen is only beginning its centuries of work. The moss has not thickened into soil. The tarn has not decided whether it is a pond, a mouth, or a wound.

But it is also ancient, because the forms of life that thrive here come from old instructions: cling, wait, drink, hide, molt, hatch, listen through stone, move when pressure changes, stop at the poisoned seam, breed when mist returns. These commands are older than the mountain’s present shape. The hollow is new; the hungers are not.

So what lives there?

A cold parliament of small creatures.

A colony of echoes with bodies.

A damp, high, inward ecosystem of clickers, grazers, seep-amphibians, membrane-winged roostlings, blue carrion-worms, slab predators, fungal lights, visiting scavengers, and the uncertain slow life of the tarn.

And, beneath them all, something not separate from the mountain: an emergent awareness made of customs rather than thoughts, rules rather than intentions, breath rather than voice. It does not know itself. It does not need to. It lives as the hollow lives — by holding darkness, rationing water, and letting every small mouth become part of the stone’s first language.
