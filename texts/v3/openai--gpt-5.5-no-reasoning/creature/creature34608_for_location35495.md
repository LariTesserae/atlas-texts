What lives there is not one creature, though the city has trained everything in it toward a single temperament.

The inhabitants are people of pressure, seepage, patience, and signal. They are not numerous for the size of the place, and because of that they seem less like a population than a distributed organ of the city itself: posted in terraces, wards, flats, towers, markets, cistern houses, measuring rooms, rail huts, pump chambers, balcony apartments, and warm lintel shadows. They live widely spaced, within hearing of the same immense labor, joined less by streets than by flows.

They are a damp, mineral people.

Their clothes are practical before they are decorative: sleeveless oilcloth coats, waxed shoulder capes, wrapped trousers tucked high above the ankle, sandals or rubber-soled boots with grooved bottoms, headcloths stiffened by repeated drying. In the upper terraces, clerks wear pale coats with dark cuffs rubbed shiny by ledgers and railings. In the engine wards, workers wear close-fitting garments that do not snag, with throat guards and gloves blackened at the seams. Market people wear aprons patched from conveyor canvas, hung with small tools: a awl, a gauge key, a chalk pencil, a strip of brass for tapping. Children wear soft ear-wraps and carry slates lacquered against mist.

Nearly everyone carries something against water: not an umbrella, which would be foolish in tight stairs and steam gusts, but a folded hood, a channel hook, a cloth for wiping spectacles, a tin cup with a green-glazed interior to test drinkable flow. Many carry little wax tablets, because paper swells unless specially treated. Older residents keep hearing horns or bone conductors, though these are often ceremonial by the time one sees them; most of them have learned to rely on vibration, light, and habit more than on sound.

Their bodies show the city’s conditions. They move slowly on slick paving, with a habitual outward placement of the foot. They touch walls before turning corners, not for support only, but to read tremor and warmth. They pause under awnings not because rain frightens them, but because falling condensation can carry grit from pipes. They do not shout unless distance requires it; shouting is wasteful where the city already shouts. They lean close when speaking. They tap knuckles on tabletops. They look often at drains.

Many are partly deaf. Some are wholly deaf. This is not treated as exceptional, only as one of the city’s ordinary weatherings of the body, like cracked heels, mineral-stained nails, roughened palms, or the pale crescent scars left by hot condensate. Their language is layered: spoken words for intimate interiors, broad manual signs for markets and yards, lamp flashes for public orders, flag positions for upper terraces, coded knocks through pipes, colored disks hung from balconies, boot-taps on resonant stair plates. A child learns early that a brass plate humming underfoot may matter more than a bell.

They do not think of silence as peace. Silence is diagnostic. Silence asks: which machine has stopped, which valve has closed, which pressure has shifted elsewhere? They distrust sudden quiet with the instinct other people reserve for thunder.

Their homes are inward-folded, built around water’s passage. A family apartment may be plain from the outside: concrete slab, woven metal balcony screen, blue-painted doorway, mineral bloom under the sill. Inside, it is a set of managed drynesses. Sleeping mats are raised. Hooks hang from ceilings for bread, tools, and children’s clothes. Cork panels line the noisiest walls. Low shelves hold sealed jars of grain, lemons, salt cakes, lamp oil, powdered stone soap. Each room has a groove or little tiled gutter at its edge. Even in a nursery, water is given its path.

They decorate with things that do not rot easily: glazed tiles, hammered brass, stone plaques, wirework, fired clay figures, glass beads cloudy from mineral film, ribbons of painted metal. Cloth exists, but it is treasured and watched. Rugs are small, rolled when the air grows heavy. Family records are not kept in books alone but engraved on thin plates, stamped into copper tags, scratched under balcony rails, knotted into waterproof cords. Names survive where ink would not.

They cook food suited to steam and scarcity. Dense black bread steamed in covered pans. Grains boiled until sticky and sour. Chickpea cakes fried in shallow oil that smells sharp in the wet air. Preserved lemons. Bitter greens grown in cracks near clean overflows. Fish rarely, except the thin blind cistern fish used more for broth than flesh. Goat or upland meat during festivals, brought in from beyond the saddle and treated with reverence. Tea boiled dark with minerals on the kettle rim. Turmeric, salt, old vinegar, fermented pastes, dried figs when caravans come through. Food here is warm, compact, sustaining, never lush.

They marry to vibration.

This is said jokingly elsewhere, but in the city it is nearly literal. Wedding halls are often built above turbine rooms or beside pressure galleries. During the rite, the couple stands barefoot on a floor that trembles faintly. Relatives watch the hanging lamps quiver. The officiant marks the joining not by a bell, which no one would hear properly, but by opening a narrow channel of water between two basins, letting one flow into the other. A good marriage is said to have a clear path and no hidden pressure.

Their dead are buried in porous stone on the western rise. Graves are low and channeled, never sealed against rain. The mourners pour measured water over the marker and watch it vanish through grooves cut around the name. A grave that drains well is considered at rest. A grave that pools must be tended. Ancestors are not imagined as dry bones underground, but as those who have joined the slow movement beneath the city, entering older paths.

Their festivals belong to maintenance. When the south basins are drained, children run across the crazed salt flats with flags tied to their wrists, leaving temporary footprints in bitter mud. During the stopping of the third pump, the whole city experiences an interval so strange that people laugh too loudly, speak nonsense, sing badly, or sit embarrassed by the size of their own breathing. The cleaning of the high channels is both civic duty and holiday: families climb with brushes, hooks, baskets, and lunch tins; old mineral crust is scraped away; children hunt for colored deposits; clerks record flow rates; vendors sell sour tea and hot cakes under canvas awnings.

When the wells sing, everything changes without anyone admitting fear. Workers slow their motions. Measuring houses fill with clerks. Children try to memorize pitches. Old women and retired pumpmen argue over whether a tone has shifted since the last singing. Some people pray. Some make maps. Some refuse to speak until it ends. The wells’ song is not thought to be a message exactly, but neither is it dismissed as accident. In a city built on directed water, an undirected voice from below must be treated with respect.

They are not primitive, though much of their life looks old. They understand gauges, membranes, stresses, dissolved metals, condensation, strata, gas settlement, the fatigue of iron, the moods of basalt after sun showers. Children can identify dangerous runoff by color and by the staining on its stones. A market vendor may know more about pressure seals than a distant academy engineer. A grandmother may be able to judge from a wall’s sweating whether a cellar should be emptied before dawn. Their learning is civic, inherited through practice. A foolish person is not one who lacks manners, but one who blocks a seep, wastes clean rain, trusts a dry wall too readily, or fails to leave when an engine house goes quiet.

They are cautious without being timid. The city has made recklessness expensive. They test ground with a toe before stepping. They look up before passing under pipes. They do not sleep in low rooms during belt season. They mark unsafe paving with chalk sigils that blur but remain legible long enough. They teach children the difference between steam one may walk through and vapor one must avoid. Yet they are not gloomy. They have the humor of people who live with inconvenience so constant it becomes social glue. They joke about damp bread, about walls that sweat more than laborers, about cats who know more routes than engineers, about young lovers choosing dry stairwells and being found by condensation anyway.

Their trades are many, though all seem related to the same great metabolism.

There are pump listeners, who stand with their palms against housings and know from vibration when a bearing has begun to complain.

There are channel keepers, municipal workers with long hooks and mineral-stained knees, responsible for clearing roof runnels, stair grooves, cemetery drains, and old stone cuts whose original makers are forgotten.

There are membrane washers, who handle ceramic sleeves with priestly delicacy.

There are fog clerks, who record local cloud behavior and issue colored advisories from the weather courts.

There are rail brakers, broad-handed and calm, who ride descending cars and smell of hot metal and wet wool.

There are basin rakers, their faces wrapped against bitter flats, who harvest crusts no one outside the city can name properly.

There are gasket sellers, cistern divers, tremor interpreters, roof tilers, lichen scrapers, valve apprentices, lamp signalers, powder packers, archive dryers, and makers of the woven balcony screens that filter glare and mist.

There are also idlers, poets, thieves, lovers, failed engineers, children who skip lessons, old men who feed deaf cats, women who play tile games by vibration, musicians who compose for drums no one hears with the ear so much as the sternum. The city is practical, but not without art. Its art favors repetition, endurance, and resonance: hammered patterns on brass plates, cloud charts painted in mineral pigments, songs with low intervals felt in the chest, dances timed to pump cycles, stories told in signs broad enough to read through steam.

The children are wiry, solemn in dangerous places and wild in safe ones. They sail chips of tile in the side channels. They race between dry patches after rain. They dare one another to touch warm walls near forbidden cisterns. They climb viaduct braces to look for swift nests. They collect mineral flakes like other children collect flowers. They know the city first as a playground of prohibitions: do not step there, do not drink that, do not open this, do not follow the cat into that culvert, do not put your ear to the drain when the wells begin. Naturally, they do all of these in degrees, and survival teaches them measure.

The old are repositories of routes. They remember which sealed doors once opened, which ravines carried visible water, which factory clocks are honest, which pipes were replaced and which only painted, which family was lost to gas in belt season, which courtyard has the best acoustic hollow during festival drums. An old resident may appear to be doing nothing, sitting under a blue doorway with cloudy eyes and a cup of dark tea, but they are listening through their bones to the city’s ongoing argument with the mountain.

Not all who live there are human.

The cats are perhaps the city’s second citizenry. Most are pale, soot-gray, rust-marked, or black with mineral-dulled fur. Many are deaf from birth or from the city’s roar, and this gives them an unnerving composure. They sleep where machinery radiates dry heat: transformer housings, lintels, insulated pipe saddles, the tops of sealed cabinets. They cross active yards with perfect timing, unconcerned by sirens, reading vibration through their paws. Workers tolerate them because cats know warm leaks, rat paths, and small safe hollows. A cat staring at a wall may draw a mechanic faster than a flashing lamp.

The pale swifts belong to morning. They live under viaducts and in drain holes high above ravines, emerging like thrown knives when the light is still white and weak. They hunt the sparse insects bred in warm runoff. Their cries are mostly swallowed by the city, so people know them by motion rather than sound. When swifts fly low in the afternoon, residents glance at vents and sky; the birds notice changes in air before instruments do.

Lizards live in the brief kingdom of sunlit stone. They are quick, dust-colored, nearly imaginary. Children try to catch them and rarely succeed. Their presence means a wall has stayed warm and dry long enough to be trusted for sitting.

Blind fish live below, in cisterns and old covered tanks. They are narrow, colorless, metallic in the lantern light, feeding on algal films and whatever drifts down. Some people say they were placed there deliberately by early water engineers to test poison; others say they entered as eggs from some ancient spring. Cistern workers watch them carefully. A tank where the fish rise strangely is closed until tested. A tank where they vanish becomes a matter for officials.

There are insects, but fewer than the wetness would suggest: pipe gnats, pale moths in archive rooms, beetles under damp wood, tiny red midges above warm drains. They arrive in brief blooms and disappear. Blue doorways, painted from old habit against pests that seldom come, remain part of the city’s visual language.

There are dogs, but not many. The city is cruel to them. The continuous thunder frays their nerves, and the slick stone injures their confidence. Those that remain are usually old yard dogs gone calm or half-deaf, attached to outer sheds and basin flats rather than engine wards.

And there is the larger thing beyond the eastern gate.

Whether it is one species, one animal, or a story with hooves is uncertain. It comes at night to the overflow basins and leaves split hoofprints in dark mud. Some describe a long-backed upland antelope, pale as evaporite, with a head too narrow and ears like torn cloth. Others say it is a feral descendant of work animals once used in the quarries, altered by generations of mineral water and isolation. A few pumpmen claim to have seen eyeshine above the reeds, high enough to belong to something larger than a goat. No one hunts it. The overflow basins are not good places to linger at night, and in any case the city has an old courtesy toward anything that knows where to drink safely.

The smallest life may be the most faithful. Bacterial mats bloom orange at copper leaks. White crusts flower and flake. Lichens draw slow maps over walls. Moss appears for one damp week in shaded seams like a rumor of another climate, then withdraws into black threads. Fungi take the abandoned wooden spools. Algae films the lower cisterns. These lives do not conquer the city. They annotate it. They write in color where water has passed, where heat gathers, where metal changes, where neglect has become stable.

What lives there, finally, is a community adapted not to nature or industry separately, but to their long compromise. The people do not experience the city as an environment built around them. They experience themselves as among its moving parts: softer than stone, more perishable than iron, cleverer than water only in the short term. They direct what they can. They respect what they cannot. They give water a path and hope pressure does not choose another.

They are citizens of a place that exhales steam instead of incense, that measures weather as if it were a legal matter, that treats drainage as morality, maintenance as festival, and vibration as blessing.

They are not trapped there. Many could leave. Some do, following trains downrange with sacks of mineral powder and stories of singing wells. But those who remain acquire the city’s endurance. They become difficult to startle, hard to dry out, slow to speak, quick to notice a change in hum. Their affections are mineral and lasting. Their grief drains carefully. Their joy rises as mist from heated stone.

They live in the tired weather, inside the mountain’s old wetness, among machines too large to hurry.

And beneath their floors, whether they sleep, marry, labor, bargain, mourn, or listen, the water continues moving under everything.
