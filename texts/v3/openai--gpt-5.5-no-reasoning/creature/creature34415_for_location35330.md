The inhabitants are not a single species or class so much as a civic temperament repeated in different bodies: human, vegetal, fungal, mechanical. Anything that lives there has learned, or been bred, or been built, to take instruction from dampness and delay.

The people are probably compact, practical, and quiet-skinned from the cool. Not pale exactly, though many may be sallow in the green-filtered light, their faces browned or greyed more by tannin, resin-smoke, oil, and weather than by sun. Their hands are the most expressive part of them: hardened fingertips, knuckles darkened by sap, nails clipped short, small cuts sealed with resin, wrists accustomed to twisting vines into obedience without snapping them. They have the alert calm of millworkers and orchardists combined. They are never wholly indoors or outdoors. Even at a desk they listen for moisture in the walls.

They move carefully, but not timidly. Their caution is professional. A stranger might think them superstitious because they pause before thresholds, touch lintels, smell the air in a passage, or wait for a tendril to slacken before crossing beneath it. But this is no more mystical than a sailor watching a rope under load. They know tension. They know swelling. They know that a living bridge gives warnings before it fails, and that most disasters begin as a sound someone ignored.

Their clothing is layered and close-fitting, made to shed mist without catching on growth: felted plant-fiber tunics, oilcloth aprons, cuffed sleeves, rubberized sap-boots, hoods with short brims to keep condensation from the eyes. In workshops they wear belts crowded with hooked knives, grafting awls, copper gauges, twine spools, little tins of sealant, folding calipers, oil droppers. Older workers carry their tools like heirlooms but also like conversation partners, each handle polished into a private shape by decades of damp palms.

They smell faintly of their trades. Condensation crews smell of cold leaf and wool. Sap drawers smell sweet, sharp, almost medicinal. Engine attendants carry the mineral odor of iron heat. Nursery workers smell of peat and crushed stems. Municipal pruners smell green in a violent way, as if some fresh branch has just been cut nearby. No one smells clean in the abstract. Cleanliness here is not absence but rightness: no rot where there should be curing, no sourness where there should be ferment, no oil in the nursery water.

Their speech is probably soft and full of distinctions outsiders miss. They have many words for wetness: first sheen, roof-sweat, root seep, cistern-dark, leaf-caught, pipe-cold, rain-new. They distinguish deadwood from sleeping wood, salvageable rot from dangerous rot, hunger-growth from structural thickening. They do not say a building is “old” without specifying whether it has deep roots, tired cambium, overbound joints, good scars, poor drainage, or a dependable crown.

Children raised there are quiet by habit but not solemn. They know how to play in narrow places. They race the filling of puddles, float scraps of bark in temporary rills, dare one another to touch the trembling water films on doorways, memorize which vines will curl around a finger and which will sting with irritant hairs. They learn early not to waste. A fallen leaf is not trash but assignment. A bent nail that has touched sap must be returned, cleaned, tagged, melted, or buried in the right pit. Their games likely imitate municipal duties: opening and closing vents, routing imaginary rain, sorting seeds, rescuing tools from hungry tendrils.

The elderly are important, because memory is infrastructure. They know which walls conceal sealed rooms, which root-pavements were once channels, which tree accepted iron badly and still aches in cold weather. They sit in moss courts or near warm digesters, wrapped in fiber shawls, correcting younger crews about cuts made fifty years ago. They may not walk far, but the town extends them through recollection. An old person’s warning about a certain stair in fog may carry the force of ordinance.

The gardeners are not ornamental gardeners. They are structural physicians, civil engineers, lawyers of growth. A gardener-engineer is one of the town’s central figures: part arborist, part machinist, part surveyor, part judge. They read stress in bark folds and municipal obligation in root spread. They decide whether a vine may be asked to bear another decade of bridge-work, whether a dwelling’s eastern wall must be thinned for light, whether a civic tree is being overtaxed by pipes and signal roots. They do not romanticize plants. They respect them as powerful colleagues that can crush, starve, heal, misdirect, and remember.

Mechanics are similar but a little more impatient, perhaps. They work among belts, pumps, riveted housings, sap-lacquered casings. Their craft is not to keep nature out of machines, but to decide how far in it may come. Moss is permitted on some housings, forbidden on others. A root may be encouraged to brace a boiler room wall but not to taste the copper line. A pulley may be mounted to a living support if the support is fed and relieved of excess load in spring. They know which oils offend which vines. They curse softly, because sound does not travel, and because a shouted curse wastes breath.

There are clerks too, and municipal recorders, though they may not look like clerks elsewhere. They scrape notices into bark plaques and rewrite them as the bark thickens. They track grafting licenses, canopy rights, drainage debts, sap quotas, fermentation schedules, bridge inspections, and the flowering names of streets. Their offices glow green through membrane windows. Their ledgers may be part paper, part pressed leaf-board, part copper tag, part living archive: ribbons tied around branches, coded scars, root-maps pricked into clay tablets, seasonal records kept by the pressure clock in the old tree.

Doctors, if that is the right word, understand both human damp and vegetal damp. They treat fungal lung, chill joints, sap burns, pollen fever, cuts infected by soil, and the peculiar fatigue that comes from working too long in low light. They also consult on nursery blights and fermentation imbalances. The boundary between medicine and horticulture is thin. A household might call the same practitioner for a coughing child and a weeping lintel.

Food would be modest, fermented, fibrous, and carefully rationed by season. The inhabitants eat pressed seed cakes, mushrooms, sour leaf-broths, root starch, steamed reed shoots, algae from cistern trays, tart fruits from trained espaliers, and rare eggs or meat if any outer-scrub animals are trapped. They drink infusions of bark, mineral water drawn from deep shaded cisterns, weak fermented sap, and hot bitter decoctions that keep damp from the bones. Meals are probably warm more often than lavish. Heat itself is a form of wealth.

They are not isolated innocents. They know industry. They bargain in resin, woven mats, vine cable, adhesives, seed oil, compressed leaf-board, medicinal sap, fungal felt, and repair expertise. Outsiders may come on dry roads or after rains when channels briefly run. Traders find the town strange but dependable. Its goods are slow-made and excellent, if faintly alive in inconvenient ways. A coil of vine cable from this place may need to be stored in shade and spoken of as if it has preferences.

As for beliefs, the people probably do not worship the plants in any simple sense. They live too intimately with them for sentimental reverence. But they practice gratitude, apology, witness, and accounting. Before a major cut, three witnesses attend not because the vine has a soul that demands ceremony, but because the town has learned that unobserved cutting leads to collapse, dispute, and waste. Ritual and regulation have grown into each other. A funeral might involve composting, but not carelessly: the body returned through a chosen grove, a civic tree, a family wall, a flowering season. Grief would be routed, like heat or water, into continued use.

They likely fear fire more than death. Fire is not merely danger but obscenity: a devouring that fails to return properly, waste escaping upward, memory turned to smoke. Children are taught to dread sparks. Engines are watched with religious attention. Any profession involving heat carries status and suspicion.

Their pets are few. Perhaps some households keep pale moths in warm boxes for silk or pollination training. Perhaps cistern larvae are tended by children, named and watched until they vanish. Perhaps there are domesticated mite colonies, scent-hounds no larger than a hand, or root-fed snails used to clean films from water channels. But ordinary animal companionship is rare. The town has little room in its economy for creatures that eat without also tending, sensing, filtering, pulling, or transforming.

The plants themselves are inhabitants in a more literal way than outsiders first understand. There are civic trees with jobs. Load-bearing willows whose trunks have been coaxed into wall-arches. Chimney trunks lined by heat tolerance and scar tissue. Gutter-vines trained to seek cisterns. Root-runners bred for slow muscular transport. Leaf-roofs that close against frost and open to drain. Membrane-windows that thicken after hail. Nursery groves that supply replacement beams. Digestive pits warm with fungal intelligence. Some plants are domestic, some municipal, some nearly feral but tolerated for their function.

These living structures have personalities only in the sense that old work animals, boats, and machines have personalities: real, consequential, partly projected, partly observed. One house may be thirsty, another generous with shade. A bridge may sulk after overloading, tightening its fibers for days. A workshop wall may overgrow tools if they are left too near its seams. The old tree with the sap-driven clock is not a ruler, exactly, but it is an authority. It keeps months better than hours, and the people have learned that months matter more.

The fungi are citizens too, though less visible. They felt the seams, digest the unclaimed dead, insulate walls, cure panels, clean water, soften sound. They are the town’s quiet bureaucracy of decay. Nothing dead remains unclaimed because the fungi, molds, mites, and warm pits enforce the rule more thoroughly than any constable could. The inhabitants live with this knowledge: abandonment is temporary, ownership is metabolic, and every object will be asked eventually what it can become next.

The machines live in the town differently. They are not alive, but they have been adopted by life for so long that they behave like old organs. Flywheels breathe in shadows behind bamboo guards. Pumps knock underground like restrained animals. Turbines wake only when rain permits, flare the lamps, then sleep. Looms and presses have their working moods determined by humidity and water level. A machine here is never merely on or off; it is dry-resting, wet-ready, sap-fouled, belt-hungry, heat-useful, root-threatened, or fit for heavy work after rain.

Those who tend machines may speak to them, but again without fantasy. A machine that is not addressed is more likely to be misunderstood. Its rhythm is part of the weather.

If there is governance, it is probably committee-based and extremely local: ward pruners, cistern keepers, engine house masters, nursery stewards, digestor wardens, shade assessors, bridge witnesses. Power belongs to those who understand dependencies. A charismatic fool would be dangerous and therefore limited. A person who can diagnose a failing root-vault by smell might hold more real authority than a mayor.

Conflicts happen, but they take forms shaped by the environment. Neighbors dispute light. Workshops dispute water timing. Families argue over whether a wall may be opened for municipal airflow. A young mechanic may want to replace a slow living conveyor with a faster metal one; older crews object that speed will change drainage, heat, labor, and root pressure. A household may resent giving warmth to nursery groves during frost. Someone may illegally cut a vine at night to clear a window, and months later a roof sags. Justice here is ecological because consequences are ecological. Punishment may involve labor in digesters, water duty, public repair, loss of grafting license, or enforced service as witness at cuts.

The town also produces a particular loneliness. Sound is absorbed. Shouts fail. Lamps are weak. Streets alter subtly through the day. Rooms can seal themselves into memory. People living there may grow inward, attentive to slow processes but less able to bear open, dry, echoing places. A person from this town might find a stone city brutally dead and wasteful, a desert terrifyingly exposed, a roaring river almost vulgar in its abundance.

Yet they are not melancholy as a rule. They live inside competence. The town works because countless hands have made it work and because countless living systems have accepted those hands. There is pride in that: not triumphant pride, but the durable satisfaction of a valve closing correctly, a graft taking, a bridge surviving another wet season, a cistern filling just enough to run the lamps at dusk.

The creatures of the outer scrub know the town as a dense, breathing opportunity and threat. A fox-sized animal comes near for seed and warmth but avoids the thorn posts. Grey climbers slip along cables, clever enough to time the quiet hours. Pale moths seek engine heat. Translucent larvae bloom in cisterns when water rises, then vanish into mud or into the mouths of filters. Rare birds pass over, hesitate at the strange green mechanism below, and mostly continue. The town does not welcome uncontrolled appetite.

So what lives there?

People who have become patient without becoming passive.

Plants that have become civic without becoming tame.

Machines that have become seasonal without becoming obsolete.

Fungi, mites, larvae, moths, hidden root systems, damp-loving bacteria, old trees with municipal burdens, vines with structural duties, children who know the life cycle of puddles, elders who remember sealed rooms, mechanics who oil belts made of cured growth, clerks who write laws on bark that is still widening beneath the words.

Everything there lives by maintenance. Nothing is merely resident. To inhabit the town is to participate in its circulation: to catch, mend, prune, ferment, return, reroute, listen. Even the dead are not allowed to withdraw. They become heat, loam, panel, lacquer, root-food, memory in bark.

The town’s inhabitants are therefore not wild, not pastoral, and not industrial in the usual hard-edged sense. They are cultivated people living among cultivated powers, inside a settlement that has replaced the idea of construction with the idea of ongoing consent. They are cool-handed, soft-spoken, technically exact, suspicious of waste, intimate with decay, and loyal to systems that answer slowly.

They do not master the place. They negotiate with it every morning.
