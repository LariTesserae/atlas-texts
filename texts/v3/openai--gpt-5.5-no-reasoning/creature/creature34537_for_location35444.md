What lives there is not quite a people yet.

Not in the sense that they are unfinished, or dim, or lesser than the peoples of older valleys, with their markets and quarrels and burial towers and roads polished by generations. They are complete in themselves. But they live at the beginning of their own multiplication. They have not yet become numerous enough to make noise with one another. Their customs are already intricate; their city is already planned; their obligations are already older than memory, or at least older than anyone living claims to remember. But there are too few of them for the size of what they maintain.

They move through the hollow like attendants inside the body of something sleeping.

They are small-boned, mostly. Not frail, but narrow from altitude, from damp, from a diet of roots, greens, lichen-breads, tubers grown in wet terraces, snails gathered from stone, and the occasional dried meat brought from lower slopes by rare exchange. Their shoulders are often stooped, not with servility but from ducking beneath sod eaves, leaning over channels, listening close to water. They have strong hands. Their palms are broad and blunt, creased deeply, always chapped at the edges from cold moisture and clay. The skin at their fingertips is often pale and ridged from long wet work. Children here learn to distinguish silt, seep, springwater, roof-run, and stone-sweat by touch before they are trusted with knives.

They dress in layers that never fully dry: wool felted dense with moss-oil, sedge capes lacquered with pitch, soft hide boots patched again and again at the seams, wrist-bindings of fiber cord. Most wear hoods, not against rain exactly, but against the constant settling of droplets from ledges, vines, lintels, and roofs. Their clothing is the color of the place because everything eventually becomes the color of the place: grey-green, peat-brown, clay-blue, the dull white of quartz, the black of soaked stems. Bright dyes are rare and seem almost indecent here. When color appears, it appears in small ceremonial knots: a thread of madder at the throat, a yellow lichen bead sewn to a sleeve, a strip of blue cloth tied to a sluice handle to mark a vow.

They do not walk quickly.

No one who lives there walks quickly unless something has gone wrong with water.

Their gait is a practiced negotiation with ground that may be stone, mud, moss, paving, or skin over hidden flow. The soles of their feet know hollowness. They place weight carefully and hear through it. A person born in the hollow can tell, by the minute give of a path beneath the heel, whether a channel below is clear, clogged, freezing, overfull, or beginning to undermine its cover stones. Outsiders think they are cautious. They are not. They are reading.

Their speech is soft and full of water terms.

They have many verbs for letting something pass without owning it. A channel does not simply carry water; it persuades, delays, cools, turns, tastes, returns, loosens, remembers. A basin does not hold; it receives for a while. A roof does not shed; it offers down. To dam a flow is described with the same word used for interrupting an elder. To restore a blocked channel is described with the same word used for apologizing.

They dislike straight lines when speaking as much as when building. A direct command is considered crude unless danger is immediate. Advice bends. Requests arrive by way of observation. A parent does not say, “Clean the basin.” They say, “The green pebbles cannot see the sky today.” A child understands. A master does not say, “You set the stones badly.” They say, “This water is leaving before it has heard the threshold.” The apprentice lowers their eyes, removes the stones, begins again.

Their faces tend to be still. Not expressionless; rather, expressions rise slowly, like seepage darkening dust. Smiles come late and remain long after the cause has passed. Anger is rare in open display and alarming when it appears, because raised voices strike the basin walls and return enlarged. Disputes are taken to places where sound is broken: reed beds, moss courts, storage rooms lined with hanging fiber, the outer shelves when mist is thick. To shout in the central court is not forbidden because no one needs to forbid it. It would be like striking a sleeping infant’s skull.

They are a small clan, perhaps seven or nine households, depending on how one counts households. Kinship is not arranged only by birth. A person belongs to the water they tend. There are cistern families, roof families, seep families, rill families, stone families, garden families. One may be born of a mother who keeps the western troughs and a father who cuts reed channels, but if as a child one first hears the change in the north underpath before a collapse, then perhaps the north underpath has claimed one. Adoption here may be made by people, but it is confirmed by infrastructure.

Their elders are not the oldest bodies, but those trusted to interpret delay.

Age matters, but only when joined to attention. The most respected among them might be a woman with clouded eyes who can no longer climb the upper shelves but can sit beside the central stones and know from the staggered dripping which high seep has changed its taste; or a thin man whose knees are swollen with damp, who remembers the year moss crossed the eastern lintel too soon and what famine followed; or a person of no settled gender who sleeps in different houses according to seasonal flow, keeper of thresholds, called upon whenever water enters a room in an unfamiliar way.

They have names, but names are not fixed in the manner of lowland names. A child receives a birth-name, usually from an event of weather or a small behavior of water: First Mist After Snow, Blue Thread Under Clay, The Drop That Would Not Fall. Later come work-names, accident-names, listening-names. A person may be called Reed-Binder in the morning, Left-Hand Silt in the gardens, Three-Turns by their spouse, and Stone-Hears by a younger apprentice. The dead are not named by their final illness or genealogy, but by the last flow they corrected.

They build as if building were a form of manners.

Every new wall is introduced to the ground. Before the first slab is laid, someone pours three bowls of collected water onto the intended line and watches where it refuses to remain. A wall may be moved by a handspan, a body-length, or abandoned entirely if the water will not accept it. Children attend these pourings. They are told not to speak. They are told that the mountain is young and easily embarrassed by being misunderstood.

Their ceremonies are quiet, repetitive, and intensely practical to the point that an outsider might miss them.

At dawn, certain people visit the inner basins of their houses and remove whatever the night has given: grit, a drowned insect, a loosened pebble, a film of pollen, sometimes nothing. They do not discard these immediately. They sort them on flat shards of pottery. Grit from beneath thresholds goes to path repair. Leaf rot goes to seedlings. Insects are placed beneath moss. Clear water is tipped back outdoors along the least-used channel. Nothing that has entered a house leaves without being addressed.

On the first clear day after a run of mist, the central stones are wiped, not dry—never dry—but clean of algae that would thicken the notes of their dripping. This is done by unmarried youths, though marriage here is less a matter of romance than of merging drainage responsibilities. Each youth cleans one stone with a cloth woven by someone from another household. They must not wring the cloth into the ring trough until all stones have been touched. The mingled water is then carried to unfinished terraces. It is said that public sound must feed future roots.

Their dead are not buried deep; the ground is too wet, too shallow, too full of listening channels. Nor are they burned often, for dry fuel is precious and smoke already lingers heavily enough. Instead the dead are placed in low stone chambers along the outer shelves, wrapped in sedge mats and moss, with their hands packed in clay. Over time water enters. It is expected to enter. The chambers are built with small exits so that what leaves the body may join the hill slowly, filtered through root and stone. A death is considered complete only when the chamber’s outflow clears.

They do not fear ghosts in the usual sense. They fear blocked continuance. A bad death is not one that produces a wandering spirit, but one whose waters cannot find the correct descent.

Children are few and treated with a mixture of tenderness and grave apprenticeship. They play, but their play imitates planning. They make miniature channels in clay, float leaves through impossible cities, argue about whether a pebble should be a house, a mountain, or an elder. They raise tiny walls and wait to see if seepage undermines them. They learn patience not as discipline but as physics. A child who can sit through the interval between irregular drops from a central stone without fidgeting is considered ready to carry water indoors.

The children’s laughter is startling because it is one of the only sounds in the hollow that does not belong to water, stone, or tools. When they laugh, adults often turn, not irritated but briefly astonished, as if a bird has flown out of the ground.

There are fewer adults than there should be. This absence is present in everything.

Some houses are kept ready though no one sleeps in them. Their basins are cleaned. Their roofs are weeded. Their thresholds are inspected. Rolled mats are aired under awnings. Cups sit inverted beside hearths that have not been lit in seasons. These are not abandoned houses. They are expected houses. Perhaps for descendants. Perhaps for those who left. Perhaps for people promised by prophecy or hydrology. Perhaps the settlement itself required them before there were lives to fill them.

The inhabitants speak of the missing population obliquely. They do not say, “When more of us come.” They say, “When the upper terraces hear feet.” They say, “When smoke learns the western row.” They say, “When the third ring has enough names.” Hope, among them, is grammatical but not loud.

They trade rarely with lower communities. When traders arrive, they are made to wait at the dry shelf above the settlement until someone reads the state of the paths. Visitors are guided carefully and sometimes blindfolded, not from secrecy exactly, but because untrained feet damage more than they understand. The people of the hollow offer medicinal moss, cold-water herbs, fine clay vessels that cool milk without cracking, pitch-sealed stem pipes, and green pebbles polished by constant seepage. In return they receive salt, wool, bone needles, copper scraps, stories of rivers large enough to drown in, and news of lands where roads go straight for days.

They listen politely to such stories and privately find them obscene.

A river that commits itself to one direction and never turns back seems to them rash. A dry city, with waste carried away unseen and roofs that shed rain without acknowledging it, seems lonely and dangerous. They do not condemn such places. The world is large; perhaps older ground can endure older habits. But here, on the shoulder of the unfinished mountain, haste is not merely rude. It is structurally unsound.

There is also something else living there, and the people know it, though they do not call it a god.

It is not a spirit with a face. Not a beast under the rock. Not an ancestor, though ancestors pass into it. It is the wet intention of the mountain becoming aware of channels. It is pattern before plan, city before citizen, drainage before law. It lies in the exposed bedrock of the roofless chamber, where ridges resemble streets no human had yet conceived. It beads on apparently dry stone. It speaks, if speech is the word, by making one wall sweat more than another, by souring a cistern, by clearing a blocked rill overnight, by changing the interval between drops in the central court.

The inhabitants live with this presence as one lives with weather, parentage, conscience, and illness: intimately, unequally, without complete explanation.

They do not worship it so much as collaborate with it. They copy its lines into streets. They offer it turns instead of straight cuts. They give it basins in every house. When it enters uninvited, they seat it. When it withdraws, they do not chase. The oldest teaching says that the mountain is still soft enough to learn and proud enough to resent being forced.

Perhaps the people are its first thought of society.

Perhaps they were drawn there by dreams of wet stone. Perhaps their grandparents found the roofless chamber and saw the whole settlement already faintly mapped in the natural ridges, and simply began tracing. Perhaps no one founded the place at all in the heroic sense. Perhaps one shelter became two because water suggested where the second should stand; a path appeared because feet followed seepage; a court formed because the dripping stones wanted listeners; terraces opened because the outflows needed roots. The people may believe they are building a city, but just as often it seems the city is growing them: teaching them hand shapes, ethics, kinship, tempo.

Some among them are more marked by the place than others.

There are listeners who sit for hours with one ear near stone, their hair damp against the surface, their eyes half closed. They claim not to hear words, only readiness. A wall about to fail has a crowded sound. A new spring has a cold, clean silence around it. Clay that will slip carries a sweetness in the ear. No outsider can verify this, but the listeners are seldom wrong.

There are turners, specialists in the angles of channels. A good turner can make water slow without stagnating, speak without shouting, leave without stealing warmth from a room. Their work is revered and criticized mercilessly. Children of turners practice with milk, ash-water, melted snow, and eventually springflow. The finest turners are said to have no straight thoughts.

There are basin-keepers, mostly but not always older women, who tend the small indoor pools. They remember which houses accept which leaves, which pebbles cloud under grief, which clay figures dissolve too quickly when an illness is coming. A basin-keeper may know more about a marriage from the water near the sleeping platform than from anything either spouse says.

There are roof-gardeners, who climb the sod roofs in mist and trim grasses, replant alpine flowers, press moss back into bare patches, and judge whether a roof is drinking too greedily. They move low and sure-footed, like goats imagined by someone who had only seen snails. Falling through a roof is considered both an accident and a theological embarrassment.

There are measurers of the central stones. They keep cords knotted at intervals, clay tablets marked with punctures, bowls of known depth, and memory songs for drip rhythms. Their records are not numerical in the lowland sense, though they can count when needed. They describe rhythm as temperament: hurried, reluctant, doubled, hollow, feathered, blind, returning. When all stones drip in certain relations, work begins or ceases. When the southernmost stone falls silent, no new threshold may be made.

At night they sleep lightly.

The whole settlement changes sound in darkness. Channels cool and contract. Roof drips slow. Hidden flows become clearer. A blocked outlet may wake three houses. People rise without lamps, moving by hand along walls slick with familiar moss. They carry clay plugs, reed rods, small scoops, twisted fiber, smooth stones for weighting gates. Emergencies here are often tiny to the eye: a basin rising faster than expected, a trough overflowing on the wrong side, a new thread crossing a threshold. But a wrong thread may become a habit; a habit may become a channel; a channel may become law. So they wake.

Their dreams are full of plans they did not make. Many wake with the image of a street that does not yet exist, a courtyard under future moss, a stair leading to a shelf still bare of foundations. Such dreams are not immediately acted upon. They are brought to the roofless chamber. If condensation gathers along a matching ridge in the bedrock within three days, the dream is considered “received.” If not, it is only weather in the skull.

They are not solemn all the time. Their humor is dry, which they find funny because almost nothing else is. They tease those who fall in shallow channels, those who build a turn too elegant for a flow too stupid to appreciate it, those who speak in lowland directness after trade visits. They make songs out of bad drainage and lullabies from the names of mosses. Lovers leave small diversions of water for one another: a private rill led to a doorstep, a floating white flower arriving indoors at dawn, a pebble spiral rearranged to mean stay, wait, forgive, come by the eastern path.

Love in such a place is often expressed as maintenance.

To love someone is to clear their basin before they wake. To reinforce the terrace above their sleeping room before the thaw. To know which roof drip keeps them from sleep and soften it with moss. To turn water away from their door without depriving the garden below. Grand passion is distrusted if it ignores gradient.

What lives there, then, is a careful, damp, underpopulated civilization in larval form.

A people of hydrologists without metal instruments, priests without altars, city-planners without crowds, gardeners of seepage, nurses of an unborn urban body. They are quiet not because they lack feeling, but because sound travels too well. They are patient not because they are passive, but because the mountain punishes haste in ways that may take years to reveal themselves. They are few not because the place has failed, but because it has begun with infrastructure, with ethics, with listening, before allowing itself abundance of flesh.

And alongside them lives the young city itself.

It lives as wet stone lives: slowly, insistently, by accretion and direction. It has a pulse in the central court, nerves beneath the paths, soft bones in its walls, a skin of moss and sod, organs of cistern and reed bed and garden plot. Its inhabitants are not separate from it. They are its first moving parts, its caretakers, its thoughts given hands.

At dusk they emerge most clearly.

One can see them then: hooded figures crossing pale paving with bowls, children crouched beside a thread of water to see whether it chooses left or right, an elder seated in smoke near a doorway, a roof-gardener descending with wet knees, two turners arguing in murmurs over a channel no wider than a finger. Behind them the upright stones drip out of sequence. Under them the ground trembles faintly with shallow flow. Around them stand empty houses, not abandoned but expectant.

They are waiting, but not idly.

They are waiting the way moss waits.

They are waiting the way a basin waits.

They are waiting the way a mountain, not quite finished becoming a mountain, waits to discover what shape its waters will teach it to be.
