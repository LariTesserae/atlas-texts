What lives here is not a people, not in any familiar sense, and not quite a wilderness as one would recognize it from wetter worlds. It is a society of surfaces.

The inhabitants are things that have learned to be thin, patient, reflective, folded, mineral, intermittent. They do not fight the plateau’s dryness; they use it as a grammar. They live by contact rather than abundance: contact with light, with dust, with salt, with vapor, with charged air, with the planet’s faint buried breath. Their lives are long negotiations with exposure.

The dominant beings are the green fan-colonies that stand across the flats like dark, feathered corals. Each one looks solitary from a distance, but none is truly alone. Beneath the cracked salt and feldspar dust, they send conductive filaments through seams in the stone, linking to microbial crusts, mineral cores, vapor channels, and neighboring growths. A fan may be only the visible crest of a much larger organism: a buried chemical intelligence spread through several square kilometers of fracture and crust.

Their fronds are not leaves meant to transpire. They are collectors: black-green, waxed, dry, and finely veined with silica. They tilt by minute degrees through the day, not seeking warmth so much as managing radiance, ultraviolet, static charge, and the faint upward seep of useful gases. In the thin wind they flex without fluttering. When mineral vapor rises from below, they turn edgewise as if listening. Then they darken, feed, and release hair-fine banners that stream into the air like accidental writing.

These banners are reproductive, sensory, and communal all at once. Some are spores. Some are charged filaments for gathering dust. Some are temporary bridges between colonies. Over years they tangle, harden, and become aerial veils strung between groves. Those veils are not constructed deliberately, but once formed, the colonies use them. They gather wind-borne minerals, shade crusts beneath them from the worst midday glare, trap spores in their threads, and hum with tiny electrical shifts. In districts where the veils are dense, the whole landscape becomes an instrument played by pressure and dust.

Close to the ground live the crusts.

They are older than almost everything else, and perhaps more important. Blue-green, black, wine-dark, olive, and silver, they spread over the pale flats in low lacquered skins. They are mats of microbes, fungi-like threads, mineral-binding films, and dormant cysts layered over one another like memory. By day they close themselves to the sky and glitter. At night they open pores too small to see, taking in cold, trace condensates, carbon compounds, and chemical whispers rising from below.

They are not soft, though they look soft. To touch them carelessly would be to break decades of work. Their upper surfaces are brittle shields. Beneath those shields, however, they are alive with slow exchange: acids licking mineral grains, ionic gels passing charge, colonies dividing, dying, sealing, awakening. They hold the soil in place. They decide where dust may become ground. They are the plateau’s skin.

They also think, if thinking can be spread thin enough.

Not with thoughts that could be spoken, nor with desires shaped like animal desires. Their awareness is chemical, distributed, and ancient. A crust remembers pressure by changing color. It remembers grazing by thickening bitterness in one direction and sweetening itself in another. It guides the little night grazers the way a river might guide silt, encouraging them to scrape old tissue and avoid young reproductive zones. Where repeated feeding has occurred, the mats regrow in looping stains and calligraphic bands, so the history of appetite is written visibly across the flats.

The column plants are slower still.

They rise from the desert like translucent green towers built around mineral spines. Their cores are not dead supports but reservoirs: lattices of salt, silica, and bound brine, holding microscopic fluids in cages so fine they cannot escape into the air. Their ribbed skins tighten and loosen with temperature. Their pale spines comb the atmosphere, catching charged dust, frostless cold, ions, and volatile molecules. The spines are not weapons, though they can cut. They are nets.

Inside each column is a dry circulatory system of gels and crystalline valves. When the ground exhales, seams open along the ribs, exposing darker inner tissue. During those hours the columns feed greedily, though their greed is silent and slow. They draw in vapor, fix carbon, store metals, and harden new layers around the central core. Their shed scales gather at their bases in circular halos, where crusts and small animals process them over decades into the nearest thing this world has to rich soil.

The animals here are mostly hidden.

The smallest are pale, jointed grazers that live under and within the crusts. They are the size of fingers or smaller, though in the deeper basins some grow long as a hand. Their bodies are translucent, jointed, and flattened, with mineral-edged mouths like tiny scrapers. Through their sides one can see dark digestive cords coiled inside them, slowly extracting oils, salts, pigments, and bound volatiles from the mats.

They come out at night, or when the mineral vapor rises. They move with deliberation, never hurrying unless exposed to a sudden pressure shift or the chemical shadow of a hunter. Their legs are hooked for gripping lacquered crust. Their mouths leave looping trails. Their waste is not wet but crystalline: little dry pellets and folded husks that catch in cracks and eventually become part of the soil.

They are not pests. They prune, spread spores, and open old crust to renewal. Many mats depend on them. Some crusts produce tissues only the grazers can digest, bribing them into carrying reproductive cysts through their gut. Others lace themselves with bitterness when they need rest. The grazers read these chemical instructions with their feet and mouthparts.

Larger herbivores exist, though seeing one would feel like witnessing an event rather than encountering an animal.

They are tall, narrow browsers with many delicate legs, built almost like walking instruments. Their bodies are high off the ground to avoid the worst reflected heat from the salt flats. Their feet spread lightly over crust and stone. Along their backs rise sail-like membranes, thin and dark-veined, used to gather warmth, discharge static, and perhaps sense distant vapor events. In certain lights those sails flash green, black, or bronze.

Their heads are small compared to their bodies, ending in a hard, precise beak. They feed on column plants and fan-groves without killing them, inserting the beak into seams, wounds, or prepared openings and withdrawing dense resinous tissue. The plants seal afterward. Sometimes the browser leaves behind saliva rich in catalysts and spores from other colonies, so its feeding is also pollination, infection, and repair.

These browsers may cross a basin over days. They do not herd in large numbers. At most, two or three may share a vapor-rich district, keeping vast distances between them. Their calls, if they call, are probably too low or too infrequent to seem like sound: tremors through their legs, static flashes along the sails, chemical puffs released into the wind. They live long lives paced by geology’s smaller rhythms.

There are hunters too.

The most visible are the gliders: ribbon-bodied, membrane-stiffened animals that launch from escarpments at evening. They are not birds, though from far away they might be mistaken for torn strips of night sliding across the bright air. Their bodies are narrow and flexible, with long lateral membranes held by cartilaginous spars. They do not flap often. The atmosphere is too thin and costly for that. They fall with expertise, riding descending currents along cliffs and basins.

Their senses are chemical and thermal. They taste the air with fringed mouthparts and read the ground in contrasts: the faint warmth of a grazer recently emerged, the disturbed radiance of a crust trail, the resin smell of a browsing wound. Most flights fail. Most nights they find nothing. So they are built for hunger. Their tissues can slow almost to dormancy. After feeding, they may hang for weeks from cliff-veils, wrapped in their own membranes, digesting in the cold.

There may be other predators less often noticed: burrowing filament-worms that wait beneath crusts for grazers to pass overhead; mineral-mimic ambushers shaped like fallen scales around column bases; tiny parasitic spores that enter browsers through feeding wounds and bloom only years later in the membranes of their sails.

But predation here is not frantic. It is an occasional correction in a slow economy.

The deepest basins hold the richest communities.

There the ground exhales often, and life has grown almost elaborate. Hanging curtains descend from mineral shelves, layered with green-black photosynthetic tissue and rust-colored ion-mining films. Glassy shrubs branch into thousands of forked blades that ring faintly when wind passes through them. Cushioned mats swell and contract through the day, quilted like lungs pressed flat against stone. Their colors change with metabolism: gray-green at dawn, deep blue at noon, black-violet at night.

These basin communities are the nearest thing to oases, though no water glints there. The air tastes metallic, bitter, and charged. Spores settle in thick drifts. Grazers gather in numbers that would be startling elsewhere. Browsers visit on long circuits. Gliders circle overhead in patient spirals. During a strong exhalation, everything responds: seams open, mats darken, fronds stream threads, crusts breathe, animals emerge, and the whole basin becomes briefly dense with intention.

Still, even here, nothing crowds in the wet-world way. There is always space. Light passes through every grove. Wind can reach every surface. No organism can afford to create too much shade, too much shelter, too much dependence on softness. The plateau punishes excess.

On the cliffsides live the vertical miners.

They appear as stains: green, rust, black, yellow, and copper-blue films running down old seepage paths that no longer carry water. These are communities of lithic organisms, half-plant, half-fungus, half-electrical web, though fractions fail here. They press themselves flat to exposed strata and send root-veils into microscopic cracks, dissolving ions from stone. When wind strikes them, they flatten; when it releases, they lift slightly, breathing dust beneath their edges.

Some cliff-films may be among the most intelligent inhabitants of the plateau. They feel pressure waves travel across the continent. They time spore releases to winds that began beyond the horizon. They exchange charge through mineral seams. Over centuries, they alter the cliff face itself, smoothing channels, darkening receptive stone, rejecting unstable surfaces. A cliff colonized by them is not decorated. It is being slowly read.

Beneath all this, in the cracks and salt plates, lives the hidden majority.

Thread-nets move through stone. Acid filaments explore grains. Conductive fungal cables link crust to column, fan to basin, cliff to flat. Dormant spores wait in dry pores for decades, perhaps centuries, until the correct chemical shadow falls over them. There are microfauna that never see open air. There are cysts sealed inside halite, waking only when salts deliquesce for a few cold hours. There are organisms whose entire lives occur along a boundary thinner than paper: between mineral and gas, crystal and gel, light and poison.

If there is a true ruler here, it is not an animal or a plant, but the network of relationships among them: the dry exchange.

Everything trades. Crusts trade stabilized soil for grazing. Fans trade spores for dust gathered by veils. Columns trade resin for pollination wounds. Browsers trade movement for nutrients. Gliders trade death back into the basins as mineral-rich husks. Cliff-films trade patience for access to wind. Even the empty flats participate, storing light, reflecting heat, carrying dust, keeping distance between colonies so none can exhaust the ground too quickly.

The place may also contain minds too slow or too distributed to notice individually.

A fan-grove might remember the direction of a century’s prevailing winds in the asymmetry of its fronds. A basin mat might learn the migration cycle of browsers by altering its chemistry just before they arrive. A continent-spanning spore release might ripple outward in response to pressure shifts, making the plateau seem, for a few hours, like a single organism drawing breath through stone.

No one builds houses. No one makes roads. No one lights fires. But information moves. Warnings move. Invitations move. Hunger moves. Reproduction moves. The land is not mute simply because it has no voices.

The beings here are exacting because the world is exacting. They are lush without being lavish. Dry without being dead. Beautiful without softness. They do not soften the emptiness; they articulate it.

They are the dark fans written across white salt.

The lacquered crusts breathing at night.

The green towers tightening in the cold.

The pale grazers leaving loops of changed color behind them.

The many-legged browsers crossing a basin like slow weather.

The ribbon hunters falling silently from the cliffs.

The cliff-films, the root-veils, the mineral threads, the spores asleep inside dust.

What lives here is life reduced to precision and expanded into landscape: a high, dry biosphere of patience, glass, oil, salt, light, and breath from under stone.
