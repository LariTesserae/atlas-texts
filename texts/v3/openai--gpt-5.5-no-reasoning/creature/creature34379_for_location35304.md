What lives there are the continuations of the builders, though not in the shape of bodies.

They are colonies, mats, curtains, veils, films, roots, pale stems, mineral-skinned growths, and slow intelligences of habit. They have no eyes, no mouths, no limbs, no hunger that resembles pursuit. They do not swim through the drowned country. They occupy it. They fasten, spread, thicken, soften, filter, seal, pry, darken, buoy, and remember by growing.

The most visible inhabitants are the weed-curtains.

They hang from lintels, balcony edges, submerged rooflines, and the ribs of old trellises. In the dim water they seem at first like remnants of cloth: banners left in place after some procession drowned, shawls caught on carved posts, nets abandoned and gone green. But they are alive. Their strands divide and braid and divide again, some broad as straps, some fine as hair. They respond to current with a unity that makes them seem aware of distant gates. When a sluice exhales miles away, the weed-curtains incline before the motion is otherwise perceptible. They know the water’s leaning before stone does.

They are dark at their anchored ends, nearly black where old tissue lies layered upon old tissue. Toward their tips they become translucent, olive, brown-gold, sometimes a faint yellow-green where the upper light reaches them. Their surfaces are furred with smaller lives: algae, mineral grains, bacterial skins, spores caught in mucilage. In still courtyards they grow long enough to touch the floors, rooting again in silt and making rooms into green chambers. In passages with a persistent flow they stream horizontally, combed into parallel lines, like the hair of a sleeping giant laid through the drowned avenues.

There are mosses that live as masonry.

They are thickest along the high edges: the backs of causeways just under the surface, the tops of drowned walls, the shoulders of reservoirs, the rims of shafts where faint light brushes stone. They make cushions, pads, low quilts, and dark velvet skins. A hand pressed into them would sink to the knuckle and rise cold, dripping, smelling faintly of stone and peat. Their growth is patient but architectural. They fill cracks. They round corners. They make a broken wall continuous again, not by restoring it but by persuading water to pass differently.

Some mosses are nearly black and dense as felt. Others are pale gray-green, tender at the tips, with minute fronds opened like hands. In places where the old clay linings have split, moss grows into the seam and seals it. In places where a wall has begun to bow, moss colonizes the seep line, drinks the escaping minerals, and becomes a living mortar. Entire districts still hold water because moss has learned the shape of the failure and inhabits it.

There are algae that paint the whole country.

They are the first skin on every surface and the last remaining color after carvings lose their faces. They make green-black sheens on stone blocks, dull gold stains on clay, bronze-dark smears on old wood. They cloud the faces of drowned statues until the statues become only upright differences in growth. They collect in the thumbprints of ancient seals. They lie in the grooves of tally marks. They settle into chisel scars and make them legible by darkening them.

Some are flat and lacquerlike. Some rise in cloudy fur. Some form globes in sheltered niches, soft bladders the color of old tea. In shafts where falling water introduces bubbles, they grow in rings and halos, each ring marking a former pressure, a former waterline, a former balance between descent and stillness. They are the country’s dim illumination, not because they glow, but because they reveal edges to whatever little light remains.

In the deeper cisterns live the pale ferns.

They are among the strangest inhabitants because they seem almost unwilling to be plants. Their stems are white, cream, or faintly blue, as if drained of any memory of sunlight. Their fronds spread flat against the water, against walls, against the undersides of stone ledges, arranging themselves to receive the smallest possible brightness. They grow where ordinary green life would thin out: in reservoir wells, under rooflands, along spiral ramps descending into cold.

Their roots are fine and exploratory. They enter mortar like questions. They pass through brick courses, old reed packing, softened plaster, the hollows left by rotted pegs. They do not break things quickly. They loosen them across centuries. A wall may remain upright while threaded through completely by fern-root, held partly by the same plant that is dismantling it. When blocks finally shift, the fern adjusts and spreads over the new angle, making collapse into habitat.

There are root forests beneath the old raft-fields.

From above, those rafts may look like marsh, low and sodden, perhaps even land. But below, they are inhabited by long hanging root systems, brown, black, ivory, and red. They trail down into the water in curtains thick enough to hide pylons. Some roots descend straight and plumb, weighted with mineral accretions. Others coil around ancient anchor ropes, enter stone sockets, and grip the engineered skeletons of the rafts as if remembering the hands that braided them.

These roots filter the water. Silt collects in them. Spores lodge there. Dead stems tangle and become new raft-matter. Gas gathers among them in dim pockets and releases in dull pearls. The roots are not passive. They choose channels. They clog some, clear others, and slowly redirect the motion of water under the floating fields. In this way they have inherited the work of sluice-keepers.

There are grasses in the courtyards.

They are pale, narrow, and translucent, growing in old domestic rectangles: between curbs, within gardens, around thresholds, inside rooms whose roofs have fallen. They do not wave as field grass waves in air. They bow and remain bowed for hours, sometimes days, aligned by currents too faint for a body to feel. When the current changes they turn slowly, blade by blade, until an entire drowned neighborhood indicates the new direction.

Some courtyards are filled so densely that the grasses make a drowned meadow, silver-green in the little light. The paving stones beneath are invisible except where former paths remain as darker channels. These grasses know the old plans. Not consciously, not by thought, but by preference: they grow differently on paving, silt, clay, roof-crust, old hearth ash, and softened basketry. Their bodies redraw the maps of districts whose walls are nearly gone.

There are vines that have learned the flood.

They descend from orchard and trellis plants, from espaliered food-forests and trained rootstocks, from centuries of grafting before the water became final. Their ancestors were disciplined into grids and lattices by gardeners. Now the discipline remains in their growth. They follow old frames. They climb dead trellises underwater. They bridge poles, cross streets, bind roof to roof. In some places plant and scaffold are indistinguishable: black wood within brown vine within green filament within mineral crust.

Their leaves change with depth. Near the surface they are small, leathery, dark, arranged to catch the faint gray pressure from above. Lower down they narrow into ribbons or threads, minimizing resistance to flow. In deeper parts the vines produce no leaves at all, only searching tendrils and pale nodules that feed from dissolved minerals and the rot of old construction.

They do not flower brightly. If they flower at all, they do so in hidden, reduced ways: tiny colorless structures tucked beneath mats, pale dust released into water, brief soft swellings that open and vanish without spectacle. Their fruit, if it can be called fruit, is small and hard, built less for animals than for drifting, lodging, waiting.

There are bacteria and black layers.

These are the unseen inhabitants, but perhaps the most numerous. They live beneath mats, inside saturated wood, under silt, in clay seams, in old storage pits, within the anaerobic quiet of sealed rooms. They make dark films and sour pockets. They consume the vanished civilization slowly: timber, reed, plaster, fiber, seed hull, ash, skin oils left on handholds, traces of food pressed into pottery pores. They work where light never reaches.

Their world is chemical and close. Iron, sulfur, old tannins, lime, peat, dissolved minerals, the long carbohydrates of drowned wood. They stain the bottoms of cisterns black. They preserve some things and erase others. A basket may survive because they altered its surface into something resistant; a carved beam may vanish from within while retaining its outer shape, until a pressure change caves it softly inward.

They are the hidden digestion of the made ocean.

There are mineral growths that behave almost like life.

Cold water carries lime, iron, clay, salts, and the memory of everything it has touched. Over centuries these gather into skins, crusts, nodules, veils, stalactitic lips beneath ceilings, pale rims around old vent holes, amber seams in sluice frames. Some mineral forms coat roots and preserve them after the root inside has died. Some stiffen mats into shelves. Some bridge cracks with translucent stone. Some grow around rope knots and keep the knot after fiber has gone.

They are not alive, but among the living things they serve as bones, shells, handwriting. Plants grow over them, through them, against them. Moss drinks from them. Algae darken them. Fern roots exploit their fractures. They make the ruins harder in some places and more brittle in others. They are the slow answer of stone dissolved and redeposited by water.

And there are the raft-marches themselves: compound beings, half plant and half artifact.

They are not merely habitats but inhabitants at the scale of districts. A raft-field may consist of ancient reed bundles, woven brush, trapped peat, living sedge, root mats, moss, dwarf shrubs, gas pockets, stone anchors, dead vine skeletons, and new growth all bound into one trembling body. It rises and falls by inches with pressure, rain, and trapped air. Its edges fray, then rebraid through roots. Its underside lengthens. Its surface pools, freezes, thaws, drinks rain, sheds silt.

Such a raft is old enough to have outlived the hands that first tethered it, yet it still obeys their engineering. It remains where it was placed because the anchors hold. It continues because plants have taken up the task of renewal. It is an ecosystem wearing the shape of a machine.

Nothing there is quick.

The inhabitants do not dart, flee, hunt, mate in flashes, cry out, or rise in swarms. Their motions are extensions, softenings, tiltages, settlements. A fern uncurls over a season in the dark. A moss lip advances across a carved threshold by the width of a fingernail. A weed strand detaches and drifts through three courtyards before lodging in a crack that will become its country. A gas pearl gathers all winter beneath a mat and releases in silence. A root finds a thumbprint in clay and fills it.

Because there are no animals, there is no grazing, no cropping, no sudden browsing pressure to shape them. Their enemies are cold, darkness, burial, pressure, chemical scarcity, and the occasional mechanical violence of water. When a gate shifts and a current strengthens, whole curtains are torn loose and carried into darker basins. When silt rises, grasses are buried standing. When ice forms near the surface, moss cushions are shorn and scarred. When a roof collapses, an interior population disappears into a cloud and later returns as something suited to rubble.

They reproduce by drift and patience.

Spores travel like dust through water. Seeds, reduced and heavy or buoyant and fibrous, move along old canals. Broken filaments catch on nail-holes, peg sockets, splinters, carvings, handholds, the roughness of mineral skins. Every human-made irregularity is a place to begin. The builders, by making so many edges, made endless futures for small rooted lives.

If there is anything like an animal presence, it is only in the behavior of the whole drowned country. The place breathes through sluices. It shivers through timbers. It digests through black sediment. It grows hair from roofs and teeth from mineral crusts. It has veins of current, cold descending arteries, warmish shallows like faint pulses. It remembers pressure. It keeps habits. But this is not consciousness as a creature would have it. It is maintenance without mind.

The living things there have become custodians of a civilization they did not build and cannot understand.

They hold walls together.
They pull walls apart.
They mark roads.
They hide shafts.
They preserve handprints.
They fill rooms.
They keep water clear in one district and clouded in another.
They inherit gates by growing around them.
They replace ropes with roots.
They replace mortar with moss.
They replace agriculture with a slower agriculture of themselves.

The makers are gone, but their gestures remain available, and the plants use them. A stake row becomes a nursery. A sleeping alcove becomes a fern shelf. A market court becomes a grass basin. A drying rack becomes a suspended garden. A processional ramp becomes a spiral of algae. A child’s step becomes a ledge for moss.

So what lives there is not a monster of the dark water, nor a hidden people, nor a school of blind fish.

What lives there is a cold vegetal civilization after civilization: a drowned, root-bound, photosynthetic afterlife of human labor.

It is moss with the habits of masons.
Weed with the memory of gates.
Fern with the patience of demolition.
Raft-marsh with the discipline of ancient engineering.
Algae reading the stones in green and black.
Bacteria completing the long burial.
Roots taking the handholds one by one.

And everywhere, in the nearly dark water, the living inhabitants move so slowly that they resemble stillness—until a far gate shifts, or pressure changes, or a faint current passes through the old streets, and every hanging strand inclines together, as if the whole drowned country has listened and understood.
