They are not a hidden people, though a traveler might at first mistake them for one.

What lives there is a whole society of altitude: small, furred, feathered, horned, shelled, rooted, crusted, and half-frozen things, each keeping to its allotted distance, each fitted so precisely to the basin that none appears to own it and none appears out of place. The inhabitants do not gather into abundance the way lowland life does. They are spread thinly across the stone, but the stone repeats them. A whistle under one boulder is answered by another on a slope a mile away. A horned animal standing alone on a ledge belongs to a herd hidden by perspective. A wet thumbprint of moss contains its own grazing, hunting, breeding, and dying. The basin’s life is distributed like stars: sparse only until the eye adjusts.

The pikas are among the most immediate residents. They live in the broken rock at the bases of cliffs, in those old talus fields where the stones have settled into chambers, corridors, and cold little rooms. They are round-eared, gray-brown, almost the color of the granite that shelters them, with quick black eyes and no patience for stillness. Their bodies seem made of alarm and industry. All through the brief bright hours they hurry in and out of the rocks carrying stems, sedge blades, seedheads, scraps of alpine grass. They lay their hay in hidden crevices where the winter cannot quite steal it. They do not sing, but they announce themselves with sharp, piercing cries that ricochet from stone to stone until the whole slope seems nervous. To them the basin is not empty at all. It is divided into territories, watch-posts, hayfields, bolt-holes, sunning stones, and dangerous open crossings.

Deeper in the slopes live the marmot-like ones: heavier, slower-seeming, though not slow when danger comes. They have blunt faces, pale whiskers, and fur washed in the colors of dust, lichen, and old snowmelt. They sit upright on warm slabs with their forepaws tucked to their chests, solemn as judges, turning their heads to test the wind. Their burrows go into frost-hardened ground where roots are few and every entrance must be chosen with care. They spend the summer enlarging themselves, harvesting the thin green edges of the basin, converting sedge and flower and bitter leaf into fat. Their lives are organized around sleep—long, profound, communal sleep below the reach of the worst cold. In the warm months they seem almost ceremonial, appearing at the mouths of burrows, calling, feeding, vanishing, as though performing the visible part of a much larger underground life.

The horned animals are the basin’s most stately inhabitants. They are not large in the way forest beasts are large, but they carry themselves with the authority of creatures that have made an agreement with cliffs. Their coats are shaggy and pale along the back, darkening at the legs and muzzle; their horns curve like weather made solid, black or smoke-brown, ridged by years. Their hooves are hard at the edges and soft within, able to grip sloping stone and narrow ledges that would seem unusable to any animal born lower down. They travel in loose herds—females and young spread across the shelves, old males solitary or in small severe groups, all of them held together by sightlines rather than closeness.

They graze with patience. A tuft of grass here, a mineral lick there, a slow procession toward water as the sun lowers. Their trails are almost invisible unless one knows the angle from which to look: faint gray rubs on gravel, a polishing of stone, pellets of dung gathered in the lee of boulders, a few hairs caught on a schist edge. They are the makers of many small gardens. Where they rest, where they drop dung, where their hooves break crust and press seed into dampness, the basin grows a little greener. Tiny flowers mark old bedding places. Moss thickens where they return year after year. They do not alter the land loudly, but the land carries their habits.

Above them are the birds, who seem less like visitors than weather with bones.

The choughs are black against the brightness, red-billed or yellow-billed depending on the lineage of that height, acrobatic and quarrelsome. They inhabit the wind as naturally as pikas inhabit talus. They tumble along cliff faces, fold and open, shout to one another, steal insects from exposed turf, investigate dung for beetles, and ride updrafts for the pleasure of not falling. They are intelligent in the restless, opportunistic way of high birds. Anything new in the basin interests them. A shed horn, a carcass, a dropped scrap of food, a traveler’s pack left unattended: all are inspected, commented upon, remembered.

The raptors are fewer and quieter. They hang in the air with dreadful economy, pale-bellied, long-winged, their heads making minute corrections while the body appears motionless. Their lives are made possible by all the small hurried lives below. A pika careless for one breath, a young marmot too far from its hole, a ground bird flushed into the open, a weak kid separated from the herd—these are the raptor’s opportunities. They do not need many. Their hunger is patient and mathematical. They nest on inaccessible ledges where old bones whiten beside the young, and their shadows passing over the basin are understood by every creature that can see.

There are ravens too, darker-minded and more social with death. They know the cliffs, the carcasses, the afterbirth of the horned herds, the places where winter reveals what summer concealed. They bark and croak from black ridges, walking with sidelong dignity over stone. They remember routes, faces, habits. They will follow a predator, a wounded animal, a human party. Not eagerly; nothing here is eager for long. But with the steady interest of beings who know that patience feeds.

The smaller ground birds are harder to notice until they move. They run along the gravel and between moss cushions on quick legs, backs mottled like pebbles, bellies pale, bills fine and probing. They are birds of margins: tarn edges, damp silt, sedge fringes, insect clouds. Some nest almost openly, trusting disguise and distance. Their eggs look like speckled stones. Their chicks, when they hatch, are mobile flecks of down that learn immediately the art of freezing in place.

In the wet hollows lives another order of inhabitants: brief, cold, numerous, easily missed.

There are gnats and midges that rise in sudden smoky columns whenever the sun warms a sedge patch. There are beetles with bronze, blue, or oil-black backs moving slowly beneath stones, conserving heat. There are larvae in the melt-thread pools, pale and translucent, bending in the current. There are worms in the saturated silt, their bodies working the thin organic matter that accumulates grain by grain. In the moss are tardigrades and rotifers and mites, minute animals that endure drying, freezing, and revival as if death were a season rather than an end. The damp skin of the basin is full of them. When the morning condensation swells the moss, a metropolis awakens underfoot.

The frogs are small, dark, and astonishing. They live in places that seem too cold for softness: sedge pockets, seep-fed hollows, shallow tarn margins where the sun can warm the upper finger-width of water. Their bodies are no bigger than thumb joints, mottled brown, olive, or stone-gray, with gold or black eyes set high. They do not announce themselves with lush choruses. Their calls are slight, clicking or creaking sounds that vanish under wind unless one kneels very near. Their eggs develop quickly, or slowly with great endurance, depending on the cold’s terms. Tadpoles graze on films of algae in water clear enough to expose them to every bird’s eye. Many are eaten. Enough remain.

The tarns themselves are inhabited, though they seem at first like pure mineral mirrors. Along their bottoms lie mats of algae, nearly invisible except where they make the sediment faintly green or brown. Tiny crustaceans pulse through the cold water. Larvae cling beneath stones. At the edges, where sedges make a narrow broken fringe, waders come to feed—long-legged, fine-billed, deliberate. They pick life from mud so shallow it is almost not mud, stepping with care among hoofprints, froglets, and reflected sky.

The plants are not passive scenery here. They are among the oldest and most disciplined inhabitants.

The lichens live on boulder faces, cliff ledges, and old exposed slabs, making maps in silver, orange, black, and green. They grow slowly enough that a human life is a brief weather passing over them. They are partnerships, fungi and algae or cyanobacteria braided into a single survival, eating light and stone dust, drinking fog and condensation. They loosen the rock by persistence. They stain the basin with the first thin promise of soil. Some form crusts hard as paint; others leaf outward in brittle scales; still others make dark knobs like charred grains. Their lives are almost geological. To break one with a careless boot is to erase centuries.

The mosses are softer but no less severe. They occupy cracks, seeps, shaded depressions, places where water lingers for an hour longer than elsewhere. Dry, they become dark and contracted, nearly dead to sight. Wet, they swell into green-black velvet, holding droplets in tiny forests. Whole generations of insects and microscopic animals depend on these brief awakenings. The moss does not spread far; it thickens where conditions permit, building a living sponge over stone, a memory of water.

The flowering plants are the basin’s most concentrated expressions of urgency. They bloom low to the ground, pressed out of the wind: yellow points, purple stars, white cups, red-tipped rosettes. Their leaves are small, hairy, waxy, or folded tight against cold. Their roots go deep into improbable seams. Some may be decades old though no taller than a knuckle. They flower quickly when warmth allows, using the short season with exquisite economy. To lowland eyes they look delicate. They are not. They are built of refusal.

There are also the unseen and half-seen lives: fox-like hunters crossing at night with tails low; weasels slipping through talus after pikas; moths folded under stones by day; spiders in warm cracks; mites in feathers; ticks waiting in ungulate bedding sites; fungi threading old dung and dead roots; bacteria blooming briefly in meltwater films; the marrow of carcasses opened by cold and beaks. Nothing here is wasted, though wasting is slow. A dead horned animal may lie for seasons, altered by ravens, beetles, frost, sun, and time, until it becomes white bone, then mineral scatter, then a greener patch of sedge.

If there is a dominant creature, it is not any single animal. It is the relation between exposure and refuge.

Everything that lives there knows the open and the hidden. The pika crosses bare stone only to reach another crevice. The ungulate feeds on exposed shelves but sleeps where wind and predator cannot easily take it. The bird uses the open air as shelter because it has mastered what others fear. The moss withdraws into dryness until water returns. The frog trusts mud, the beetle trusts the underside of rock, the lichen trusts slowness. Survival in the basin is not conquest. It is fitting oneself into an exact gap in the severity.

They are noisy because sound travels where bodies cannot. Calls maintain distance, mark danger, find mates, hold territories, locate kin through glare and stone. A whistle is architecture. An echo is an extension of the body. The basin’s creatures live not only in burrows, nests, tarns, and ledges, but in acoustics: in warnings bounced from granite, in wing-cries torn by wind, in the clatter of hooves magnified until a herd half a mile away seems to be passing just behind one’s shoulder.

They are also ancient in habit. Not unchanged—nothing living is unchanged—but repeating patterns older than memory. The herds descend at evening because water and safety have taught them so across generations. The pikas cut hay because winter has always come. The birds nest where the ledge defeats the paw. The moss wakes when the stone sweats at dawn. The frogs breed in the shallow margins before the water vanishes or freezes. Each life is temporary; the choreography is not.

Humans do not live there in the full sense. They may enter, and some may know it well enough to survive its moods: hunters, herders, wanderers, gatherers of bitter lichen or eggs, people following animals rather than paths. But they would be guests under strict terms. The basin offers no surplus for settlement, no wood for building, no soil to forgive carelessness. A person could belong there only by restraint: by moving lightly, speaking little, reading weather from stone and animal behavior, and accepting that the place cannot be made convenient.

So the true inhabitants are those that leave almost nothing resembling possession.

They live in whistles, pellets, tracks, clipped grass, shed feathers, warmed stones, tunnel mouths, dung-fed flowers, eggs like pebbles, shadows passing over gravel, and hoof-polished ledges. They live by condensation and meltwater, by reflected light, by the hour of warmth stored on the surface of rock. They are abundant without crowding, audible without filling the space, visible only after patience.

The basin is not empty.

It is inhabited by endurance.
