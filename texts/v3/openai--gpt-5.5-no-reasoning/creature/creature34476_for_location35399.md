What lives there is not, in the ordinary sense, a population.

There may be individuals in it—figures, workers, attendants, clerks, inspectors, repairers—but they are not what the city is full of. If one waits for crowds, for the ordinary warm spill of civic life, one misunderstands the place. The true inhabitant is the system itself, and anything that walks within it lives as one of its instruments.

The city is inhabited by maintenance.

Not maintenance as labor alone, not people with tools arriving after breakdown, but maintenance as a species, as a climate of intention. It occupies the tunnels, the ledges, the stairwells, the pump halls, the frost-rimed signal cabins. It lives in brass plates, in valve schedules, in the patience of moss trained to redirect leaks. It lives in the way a cracked pane accepts ice without shattering, in the way the first falling stone is also the last, in the way a tram arrives at an empty platform because arrival itself is required.

If there are beings there, they are likely not citizens so much as custodians.

They would be few, or else made few by the scale. Seen from a gantry, one might mistake them for dark fittings on the railings, for survey markers, for bundled coats left standing in sleet. They would move deliberately, conserving breath in the thin air. They would know the city by pressure gradients, by the tone of cables, by the taste of mineral condensation on a glove. Their work would not be to build, because the place is already built; nor to govern, because the governing has soaked into stone, into gauges, into automatic doors and canal levels. Their task would be to keep the exactness exact.

They would not hurry.

Nothing there hurries except falling water, and even that has been given channels.

Perhaps they are human descendants, altered by altitude and habit rather than by visible deformity. Narrow-chested, slow-pulsed, pale from factory light and weather-reflected cloud, with hands toughened by cold metal and wet rope. Their faces might have the expression of people who listen before they speak, because every room already contains information: the pitch of a fan, the interval between hammer strokes from the gorge, the faint variation in the underfloor turbines. They would dress in felted black wool, waxed canvas, ceramic-buttoned coats, rubberized leggings, masks hung ready beneath the chin. Their lamps would be hooded. Their tools would be numbered. Their speech would be low, not out of secrecy, but because loudness belongs to the city.

They might live in the lit apartment blocks whose windows repeat with too much regularity. Behind those windows would be warm chambers, but only just warm enough: tiled floors, condensation gutters below the sills, narrow beds, shelves of ledgers, drying racks for gloves, wall diagrams of district pressure loops. Meals would steam briefly and then cool. The food would be fungal, vegetal, mineral-grown: dark leaf paste, pressed moss starch, glasshouse roots, broth from cultivated algae, salt condensed from old seas locked in stone. No meat. No milk. No eggs. Perhaps no one has a memory of animals except as prohibited shapes in technical manuals: fouling agents, carriers, entropy multipliers.

Children, if there are children, would learn silence first—not the absence of sound, but how not to add useless sound to a city already speaking. They would be taught to distinguish healthy vibration from dangerous vibration, permitted frost from invasive frost, compliant moss from wild overgrowth. They would recite districts the way other children recite family names. They would know which bridges may be crossed during sleet discharge, which stairways loop back on themselves if taken without pausing, which windows are truly windows and which are listening lenses set into the mountain.

But it is also possible that even this is too human.

The inhabitants may be smaller, stranger, more distributed.

They may be the maintenance trams that answer cable-song. Low, dark, many-wheeled things that emerge from sealed depots with no driver visible, pantographs sparking under frozen wires, tool arms folded against their sides like insect legs in a world without insects. They may pause beneath a frayed span, extend heated clamps, splice metal, seal the joint with resin, and return without witness. Their routes may be older than memory. Their bells may sound not to warn pedestrians, but to declare compliance to the surrounding stone.

They may be the pumps.

Not merely machines, but organs with tenure. Vast cast-iron hearts sunk beneath avenues, each with its rhythm, each named, registered, fed, cooled, listened to. Some may have been turning since before the newest ridges hardened. Their housings are painted and repainted until the paint forms geological strata of its own. Around them, attendants—human or otherwise—leave offerings that are not offerings: fresh gaskets, oil, ceramic sleeves, polished pressure dials, a cleaned drain. The pumps keep water moving so water cannot decide what it wants to become.

They may be the plants.

The plants are not decorative, and not wild. They live there with the status of clerks or engineers. Moss pads absorb and measure seepage. Lichens test the chemical mood of rock. Ferns mark hidden warmth. Vines bind gantries where metal fatigue would otherwise begin. The black-leaved trees in sheltered basins are not parks but regulators, drinking excess moisture, slowing wind, darkening courtyards to preserve approved freeze-thaw ratios. Their roots know masonry better than soil. Their leaves are not seasonal expressions but instruments, thin black gauges turned toward factory glow.

The flowers, those glassy small blossoms flashing violet only at an angle, may be the city’s most delicate sensors. Perhaps each opens only when some invisible balance has been achieved: enough sulfur in the condensation, enough electrical charge in the fog, enough silence in an administrative courtyard. No bee visits them. No seed is carried in a bird’s gut. If they reproduce, it is by schedule, through municipal misting, or through spores released into ventilation ducts at appointed temperatures. Their beauty is incidental, as frost’s beauty is incidental to freezing.

The absence of animals is not an emptiness but a law.

Whatever lives there has either accepted the law or become part of its enforcement. The city cannot tolerate appetite that wanders. It cannot tolerate nesting, burrowing, scavenging, swarming, breeding without permission, decay without assignment. Animals would bring accident: tracks, dung, gnawed insulation, blocked vents, surprise deaths in conduits, warmth spent on private blood. So the city excludes them as it excludes landslide, rot, and uncontrolled spring thaw. Perhaps birds avoid the air because of inaudible pressure fields. Perhaps insects fail in the chemistry of the water. Perhaps any animal that enters simply becomes still, not killed violently, but edited out by cold, by lack of food, by the gentle refusal of the environment to make room for it.

What remains is life without animal restlessness.

A civic life. A mineral-vegetal-industrial life.

There are probably archives beneath it all.

Rooms where maps are updated though no boundary changes. Cabinets of snow clearance records. Ledgers noting the condition of every fracture in the mountain. Botanical plans showing where moss will be permitted to advance over the next century. Diagrams of streets that are straight on paper but circular in experience, with annotations explaining how to walk them safely: pause at the third lamp; touch the left wall; wait for the pressure bell; proceed.

In those archives, someone or something reads compliance.

Perhaps the oldest inhabitants are archivists, or devices that have replaced archivists while preserving the posture of reading. They sit in halls warmed by waste steam, beneath lamps that never flicker, turning pages made from laminated leaves gathered from between paving stones. They record that District Seven remained audible. That condensation output met expectation. That no unauthorized fauna was detected. That the mountain’s northwest shoulder attempted settling at 03:17 and was persuaded otherwise by counterweight release. That the city continues.

Their religion, if they have one, is probably not worship but calibration.

Their prayers are inspections. Their hymns are shift bells synchronized across valleys. Their sacraments are thawing a frozen switch, clearing a gutter, adjusting a valve one eighth of a turn. They would not ask the city to change. They would ask it to remain sufficiently itself. Sin would be waste, interruption, unscheduled growth, warmth in the wrong place, silence where there should be rotation.

Death there, too, would be administrative.

Bodies, if bodies exist, cannot be allowed to rot informally. They would be dried, mineralized, composted into approved botanical beds, or sealed into retaining walls with a plaque bearing function rather than name. Perhaps the dead become insulation. Perhaps they are rendered into the black paper layers beneath courtyards. Perhaps they are placed in frost vaults high on the ridges, not as memorials but as thermal mass. The city wastes nothing, but it also does not grieve in a way that interrupts the pumps.

And yet it would be wrong to call the inhabitants emotionless.

A place so devoted to continuation must contain a profound, cold tenderness. The care is everywhere. In the heated rail switches. In the snow cleared from routes no one seems to take. In the lamps lit across empty plazas. In the moss given curbstones. In the fact that every spring is named and every fracture bridged. Something loves this difficult equilibrium—not with warmth, not with abundance, but with exactitude.

The city is loved by being kept.

Whoever lives there has become suited to loving in that way.

They do not dream of summer. Summer would be catastrophic. They do not dream of beasts returning or rivers running free. They may dream instead of perfect circulation: every pipe singing at the right pressure, every reservoir at its mark, every lichen field advancing according to plan, every mountain edge sharp against metallic distance. They dream of no district falling silent. They dream of the roar continuing after them.

Perhaps there is a central being, though not a monarch. Something deep in the plateau, below the pump archives and the gorge mills, where the young mountains are still deciding what they are. Not a brain exactly. A covenant between stone, water, machinery, and rule. It may have begun as a municipal control system, or a geological anomaly, or a city’s desperate answer to living on unstable new mountains. Over time, it learned not merely to respond but to require. It required drainage, then pressure, then roads, then schedules, then absence, then plants, then people shaped into maintenance. The city was built to survive the mountains; then the mountains were organized to survive the city.

This central inhabitant does not speak. It expresses preference through gradients.

More frost here. Less flow there. A bell before failure. A tram sent into fog. A door unlocked in an empty administrative wing. A flower blooming violet in a courtyard no one has entered for years.

The others, if others remain, obey because obedience is how one hears it.

So what lives there?

A city-machine with a mountain for a skeleton and weather for breath.

A disciplined remnant of people, perhaps, thinned into custodial castes.

Autonomous trams, pumps, valves, clocks, and furnaces, all old enough to possess something like instinct.

Plants that have entered civic service.

Archives that remember conditions rather than histories.

Cold that is not hostile but regulatory.

Water that is prevented from becoming chaos.

Stone that has been persuaded not to fall.

The inhabitants are the keepers and the kept, the regulators and the regulated. They live by preventing events. Their civilization is not progress, not conquest, not expansion, but the endless preservation of a precise unfinished state.

They are what remains when life gives up abundance in exchange for continuity.

They are the ones who can bear the roar because, to them, it is not noise.

It is the sound of being alive.
