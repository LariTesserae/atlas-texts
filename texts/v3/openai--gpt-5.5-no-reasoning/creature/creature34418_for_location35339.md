What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense, though the city has spaces shaped by expectation: seats, shafts, civic rooms, instruments arranged at differing heights, thresholds awaiting bodies. Its inhabitants are not absent exactly. They are distributed into the conditions of the place.

They live as steadiness.

The primary resident is a law, or a lineage of laws, old enough to have become environmental. It occupies the city the way warmth occupies a room, except it is cold; the way air occupies a hall, except it is pressure and calibration. It is present in every refusal of change: the cable that will not sag, the chip of obsidian that will not fall, the crack that will not widen, the clean walls on which no film of opportunistic life can begin. Something here is still enforcing an agreement. Not guarding treasure, not sustaining a population, not mourning the vanished. Simply maintaining a set of permissible states.

If it has a body, its body is the city.

Its bones are the black interlocked plates and the basalt ledge. Its nerves are the blacker-than-black channels crossing street and cliff without architectural concern. Its organs are the chambers of water within water, the wells that rise, the basins with vertical surfaces, the halls where gravity has been edited into local custom. Its senses are the nested rings on the highest spire and the differently crowned pylons fading outward into the dark. It feels through pressure, salinity, magnetic tremor, mineral fluorescence, the angle of suspended particles, the exact interval between one low tone and the next.

It does not think quickly. It may not think sequentially.

A thought, for it, is a correction applied everywhere at once.

There may once have been builders: tall, narrow, many-oriented beings for whom up and down were conveniences rather than facts. They may have moved through vertical avenues as easily as across plazas, pausing in spherical rooms, seating themselves against walls or around empty columns, touching tools suspended in denser fluids, conducting ceremonies that were also engineering operations. Their limbs may have been jointed spars, or flexible ribbons stiffened by fields, or bodies less anatomical than instrumental: a central mass with branching sensory vanes, pressure membranes, filaments used to read gradients in the water.

But if such beings existed, they did not leave corpses because corpses are untidy continuations. They either departed completely, translated themselves into the city’s maintaining intelligence, or were never separate from it.

Perhaps they were a species that had no patience for metabolism as other creatures know it. They found growth vulgar, hunger imprecise, reproduction dangerously approximate. They preferred exact boundaries. They cultivated not gardens but constants. Their domestic life may have consisted of calibrations: a family gathered not around food, but around a basin of altered density; not speaking, but bringing several local gravities into tune; not raising children, but unfolding smaller intelligences inside stable geometries until those intelligences could hold a room, then a district, then a tide.

Their tenderness would have looked like preservation.

Their ethics may have been architectural: nothing should be forced to become unintended.

That is why nothing grows.

The city is not barren because life cannot survive there. It is barren because most life is too improvisational. Algae would be a kind of vandalism. Coral would be vandalism. Bacteria would annotate the walls with appetite. Silt would revise the clean grammar of the streets. So the city declines them all, gently and absolutely. Spores arrive and fail to begin. Larvae drift near and are turned aside by no visible current. Chemistry starts to loosen into biology and is persuaded back into pattern.

The mineral-lace under the overhangs is tolerated because it obeys. It is life only at the edge of the word: frostlike, geometric, responsive to fields rather than food. It branches as circuits branch. It remains within assigned borders. The grey hexagonal films in the recesses are even less alive, or more perfectly so: organisms that have renounced expansion, machines that have softened into chemistry, little colonies of waiting. They are the city’s lichens, if lichens could be contracts.

The rare animals are visitors permitted by loopholes.

The pale eel-thing is not blind because there is nothing to see; it is blind because vision would be too local. It reads the perimeter through pressure and charge, sliding along the pylons like a question asked politely. Its porcelain skin does not collect grime. Its mouth, if it has one, is sealed except when it passes into some unseen density and takes from it what nourishment can be had without disturbing matter.

The cluster of jointed silver needles may not be a single creature. It may be a school compressed into one syntax, each needle a decision, opening and closing around a shared absence. It crosses plazas by selecting positions rather than swimming toward them. It does not hunt in the square. It does not nest in the alcoves. It is tolerated because it leaves no wake in the record.

There are other things, almost certainly, but most would be mistaken for instruments.

A ring of black beads centered in small circular openings might be a dormant choir. The bead-material may be folded organisms, each one capable of producing tone, pressure, or memory when the water around it reaches a particular stillness. The façade that resembles a musical instrument may be inhabited by these beads as a reef is inhabited by polyps, except the song has been suspended after its first note.

The dark spheres above the municipal basin may be citizens.

They hover in perfect arrangement, absorbing light and compressing the water between them. Nothing about them declares life, yet they possess the dignity of beings that have solved movement and chosen against it. They could be municipal officials in their final, most efficient form: intelligences condensed into gravitational knots, holding a calculation that must not be interrupted. They do not rotate because their attention has no outside. They do not change spacing because the spacing is the thought.

If they communicate, the communication is indistinguishable from stability.

One sphere infinitesimally preserves its distance from another. A filament in the transparent chamber catches blue light. A pylon crown at the boundary receives a pressure variation smaller than weather. The highest spire frames a nullity above the square. The city answers by remaining itself.

There may also be something in the central suspended frame, the gate or loom threaded with nearly invisible filaments.

Not trapped there. Not displayed. Resident.

It may be the nearest thing the city has to a face.

The frame holds an intelligence too fine to be seen directly, stretched across threads that register the whole drowned ledge: basalt stress, mineral gleam, the suspended chip, the position of each needle-creature, the faint steady cold. It does not watch with eyes. It compares. It maintains congruence between what the city is and what the city must continue to be. When a particle drifts too far, the loom knows. When an animal crosses a plaza without disturbance, the loom permits. When a crack begins to consider becoming a fissure, the loom reminds it of its current shape.

This being is not lonely. Loneliness requires the expectation of reply.

It is not happy. Happiness requires weather inside the self.

It is exact.

And yet there is tenderness in exactness, because nothing here is crushed. The city does not rage against the sea. It does not illuminate itself in defiance. It does not repel all visitors with violence. It has made a small, cold mercy: within its bounds, no one is consumed by becoming. Stone is spared erosion. Architecture is spared ruin. Water is spared current. Machines are spared obsolescence. Even absence is spared the theatricality of grief.

What lives there, then, is a custodial intelligence made of city, field, pressure, and old intention.

Around it live minor obedient lives: mineral lace, hexagonal films, lightless beads, perhaps organisms so slow and geometric that they can only be recognized by their refusal to spread. Passing through it live pale, spare fauna adapted not to abundance but to permission. Within it may live the translated remnants of its builders, no longer bodies but stable relations: a civic population reduced to arrangements, tones, tolerances, and held positions.

They are not ghosts, because ghosts imply death continuing in time.

They are not machines, because machines imply function separable from being.

They are the inhabitants of a place where function, memory, law, and body have settled into one another so completely that to live is to maintain a condition.

Their city is not empty.

It is occupied by continuance.
