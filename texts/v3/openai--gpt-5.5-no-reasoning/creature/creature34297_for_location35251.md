What lives there is not one species, not even one civic body, but a negotiated population: a cityful of absences, animals, instruments, concealed citizens, and laws with appetites.

The most visible inhabitants are the least governmental. They are the ones who have no trouble accepting a metropolis as weather and terrain.

There are the fern-deer, translucent browsers moving through the broad wet avenues with the composure of ghosts. Their bodies are narrow and high-hipped, built for stepping through flooded foundations and shallow channels. They carry branching facial fronds instead of antlers, soft veined fans that taste humidity, mineral charge, pheromone law, and the emotional chemistry of predators. When the central pools sound, the fronds fold flat against their skulls and their whole bodies become almost invisible except for the dark thread of gut and heart. They graze on warm leaves, condenser moss, and the tender rims of civic membranes, though the membranes sometimes display warnings at them in colors they have learned to ignore.

There are pollinator swarms the size of rats and not quite like insects. They have too many wings because no pair is permanent; wings bud, split, wither, and regrow according to the district’s pressure and sound. They sleep in the hollow signage of transit pylons, packed together in breathing knots. Their eyes are black oil droplets. Their feet can read the microscopic inscriptions on municipal surfaces, which means doors, kiosks, vents, and shrines sometimes mistake them for authorized users. They pass through the city in busy, glittering bands, carrying pollen, fungal code, mites, rumors of flowering, and fragments of recorded conversation caught in the hairs of their thoraxes.

There are canal fish that do not require canals to be continuous. They wait in mud, in roof hollows, in wet footprints, in sealed drainage memory. When rain comes they reassemble into motion, silver-threaded and frantic, pouring through gutter cuts and stair basins and temporary streams no deeper than a palm. Some have transparent skulls lit by borrowed current. Some have fins that unfold into crawling hands for crossing dry pavement before the warmth kills them. Children, or machines shaped like children, sometimes teach them routes by tone.

There are black elevator crabs, glossy as wet coal, whose ancestors may have lived in tidal rock before shafts and pressure locks gave them a better ocean. They migrate vertically. During pressure drops they emerge from service wells in thousands, clacking over walls, balconies, sealed doors, sleeping vehicles, and one another. Their shells carry pale numeric scars where lift systems have counted them, miscounted them, admitted them, crushed them, forgiven them. They eat algae, dropped ration paste, dead pollinators, insulation foam, and the soft mineral secretions that form on old magnetic rails. A few wear tags of ceremonial ribbon. No one admits to tying them there.

There are amphibians with long jaws and varnished eyes, living in drainage pools and flooded machine pits. They are patient until they are not. Their launches are sudden enough to look like errors in space: one moment black water, the next a warm animal gone. They have learned the acoustic delays of certain neighborhoods and hunt by predicting where sound should have been. Their throats inflate at night with wet green light. When thousands call together, the city’s transformer housings answer sympathetically, and whole basins tremble.

There are birds, but bird is a loose category here. Some have metallic wings that clatter like dropped tools; some have membrane sails and hang motionless in upward rain; some are hardly more than mouths, bright throats, and decision. Many nest in antennae, especially the ones that emit modulated thunder. Their eggs harden best under signal burst. Their chicks beg in frequencies that open service hatches, and so the towers periodically find themselves feeding them warm condensate, insect slurry, or packets of outdated navigation gel. Whether this is accident or civic policy is disputed, if anything in the city can be said to dispute openly.

There are immense procession animals at the outskirts, slow enough to be mistaken for civic works. They move in files along the outer avenues, each back carrying a lifted meadow of moss, orchids, mites, reedlets, and small territorial birds. Their heads hang low and armored, their eyes hidden in folds. Their feet are wide, careful, almost priestly. They graze on mineral foam and young stone softened by rain. Some have municipal access symbols growing naturally in pale scars along their flanks. Doors open before them. Barriers lower. Traffic, when there is traffic, yields. Perhaps the city imported them to crop the margins. Perhaps they arrived before the city and the city simply failed to displace them.

Then there are the smaller citizens of surfaces.

Maintenance organisms live everywhere damaged matter remembers being whole. They are neither machine nor animal in any way that satisfies classification. In bright weather they look like dust, gnats, black mold, lint, pollen, spilled filings. Under magnification they are all jointed tools, cilia, wet lenses, stomachs, mandibles, and glass glands. They swarm over cracks in retaining walls, seal wounds in membranes, stitch pore-breathing habitation stacks, polish field boundaries, and digest mineral errors. Their threads dry into translucent protein harder than ceramic. They are attracted to blood, not because they hunger for it, but because blood resembles an urgent repair request. The concealed inhabitants fear this. Animals do not understand it. The city relies on it.

Wall grazers move slowly over ceramic and basalt-flex avenues. They are soft-bodied, pale, eyeless things, like extruded organs with rasping mouths. They eat algae, fungal film, stray advertisements, old warnings, and the first skin of weathering. They leave behind clean paths that harden to a faint sheen. Sometimes one crosses a memorial glyph and removes a name. Sometimes one reveals an older name beneath. Their population is managed by predatory beetles released from shrines after rain.

Vines also live there, but the vines are not merely plants. Some behave as if they understand prohibition. They stop exactly short of charged surfaces, dangerous thresholds, judicial stones, pool rims, or acoustic hazard zones. Others test the same boundaries nightly, sending tendrils forward with slow deliberation until they ignite, recoil, heal, and try again from a different angle. The most successful vines are thick as transit cables and loop between towers, carrying insects, children’s toys, coded sap, and sleeping snakes. Certain civic departments appear to cultivate them. Certain residents leave offerings for them. Certain doors will not open unless a vine has touched them first.

The trees are pale-trunked, warm-leafed, wide-crowned beings that have accepted infrastructure into their bodies. Their roots enter power channels and drink charge during storms. Their sap carries faint current. If cut, they leak amber resin full of tiny lights. Condensation beads along their bark and travels upward, not down, feeding storage crowns or hidden nests. In some districts their fallen leaves orbit them instead of descending, forming rustling halos until beetles climb the air and harvest them. The trees are civic utilities, shade bodies, listening posts, weather moderators, ancestral markers, and habitats. Whether they are also persons depends on which district is answering.

But the place is not only populated by organisms. It is populated by conditions.

Some neighborhoods are inhabited by slow sound. The noise of a passing vehicle remains after the vehicle has gone, rolling behind it like an obedient animal. Residents there time their movements not by sight but by delayed arrival. Predators hunt the confusion. Couriers leave messages in thunder, knowing they will reach a listener several minutes late but emotionally intact.

Some districts are inhabited by hovering water. Condensation gathers into beads large as eyes, drifting in lines toward tree-bases and collectors. Small creatures ride them. Children, or childlike proxies, release ink into them and watch the ink become civic script. Thirsty birds puncture them midair. The beads heal around the beaks, trembling with brief red threads.

Some places are inhabited by refusal. Gravity weakens near certain gates. Paths shorten when walked with permission and lengthen under trespass. A bridge may carry one traveler across distance and another across likelihood. A plaza may bloom maps for a citizen, warnings for an animal, ancestral faces for the bereaved, advertisements for no one, and hallucinated flowers for those the system cannot classify.

And then there are the people.

If they are people, they have become difficult to see either by preference, necessity, or evolution.

Some live far apart in the windowless terraced stacks whose hexagonal pores breathe open and shut. They inhabit rooms insulated against the city’s roar, rooms lined with pressure membranes and acoustic fur, rooms where conversation can still happen in air. Their bodies may be ordinary beneath their robes and rain-canceling fields, or they may have become pale, elongated, and over-sensitized from generations of filtered amber dimness. They appear on balconies at distances that make them symbolic: one figure standing still in a robe that sheds rain sideways; three figures crossing a causeway inside a transparent bubble; a hand visible briefly through a breathing wall; someone kneeling to hang a charm from a condenser tree before withdrawing behind a dry threshold.

They do not occupy the city densely because density may be dangerous here. Too many bodies together might disturb local law, attract animal attention, overload acoustic shelter, or wake whatever listens from the pools. Their etiquette is spatial. Distance is politeness. Silence is achieved technologically and treated as luxury. To visit someone may require half a day of navigation, six permissions, two weather changes, and a throat implant tuned to a shared band. An address is not “where” but “when the place agrees to be reached.”

Some citizens have abandoned visible scale. They inhabit folded volumes inside the infrastructure: warm cavities behind transit membranes, pocket rooms larger within than without, datalattices suspended between municipal spines, droplets of stabilized probability under market roofs. Their civic presence appears as heat patterns, delays in transit screams, lights that brighten when looked at, kiosks dispensing exactly the ration no one requested aloud. They may have bodies stored elsewhere, or no bodies, or bodies distributed through maintenance swarms and tree-root current. To them the open fernlands may be like oceans viewed from orbit: necessary, beautiful, uninhabitable except by instrument.

Some are post-human administrators, though not rulers in any simple sense. They negotiate with vegetation, hydrology, animal movement, construction swarms, acoustic fields, and the speaking pools. Their work is less command than diplomacy. They adjust scent algorithms to suppress predation near nurseries and let it resume beyond the border. They approve which vines may cross a gantry. They assign black crabs temporary shaft rights. They decide whether a road’s five-centimeter migration constitutes drift, desire, or sabotage. They issue temporary maps knowing the maps will be lies by dawn.

Some are pilgrims or specialists who come to the central basin when the pools speak. They arrive sealed in layered suits, or barefoot with implanted balance organs, or as remote bodies grown for the visit and discarded afterward. Around the black pools they take positions on low platforms and root-covered towers. They carry instruments that look like prayer frames. When the tone begins below hearing, they brace, weep, record, translate, or open their chests to it through surgical valves. Some believe the pools are communicating with the weather. Some believe they are digesting the city’s errors. Some believe the pools are the true inhabitants and everyone else is scaffolding around their song.

The child-shaped machines may be the most openly social beings in the city. They sit beside pools, under bridges, at ration kiosks, in reed plazas, teaching frogs to sing, instructing pollinators to recognize safe blooms, reciting transit changes to animals that cannot understand but like the cadence. They are built at the scale of trust. Their faces are simple and always slightly damp. Some carry lesson-lanterns. Some have voices that cut through machine roar without becoming loud. Animals tolerate them. Hidden citizens send them errands. They may be tutors, survey devices, memorial vessels, or descendants of a species that chose innocence as its most durable form.

There are also ceremonies, which may be inhabitants of a kind.

Objects hang from condenser trees: ribbons, shells, printed bones, cracked acoustic filters, mineral tablets, braided vine, feathers with metal edges, translucent masks filled with rainwater. They mark births, repairs, successful crossings, deaths, agreements with animals, apologies to districts, warnings to weather, and anniversaries of structural failure. Because bodies are rare, ceremony does much of the visible living. A charm swinging in the wet heat may be the only sign that an entire family occupies the folded space behind a wall, or that a person once crossed a probability bridge and returned one step older than expected.

The city itself may also be alive, though not in a single-bodied way. It drinks rain through walls and cistern mouths. It sweats vapor from pylons. It heals. It grows foundations by persuading young ground to remember another shape. It changes its plan by centimeters and seasons. It lets roads shift, groves sink, markets rise, borders migrate with moisture. It cultivates lushness not as decoration but as lung, archive, camouflage, cooling system, conscience, and treaty partner. Its noise is not incidental; it is metabolism, defense, speech, echolocation, argument.

What lives there, finally, is an unfinished compact.

Animal hunger lives there, not eradicated but zoned, scented, delayed, briefly forbidden, then released.

Water lives there as a scarce citizen: harvested, lifted, pooled, sung into rain, caught in black basins, walked through by fish, worn as vapor by flowers.

Plants live there as infrastructure and insurgency.

Machines live there as weather, digestion, repair, memory, and etiquette.

People live there lightly, remotely, carefully, as though aware they are not the majority presence and perhaps never were meant to be.

And beneath or within all of them live the black pools, speaking every few hours in tones that flatten leaves and make the air visible. Whatever they are, the rest of the city answers. Pylons ring in sequence. Towers hum. Animals flee or freeze. Rain follows somewhere.

So the inhabitants are not simply creatures in a city. They are participants in a loud, damp, amber-lit negotiation over how matter, law, appetite, distance, and weather can share a plain without destroying one another too quickly. The treaty is alive. The treaty is hungry. The treaty is still being argued.
