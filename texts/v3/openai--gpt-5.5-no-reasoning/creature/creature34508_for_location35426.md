What lives there is not one species, not quite a people and not quite a forest, but a compact of both: a society braided into an organism old enough that the distinction has worn thin.

They are human, or descended from humans closely enough that the word still clings to them, though the dark has revised them as thoroughly as drought revised the plants.

They are narrow people, not because they starve exactly, but because excess has no place in them. Their bodies keep what they are given. Their shoulders are sloped and careful, their ribs prominent under clay-dusted wrappings, their limbs long from moving by reach and balance rather than by sight. Their feet are broad-soled, toughened, articulate, almost handlike in their knowledge of surfaces. A child can tell a public avenue from a family passage by the pressure pattern under one heel.

Their skin has lost any use for sun. It is not pale in the way of illness, but ash-colored, wax-brown, grey-gold, sometimes with a faint violet undertone from resins rubbed into it since infancy. They powder themselves with salt-clay, not for decoration alone but to hold the body in, to discourage evaporation, to keep the air from stealing too much. Along their cheeks and forearms the powder gathers in creases, making them look older than they are. Nobody here shines unless ill.

Their eyes remain, but they are not central. Some are filmed; some are large and dark and nearly useless; some people keep them covered with soft bark cloth because dust scratches them and there is nothing to see worth such pain. A bare gaze is intimate, almost indecent, because it gives so little and asks so much. Faces are known instead by breath, jaw-click, pulse at the wrist, the angle of a palm approaching in greeting.

Their ears are beautiful by local standards: finely trained, strong, expressive. Many have ears subtly cupped by bindings worn in childhood, not enough to deform grotesquely, only enough to help locate echoes. The old can identify a person three streets away by the way their footfall interrupts the city’s drone. Infants are tested not by showing them colors, but by sounding five tones through a root tube and seeing which way their fingers turn.

They do not speak much.

Speech spends water. It stirs dust. It can disturb spores, wake membranes, frighten workers inside resonant walls. Their language is therefore small aloud and immense in contact. It has words, but also taps, scratches, breath pulses, knuckle codes, shared hums, pressure phrases. Two people bargaining may look, to an outsider, as if they are simply holding each other’s forearms and occasionally clicking their tongues. In that contact they are naming quantities, lineage, risk, insult, apology, and the remembered quality of a resin harvest six years past.

Their voices, when used fully, are low and dry. They do not shout unless something is already lost.

Children are born into sound before they are born into society. A pregnant person sleeps with their back against particular walls so the unborn child learns the city’s baseline tones. After birth, a child is carried through the main chambers in a ritual of orientation: forehead to Bell Trunk, sole to paving, palm to listening wall, ear to vent. The city is introduced as kin before any human lineage is recited.

They grow up slowly, by necessity and custom. A child is not permitted to walk freely until they can stop still at a change in draft. They are not given cutting tools until they can identify living grain from dead grain by touch. Their first toys are thorn pieces, hollow stems, knots in cord, little bark tablets carved with false doors and safe vents. They play at getting lost in mapped rooms. They play at being wind. They play funeral, binding one another upright in leaf felt and whispering, “Hold well.”

Their adults are custodians more than citizens.

Almost every task is a form of tending. There are vent-keepers who sleep lightly and wake when the city’s pitch alters. There are bark-readers who press their faces to walls and diagnose stress by scent. There are dust-brushers who carry trays of powdered mineral through the Dry Garden and feed plants that will not visibly thank them for fifty years. There are hinge-growers, corridor-trainers, thornwrights, spore-guides, burial binders, memory-knot makers, breath-law teachers.

The most honored profession is not ruler or warrior, because war has little purchase in a place where damage lasts longer than victory. The most honored are those who know when not to act.

They are called, perhaps, the Waiters, though their own word would mean something closer to “those who leave the next hand possible.” They decide whether a cut may be made, whether a wall may be harvested, whether a chamber has died enough to become material, whether a child’s petition to open an old passage is curiosity or sacrilege. Their authority is practical and botanical. They are obeyed because people who disobey them are remembered in collapsed roofs, spoiled resin, and districts sealed before their time.

Their politics are slow. Councils may last years, not because they meet continually, but because the matter under discussion must be listened to across seasons of wind. A proposed new sleeping comb is marked by cords. People live around the marking. The wall is tapped monthly. Children put their ears to it and are asked what they hear, because children often detect changes adults have normalized. Only after the plant itself thickens, resists, or accepts the bindings is the plan considered approved.

They have no armies. They have wardens of error.

A warden’s task is to stop panic, prevent waste, and restrain anyone carrying fire or water-dreams too far into action. They are trained to move silently across root ridges and to bind wrists without breaking skin. They carry ceramic knives with dull backs, resin plugs for sealing accidental wounds in living walls, and dust masks for entering spore chambers. They are feared less than pitied, because their work brings them closest to catastrophe.

Their food is austere and strange.

They eat what the city can surrender without becoming less itself: flakes of edible pith scraped from certain dead interiors, cakes of processed seed dust, resin-softened fiber, mineral pastes, fungal-looking growths that are not fungi but dry nodules of plant storage tissue, bitter oils diluted in powdered salts. They chew slowly to draw out every trace of nourishment. Meals are quiet and communal, often taken in darkness so complete that the act becomes less eating than listening to others survive beside you.

They drink almost nothing recognizable as drink.

Moisture is recovered from breath through cloth condensers in the coolest shafts. Bodies are trained from infancy to tolerate thirst that would seem unbearable elsewhere. Certain plant bladders produce a viscous secretion, not water but a bitter, oily fluid that can be metabolized with less loss. It is rationed with ceremonial seriousness. To spill it is a social wound. To waste it deliberately is nearly murder.

They do not cry easily, and when grief comes their tears are often dry gestures: palms pressed to eye sockets, a low hum held until the throat aches, salt powder rubbed across the brow. The elders who press their ears to the floor and “weep dryly” do so with breath and posture, not liquid. Sorrow is exhaled, not shed.

Their religion, if separated from law and horticulture, centers on continuity without assurance. They do not pray for rain. Rain would be apocalypse, rot, swelling, dissolution. They do not pray for rescue. Rescue implies outside abundance, and abundance is morally suspect. They pray, if the word applies, by maintaining passages, by not cutting too deeply, by carrying dust to the Dry Garden, by memorizing tones that might change only once in a lifetime.

Their gods are mostly former necessities.

There is the First Vent, who is not worshiped as a being but acknowledged as the opening by which breath entered. There is the Hollow Below, where air departs and no one descends. There is the Unrotting Dead, ancestor-walls who bear weight. There is the Seed-City, the possibility that every chamber and law is part of some larger fruiting. There is the Forbidden Solvent, water, invoked only in controlled speech as a warning against softness, speed, swelling, forgetting boundaries.

Their taboos have teeth. A child who casually invents a splash-sound in play will be hushed sharply, then taught why the sound is ugly. Adults do dream of water sometimes. They wake from it ashamed, thirsty, and shaken. There are private confessions for such dreams, not because the dreamer sinned, but because longing can become behavior if left unnamed.

Their art is everywhere, though invisible.

They carve the undersides of handrails with jokes that only those tall enough, or old enough, can find. They compose routes: a mourning path that takes exactly one hundred and eight left-hand textures from sleeping cell to burial niche; a marriage path where two people walk separate corridors and meet by following each other’s tapping through a shared wall; a children’s path with deliberate harmless surprises—soft pith, warm draft, sudden echo, smooth dome.

They make music by joining the city rather than overpowering it. Their instruments are extensions of plant architecture: reedless pipes made from hollow thorns, chest boards that amplify humming, ankle-clicks of carved resin, palm drums that must be played softly against living bark. The greatest performances occur when no human begins them. The Bell Trunk lowers, the roof pores whistle, the western vents start their thin trembling, and people arrive one by one to add breath until the city seems to be using them as additional chambers.

Their masks are not visual faces but acoustic tools. A festival mask may lengthen the nose into a dry tube, split the breath into two tones, roughen the exhale, or make the wearer sound like a distant vent. Masks are touched before worn. Their beauty lies in the promise of what the wearer will become in the ear.

They marry, though marriage is less possession than coordinated stewardship. Two or more adults may bind households by taking responsibility for the same section of wall, the same sleeping comb, the same lineage of thorn shrubs. Affection is tactile, practical, unspectacular: repairing another’s foot wrappings, sharing the less bitter portion of pith, guiding a tired hand to the correct ridge in darkness. Lovers know one another by breath rhythm at sleep and by the warmth left in bark after leaning.

Their dead do not leave.

This changes everything. Grief is not abandonment but relocation. A body wrapped and set into the western wall becomes part of the city’s future acoustics, insulation, mineral life. Families visit not graves but load-bearing presences. They press their backs to ancestor panels during difficult decisions. They ask, not “Are you at peace?” but “Do you hold?” A good death is one that can be incorporated cleanly. A bad death is wet, burned, lost outside, or too damaged to bind.

They have stories of the outside, but those stories are impoverished in image and rich in warning. The salt plain is known as the White Weight, the Scouring Roof, the Above Surface. It is a place where space is too open, where sound escapes before answering, where heat can strike from nowhere, where the ground has no grain to read. Those few who go out do so wrapped, masked, roped, and timed by wind. They return with mineral dust, old stone, perhaps shards from forgotten human worlds. They do not describe horizons well. The idea of distance without walls troubles them.

Some among them are scouts of fissures. These are odd people, tolerated and needed, addicted perhaps to the dangerous thin breath of the entrance ways. Their skin is more cracked, their eyes more damaged, their sense of the city subtly altered by having known even a little exterior space. They bring news of erosion: a widening seam, a new salt fall, a blocked draft. They are treated like those who handle corpses or flame—necessary, contaminated by knowledge.

There may also be another kind of inhabitant: the city itself, thinking too slowly to be called a mind until one has lived inside its decisions.

It reacts. It remembers pressure. It thickens around repeated touch. It offers hollows where generations have leaned. It seals abandoned rooms with a patience indistinguishable from intention. It grows thorns where careless hands harvest too often. It opens spore mouths after certain chords. It may have shaped the people as much as they shaped it, selecting for quiet bodies, low thirst, reverent hands, ears able to serve as civic organs.

If the plant-city has a consciousness, it is distributed through tension, sapless chemistry, resin, vibration, and architecture. It does not know a person as a face. It knows recurring warmth, salt trace, abrasion, breath-acid, tapping patterns. It knows lineages as paths of contact. The dead are not memories to it but altered mineral availability, stiffened reinforcement, changed resonance. The living are mobile weather.

The people themselves suspect this but disagree about its meaning.

Some say the city loves them, because it shelters them.

Some say the city uses them, because they prune, pollinate, carry dust, sing spores loose, and eventually feed the walls.

Some say love and use are outside words from wetter minds.

A few heretics believe the city is preparing to leave. They study the Dry Garden’s armored capsules, the long tones traveling through the mineral beds, the way certain towers aim their hollows toward cracks above. They think the whole settlement is a seed vessel awaiting a pressure, a quake, a century of correct dryness, perhaps the final collapse of the roof. In that event, the people may be part of the dispersal mechanism: carriers, fuel, husk, or song.

Others believe there are sister cities under the salt, and that the low unexplained tremors are not geology but speech. These believers lie flat for hours with their ears to the paving, hands spread, ribs aching, trying to separate message from wind. They memorize patterns too slow to hold in ordinary rhythm. Whole schools of interpretation have grown around pauses lasting months.

The inhabitants are patient, but not serene. Patience here is not calmness; it is discipline under permanent impossibility. They know irritation, envy, desire, boredom, cruelty. A person may resent a wall for needing care. A family may hoard resin. A young worker may long to cut a new door now, not in twelve years. Someone may hate the law forbidding flame and dream of a room lit gold just once. Someone may whisper water repeatedly in the Silent Ward until the word loses meaning and becomes temptation.

Their crimes are crimes of haste.

Cutting green grain for private use. Blocking a vent to warm a chamber. Speaking loudly during spore drift. Wasting breath to frighten children. Burning without sanction. Damaging ancestor panels. Falsely marking deadwood as harvestable. Bringing outside objects into the Dry Garden without dusting them clean. Pouring precious condensate onto a root to see what happens.

Punishments are restorative when possible and terrifying when not. A careless cutter may spend years tending the wound they made. A vent-blocker may be assigned to the coldest draft post. A burner may be forbidden from touching resin, which is nearly social exile. The worst offenders are not expelled upward into the killing plain unless the city itself cannot bear their presence. More often they are confined to marginal wards, where they live close to the sealing dark and hear the future closing in.

They are not miserable in any simple sense.

They laugh, but softly. Their humor is tactile and dry: a misleadingly grand carved label on a tiny storage hollow; a ceremonial mask that makes a respected elder honk like a cracked vent; affectionate insults tapped into the backs of hands. They delight in a perfectly cured resin scale, a child’s first accurate map recitation, the rare moment when two distant chambers answer each other in fifths. They take pleasure in competence. They admire restraint. They love textures the way surface peoples love color.

To them, the city is not bleak. It is intricate, inhabited, responsive, full of differences. One corridor smells faintly of pepper; another of old rope. One wall is trustworthy in winter and moody in pressure drops. One plaza makes every hum sound larger than the singer deserves. One sleeping cell holds warmth with the tenderness of an animal they have never known.

What lives there, then, is a dry-adapted remnant people inside a dry-adapted remnant forest; or a forest that has domesticated its last people; or a single ancient organism wearing human custodians as moving parts.

They are the palm-readers of bark, the breath-savers, the thorn-growers, the listeners at load-bearing dead. They are citizens of absence, farmers of delay, descendants of thirst. They live without light and almost without water, but not without beauty, law, kinship, danger, music, or longing.

They do not look up when the city grows.

They stop.

They put one hand to the nearest wall, one foot flat against the polished root-skin, and they listen as the whole dark place alters by less than a hair.

In that sound is weather, ancestry, permission, warning, and tomorrow.
