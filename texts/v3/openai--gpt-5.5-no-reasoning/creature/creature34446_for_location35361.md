What lives there is not a people first, not an animal kingdom first, not even a forest in the old sense. What lives there is a continent-sized metabolism, and everything else is either a symptom, parasite, instrument, grazer, courier, wound-cleaner, pollinator, trespasser, or temporary thought inside it.

The reigning life is the plant crust itself.

It is not one organism, though some travelers might make that mistake when the ground answers miles apart with the same pressure-throb. It is a federation of ancient lineages fused by root, chemical treaty, theft, parasitism, and exchange. A dry green nation without borders. Its members overlap so completely that individuality becomes a seasonal condition: a tower-plant may live alone for three centuries, then flower into a basin-wide reproductive net and spend the rest of its existence as root infrastructure for younger things. A photosynthetic plain may be one individual stretched over leagues, or a thousand plates locked edge to edge, or dead tissue being farmed from below by filaments too fine to see.

The dominant plants are architectures more than shapes.

There are vent columns: immense fluted stems rising from the high flats like organs of respiration. They have no crowns in the familiar sense, only slitted sides, heat-blackened mouths, and ribs packed with pressure chambers. Inside them resinous sap is cracked, vaporized, condensed, and driven through nested tubes. At dawn they stand silent, hoarfrost glittering along their seams. By afternoon they beat and cough like furnace stacks. Around their bases live entire communities—grazers licking condensed mineral films, gliding animals nesting in warm exhaust, fungal-looking dry symbionts that are not fungi but spore-plants specialized to feed on vapor.

There are shield-leaves wide as districts, flattened against the world. Their upper surfaces are waxed, dark, and reactive, shifting from green-black to violet-gold as they rotate their pigment bodies under the sun. Their shed skins make the pale brittle plains that resemble salt from afar. Beneath each shield is a honeycomb of old vascular chambers, some dead and empty, some still dangerous with sleeping pressure. Animals shelter there, but only briefly. Stay too long and root-hairs descend, tasting breath, salt, skin oil.

There are root-basalt forests, where roots have become stone-like without ceasing to grow. Their arches span valleys of their own making. In some places the roots braid into elevated roads polished by dust storms and animal passage. In others they dive into the substrate and return bearing metal salts, volcanic bitterness, old heat. When they crack, the fissures glow faintly green inside, not with softness but with a hard, wet-looking life that almost never sees air.

There are turbine flowers. They are reproductive organs engineered by wind and impatience: rigid vanes, toothed rims, flexible stalk-bearings, pollen chambers locked behind pressure membranes. When the seasonal gradients align, whole fields of them stand up from the flats and begin to turn. Their screaming is a mating call, a dispersal mechanism, and a warning. Anything too close may be sanded open by pollen, seed shot, or the vanes themselves when they shear loose and go cartwheeling across the plateau for kilometers.

There are hammer groves: jointed stems that rise and fall in dreadful synchrony, driving root-spikes into buried mineral layers. They are among the loudest inhabitants of the continent. Their rhythm is not mechanical imitation but appetite expressed as percussion. Herd animals follow them at a safe distance because each cycle brings up dust rich in salts; predators follow the herds; scavengers follow the predators; tiny glass-bodied insects follow everyone.

There are black annexing walls, slow colonial roots that advance across older flats by night. From a distance they look like low burned ridges. Close up, they are millions of hooked tips crawling over the surface, testing, fastening, dissolving. They do not chase, but they arrive. Anything unable to move for a night is measured by them. Anything remaining for two nights is entered. By the third, it is part of the frontier.

There are also brief plants, violent plants, festival plants. Basins that sleep for decades under husk dunes may one day receive the correct pollen charge and erupt into a single afternoon of color: red lures, blue conductive threads, yellow seed smoke, white explosive calyces. By nightfall the whole basin is a ruin of dry ribs. But the ruin is also the seed-machine’s emptied body, and beneath it something has already begun to eat the architecture back into the continent.

The plants here are intelligent only if intelligence can be slow, distributed, and unconcerned with faces.

They remember in chemistry. They decide in pressure. They warn one another through static fields, scent fronts, root knocks, and the movement of shade. A distant fire may cause vent towers fifty kilometers away to close their slits and flood their skins with flame-breaking vapor. A predator migration may be answered by bittering the rind along expected paths. A pollen storm may be invited, rejected, filtered, or weaponized. The continent has no single mind, but it has moods large enough to be weather.

Among this vast plant machinery live animals made for dryness, impact, and signal.

Most are armored lightly but cleverly: chitin like thorn, bone like ceramic, skin like parchment, membranes coated in dust-shedding oils. Water is too rare to build soft abundance. Bodies conserve, distill, reclaim. Breath passes through labyrinths that condense internal vapor. Blood, where it exists, is syrupy and mineral-rich. Many creatures excrete crystals, flakes, or dry pellets that plants eagerly harvest for salts. Death does not rot here; it is unstitched, shaved, powdered, drunk by roots in the form of chemistry.

The smallest animals are everywhere once the eye learns their scale.

Click mites live on vibrating stems and speak by amplification. They gather in black constellations along resonant ridges, each individual no larger than a seed burr, each with forelimbs shaped for drumming. A swarm can turn a hollow reed into a broadcast tower. They announce frost, predators, pollen toxicity, mating readiness, and the opening of sap-cracks. Larger animals listen. Some plants listen too, or at least have learned that certain clicking patterns precede useful events.

Glass-thorn insects move in flashes. Their bodies are angular and translucent, with internal organs hidden behind mirrored panels that scatter the brutal light. When still, they look like shed plant spines. When airborne, they glitter like splinters. Many feed on pollen, but some have become pollen thieves, piercing reproductive vents before they open. Others live inside seed pods and time their emergence to the pod’s detonation, launching themselves with the seeds into the wind.

There are static fleas that ride husk storms. They cling by charge rather than claw, leaping between airborne particles. During blue pollen weather they glow faintly, tiny sparks traveling through the cloud. Animals caught in such storms may emerge coated in them, jittering and frantic as the fleas drink trace salts from eye rims, nostrils, wounds, and mating glands.

Under bark-shelves live pale raspers, flat creatures with mouths like files. They scrape edible films from the underside of leaf-plates. Their young are translucent and blind; their adults grow mineral masks shaped by whatever surface they fed upon while maturing. Colonies of them can be heard at night as a dry whispering, like sand poured over bone.

The larger grazers are built low, long, and separated from themselves.

Many have six or eight legs so no single stumble in a shifting husk dune becomes fatal. Their feet spread into gripping pads on waxed flats and contract into points on ceramic ground. Their heads are narrow wedges with beaks capable of shaving rind from living plates without being glued shut by resin. Their tongues are dark, tough, and chemically discerning. They can taste whether a sap-crack is food, lure, poison, or sleeping mouth.

Some are solitary because the wind punishes herds. Others travel in loose lines across the plateaus, each animal keeping tens of body-lengths from the next. They communicate by flank flashes, tail rattles, and foot percussion transmitted through the living ground. Calves learn early never to stand in another’s wind shadow during pollen weather; the turbulence there can dump enough seed dust to bury them standing.

One common browser, if anything here can be called common, is the ridge-shaver: a lean, high-shouldered animal with a ceramic beak, four walking limbs, two smaller grasping limbs folded under the chest, and a back plated in overlapping mineral scales. It feeds on old photosynthetic rind, especially after frost, when surface tissues soften microscopically. Its gut hosts dry fermenters that break wax and lignin into usable sugars. It smells of dust, sour resin, and hot stone. Predators find it difficult to kill, but once overturned it is almost helpless; scavengers listen for the ringing crash of one falling on hard ground.

There are burrowers too, though they do not dig soil. They chew dead root layers.

Pale auger-beasts tunnel through the older lignin strata beneath the flats, grinding dry fiber with rotating tooth plates. They are blind except to heat and pressure. Their bodies are segmented, muscular, and dust-colored, with vents along the sides to release powdered waste. Their tunnels aerate the crust and are quickly colonized by rootlets, mites, and small hunters. After pressure pulses, auger-beasts come near the surface to feed on newly cracked tissue. Predators wait above, feeling for the tremor. Plants wait too, sometimes collapsing old chambers behind them, not maliciously, but because the burrower’s passage has made a convenient channel for growth.

The sky has its own fauna.

Membrane-backed gliders pour through updrafts in ragged flocks, their bodies light as husk and their wings reinforced with black spars. They do not flap often. They tack through heat columns, fold during static bursts, and skim pollen fronts to feed on suspended grains. Their calls are thin, metallic, and easily shredded by wind. When they breed, they fasten egg ribbons to the lee sides of vent towers, where warm chemical breath keeps the embryos from freezing before dawn.

Above them hang the long-winged predators. They are narrow, severe animals, more blade than bird, with locking wings and heads shielded by transparent nictitating plates against dust. They hunt by waiting in thermals until movement below aligns with open ground. Then they fall almost vertically, closing their wings at the last instant and striking with talons hardened by mineral diet. Some impale prey and let the wind tear it against the ground. Others carry smaller animals to root arches and wedge them in cracks, returning after desiccation has made the meal lighter and richer.

There are kite-serpents, too: limbless or near-limbless aerial animals with rib membranes that unfurl into long diamond sails. They breed in turbulence that would kill other creatures. A kite-serpent’s young begin life as parasitic threads inside turbine flowers, feeding on pollen oils; when the flower spins itself to pieces, the young are flung into the sky already unfolding.

The scavengers are meticulous.

Nothing dead remains whole. Hook-jawed skitterers arrive first, cutting joints and soft membranes. Dust beetles drink the last internal vapors through capillary tongues. Salt-crows—neither crows nor birds, but black, hopping gliders with white mineral masks—fight over eyes, glands, and gut stones. Root-filaments arrive last or first depending on where death occurs. They smell exposed electrolyte. They rise from cracks as pale threads and lace the corpse to the ground, not fast enough to catch the living unless the living sleeps, but fast enough to make ownership clear.

Some animals do not eat plants or flesh directly. They eat events.

Frost-lickers wake before dawn and climb to the highest ridges, where silver condensation forms for minutes only. They are small, frantic, beautiful creatures with oversized tongues fringed like brushes. Their bodies are dark to gather the first hint of light, but their faces are pale with frost dust. They defend good edges with squeals and stabbing wrist-spines. Many die there—taken by dawn hunters, stunned by sudden cold, or trapped when a leaf-plate tilts to harvest the frost beneath them.

Pollen-drunk swarms hatch only after charged storms. Their eggs lie dormant in bark seams until blue pollen conducts enough static through the crust. Then the larvae emerge all at once, feed, pupate, mate, and die within a day. Their adult bodies are little more than wings, reproductive organs, and chemical sensors. During outbreaks the air thickens with them, and predators gorge until they can barely fly.

Heat-seam grazers appear where vent towers have opened new cracks. They lick condensed volatiles and microbial films from warm stone-like root. Their horns are not for combat but grounding; they press them against conductive veins during electrical weather, shunting charge through specialized plates in their skulls. Old individuals bear branching burn scars like maps.

There are symbioses so tight they seem like customs.

Certain gliders carry pollen from dangerous red territories because their wing oils neutralize the territorial compounds. In return, red-flowered basins produce edible seed bladders shaped exactly for their beaks.

Ridge-shavers avoid young black annexing roots, but old individuals sometimes deliberately scratch themselves on the root tips. The plants drink blood-salts; the grazers receive bitter resin that repels static fleas.

Click mites farm resonant lichen-like crusts on hollow stems. They keep the crusts clean of competing dust. The crusts alter the pitch of the stem, making mite signals travel farther.

Predatory long-wings nest only in dead turbine frames. Their droppings enrich the reproductive basins. When nesting season ends, the plant tissue beneath the frame often wakes and consumes the lower struts, folding the abandoned nest into new growth.

Even fear here has ecological uses. Animals fleeing a chemical inhalation zone churn the ground, dislodging husks and exposing receptive skin. Herd paths polish safe ceramic surfaces that spores then use as runways. Predators keep grazers moving, preventing them from over-shaving young rind. Scavengers open bodies; roots finish them.

There may be stranger inhabitants hidden within the plant continent’s older layers.

Deep in hollow stems large enough to contain weather, blind communities live by warmth, salt, and falling dust. Their creatures are pallid, soft by local standards, and dangerously dependent on the breathing cycles of their host towers. Some never see the sky. They climb internal ridges of cellulose, drink condensed chemical films, and flee the periodic purge-winds that roar through the shafts.

In root caverns beneath ancient shield-plains, there are slow animals plated with dead bark, almost indistinguishable from debris. They wait near sap arteries for years, extending hairlike feeding limbs whenever pressure drops and crystals form. Their metabolisms are so slow that mites build colonies on them, and plants sometimes incorporate their outer plates before the animal inside has died.

There are colonial things that blur plant and animal categories: motile seed mats that crawl toward heat; thorn clusters that unfold legs during drought extremes; pollinator bodies grown by plants from their own tissues and released for a single migration before dissolving into reproductive dust. Some “animals” may be temporary organs of plants. Some plants may once have been animals that surrendered movement for root access and light.

If there are minds here in the sharper, quicker sense, they would not build cities of stone or wood. They would read wind.

A tool-using creature in this place would have to be light, patient, and chemically literate. It would carry no water openly. It would live in the lee of root arches, in old vent hollows during quiet cycles, or on moving scaffold-colonies that never remain long enough to be claimed. Its technologies would be braided fiber, resin sealant, static grounding rods, scent masks, ceramic scrapers, glider membranes, maps made not of place but of seasonal smell and safe sound. It would know that a sweet blue haze means crouch low and discharge metal; that a lull in a basin means leave; that white dunes are crossed only with hooked shoes and no loose fur; that the safest road is the one that rings.

But if such people exist, they are not masters of this continent. They are listeners in the machinery.

The true inhabitants are the processes.

Pollen fronts live there, behaving almost like migratory herds. They rise from basins in walls, sort themselves by charge and grain weight, collide, mate, poison, nourish, and settle. A red front may claim territory, staining every surface and driving grazers away. A gold front may feed half the sky. A blue front may turn the air into a circuit, inviting lightning that writes branching scars across the landscape.

Resin fires live there. Blue, low, almost smokeless, moving along veins rather than over surfaces. They burn what is ready to be burned and trigger what is ready to open. Animals flee them, follow them, or use them. Some seeds will not germinate until kissed by that heat. Some predators hunt by driving prey toward active veins. Some plants exhale counter-gases in pale sheets, smothering the fire at borders known only to their chemistry.

Wind lives there as a partner rather than background. Plants grow corridors for it, instruments for it, traps and runways and mills. Animals are shaped by it from embryo to elder. A calm day would be ecological crisis. Sound, scent, reproduction, navigation, predation, cooling, dispersal—all require the moving air. The continent does not merely endure wind; it farms it.

Dust lives there too: mineral dust, bark dust, seed dust, old climate ground into powder. It feeds leaf skins with trace metals. It abrades dead tissue from living tissue. It paints animals until they vanish. It stores charge. It becomes dunes, storms, veils, armor, burial. Every creature has a relationship with dust: shedding it, eating it, hiding beneath it, filtering it, weaponizing it.

And beneath everything lives the old hunger of the plants.

Not thirst. Thirst would imply an expectation of rain, a wound shaped like water. These beings have passed beyond that. Their hunger is for light, salts, motion, carbon, charge, frost, volatile traces, exposed tissue, timing. They do not dream of rivers. They dream, if they dream, of pressure gradients and open sky. Their abundance is not lushness but command: the ability to turn absence into structure.

So the place is inhabited by dry giants and their sparks.

By root continents with mineral bones.

By venting towers that breathe like engines.

By leaves broad enough to become weather maps.

By flowers that scream themselves fertile.

By insects like broken glass.

By grazers with ceramic mouths.

By burrowers that eat ancient wood as if mining coal.

By gliders born from updraft.

By predators that fall out of white light.

By frost thieves, charge drinkers, salt lickers, bark raspers, corpse unmakers.

By pollen that travels like armies.

By fire that prunes.

By wind given corridors and voices.

Nothing there lives gently, but much lives well. Its creatures are not adaptations to scarcity so much as citizens of another economy, one where water has been removed from the center and replaced by air, light, chemistry, and force. They are lean, loud, bright-edged, and exact. They waste little softness. They make music accidentally by surviving.

The continent itself is the largest resident: not a sleeping giant, because it never sleeps; not a machine, because it was never made; not a forest, because it has become ground, industry, climate, archive, and appetite all at once.

Everything else lives on it, in it, from it, against it, and briefly.
