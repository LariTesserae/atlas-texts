What lives there is not one people, exactly, and not one species in any simple sense. The place is too old for clean categories. Anything that has survived in it has been altered by height, damp, patient danger, and the necessity of making bargains with growth.

The primary inhabitants are a canopy people: light-boned, quiet-moving, and intensely attentive. They are built for tension more than speed. Their feet are broad and clever, with long grasping toes hardened by polished bark. Their hands are narrow, strong, almost delicate, marked by scars from fiber cuts, thorn punctures, and sap burns. Their shoulders and hips are loose in their sockets, giving them a hanging ease; they can rest from one hooked knee, sleep curled inside a sling, or move along the underside of a bridge as naturally as across its top.

They are not tall in the open-ground sense. Height would be wasteful here. Most are compact, corded, and flexible, with long forearms and deep chests adapted to wet air and climbing labor. Children cling before they walk. The old often become almost vine-like themselves: sinewy, bent, fingers permanently curved as if around a rail.

Their skin ranges through bark-browns, olive grays, damp umbers, greenish undertones in some lineages, and pale fungal creams in those whose families dwell deeper under the canopy. It is common for skin to be patterned: faint mottling along the back and limbs like lichen, darker lines at joints, freckles clustered like spores. Whether this began as adaptation, adornment, or inheritance from older bargains with the forest is no longer clear.

Their hair is usually worn bound close, braided with seed-threads, scent knots, and bits of living moss. Loose hair is dangerous around tendrils, hooks, thirsty flowers, and pulley-vines. Some shave one side of the head to show clan graft marks: raised scars made in childhood with plant resins that heal into ridges, curls, dots, or leaf-like whorls. Others train tiny symbiotic lichens in the hair, not as decoration alone but as identifiers; a person’s home district may be known by the smell of their braids after rain.

Their eyes are large, dark-adapted, and patient. They do not stare the way ground people might. They glance, pause, listen, let peripheral motion speak. A direct gaze is used carefully: for teaching, warning, courtship, ritual, or accusation. Much communication happens through hand signs, pressure on shared ropes, low tongue-clicks, breath sounds, and small changes in posture. Spoken language exists, but it is not wasted. The air swallows words, and the wrong word in the wrong hollow may travel strangely.

They dress in woven fiber, cured leaf, softened barkcloth, glider membrane, and fungal felt. Clothing is close-fitting where snagging is dangerous and layered where moisture or insects are persistent. A common garment is a wrap-harness: part clothing, part tool belt, part safety tether, with loops for knives, seed packets, hooks, line, grafting needles, water bladders, and small living containers. Children wear many tethers. Adults wear fewer, partly from skill and partly from pride.

They ornament themselves with things that do not fall easily: braided cuffs, embedded shell clamps, tattooed sap-lines, seed beads sewn flat against cloth, small copper pins reserved for marriages, mourning, or offices of care. A dangling ornament is either a sign of recklessness, ritual immunity, or bait.

They are gardeners, architects, climbers, hydrologists, singers, surgeons, and archivists of growth. Their chief art is not making objects but maintaining conditions. A master builder may never “build” anything in the crude sense; instead she persuades a dozen roots to meet over a span her grandchildren will use. A water-keeper may spend his life listening to cistern walls, tasting seepage, trimming inlet moss, and deciding which district may draw from which vein during a dry spell. A bridge-thinner removes living weight without killing structure. A shadow-pruner opens narrow paths for light to reach nursery terraces. A fall-catcher maintains nets, compost throats, and lower salvage gardens. All are necessary. None are low work.

Their settlements look empty until one learns how to see them. A village may occupy a vertical grove of living columns: sleeping pods grown into trunk hollows, kitchens of fermentation jars and warm stones, fungal light rooms, hanging infant cradles, seed libraries, rain courts, mourning platforms, and animal roosts. Most doorways are leaf curtains or sphincter-like openings trained to recognize pressure patterns, scent, or song. Privacy is created less by walls than by angle, height, and silence.

They do not divide nature from construction. A house is a relationship under maintenance. A bridge is a relative with dangerous moods. A cistern is both tool and elder. They speak of structures as if they have temperaments: a ladder may be vain, a roof jealous of sun, a platform too fond of weight, a corridor sleepy in fog. This is not mere metaphor. In a place where living tissue holds every life above the void, temperament matters.

Their religion, if named from outside, would seem like ancestor worship, ecology, engineering ethics, and weather practice braided together. The dead are not buried; there is little earth for that. Bodies are wrapped in spore cloth, placed in chosen decomposition chambers, or lowered to root mouths depending on lineage, offense, season, and need. To be absorbed well is a blessing. To fall unclaimed is ambiguous: feared, pitied, sometimes holy. The greatest ancestors are not imagined as ghosts wandering halls, but as thickness in the beams, sweetness in fruit, resilience in grafts, memory in cambium.

They carry seeds always. This is law, courtesy, and prayer. A person without seeds is either destitute, exiled, cursed, or undertaking some severe vow. Seed packets are worn close to the body to keep them warm and scented with the bearer. Children receive their first seeds before their first knives. Lovers exchange seeds from home structures. The dying choose which seeds should be tied into their wrappings.

They fear fire with a depth that is difficult to exaggerate. Flame is not merely destructive; it is socially obscene, like murder let loose without aim. Ember pits exist, guarded by specialists who are treated with a mixture of respect and unease. These fire-keepers live somewhat apart, in damp chambers lined with mineral paste traded from below or scraped from rare deposits. They know how to wake heat and smother it instantly. Their hands are scarred in ways others find both admirable and disturbing.

They eat carefully. Fruits, cultivated fungus, moss-cakes, fermented leaf paste, grubs, eggs, small fish from cistern pools, nectar curds, steamed root-pith, glider meat on rare occasions, and the milk of certain browsing animals kept on meadow rafts. Nothing is casually discarded. Peel, bone, feather, shell, hair, frass, spoiled mash: all is sorted by destination. Compost songs are among the first songs children learn, because a mistake in decay can poison a root or starve a terrace ten years later.

Their animals are semi-domestic rather than owned in the hard sense. Furred gliders nest near villages and carry scent tags or message knots. Long-legged seed crabs clean moss beds and are guided with food trails. Small soft-winged birds are encouraged to roost in certain rafters because they warn of pressure shifts. Slow armored pollinators are treated almost like visiting dignitaries; entire paths may close to let them pass. There are browsing climbers kept to trim aggressive growth, though they are stubborn and sometimes dangerous. Some families maintain lineages of ribbon-snakes that hunt wall-mites and sleep coiled in rafters.

Predators are not hated. They are negotiated with, avoided, named, sometimes fed at boundaries. A village that kills every ambush-creature near it may find itself overrun by borers, biting insects, or hungry flowers. Balance is practical before it is philosophical. Children are taught predator etiquette alongside knotwork: never run on a flexing floor, never slap a vine you have not identified, never bleed into dry moss, never answer a birdcall from below at night.

Because space is vertical and unreliable, their maps are living things. They use knotted cords, scent trails, trained leaves, pulse-vines that tighten in sequence, and fungal marks visible only under certain dampness. A dead map is distrusted. A carved sign can drift in meaning, location, or allegiance. But a plant trained by repeated touch remembers pattern better than wood remembers inscription.

Their sense of time is botanical. They count by bloomings, molts, wet cycles, bridge-thickenings, fruit returns, and fog tastes. A person may be described as “born during the second yellowing of the west cistern moss” or “old enough to remember when the Three-Root Span still heard footsteps.” History is kept in songs, scar patterns, pruning traditions, and graft lines. Some trees are archives: their bark is cut, healed, cut again in coded sequences read by specialists with wetted fingertips.

They have specialists for the strange portions of the world. Path-waiters know when a corridor has not finished deciding where it goes. Reflection-diviners read the Mirror Swales but do so veiled, so their own reflected selves cannot easily meet their eyes. Sleep-crossers train from childhood to traverse bridges that reject intention. Flower-speakers whisper into old trunk tunnels and wait days or months for an answer to bloom elsewhere. These people are not considered magicians in the theatrical sense. They are more like weather experts, plumbers, midwives, and diplomats assigned to reality’s softened places.

They are cautious with identity. In a world where echoes may emerge from flowers and reflections may act with appetite, names are layered. One has a child-name, a household pressure-name, a public name, a seed-name, and perhaps a hidden fall-name known only to those who would retrieve or mourn the body. Calling someone too completely in fog is rude and risky.

Their children are astonishingly free and closely watched. They run nets, climb underside routes, sneak into cistern gardens, dare each other to touch sleeping tendrils, and learn early which hollows return sound strangely. Childhood games are rehearsals for survival: knot races, silent crossings, fruit-catching, “dead vine or snake,” balancing in mist, pretending not to intend to cross forbidden planks. A child who falls and is caught by a safety net is not scolded immediately. First they are thanked for not being lost. Then, later, everyone involved examines which habit failed.

Their elders may gradually withdraw into maintenance roles. Some become stationary by choice or infirmity, dwelling in warm trunk chambers where they listen to the structure and advise younger workers. There are stories of elders who, near death, graft themselves into listening seats: not literally becoming trees in a crude way, but allowing roots and moss to grow around their chambers, their bodies feeding the place from which their counsel once came. Whether this is sacrifice, burial, or architecture depends on whom one asks.

They do not dream of conquest downward. The lower world is real but distant, glimpsed in cloud gaps: bronze rivers, dust plains, other ecologies, perhaps other peoples. There is trade, but it is rare and ritualized. From below come mineral pigments, salt, shell, bone, occasional metal, hard stones for heat, and stories of horizons. From above go medicines, seeds that survive mist, woven light fiber, fungal cultures, living water filters, and trained saplings. Ground-walkers find the sky-forest terrifying. Canopy people find open ground obscene: too exposed, too flat, too lacking in handholds, with a sky that does not have the decency to be filtered through leaves.

But the canopy people are not alone.

The plants themselves live there with an agency too slow for ordinary conversation and too intimate to ignore. Load-bearing vines respond to years of touch. Cistern flowers learn the rhythms of households. Door leaves recognize bloodlines or perhaps only their oils. Certain roots refuse graft from particular districts. Some old bridge-forests seem to anticipate crowds and stiffen before festivals. Whether these are plant behaviors, cultivated reflexes, distributed intelligence, or the remaining will of absorbed ancestors is a question no one answers simply.

There are also the quiet cohabitants: transparent frogs in rain cups, pale ceiling insects, seed crabs, gliders, pollinator-beasts, browsing climbers, soft birds, ribbon-snakes, mites inside walls, armored larval things in wet bark, night-descenders with hooked limbs, and patient predators whose bodies imitate railings, fruit, shadows, or openings. Many of them have adapted to human maintenance. Some depend on pruning cycles. Some sabotage them. Some are considered pests in one province and sacred assistants in another.

And beneath all this, in the lower layers and forgotten hollows, live the incorporated ones: abandoned gardens that became thickets, houses that became organs of larger growth, shrines sealed behind bark, feral domesticates, pale descendants of people who chose or were forced into deeper green. Stories speak of underfolk who move through absorbed ladders and breathe through moss, of orchard-ghosts who are not dead but no longer separate from the structure, of children stolen by flowers and returned years later knowing root routes no person taught them. Most such tales are warnings. Some are probably true.

The dominant quality of all life there is attentiveness. Nothing survives by mastery alone. The people do not command the forest-city. They tend, read, request, brace, prune, feed, and apologize. They are brave, but not bold in the usual sense. Boldness is a quick way to become compost. Their courage is slower: stepping onto a bridge that has groaned for three generations; cutting a beloved vine because its hidden rot will kill a district; waiting motionless for hours while a path chooses; carrying seeds across a sleeping span above kilometers of mist.

They are a civilization of held breath and callused hands, of green dimness and inherited knots. They live inside a handmade ecology so ancient that every craft has become a form of listening. They are not separate from their suspended world. They are one of its maintenance organs: warm-blooded, tool-using, story-keeping creatures moving softly through the vast wet architecture, catching what falls, guiding what grows, and trusting the living structure to hold the void open a little longer.
