What lives there are not the children of a green world. They are not descendants of forests, fields, or sun-warmed seas. They are the wet, mineral offspring of pressure, seepage, cold, charge, and repetition.

They live as if the world were a machine because, for them, it is. Not a machine built by hands, but a machine older than intention: a plain of drowned stone that beats, compresses, exudes, rotates, exhales, and remembers. The creatures here have never known still ground. They have never needed leaves. They feed on gradients, precipitates, conductive films, warm leaks, chemical foam, electric tension, and the slow metabolism of the flats themselves.

At the smallest scale, the place is inhabited by crowds.

They gather in black mats along the lips of pools and in the seams where warm mineral water rises through the stone. Some are no larger than seeds; others are the size of fingernails or fists. They move with the nervous certainty of mites and shore-crabs, though many have too many joints, or no proper limbs at all. Their bodies are flat, glossy, and armored with mineral skins. Some are translucent, showing within them not organs exactly, but dim pulses, little tides of blue-white charge moving through soft tubes and glassy chambers.

They scrape the stone constantly.

Their mouths are hard, pale, and ceramic, grown not from bone but from accreted minerals taken into the body and rearranged. They rasp at films that no eye from a greener world would recognize as food: dull metallic slicks, frost-bacterial stains, iron veils, oily electrical crusts, the faint edible residue left where hot seep meets freezing air. Around a vent they arrange themselves in dense rings, each animal aligned to a preferred temperature, salinity, vibration, and charge. Too close, and they cook or calcify. Too far, and they starve in the cold. So they make living diagrams around the warmth.

Many have no eyes. Eyes would be almost useless in the fog and dimness, and dangerous besides, since light itself is not fully reliable here. Instead they feel pressure through their shells. They taste voltage through whiskers of conductive tissue. They read the direction of water by the drag across hundreds of tiny hairs. A piston striking half a mile away tells them more than a sunrise would.

Some live on the moving machinery of the land. They attach themselves to the rims of brine-wheels and allow the slow rotation to drag water through their porous bodies. They are like filters, like barnacles, like valves with hunger. When the wheel turns steadily they bloom, their sides swelling with captured salts and microscopic charge-eaters. When the wheel stutters, they release themselves all at once and fall into the surrounding trenches in a soft clatter of shells.

Others live inside the piston wells.

These are pale, compressed things, built to survive crushing. They occupy the wet darkness around the stone plungers, flattened against the walls when pressure descends, then erupting outward when the basalt pistons rise. For a few moments they spill across the surface in glistening ropes and heaps, feeding frantically on exposed mineral froth. Then the next downward stroke begins, and they pour back into the well as if inhaled. Their whole life is timed to being nearly killed and briefly released.

There are shoals in the puddles, though the puddles are not always connected in any reasonable way.

The shoal-creatures are like commas made from muscle, cartilage, and dark glass. Alone, each one is simple: a curved sliver with a mouth at the narrow end and a tail that is more ripple than limb. Together they become something more difficult. They vanish from one basin and reappear in another that should not touch it. They use underground capillaries, folded water, or perhaps the memory of old channels. When alarmed, they rise as one into the surface tension and arrange themselves into hovering figures: branching glyphs, spirals, ladder-forms, maps of impossible drainage. For several seconds they are not a school but a sentence. Then fear breaks, the symbol collapses, and they scatter back into black water.

Predators know this. Some hunt the individuals. Others hunt the pattern.

The larger grazers—or what passes for grazers—are broad, low animals shaped by the need to remain close to the warm, charged stone. They move through the shallows like sections of soaked carpet being pulled from underneath. Their bodies are wide and soft below, plated above with frost, slag, and scavenged mineral. They do not lift themselves so much as flow forward in muscular sheets. Along their front edges trail antlering arrays of sensory filaments, delicate as frost and constantly renewed. These sweep the ground, tasting heat, vibration, salt, death, and the recent passage of other bodies.

They feed by laying themselves over productive stone.

When they find a living seam—a place where mineral water wells upward with just the right chemistry—they settle and seal their undersides to it. Their bellies open into thousands of tiny mouths. For hours or days they drink the seep, extracting metal ions, heat, microbial films, and faint electrical charge. Around them, smaller creatures gather. Some are parasites, boring into the grazer’s armored back to drink its fluids. Some are cleaners, eating dangerous crust before it hardens too deeply into the plates. Some are opportunists that wait for the grazer to move, exposing softened stone beneath.

Old grazers become landscapes.

Sediment accumulates on their backs. Frost grows in ridges. Vents open in their plated hides where internal chemistry leaks heat. Whole colonies of small animals live there, migrating from shoulder-plate to tail-scarp as if crossing a chain of islands. When such a grazer dies, it may not immediately stop moving. The soft parts sink into mineral film; the armor remains articulated by cold, gas, and scavenger traffic. Sometimes, months or centuries later—if such measures mean anything there—the carcass becomes a low mobile vent-field, creeping under the influence of pressure below it. By then it is unclear whether it is animal, habitat, or geological behavior wearing an inherited shape.

The tall predators are more visibly terrible.

They come through the fog on many needle legs, lifting each limb with exaggerated care and setting it down between pools. Their bodies are narrow, jointed, and high compared with most life there, though nothing in that country rises far. They resemble derricks, folded cranes, wet insects, and walking fractures. Their skins are dark and matte except where frost has collected along the joints. When they move, they click dryly, even in rain, a sound produced by internal plates striking together to map the space around them.

They hunt by interference.

The broad grazers and smaller animals depend on vibration, on reading the ground. The predators wait for piston strokes, compressor booms, vent-coughs, and brine-wheel grinding. At those moments the sensory world floods with noise. The predators step inside the thunder. Their needle legs barely disturb the water. Their feeding limbs unfold from beneath them in sudden pale fans, pinning prey against stone before the prey can distinguish danger from machinery.

Their mouths are not mouths in the ordinary sense. They are arrays of cutters and hollow spines, folded into the chest. They do not bite off flesh so much as drain structures: fluids, charge, dissolved minerals, nerve-equivalents, heat. A victim may remain mostly intact afterward, emptied of direction. Such leftovers are valuable. Scavengers enter them. Fog-outlines form around them. Eggs may be laid in the space where the animal’s intent once was.

Some predators specialize in those outlines.

Because death in the lowland does not always remove a thing completely. In certain fogs, the shape of a killed animal persists: a pale behavioral afterimage that flees, grazes, trembles, or hides as if the body had survived. These outlines have no flesh, but they affect attention. They draw hunters away. They disturb shoals. They cause small scrapers to scatter. For many species they are nuisance, hazard, or camouflage.

But there are lean, almost two-dimensional hunters that feed on them.

These drift through the ankle-fog like torn strips of wet membrane. They have hooks along their edges and hollow pits where sensory organs might be. When they find an outline, they wrap around it, tightening until the fog-shape collapses into droplets. What nourishment they take from this is uncertain. Perhaps they feed on residual heat, perhaps on memory, perhaps on the local error that permits an afterimage to behave like life. Their bodies glow faintly afterward with the posture of whatever they consumed.

The flying things are less like birds than like failures in distance that have learned to breed.

They gather over vents, compressors, suspended pools, and regions where the air has become thin to itself. Their bodies are flat and translucent, riddled with holes. Through those holes one does not see what lies behind them. One sees elsewhere: the underside of a nearby shelf, a future ripple, a dead animal not yet dead, the open mouth of a predator, a pale shoreline hidden inside a small basin. When they move, they do not flap. They shift, slap, vanish, and resume. Each displacement makes a papery sound, as if a map has been folded too quickly.

They feed in the air above chemical disturbance.

Some skim steam and condense minerals through their porous membranes. Others dip their edges into tilted pools and drink from surfaces no other animal can reach. A few seem to prey on misalignment itself. They gather wherever reflections disagree, where a pool shows the wrong sky or a stone appears twice, and they worry at the discrepancy until it frays. Their young hatch in holes in perspective: tiny transparent flakes that cling to the edges of shadows and grow by stealing thickness from fog.

When flocks pass overhead, the life below changes behavior. Shoals form protective diagrams. Needle-legged predators freeze, lest parts of themselves be seen from the wrong angle. Small scraping creatures press flat against the stone. The broad grazers contract their filaments and become, for a while, indistinguishable from low ridges. Nothing here fully trusts the sky.

Beneath the visible animals are the colonial films.

They are not plants, though they spread. They are not fungi, though they stain and digest. They are conductive mineral-biological skins, purple, silver, black, and oil-dark, growing in rings around vents and along the belts of moving water. They thrive on pressure shifts, metal ions, cold shock, and the faint continual abrasion of passing bodies. Touching them can numb tissue, not from poison exactly, but because they borrow the signal traveling through it.

Many animals depend on these films. Scrapers eat them directly. Grazers cultivate them on their undersides. Predators carry patches of them in their joints, where the films help interpret vibration. Some films invade carcasses and replace muscle with responsive mineral lace. A dead animal taken over in this way may flex when compressors boom, crawl when rain connects the basins, or open and close its emptied mouth in time with distant pistons.

The eggs of many species are laid not in nests, but in processes.

One creature lays beadlike eggs in the teeth of brine-wheels, where each rotation abrades the shells thinner until the young are ready to spill free. Another cements its larvae inside frost whiskers; they hatch when a heat pulse melts the ice from within. The tall predators suspend egg-cases from the undersides of tilted pools, where the young hang inverted, drinking gravity’s confusion until their legs harden. Shoal-creatures seed their young into channels that flow toward sound. Broad grazers release slow, armored embryos into compressor fields, where the beating ground presses them flat enough to begin life.

There are also animals that appear to be born from breakdown.

When a glassy weather-nodule bursts and releases its puff of snow and shriek of torn wire, small white things sometimes scatter from the fracture. They may have been embryos. They may have been pressure-patterns given appetite. They immediately seek cracks, hide there, and spend their early lives learning the local rhythm of expansion and contraction. Later they emerge as hinge-bodied scavengers, able to fold themselves through gaps no wider than a line of frost.

The inhabitants do not divide cleanly into animal, mineral, machine, and weather. Those are categories from elsewhere. Here a creature may begin as an egg, live as a filter, die as a vent, and return as a mobile crust. A shell may continue migrating after its occupant has dissolved. A bone-lattice may become a conductor for a colony that was never animal. A feeding ground may acquire reflexes. A pool may become crowded with bodies that are also measurements of pressure.

Their senses are tuned to a world where distance lies.

They do not ask where something is in the simple way. They ask when its sound arrives, which version of the vent it occupies, whether its reflection is heavier than its body, whether water is flowing toward it or away from it, whether the fog remembers it after it moves. A predator can be beside you, under the same cold sky, and also approaching from the horizon in the sequence of echoes. A grazer may follow the same warm seam for years and cross the same hundred yards repeatedly, each passage occurring through an older version of the basin.

Some creatures exploit this.

There are loop-hunters: small, hard, patient animals that wait beside recurring stones. They learn where a prey animal will pass if it walks straight and is returned, unknowingly, to the same vent. The loop-hunters build little barricades of shells and mineral pellets, not intelligently in the human sense, but instinctively, the way spiders build webs. Their traps occupy the repeating portions of space. A victim may avoid the trap once, twice, and then meet the older form of it on the third passage.

There are distance-parasites too.

They attach not to bodies but to routes. A grazing animal migrating between two vent fields may gradually find the path lengthening. More pools appear. The fog thickens. Familiar stones recur with additional frost. The parasite is feeding on the animal’s arrival, stretching the interval between hunger and food. Eventually the host weakens, not because anything has bitten it, but because its destination has become too far while remaining visibly near. When it dies, the parasite collapses the path, and scavengers find the carcass lying only a few body-lengths from the feeding ground it could not reach.

Intelligence, if it exists there, is distributed and cold.

No species builds huts or tools. Nothing makes roads. Nothing gathers to speak beneath a sky. But the lowland as a whole learns in cycles. Vents alter their timing after migrations. Compressor fields change phase when too many bodies cross them. Mineral films thicken in the shapes of frequent traffic. Pools adjust their reflections to include new presences from below. A dead grazer may become a feeding station, then a vent, then a landmark, then a trap, then an animal-like motion again.

The creatures participate in this learning without owning it.

A scraper follows charge and leaves a cleaned path, which changes where frost forms, which redirects water, which brings shoals, which attracts predators, whose kills mineralize, whose lattices conduct pressure, which alters the pistons below. No one intends the pattern. Yet the place becomes denser with consequence. Every appetite is also a gear. Every corpse is raw material. Every migration is an adjustment in the factory of the flats.

Some of the larger beings may be less individual than they appear.

In the dimmest districts, where the engines glow weak blue and corpse-green, there are slow masses under the volcanic glass shelves. Pale animals press their bellies to the underside and listen. They are long, soft, and segmented, but their segments do not always agree on direction. Their bodies extend through cracks into neighboring basins; parts of them may surface miles apart as separate humps, mouths, or listening organs. Small creatures live in the folds of their skin. Shoals pass through their internal canals. Mineral belts carry their shed tissue elsewhere, where it sprouts into new listening pads.

These shelf-dwellers do not hunt quickly. They wait for pressure changes and drink the information carried through stone. They feed on low-frequency tremors, on the heat of distant compression, on the dissolved remains of anything that seeps into the cracks beneath them. When the whole plain rings with a vast tone, their bodies answer with subtle contractions. It may be mating. It may be speech. It may be digestion.

In the marsh without plants live the hidden ones.

The floating mineral mats conceal deeper, warmer black channels. Under them move animals rarely seen whole: thick-bodied, blind, muscular things with hides like wet slag and mouths arranged in spirals. They browse the undersides of the foam crusts, shaving away films and trapping bubbles rich with reactive gases. Their backs carry forests of mineral tubes, each tube inhabited by smaller symbionts that flare with faint light when chemicals pass through them.

Predators enter the marsh at risk. The mats may hold, or break, or grip. The black channels beneath are connected in folded ways. Something can surface in front of itself. A hunted animal may dive and emerge behind the hunter, older by a few breaths and covered in new frost. The marsh-dwellers use this. They are not fast, but they know the secret traffic under the crust. Their young are born in warm pockets beneath mineral foam and rise only when rain connects the marsh to the surrounding flats, at which time many are devoured and enough are carried elsewhere to colonize new basins.

During rain, all inhabitants change.

The small scrapers climb. Shoals disperse into sudden connected waters. Predators become frantic or cautious according to species. Broad grazers lift their edges to avoid being sealed to the wrong chemistry. Flying things multiply in the unstable air, appearing between drops. Compressor fields beat harder, and animals that rely on vibration are briefly blinded by too much world.

Rain is also feast.

Channels overflow. Usually separate populations mix. Carcasses dislodge. Mineral films soften. Warm jets emerge in places long dormant. Eggs hatch prematurely. Predators trapped in freezing shallows become prey for things they normally tower above. Suspended pools sag and spill upward, releasing larvae that have never touched ground. The whole food web loosens and knots again in a new configuration.

After rain, the survivors inherit a rearranged world.

The creatures must relearn the flats. A reliable vent may have become poisonous. A shallow basin may now contain a visible distant shore with animals grazing inside it. A loop in space may have closed. A predator’s territory may be split by a channel that flows toward noise. Colonies die in millions; others bloom explosively. The plain does not recover. It revises.

What lives there, then, are beings of revision.

They are cold-adapted, pressure-timed, mineral-fed, blind or nearly blind, intimate with vibration, chemistry, and error. Their bodies are low, plated, translucent, conductive, jointed, membranous, or spread thin across stone. They make use of machines that are not machines, of weather that is also digestion, of death that remains active, of water that chooses directions no river should know.

They are abundant because the world is abundant—not with sunlight or leaves, but with gradients, with unstable boundaries, with endless conversion. Heat into charge. Charge into film. Film into flesh. Flesh into shell. Shell into vent. Vent into feeding ground. Feeding ground into trap. Trap into migration. Migration into pattern. Pattern into hunger again.

Nothing there is comfortable. Nothing is innocent. But very little is wasted.

The inhabitants are not visitors in an alien industrial marsh. They are the marsh’s moving organs. They are the teeth of its water, the nerves of its stone, the mites in its pistons, the thoughts briefly written by its shoals. They live in the only way life can live there: by becoming process, by accepting the rhythm of clank and seep and freeze, by letting the low drowned world use them and being fed in return.

And if something new stands among them long enough, they will begin to test it.

First the films will taste its outline. Then the scrapers will gather at the wet edges of its shadow. Shoal-creatures will include it in a diagram and abandon it. A flying thing will show, through one of its holes, the underside of the stranger’s future carcass. The nearest pool will learn its reflection from below. Somewhere, a piston will take up its pulse.

After that, the place will know whether it is food, terrain, weather, error, or the beginning of another kind of animal.
