The ones who live there are creatures of margin and interval: not citizens of darkness exactly, but readers of it. They do not dominate the plateau. Nothing dominates it except heat, mineral pressure, and time. The inhabitants are fitted to vastness, scarcity, and sudden abundance; they move through the upland as if across the back of some sleeping engine.

Most of them do not look toward the sky. Sight is useful only nearby, and only sometimes. Their true attention is downward and outward: into stone, vapor, tremor, scent, warmth, the metallic taste of rain forming miles away.

They are low-voiced, wide-ranging, patient animals.

The great grazers are the most visible, though even they are more often sensed than seen. They are tall at the shoulder but carry their heads low, with long necks sloping forward and heavy, soft muzzles built for scraping waxy leaves from thornless mineral shrubs. Their lips are broad, muscular, and dark, split into sensitive pads that can feel droplets in cracks and separate edible lichen from bitter salt crust. In the dark, a grazing animal may stand for an hour beside one basalt boulder, licking the night’s condensation from its shaded side with slow, deliberate rasps.

Their bodies are deep-chested and rangy, all endurance rather than speed. The legs are long, with flexible joints and wide hooves that spread slightly on ash, grip on slick basalt, and fold firm on clinker. They cross plains that would exhaust a softer creature. They sleep standing or kneeling in shallow windbreaks, facing different directions, ears open to stone.

Their hides are dull charcoal, umber, or soot-brown, with subtle mineral striping that only appears when moisture darkens them. Along their ribs and flanks are faintly luminous thermal patches: not bright enough to see by, but enough to make them ghostly to anything that reads heat. Calves are darker and warmer, their glow muffled under shaggy coats. The young press beneath their mothers’ bellies during mineral rain and learn early to freeze when the herd freezes.

Some carry antlers, but not branching forest antlers. These are flattened, backswept structures like weathered basalt shelves, used to shed rain, scrape mineral crust, and test one another during slow contests at seep-lines. Others have horn plates along the brow and nose, polished by rubbing against warm stone. Their social life is spacious, like the land itself. A herd may contain hundreds, but it is rarely a tight mass. It is a procession spread across ridges and gullies: kin groups moving within hearing, scenting, and tremor range of one another.

They communicate with throat vibrations too low for most ears. A warning travels through the herd not as a cry but as a change in posture, a tightening of breath, a sequence of hooves placed differently on stone. They know old routes between seep, shrub-field, ash hollow, and condensation fin. Their migrations are not seasonal in any simple sense. They follow pressure changes, vent warmth, mineral rain, the flowering of low black shrubs, and the opening of underground water into surface channels.

Their predators are fewer, but more intricately made.

The gully-hunters are long-bodied, close to the ground, with flattened ribs and flexible spines that allow them to pour themselves along channel walls and under tilted slabs. Their skins are patterned not with color but with texture: matte patches like ash, slick bands like wet clay, crusted ridges like sulfur deposit. When still, they become part of the vent-stained rock.

They do not chase unless forced. They listen through their feet.

Their forefeet spread into fanlike pads full of pressure organs. Resting against stone, they can distinguish the separate weights of a browsing herd, the light scrape of a beetle swarm under shale, the hollow drum of water moving below a dry wash. A gully-hunter waits where paths narrow: near seep-lines, below condensation fins, beside the lip of a pool, under overhangs where grazers must lower their heads. Its jaws open wider than they appear able to. Its teeth are dark, translucent at the edges, replaced often because grit wears everything here down.

Another kind of predator lives among warm rocks near vents. These are heavier, almost seal-like when at rest, though they move on jointed limbs tucked beneath them. Their skins flush and dim with heat as blood shifts through surface vessels, matching the thermal confusion of fissures and steaming ground. They are ambush animals of furnace margins. Moths, small grazers, carrion birds, anything heat-dazzled or rain-driven may come too close. Their tongues are quick and barbed; their bite carries mineral-rich saliva that thickens blood rapidly. After feeding, they wedge themselves into warm cracks and may not move for days.

There are also rim-stalkers along the escarpments: lean, high-footed hunters with long tails held stiff for balance in cliff wind. They patrol the edges where storm migrations disorient the young and old. Their eyes are larger than those of the gully-hunters, but cloudy, adapted to glow and motion rather than image. Along the sides of their heads are slit-like heat pits, and along the throat a ridged plate used for clicking calls that bounce off cliffs. They hunt in pairs or loose family groups, driving prey toward broken ground.

Above and between them all are the winged scavengers.

They nest in vent chimneys, basalt pockets, escarpment hollows, and the warm leeward sides of fins. They are not birds exactly, though from far away they might seem like them: narrow leathery wings, long wrists, grasping hind feet, heads carried forward on sinewed necks. Many have no true feathers, only heat-insulating filaments around the chest and shoulders, often stained rust, black, or yellow-white by mineral fumes. Their faces are bare, toughened, and ridged. The nostrils are elaborate, folded chambers for reading rot, steam chemistry, rain, blood, and egg-musk across miles.

They fly by heat more than light. Rising columns from fissures hold them aloft in the dark. They hover awkwardly over vents, drop sideways into updrafts, and fold suddenly into cracks when starlight appears. Their wings make that dry, distant canvas sound when many gather at a carcass. They bicker with throat clicks and papery hisses, but they are careful around the large predators. Their beaks or jaws differ by kind: hooked carrion-tearers, long probing vent-feeders, blunt shell-crackers that break mineral beetles and eggs against stone.

Some of them are almost religiously tied to death routes. They follow herds for weeks, sleeping in shifts, waiting for weakness, birth, flood, or misstep. Others specialize in vent crust and the soft blooms that grow on carcasses after warm rain. When a large grazer falls, the first flyers circle without calling, mapping the danger below. Only after the predators have fed do they descend in a dark folding spiral.

Beneath the stones lives another population, more numerous than the visible one.

There are segmented burrowers pale as steamed clay, with tiny heat-sensing nodules along their sides. They chew roots, dead lichen, mineral films, dung, and the softened undersides of old bones. In dry months they curl into sealed chambers and line the walls with mucus that hardens like varnish. When floodwater comes, they wake so suddenly that a dry gully may seem to boil.

There are beetles with metallic shells colored iron-blue, oil-green, and burnt copper. Their wing cases are pitted like slag. Some collect sulfur grains in small baskets along their legs. Others farm pale fungi under flat stones warmed by geothermal seepage. A few are large enough to attract dedicated hunters: quick, nearly blind creatures with pointed snouts and snapping jaws, moving in bursts from crack to crack.

In the seep pools live translucent amphibians with wide heads and delicate external gills that pulse red-black in mineral water. Their bones show faintly through the skin. They cling to the undersides of stones during dry periods, sealed in mud cocoons, their hearts slowed almost to nothing. When rain reconnects the pools, they emerge in masses, singing not with voices but with vibrating membranes that make the water tremble. Their eggs adhere to basalt in clear ropes, and many never hatch before the pools shrink again.

Deeper down are blind fish in underground streams, silver-white and soft-scaled, guided by pressure and chemistry. Some have flat heads for nosing along warm channels. Others are long and ribbonlike, living where water passes through lava tubes. The eel-things that surface after floods are older-looking: dark-mouthed, whiskered, with muscular bodies able to cross wet stone between pools. They feed on eggs, larvae, drowned insects, and sometimes the tongues of unwary drinking animals.

The plants, too, seem less like background than like slow inhabitants with hard disciplines.

The black shrubs are solitary and possessive. Each one occupies a radius of earth like a claim. Their roots go wide, then deep, entering cracks hair-thin and prying minerals from stone. Their leaves are small, waxed, and nearly lightless, arranged to catch condensation rather than sun. Some breathe through their undersides, taking in sulfurous vapors and warm mineral exhalations. During rain they unfold hidden membranes, glass-clear and veined, making the shrub briefly larger, almost animal. Then, when the wetness passes, they close into fists.

The ribbed reeds of gullies are hollow and dark, jointed like insect legs. Wind through them makes a dry rattle, but during flood they bend flat and survive under rushing black water. Their seeds are hard, magnetic, and travel in mineral foam. Copper moss around seeps grows in steaming pads, soft enough to take an animal’s footprint and slow enough to keep it for days. Pale lichens under overhangs glow when wet or bruised, not brightly, but with the color of old bone seen through water.

The most elaborate living communities gather at condensation forests. There, on the windward faces of basalt fins, moisture becomes almost dependable. Sideways shrubs cling in black knots. Root curtains descend through mineral streaks. Filament lichens hang like hair, beaded with droplets. Tiny grazers climb them at night, their split toes gripping wet stone. Shell-backed browsers rear up on hind legs to lick the water tracks, balancing with thick tails. Flyers sleep above them, warmed by the lee, occasionally opening one eye when the stone moans.

Even the carcass sites become temporary settlements.

When a grazer dies, its body is not merely consumed; it becomes a brief country. Predators open it. Flyers strip it. Beetles arrive in shining crowds. Pale burrowers rise from below. Fungi bloom in the warm cavities, some rust-red, some black and velvety, some luminous green. Shrub seeds sprout in the phosphate-rich soil beneath the bones. Months later, when the hide is gone and the skeleton is loosened, one may find a denser patch of vegetation there than anywhere nearby: a dark shrub rooted through a rib cage, copper moss under the skull, beetle tunnels in the marrow channels. Death is one of the few ways the plateau becomes locally generous.

What lives there is not only animal and plant, but behavior shaped by the plateau’s rules.

When rain begins, everything changes.

Creatures that seemed absent appear from sealed mud, stone flakes, vents, roots, and air. Grazers move toward open channels but hesitate at soft banks. Predators take bends and constrictions. Amphibians split from the ground. Beetles climb stems to avoid drowning. Flyers descend low, catching flushed larvae and weakened moths. Shrubs open. Moss swells. Lichens brighten. The whole gully becomes crowded, but only for hours. Then water vanishes underground, and the living world retracts.

When dryness returns, movement becomes economical again. Animals widen their spacing. Calls cease. The great herds walk at night-long pace across benches of basalt and ash. Predators sink back into mineral stains. Pool-creatures settle under silt. Plants harden. The plateau resumes its enormous restraint.

The beings here are ancient in manner even if individuals are not ancient in years. They behave as though excess is dangerous. They waste little sound, little heat, little water, little motion. Their bodies are tools shaped by pressure, steam, rust, hunger, and darkness. Many are dark, matte, low, plated, broad-footed, or translucent. Many have organs for sensing warmth, vibration, mineral traces, and electrical change. Eyes exist, but they are not trusted completely.

They are not monsters, though many would look monstrous in sudden light. They are not gentle, either. They are fitted.

A herd lifting its heads all at once before a pressure tremor; a gully-hunter flattening itself into wet clay; a flyer hanging motionless in a thermal column; a black shrub opening its glass membranes to warm rain; blind fish turning underground toward a pulse of fresh water — these are the plateau’s inhabitants as much as any named species.

They live as the land lives: sparsely, massively, intermittently awakened.

They are the life of a place where the world itself breathes like buried machinery.
