The ones who live there are not easy to separate from the plateau.

At first they seem like animals, if one sees them at all: a pale joint withdrawing behind a curtain of ferns, a long hand pressed flat to wet stone, an eye reflecting from under a shelf of root-shadow and then gone. They have learned the oldest skill of that place, which is not concealment exactly, but agreement. Nothing on the plateau survives by standing apart. Moss agrees with stone. Water agrees with gravity only where it must. Trees agree with the old fields around the towers and bend their roots accordingly. So too do the inhabitants.

They are tall when fully unfolded, though they are seldom fully unfolded. Most of the time they move low, or sideways, or with their limbs arranged along branches and buttress roots, making themselves into a continuation of whatever holds them. Standing upright would make them too obvious and, more importantly, would make them vulnerable to the changing weights of the ridges and sink gardens. Their bodies have a gathered, articulated look, as if designed by canopy, marsh, and cliff in committee. Long limbs, narrow torsos, wide flexible feet that can spread over floating mats without breaking the skin of water plants. Fingers divided into delicate gripping branches, black at the tips, with pads sensitive enough to read condensation patterns on ceramic and the mood of leaves.

Their skin is not one color. In youth it is translucent amber-brown, veined faintly green and blue, the way wet leaves are colored when held against light. In age it darkens into slate, bark, bruised copper, or the pale mineral gray of limestone under algae. Many are overgrown in a controlled way: not dirty, not neglected, but inhabited. Fine moss grows along their shoulders and spines. Lichens mark the high ridges of cheek and brow. Some cultivate little hanging gardens from braided hair or from the fibrous mantles they wear, orchids and filament ferns and scented fungi growing from woven mats against their backs. These are not decoration alone. They cool the blood. They signal health. They host useful insects. They remember paths.

Their faces are difficult for humans to read. The eyes are large, dark, and wet-looking, but not soft. The mouth is usually small and held closed, since speech through the mouth is not their primary language. Their noses are broad and low, adapted less for sharp smell than for tasting humidity, mineral trace, fermentation, electrical charge. Along the throat, collarbone, and ribs are membranes that can tighten and tremble. Much of their communication passes there: low tones felt through wood, water, mud, and the ceramic towers; clicks placed into the acoustics of ravines; exhalations that alter the behavior of tiny symbiotic gnats; slow color changes under the skin, visible only at intimate distance or under certain angles of wet light.

They speak, when they must, in a language that sounds unfinished to outsiders. Half of it seems to be pause. A phrase may begin with three knocks on a root, continue as a hum below hearing, and end with a fingertip dragged through pooled water. Their conversations can last across a valley. One speaker leaves a vibration in a bridge-vine; another, hours later, answers from inside a fog bank by disturbing the mist into a ribbed fan of pressure. They do not consider this slow. Their sense of exchange follows the plateau’s own layered time. Some matters are answered immediately, in ravines where thought moves fast. Other decisions are left near towers or still pools and revisited over years.

They are descendants of the builders, or the builders altered by their own listening, or the servants of those builders who outlasted the masters and became something freer. The distinction matters less to them than it would to a visitor. They do not preserve history as a line of rulers, wars, inventions, collapses. They preserve it as agreements with place.

That cliff-door must not be warmed by hand.  
The black uphill channel is to be cleared only when the moon is absent from the water.  
The tower that leans toward the reed plain is ill-tempered after long droughts.  
The pool with false stars will answer questions, but only with directions, never with reasons.  

Their settlements are nearly invisible because they learned long ago that anything emphatic becomes food, nest, substrate, or instrument for the plateau. They live in lifted houses suspended from buttress roots, under overhangs, inside hollow trees enlarged without killing the tree, and in chambers behind cliff seams that open only for the right sequence of pressure, heat, and pulse. A dwelling might be recognized by a circle of water bowls, a ladder of living vine trained to contract under certain feet, or a cluster of pale fungi that glow only when the family within is awake.

Their architecture is less made than persuaded. They coax roots around frames of old ceramic. They grow walls out of waxy leaf layers and reinforce them with mineral secretions from patient colonies of insects. They salvage panels from ancient platforms and set them not as monuments but as weather organs: one piece to condense drinking water, another to repel rot from sleeping mats, another to keep time from thickening too much around the cradles of infants. Their bridges are temporary when seen by human standards—woven, regrown, dissolved, shifted—but some have been maintained in changing form for thousands of years. A bridge may not contain one original strand and still be considered the same bridge.

They do not farm in cleared fields. Clearings invite heat, biting swarms, and the wrong attention from the air. Instead they cultivate gradients. They manage marsh edges, fermentation logs, floating gardens, bromeliad pools, fungus shelves, fruiting vines, and mineral seeps. They plant edible reeds in water that moves at one speed and medicinal flowers where time rubs against itself. They raise crabs in tree hollows and blind silver fish in cisterns that refill from condensation rather than rain. They harvest root-milk from plants that must be sung to before cutting. They gather fruit only from branches that lean toward the hand. If the leaves turn away, the fruit is left, no matter how hungry the gatherer.

Hunger exists among them, but greed is treated as a sensory failure, like vertigo or fever. To take too much from one tempo, one basin, one season, is dangerous. The plateau keeps accounts in mold blooms, in reversed echoes, in sudden miscarriages of gravity. Children are taught early not morality first, but consequence. Do not run across the sink garden when leaves are falling. Do not shout into the ravine that answers too quickly. Do not sleep beside a pool that reflects more stars than the sky owns. Do not strike old metal with new stone. Never follow a straight light under water unless you have first been invited by something older than your blood.

Their children are small, quick, and almost transparent at birth, carried against the chest or back in damp woven slings packed with moss. They must be kept moist for the first seasons. A dry wind, rare as it is, can kill them. They learn climbing before walking, listening before speech. Their first lessons involve distinguishing ordinary water from remembering water, harmless mist from measuring mist, fungal light from marker light, animal silence from tower silence. They play by dropping leaves in time-boundary places and racing them downward, or by calling echoes into bent channels to make them return wearing different voices.

Their elders become increasingly still. Age in this place is not marked by frailty alone but by rootedness. The oldest inhabitants move rarely and may spend whole wet seasons in one chosen grove, advising through vibrations sent into the ground. Moss thickens over them. Their skin toughens and cools. Birds perch on them. Small amphibians lay eggs in the folds of their garments. Some elders eventually enter what outsiders might mistake for death: they are seated or bound gently into a living lattice of roots near a tower, a cliff seam, or a pool of false stars. There they become “deep listeners,” no longer social in the ordinary way but not gone. Their descendants consult them by pouring warmed water over the roots and waiting for changes in steam, fungal glow, or the taste of condensed droplets.

They have tools, but they are understated. Ceramic knives that never dull but refuse to cut living greenwood. Cordage spun from vine fibers and the silk of marsh moths. Hollow reeds tuned to insect swarms. Masks of polished bone-like material worn near certain towers, not to hide the face but to prevent one’s thoughts from being pulled into repeating loops. Nets weighted with smooth black stones that fall more slowly than they should. Bowls designed to collect not rain but particular kinds of condensation: mineral-sweet, sleep-heavy, fever-breaking, memory-bitter.

They do not use fire much. In many parts of the plateau fire sulks and dies. Cooking is done by fermentation, heated stones from thermal seams, chemical warmth from old platforms, or the slow heat of compost chambers. Their food is fragrant, soft, and wet: steamed tubers, sour fruit pastes, crab meat, translucent eggs, sheets of algae, fungus loaves, nectar thickened with mineral salts, lake-root shaved thin and soaked until it stops moving. They drink constantly. Dehydration is almost unknown, but water-sickness is a common danger, because not all wetness is fit to enter the body.

Predators know them and are known in return. The inhabitants are not masters over the beasts. They are neighbors with protocols. Certain branch-hunters will not attack if greeted with the correct throat-hum and a gift of fermented fruit. The broad-bellied mud sliders are avoided during breeding months but followed in dry spells, because their channels expose buried tubers. The unseen canopy giants sometimes take livestock, children, or the unwary; in exchange, the people leave mineral cakes on high platforms and remove parasites from sleeping juveniles when they find them tangled in vines. War against such beings would be absurd. One does not declare war on monsoon, cliff, fever, or night.

Yet they are not gentle in any simple way. The plateau has made them patient, and patience can be terrible. Their hunters can wait motionless in reed water through a day and a half, lowering their pulse until insects settle on their eyes. Their guardians can lure invaders into places where distance lengthens and thirst becomes impossible to satisfy despite all the water in the world. They poison with spores, with time, with reflections. A trespasser who cuts a sacred root may not be attacked at once; instead every path back will become subtly unfamiliar, every echo slightly wrong, until the trespasser returns in circles to the same black pool and sees, below the surface, stars that were never born above them.

What they fear most is not death. They fear separation from pattern. To be unable to sense the wet electrical taste before a tower wakes; to step where plants have turned away; to hear water running uphill and not know whether it is healthy or fevered—these are nightmares. Madness among them is described as dryness even when the sufferer is drenched. A dry mind cannot listen. A dry mind insists on straight roads, fixed clocks, clean edges, ownership that does not rot and rejoin.

Their ceremonies are mostly acts of maintenance. They clear calcite growth from old seams without opening them. They braid new guide-knots along safe paths before storm season. They sing to the towers in tones that make puddles tremble. They release captive insects at cracked light-markers so the swarms do not become too ecstatic and starve. They transplant moss onto landslide scars, not to hide damage but to teach the wound how to hold water again. Their festivals occur when two or more cycles meet: the blooming of boundary flowers, the surfacing of lake-browsers, the night when the false stars in the pools align with the true fragments overhead.

On those nights they gather on the warm bone platforms above the marsh. They paint their throats with blue mineral paste. Their cultivated back-gardens are trimmed and hung with beads of condensate that glow faintly from fungal infusion. Children are allowed to stay awake. Elders are carried or summoned through root-hum. The kite-creatures drift overhead in slow, pulsing constellations. No central speech is given. Instead, each family contributes a tone, a scent, a bowl of water, a remembered warning, a new observation. Together these form a map too complex to draw: the current state of treaties between life, stone, machine, weather, and time.

Their relationship with the ancient technology is intimate but wary. They know how to wake some systems and soothe others. They do not understand all of it, and they consider total understanding a dangerous appetite. The old builders, in their stories, tried once to ask the plateau a question too large. The answer lifted the marshes, warmed the clouds, bent the water, and broke the sky’s memory into the pools. Whether this is myth or history does not matter. It is true enough to guide behavior.

A few among them serve as keepers of specific anomalies. One lineage tends the uphill channel, scraping mineral films from its black bed with shell tools while singing in counter-rhythm so the water does not reverse too far. Another watches the valley where echoes return altered; they use it to send messages, but also to detect when something from behind the cliff-doors has shifted in its sleep. A third lives near the sink gardens, raising children there so they grow with excellent balance and a lifelong suspicion of ordinary falling.

They know of humans, or of humanlike visitors, if such visitors ever came. They would not greet them openly at first. They would watch from heat-shimmer and leaf-dark, judging not weapons or language but posture: whether the strangers step on breathing soil, whether they drink from unpermitted pools, whether they look too long into night reflections, whether they cut before asking. A respectful visitor might find gifts appearing near camp: a cord tied in the direction of safer ground, a bitter fruit that prevents marsh fever, a bowl placed where condensation will fill it by morning. A careless one might find all paths gradually ending above reed-choked water.

They are not primitive. They are post-conquest, post-ambition, post-separation. Their sophistication lies in restraint, in knowing which doors should remain seams, which machines should remain half-asleep, which facts are best held by orchids and fungus rather than written in dry archives. They possess mathematics of flow, kinship systems based on watershed and tempo, medicine that treats the patient and the surrounding humidity together, music that can measure distance distortions more accurately than instruments, and memory distributed among elders, root lattices, trained insects, carved ceramics, and pools that reflect another sky.

If one had to name them, one might call them the Condensate People, the High Marsh Listeners, the Root-Bound, the Treaty-Keepers. None would be exact. Their own name for themselves is not a noun but an action: something like “those-who-remain-in-right-wetness,” or “the listening that has taken shape.”

They are what lives there because they have become a way for the plateau to know itself.

Through them, the marsh checks its paths. Through them, the towers are remembered without being obeyed too fully. Through them, fruit is harvested and spores are carried, old mistakes are not repeated too loudly, and children learn which stars belong overhead and which belong only in water. They are animal, citizen, gardener, instrument, and weather-sign. Warm rain beads on their skin. Moss grows in their scars. Their songs move through roots and return altered from stone.

At night, when the plateau steams and the pools show tilted constellations, they can sometimes be seen crossing the bog on stones set too far apart for human legs. They move without hurry, each step placed where the land has consented. Their backs are hung with small gardens. Their throats pulse with inaudible speech. Around them insects rise in luminous swarms, not fleeing, not attacking, but accompanying.

Then mist leans in, the reeds close behind them, and they are gone—not vanished, only resumed.
