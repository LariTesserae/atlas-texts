What lives there is not one species, but a whole patience of life compressed close to the ground.

The inhabitants of the high wet country are mostly small, low, water-minded beings: creatures and plants shaped by saturation, by fog, by cold abundance, by the need to move without tearing the skin of the world. Nothing there rises arrogantly. Even the old things crouch. Trees become mats. Predators become shadows. Grazers learn to spread their weight. Birds nest near the earth. Amphibians seem less like visitors to land than like water briefly given limbs.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the mosses.

They are the true architecture of the plateau. They live over stone, over rot, over one another, over forgotten pools, over the backs of roots and the lips of cracks. Some grow in swollen cushions that hold water like lungs. Step near them and they darken, not from injury but from the redistribution of stored rain. Others hang from shelves and dwarf branches in soaked curtains, dripping continuously, making small climates beneath themselves. Pale fog-mosses live where cloud drags along the ridges; their bodies are almost white, with faint green hearts, and they drink directly from vapor. Black heat-moss grows in shallow basins and on south-facing stone, dense and velvety, warming faster than the world around it and sheltering larvae in its soaked pile.

There are tower mosses too, slow-growing columns no taller than a hand, built by generations of green rising over brown. Their lower parts are dead but not gone, preserved by cold wetness, packed into peat. Their living tops are bright and tender. In certain districts, around the warm mineral vents, metallic moss forms thick plates the color of tarnished copper and deep bottle glass. In rain it shines so fiercely it seems enamelled.

Among the mosses live the liverworts: flat, scaled, intimate plants laid against stone and bark like green inscriptions. They occupy the undersides of ledges, the margins of constant seepage, the shadowed grooves between root and rock. Their spore cups fill with beads of water and release their contents when struck by rain. To a passing animal they are almost texture only, but they pattern the country in thousands of tiny maps.

The lichens are older-seeming still. They live where even moss hesitates: on exposed stone backs, on wind-combed ridges, on the gray shoulders of boulders. Silver lichens make the rocks look furred with frost even in seasons without ice. Orange crust lichens stain iron-rich seams. Black branching lichens grow like miniature antlers, brittle when dry, pliant when wet. Whole stones are made into cities by them, though nothing in those cities hurries.

Then come the shrubs and dwarf trees, the largest rooted citizens of the place.

They are not tall, but many are ancient. Their trunks are twisted low and hard, bent by wind and weighted by moisture, their bark dark and slick where exposed, pale where lichens sheath it. They grow in thickets so dense they make an upper ground: interlaced branches, mats of glossy leaves, tiny berries held under rain. Their roots do not dive so much as grip and braid, binding peat to stone, bridging hidden channels, creating the narrow firm ribs used by animals.

Some shrubs bear red berries, sharp and waxy, that persist into cold weather and feed birds and grazers. Others bear blue-black fruit under a pale bloom, staining the mouths of small mammals. There are white berries too, hard as beads, half-translucent in mist, eaten only by certain birds and by the shaggy grazers when other forage is low. Many leaves are leathery, rolled slightly at the edges to shed water. Their undersides are pale and hairy. Their upper surfaces reflect the sky.

The dwarf forests shelter a hidden multitude: spiders strung between twigs, beetles wedged under bark, moth pupae in silk sacks under leaves, snails grazing films from wet wood, mites moving through moss like red grains of dust.

The sedges and reeds are the harsher vegetation. They own the marsh pans and pool edges. Their blades are narrow, cutting, saturated with cold sap. Their roots make dense tough mats that may support a hoof one day and tremble open the next. In wide basins they grow in bands, each species occupying a precise depth of water. The shallowest sedges are tawny and stiff; the deeper ones are green-black and soft at the base. The segmented reeds stand in districts where water is mineral-rich and slow. Their hollow stems knock faintly when draining, so that whole reedbeds murmur in sequences, a dry mechanical sound arising from a wet world.

Below all of this is peat: not dead ground, exactly, but stored life. It is inhabited by bacteria, fungi, sleeping seeds, root hairs, worms, larvae, beetle grubs, pale blind things that feed on buried stems, and old gases waiting for release. It remembers forests that never grew tall, summers that never fully dried, animal paths, drowned nests, ash from far-off fires, pollen blown from lower lands. It is the plateau’s archive and stomach.

The fungi are everywhere, though often invisible until conditions invite them. Thread networks run through peat and root-mats, digesting what the cold permits. Small waxy mushrooms appear after heavy rain, gray, ocher, or translucent, their caps beaded like skin. Bracket fungi grow on the undersides of fallen dwarf trunks, soft-edged and pale, glowing faintly at night with a greenish light. There are black cup fungi in old hoofprints. There are orange pins no taller than sedge seeds. Around warm seeps, mineral-loving fungi build crusts over bacterial mats, forming living films that ripple with chemical color.

The pools have their own inhabitants.

Some are black on the surface but clear underneath, the color coming from peat tannins. Others are green with suspended life or amber with iron. Their margins are soft and treacherous, feathered with floating plants. Underwater stems make submerged forests in miniature, and among them move pale fish, cold-bodied and quick, surviving in basins that seem isolated but are linked beneath the ground. They have large eyes, small mouths, and translucent fins. Some are nearly colorless except for a smoky line along the spine. They feed on larvae, tiny crustaceans, drowned gnats, and the soft eggs of midges attached to stems.

In the shallower pools live snails with dark shells polished by acidity, flatworms like wet ribbons, caddis larvae in cases made of reed splinters and mineral grains, water beetles that carry silver bubbles beneath their bodies, and small crustaceans that pulse through the water in jerks. Dragonfly relatives exist there too, but in reduced, cold-country forms: their nymphs squat among roots for long seasons before emerging during rare warm intervals as narrow-winged adults that fly low and briefly.

The amphibians are among the most characteristic animals of the plateau.

Broad-headed newts live in the dark pools and under moss shelves. Their backs are mottled gray, brown, and green, indistinguishable from wet stone. Their bellies are pale, sometimes stained rust-orange by mineral water. They walk more often than they swim, climbing submerged roots, pausing with toes spread on leaves. In breeding season the males grow low translucent crests, not flamboyant but enough to tremble in still water.

There are glassy frogs, small and nearly translucent, tucked into moss hollows where water beads perpetually. Their bones show faintly blue through the skin of the legs. Their eyes are copper or black. They call rarely, often only during thick mist, a short clicking note like a droplet striking a hollow stem. Their eggs are laid not in open pools but in water pockets held by moss, so the tadpoles begin life suspended in green.

The dark salamanders are more terrestrial. They emerge after rain from cracks, peat fissures, and the undersides of root mats, as though the land extrudes them. Their skins are black-brown, slick, sometimes sprinkled with pale speckles like lichen chips. They hunt worms, springtails, midge larvae, and soft-bodied beetles. When threatened, they flatten themselves against moss and become a shadow in the wet.

There may also be larger amphibians in the old basins: heavy, slow mud-newts with blunt tails and lidless-looking eyes, animals rarely seen except when a pool floods into another and they move at night across saturated ground, leaving wide belly trails in the moss.

Insects rise and vanish according to brief permissions granted by weather.

For much of the year they are eggs, larvae, pupae, burrowers, sleepers. Then a few warmer days align, and the air thickens with delicate bodies. Crane flies lift out of sedge and drift on long legs, frail as blown roots. Midges form columns in sheltered hollows, each column holding its shape in the still wet air. Beetles emerge polished and dark, many shaped like seeds or water drops. Some feed on algae films, some on fungi, some on the eggs of other insects. There are cold-adapted moths whose wings match lichen and whose caterpillars browse the dwarf shrubs at night. Tiny wingless insects live deep in moss, white or amber, springing away when exposed.

The plateau has many spiders. Some build low horizontal webs between sedge stems to catch midges rising from pools. Others hunt without webs across the moss skin, their legs water-resistant, their bodies patterned gray and green. In reedbeds, long-jawed spiders hang along stems like knots. Under stone shelves live pale cave-like spiders that never encounter true caves, only the damp overhangs and root-dark hollows that imitate them.

The birds are secretive, close-colored, and economical with sound.

Marsh-stalkers stand in the mirror pools on long greenish legs, their bodies streaked brown and gray. They can remain motionless so long that moss seems to gather on them. Their beaks are narrow, made for striking frogs, fish, and beetles from black water. When they move, they do so with extreme deliberation, lifting each foot from the sucking ground without ripple.

Small sedge-nesting birds live almost invisibly among the cutting clumps. They have mottled backs, pale throats, and sharp short calls that do not carry far. Their nests are woven just above the wet, fastened to stems that rise and fall with changing water. Their chicks learn silence before flight.

The upland hunters are compact and swift. They skim inches above shrubs and moss, using ridges and fog as cover. Their wings are pointed but not broad; their tails steer through uneven air. Some hunt insects during warm emergences. Others take frogs, small birds, and voles. Their plumage breaks apart in mist: slate, brown, lichen-green, white flashes under the throat or wing only visible when they turn.

Night birds move by softness. They have wide faces, damp-muffled feathers, and calls used sparingly. Some nest in cavities beneath dwarf roots or under overhanging peat banks. Their eyes catch what little light the pools and fungi offer.

The small mammals are numerous but seldom seen.

There are peat-voles with dense waterproof fur, rounded ears, and pale whiskers. They tunnel through sedge roots and moss layers, feeding on stems, seeds, fungus, and berries. Their burrows often flood, so they build multiple chambers at different heights and maintain emergency routes to shrub mats. In heavy rain they are forced out into the open, blinking and frantic, where predators wait.

There are shrew-like hunters, tiny and voracious, that move through moss galleries after insects, worms, and amphibian eggs. Their metabolism burns fiercely in the cold. They cannot pause long. Their noses tremble constantly, reading the dense traffic of scent.

There may be gliding or climbing mammals in the dwarf forests, though “forest” there is only waist-high to a human: small furred animals with gripping toes and long tails, traveling through branch mats without touching the soaked ground. They eat berries, beetles, and fungal growths. Their nests are woven of moss and hair in the densest shrub tangles, lined against damp with waxy leaves.

The grazers are the most visible large animals.

They are shaggy, narrow-faced, built low and sturdy for cold wind and uncertain footing. Their coats hang in oily, water-shedding locks, darker along the back, paler around the muzzle and belly. In rain they look smaller than they are, compressed by wet fur. Their eyes are large, dark, and placed to watch the wide misted country. Their ears are short and furred. Their nostrils are slitlike and constantly testing the air.

Their hooves are broad and splayed, with flexible edges that spread weight over peat. Between the toes grows coarse hair that sheds mud. They know where ground will hold. Calves learn by following exactly, stepping into the prints of elders. A careless young animal may break through a floating mat and have to be hauled out by its own thrashing, or may not emerge at all.

They graze sedge tips, crop dwarf shrub leaves, strip bark from certain roots, nose berries from under wet foliage, and lick mineral deposits around warm seeps. In summer they move in loose bands, never tightly herded unless fog thickens or predators trouble them. In colder seasons they climb wind-exposed ridges where snow, if it comes, does not lie deeply and where lichens can be scraped from stone.

Their paths are not roads but accumulated habits: firmer lines over root-ridges, shallow depressions through shrub mats, careful crossings of seep channels. Other creatures use these paths too. Water uses them when it can.

The predators that follow them are long, low, and patient.

They are not built for dramatic chases across open land, because there is no truly open land and footing betrays speed. They hunt by knowledge. Their bodies are flexible, otterlike or catlike in different lineages, with short powerful legs, wide padded feet, and coats that shed water. Their fur is dark brown, black-gray, or moss-shadowed, often lighter along the throat. Their heads are narrow enough to push through shrub tunnels. Their ears are small. Their whiskers are long.

They know which moss skins conceal pools, which sedge beds force grazers toward stone, which hummocks remain firm after three days of rain. They travel the same hidden ribs for generations, placing feet in ancestral certainty. Often the only sign of one is a place where frogs stop calling, or a sudden lift of birds from a basin, or the way a grazing band tightens and faces the same patch of fog.

They eat voles, birds, amphibians, fish stranded by falling water, and the young or weakened of the hoofed herds. A large predator may wait half a day beside a false-looking patch of bright moss because it knows a fleeing animal will choose it and sink to the chest.

There are scavengers too: dark birds with water-sleek feathers, beetles that arrive under cover of mist, pale larvae in peat, fungi that bloom from old bones hidden in sedge. Nothing large remains singular for long. The plateau takes every body into distributed work.

Around the mineral vents live stranger specialists.

Warmth there is slight but reliable, and reliability is rare enough to shape communities. Moss grows thicker. Insects breed earlier. Amphibians gather in winter around seep-warmed pools. Bacterial mats spread in layered colors: green, amber, iron-red, blue-black. Some form films that shimmer when disturbed, giving the illusion of submerged circuitry. Small snails graze these mats. Beetles with metallic shells feed along their edges. Worms thread through the softened mineral terraces. At dusk, algae flicker where droplets strike, each flash brief and cold, like a thought disappearing.

Animals visit the vents for salt, warmth, and food, but few remain too long. The water chemistry is strong. Bones laid there stain orange. Hooves leave prints rimmed with mineral crust. Feathers dropped into the seeps acquire pale edges.

The reed marshes house another set of lives adapted to soundless density. Stem-boring larvae live inside the hollow reeds. Tiny frogs cling within the joints during cold rains. Spiders wait where the reed walls split. Birds nest in woven platforms suspended above black water. When condensation drains through the segmented stems, the marsh clicks and counts, and the creatures inside freeze or move according to those rhythms. To them the knocking is weather, clock, and warning.

Above all, the place is inhabited by processes that behave almost like organisms.

Mist lives there in recurring bodies, pooling, lifting, flowing through shrub corridors. Water lives as seep, film, bead, pool, vein, buried stream, vapor, rain. Peat lives as memory that eats slowly. Iron migrates. Bacteria bloom and fade. Algae wake to disturbance. Roots negotiate with stone. Entire slopes creep. A basin may spend centuries becoming more pond than meadow, while another pond fills from below with plant matter until sedge can cross it.

The living things of the plateau are therefore not only residents of wetness; they are instruments of it. They hold water, slow it, stain it, breathe through it, hide under it, shine within it, and are preserved by it after death. They do not conquer the place. They thicken it.

If one had to name the dominant creature, it would be the moss bank: green at the surface, black beneath, full of eggs, spores, roots, beetles, frogs, memory, and hidden water. If one had to name the most characteristic animal, it would be something low and wet-eyed, pausing before each step, listening through its feet. And if one had to describe all the inhabitants together, they are a society of saturated patience: ancient plants crouched in cloud, amphibians born from the seams, birds colored like weather, grazers with careful hooves, predators made of silence, and countless small lives turning cold water and slow decay into a crowded, working world.
