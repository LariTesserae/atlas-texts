What lives there is not a single creature, though there may be one that the keepers speak of as if it were.

The constructed marsh is inhabited by an ecology that has learned architecture the way wild things elsewhere learn weather. Its lives are small, wet, quick, and patient. They do not belong to the place by origin; they belong by persistence. Generations have passed in channels no wider than a hand, in clay cups mortared to walls, in rafters blackened by lamp smoke, beneath warped boards and grilles and leaking valves, until the distinction between animal and mechanism has become less important than the rhythm each keeps.

The most visible residents are the little marsh birds.

They are descendants of larger waders, perhaps herons, rails, bitterns, ibises, or something once brought here for utility or omen. The ceiling made them smaller. The dimness changed their eyes. Their legs remain long in proportion, but thin, jointed, almost tool-like, suited to stepping across tiled seams, trough lips, shallow runnels, and the rims of cisterns. Their feet have become clever. They do not sink; there is nothing soft enough to sink into. Instead they grip wet stone, spread over slick algae, hook themselves into wicker panels, balance on ropes, and stand motionless on copper pipes gone green.

Their plumage is mostly gray, white, soot-brown, and the pale yellow of old bone. Some are streaked with mineral stains from leaning too long under drips. The oldest males develop dark necklaces of mildew-colored feathers around the throat, and the keepers, if they name them at all, probably name them by these markings: Ring-neck, Ash-breast, Green-collar, Split-beak.

They nest wherever a niche can hold a twig. Tool holes, beam pockets, unused gutter mouths, abandoned shrine recesses, the mouths of sealed drains. Their eggs are narrow and bluish, speckled like damp plaster. Their chicks are ugly, naked, and loud, all mouth and tremor, with feet too large for their bodies. They are fed larvae, tiny frogs, pale crabs, eel fry, midges, snails cracked against stone, and occasionally the soft inner parts of their weaker siblings.

They are not graceful birds, not in flight. There is no room for grace. Their wings open halfway, slap air, close, open again. They cross the basin in low, startled bursts, more like thrown rags than flying animals. But when they hunt, they are exact. A bird may stand ten minutes beside a trembling cup of water, its head tilted so one eye can see beneath the surface. Then the beak strikes. The sound is a sharp ceramic click. Something disappears.

They fear the center.

Not all of them, not always. Hunger will send juveniles across the concentric paving, and mating rage will make males fight anywhere. But the experienced birds avoid certain stones. They step wide around dry patches where no slime grows. If frightened toward the drain-pool, they rise rather than run, even though flight under the low ceiling risks striking beams. This avoidance has been inherited or learned or both. A chick led by its parent around those stones remembers the path long before it understands danger.

Below them, in the water’s cracks, live the eel-things.

They are not quite eels, or else they are eels altered by tile, darkness, shallow currents, and old mineral water. They are black, brown, smoke-gray, sometimes translucent at the edges when young. They have narrow heads and tiny eyes filmed over with silver. Their mouths are not large, but they are full of backward-pointing teeth fine as hair combs. They live in culverts, under grates, between loose slabs, inside ceramic pipes, in the throat of the central drain, in the damp dark below any stone that rings hollow.

They are mostly unseen except when water pulses through the eel court. Then they emerge like loosened cords. Their bodies taste the current before their heads commit. One appears, then five, then twenty, overlapping in a slow knot. They feed on eggs, drowned insects, tadpoles, worms, dead birds, bits of offal scraped from keeper buckets. They also feed on things the keepers do not throw in. Lost fingers. Newborn mice. A frog that called from the wrong crack. A chick fallen from the rafters.

Some grow old enough to become pale.

The pale ones are seldom seen in full. A keeper lifting a grate might glimpse a body thick as a wrist sliding away beneath the flooring, white where no light has touched it, scarred by rust flakes and masonry grit. These elder eels know the hidden reservoir, if there is one. They come up when the lamps are low and gather beneath the forbidden stones at the center. They rise to taste brine when fresh water should be flowing. They press their heads against the underside of tiles and listen.

It may be the eels that make the marsh feel watched from below.

The frogs are everywhere.

They are cup-frogs, gutter-frogs, jar-frogs, tile-frogs, little damp throats housed in every niche of water. Most are no larger than a thumb joint. Their skin ranges from clay-brown to translucent green to the color of old candle fat. Many have wide toe pads suited for glazed surfaces. Some live their whole lives in one bowl of the wall of cups, breeding there, calling there, dying there, their bones dissolving into the same mineral ring that held their parents.

Their voices are absurdly large. A frog inside a drainage jar can sound like a child knocking on a door. A cluster in the wall cups can make a pattern of ticks, knocks, croaks, and swallowed notes so regular that a sleepless keeper begins to count with them. They call before the ceiling rain. They call when cisterns fill. They call when certain tiles sweat brine. They call in quickening sequences before the hidden pump exhales.

This has led, inevitably, to beliefs.

Some keepers say the frogs measure the water. Some say they warn the pump when to breathe. Some say the pump taught them to count. Some say none of this, because saying it aloud gives the pattern too much shape.

Their breeding is one of the keeper’s responsibilities. Too many frogs sour a trough; too few and the insects rise in choking veils. Egg strings are lifted on reed forks and moved from warm basins to cold ones, from crowded cups to empty bowls, from dangerous runnels to safe jars. The frogs tolerate this badly but survive it well. A keeper’s hands are always marked with their slime.

The crabs live where mortar fails.

They are pale, flat, and patient, no wider than a coin when young, as broad as a palm when old. Their shells match limewash, green stone, ash, and wet clay. They pick at caulking, algae, bird droppings, dead insects, and one another. They move sideways along seams and vanish when shadow changes.

They are hated by the keepers because they widen damage, but useful because they clean what would rot. A wise keeper removes half and leaves half. A foolish one clears them all and spends the next low-water time scraping black decay from the channels. A lazier one lets them multiply until whole tiles loosen and the basin floor begins to breathe in the wrong places.

The snails are the slow scribes of the marsh.

They graze the walls and leave pale loops through green film. Their shells are small, dark, and glossy, often banded with mineral white. Some seal themselves inside dry cups and wait through low-water time like thoughts paused in the wall. Others live on the underside of beams, where condensation feeds the algae that feed them. They are eaten by birds, crabs, frogs, and the small mammals of the northern shelf, but never vanish. Their trails mark the passage of nights better than any clock.

There are also insects beyond counting.

Midges, gnats, moths with damp-powdered wings, beetles that skate on skin-thin water, larvae curled in bowls, pale grubs beneath wicker panels, mosquitoes whining near lamp heat, water-striders adapted to puddles no larger than plates. Their hatches define abundance. When they rise, the upper air becomes a trembling net of bodies. Bats or swift-like birds cut through them. Frogs gorge until their sides swell. The small waders stab and shriek. Spiders tighten their low bridges and wait.

The spiders are delicate, dull-colored, almost invisible until dew beads on their silk. They do not build high webs; the birds would tear them apart. Instead they string ankle-low nets between pegs, cup rims, reed stumps, and tool handles left too long in place. A careless keeper learns to feel silk against the wrist, the face, the back of the hand. Some spiders live in the handles of broken jars and rush out only when vibration tells them a midge has blundered near.

In the northern shelf live the sleek mammals.

They are perhaps descended from rats, stoats, ferrets, or some domestic thing brought in to control vermin and then abandoned to the basin’s laws. They are long-bodied, dark-furred, slick even when dry, with small ears and bright bead eyes. Their paws are clever. They raid nests, steal eggs, drag frogs from jars, and cache dead things in cracks above the waterline. They are fearless in the way of animals that know every escape route.

They are half-tame only because they do not flee immediately from people. They watch. They wait for the keeper to lift a grate or overturn a basket. They know the sound of a jar lid, the scrape of a reed knife, the soft curse that precedes spilled larvae. Some individual animals follow the keeper at a distance, not affectionate, not loyal, but interested in opportunity.

The birds hate them. The eels take their young when they fall. The keepers tolerate them because without them the northern shelf would fill with nest-rot, beetles, and worse things.

Then there are the bats, or the swifts, or whatever intermediate gloom-fliers own the upper dark.

They sleep in seams where beams meet masonry. When resting they look like knots of wet cloth. When flying they are quick, angular, almost soundless except for a papery snap at the turn. Some have fur. Some have feathers. Perhaps both kinds live there and are merely confused by the light. They feed during hatches, skim the wall of cups, vanish through ceiling slits too narrow for a human hand.

They are the only creatures that regularly leave.

Because of this, they carry news the marsh cannot understand: pollen, spores, outside insects, traces of weather, perhaps disease. Their droppings fall into cisterns and begin small changes. A new lichen blooms. A reed sickens. A frog clutch fails. A keeper notices, eventually, and mutters at the ceiling.

The plants are residents too, though subdued ones.

Moss clings where it can. Algae skin the channels. Pale reeds stand apart like measuring rods. The dwarf willows in cracked tubs are old enough to be individuals. Pollarded again and again, they have become fists of wood rather than trees. Their roots clutch broken pottery, old bones, brick fragments, coins, and whatever offerings were once pressed into their mud. They sprout weak green wands in condensation time and shed them in quiet rot.

The keepers may cut them back, but not kill them. Perhaps the willows are useful for withes. Perhaps they are older than the current lineage. Perhaps one grows from the first basin’s mud, and removing it would be an act too much like ending the place.

The human inhabitants, if they can be called inhabitants, live at the edge.

A keeper does not occupy the marsh the way the frogs do. A keeper passes through, tends, listens, interferes, apologizes, curses, repairs, steals life from one pool to lend it to another. Their dwelling is probably just outside the basin proper: a dry alcove, a raised room, a narrow gallery with a charcoal brazier and pegs for tools. They smell permanently of damp cloth, oil, frog slime, smoke, and old leather. Their fingers are ridged from water. Their knees ache. Their hearing is unnaturally sharp.

They are not priests, though they may perform rituals. They are not engineers, though they understand machines. They are not farmers, though they harvest and cull. They are more like translators between masonry and appetite.

They know that the marsh is built, but they do not make the mistake of thinking that built things are obedient.

A good keeper knows where to step during low-water time and where never to step even when the surface looks sound. They know which troughs can be allowed to sour and which must be flushed immediately. They know the safe eels from the old ones by the pattern of ripples. They can hear when a leather valve has stiffened. They can tell from the silence of the wall cups whether bad air has entered. They mark hatchings, deaths, cracks, and water heights in scripts layered over older scripts, not because the marsh can be fully recorded, but because forgetting is dangerous.

They may have children, and those children learn first by prohibition.

Do not put your fingers in the eel court.

Do not drink from the middle cups.

Do not cross the dry stones when the lamps are low.

Do not answer if the frogs count your steps.

Do not sleep beside the drain-pool.

Do not pull a feather from the grille.

Do not trust water that appears only when watched.

The children grow up able to walk slick tile without looking down. They learn animal calls before letters. They learn to sleep through dripping but wake at a stopped wheel. Some leave, if there is somewhere to leave to. Some remain and become narrow-shouldered, damp-eyed, and quiet, as if the low ceiling has shaped not only their posture but their expectations of the world.

There may also be something else living there, though it is difficult to say whether it is creature, system, habit, or the accumulated intention of the place.

It is associated with the central drain.

Not confined to it. Associated with it.

It is present when bubbles rise through the black water beneath the rusted grille though no inflow has changed. It is present when the pump exhales in answer to frogs. It is present when certain cisterns refill unobserved, when brine seeps from fresh cracks, when birds avoid stones that have no smell a human can detect. It is present in the way the eels gather beneath the dry center and wait with their heads all pointed inward.

Perhaps below the basin is an older reservoir, a buried tidal chamber, a cistern connected to the sea, a drowned foundation, a sealed ritual tank, a natural spring trapped by masonry. Perhaps something lives in that cold hidden water: huge, slow, blind, unable to rise through the grille but able to breathe through the whole marsh. Perhaps the constructed wetland is not an imitation of a marsh but a lung built for whatever lies underneath.

Or perhaps there is no monster at all.

Perhaps the thing that lives there is the balance itself.

A mind can be made of repeated signals: dripping, calling, flowing, clogging, hatching, feeding, rotting, repair. The frogs count. The pump answers. The eels taste. The birds avoid. The keeper listens. The water shifts through grooves that should not lift it and cups that fill when unwatched. Over centuries, feedback becomes behavior. Behavior becomes temperament. Temperament becomes almost a will.

In that sense, the marsh lives there.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. It uses animals as valves and alarms. It uses moss to hold damp against brick. It uses birds to reduce insects and insects to feed birds. It uses crabs to open seams and keep rot from closing them. It uses keepers as mobile hands, memory-bearing mammals trained by consequence. It punishes neglect not with malice but with bloom, souring, hatch, crack, fever, silence.

It does not want as people want. But it tends toward continuance.

It draws every abandoned object into use. It turns broken bowls into nurseries, dead lamps into beetle houses, old bones into mineral shelves, warnings into perches, shrines into nesting walls. Nothing remains merely itself. Everything becomes habitat, instrument, symptom, or food.

So the inhabitants are many: birds with tile-click feet, blind eels under the flooring, frogs in clay cups, crabs in the mortar, snails writing on algae, insects rising like weather, spiders rigging the low dark, sleek mammals hunting the dry shelf, bats stitching the air beneath the ceiling, tired keepers moving by sound.

And beneath or among them all lives the made marsh’s deeper resident: a damp, distributed, half-mechanical life that has learned to maintain itself through hunger.

It is not wilderness. It is not ruin.

It is a creature assembled out of maintenance, and everything inside it is one of its organs.
