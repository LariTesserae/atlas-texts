What lives there are not creatures in the soft sense, but continuations of the plateau by other means.

They are dry life. Mineral life. Heat-life. Bodies assembled not from thirst and blood and green inheritance, but from silica, carbon, vitrified salts, metal oxides, pressure seams, static charge, and the old convulsive patience of geology. They live as if flesh was never invented. As if the first organisms here did not crawl out of water but condensed around vents, sparked into pattern where hot vapor met the high oxidized air, and learned, over immense time, to move without ever becoming moist.

They are difficult to distinguish from terrain until they decide otherwise.

A plain of pale stones will begin to tick in sequence and rise on a million hair-thin legs. A cliff face will loosen into ranks of shield-backed things and slide down into the basin. A field of black crystals will fold its spines inward and migrate overnight, leaving the ground beneath it brighter, scraped, subtly annealed. They have the colors of the place because they are made of it: bone-white ceramic, smoked glass, slag-black enamel, copper stain, dull chrome, mica gold, the faint blue of overheated steel. Even their shadows seem inorganic—hard-edged, machined, more like apertures cut in the light than darkness cast by living bodies.

The smallest are scarcely individuals. They are grains with intention, splinters of animated mineral chemistry moving in swarms so dense they resemble poured filings. Each has a shell like a seed of pumice or a flake of obsidian, under which clusters of jointed needles test the ground. They travel by sensing heat gradients, static fields, chemical breath rising through hairline fractures. Alone, one is nearly nothing: a clicking mote, a dry spark of appetite. Together they behave like weather. They darken slopes, comb over carcasses, assemble into rings around warm stones, dismantle anything too regular or foreign, and leave behind a changed sheen on the crust, as though they have polished meaning into it.

Some live only in the black seams.

These are seam-readers: long, flat organisms with articulated backs and sensory vanes that unfold like soot-colored fans. They do not see upward well. Their world is depth, resonance, and interval. They lower filamentous limbs into cracks and interpret the echoes from impossible bottoms. Their shells are usually pale on top, carbon-dark beneath, so that when they cross open flats they resemble fragments of shadow that have acquired legs. When alarmed, they flatten so completely that wind-driven grit passes over them as over stone. When feeding, they insert hardened proboscises into the seams and drink not fluid but differential—heat against cold, pressure against release, chemical imbalance pulled thin as wire.

Larger things roll.

The rolling herds are among the most visible inhabitants of the plateau: many-chambered spherical bodies, from the size of a skull to the size of a cartwheel or larger, plated in interlocking ceramic scales. They move with a laborious, deliberate clatter. Their outer shells are not wheels exactly, but rotating armatures around a suspended core. As they cross the flats, the plates open and close in sequence, exposing flashes of inner structure: fibrous mineral tissue, glowing ducts, ciliated rings of black glass that taste the air.

They do not leave tracks because they distribute their weight too perfectly. A herd of ten thousand may pass over a brittle enamel pan and leave only a change in reflectivity, a slight dulling where their shells harvested charge from the surface. At rest they arrange themselves into chains, spirals, lattices, or piled barricades against the night wind. During thermal storms they lock together and hum, becoming temporary instruments through which the plateau’s static discharges pass in blue crawling arcs.

There are needle-walkers too, almost delicate despite their refractory bodies.

These stand on forests of trembling legs, each leg thinner than wire and tipped with a sensory bead. Their torsos are low, elongated, plated with translucent silica that shows shadowy movement within: little pistons, flexing membranes, spark-lit cavities where gases mix and cool. They are browsers of invisible pastures. They wander the warm flats in loose, angular formations, lowering combs of filaments to sweep up charged dust and catalytic grains. When the sun is strongest, their backs open into faceted panels that track the light with a slow precision. From far away they look like spilled machinery. Up close they are unmistakably alive, though not in any comforting way. Their motion has hesitation, preference, correction. They pause at one patch of bare crust and ignore another identical patch beside it. They remember, but perhaps not with minds—perhaps with stress patterns in their shells, with magnetized scars, with the orientation of crystals grown inside them.

The tallest beings are the vent-grazers.

They gather where the ground breathes.

From a distance they could be mistaken for abandoned derricks, skeletal mills, or signal towers built by a vanished industrial species. But there was no vanished species. The towers are themselves alive. Their bodies are open frameworks of ceramic struts, grown joint by joint around a central column of rotating vanes. Mica membranes flex between ribs. Metallic tendons tighten and slacken in the heat. Their feet are wide, rootless tripods or hexapods that grip the crust around vent mouths without sinking.

They feed by standing in the exhaust of the underworld.

When black dust and transparent fumes rise from cracks, the vent-grazers unfold intake organs like dry flowers made of foil. They comb the vapor for metals, sulfurous radicals, charged particles, and heat. Inside their lattice bodies, the captured substances condense into threads, rods, and braided filaments, some of which become new tissue and some of which are expelled as curled metallic waste. During feeding, their vanes spin almost silently, though their joints creak like cooling iron. At dusk, when the vents glow red and blue-white beneath them, whole herds of these high latticed creatures stand with their membranes extended, turning slowly in the thermal currents like a forest of thinking instruments.

They are not grazers because they eat plants. There are no plants.

They are grazers because they stand in fields.

Their fields are radiance, vapor, charge, and disequilibrium.

Among them move the patient predators.

Predation here is not pursuit. Pursuit belongs to worlds with blood, panic, and short distances. On the plateau, hunters use time.

The burrowing crescents are the most feared, if fear exists among such beings. They are broad, flat, moon-shaped bodies with cutting rims and undersides of black flexible enamel. They sink beneath the crust by softening seams with secreted heat and vibrating until the mineral plates let them pass. Below the surface they travel slowly, guided by pressure and the patterned tremor of herds above. They may spend days positioning themselves beneath a vent field. Their presence is first betrayed by a change in sound: the ground’s roar thickens; clicking feet fall slightly out of rhythm; reflections on the enamel pans begin to show a dark crescent where nothing stands.

When they rise, they do not leap. The ground swells. Plates tilt. A shallow dome forms beneath the herd. Then the crescent opens like a trap beneath several animals at once. It engulfs legs, lower shells, vent-roots, rolling cores. The prey fold gradually, often without visible struggle, their vanes slowing, their panels closing, their heat draining into the predator’s broad body. A kill may take an afternoon. The final stillness is marked not by silence, but by a change in pitch as the predator seals itself and sinks again, leaving behind a glazed depression and a scatter of empty shell fragments.

There are aerial organisms as well, though the air seems too thin to hold them.

They are hinged kites, thermal gliders, furnace-bellied things with rigid spars and membranes like smoked mica. Their bodies are mostly surface area. They climb on heat columns above vent fields and crowded inclines, flexing with tiny adjustments that catch invisible gradients. Some are no larger than hands. Others cast shadows like passing sails. Their undersides glow faintly in oranges and reds, not with fire but with stored heat and internal reaction. They feed on dust-creatures carried upward by convection, snapping shut around them with the dry sound of clapped slate. When they descend to roost, they hook themselves onto stone blades or the upper frameworks of vent-grazers, tolerated perhaps because they clean the air of parasites, or because removing them would cost more energy than enduring them.

The plateau also supports organisms that scarcely move at all.

Against escarpments and around old vent scars grow colonies that resemble mineral bushes, glass cages, or metallic coral. Some of these are merely chemistry. Some are alive. The living colonies reveal themselves by rearranging over months: a thorn turned toward a new wind, a hexagonal twig thickened to catch more charge, a hollow node sealed after a storm. They are patient condensers. They collect airborne minerals, static discharge, and the faint chemical offerings of the wind. Tiny motile creatures shelter among their brittle branches, and in return abrade their surfaces clean. At dawn and dusk these colonies tick loudly as they expand or contract, creating whole slopes of dry music.

There are also mirror-herds.

They may be animals, or optical phenomena, or both.

On the salt-bright pans, one sometimes sees inverted bodies moving below the surface, although the surface is opaque and solid. These lower creatures resemble enlarged versions of the herds above: greater lattices, deeper rolling spheres, predators with many crescents nested inside one another. Sometimes their motion matches the visible animals; sometimes it anticipates them; sometimes it ignores them entirely. The inhabitants of the plateau respond to these reflections as if they are real. Rolling herds alter course when their inverted counterparts gather below. Needle-walkers avoid pans where their reflections arrive before they do. Vent-grazers sometimes align not with the sun or the vents, but with a dark tower visible only beneath the crust.

Whether these mirror-beings live in another layer of the world, in the plateau’s warped light, or in some pressure-memory of possible evolution is unclear. But they participate. Their presence influences migration, feeding, and fear. In a place where perspective is weather and causality does not always travel in the same direction as sound, a reflected animal may be as ecological as a solid one.

Communication among the inhabitants is mostly not vocal, though the plateau is never quiet.

They speak in vibration, alignment, heat-shadow, mineral scent, charge pulses, reflected geometry. A herd turning by three degrees may be answering a tremor from a vent field fifty miles away. A chain of rolling spheres clicking in sequence may be mapping a fissure’s depth for all who can feel it. The great hornlike calls that cross the plateau at long intervals may not come from mouths at all, but from hollow-bodied animals coupling themselves to resonant mesas, using geology as a throat.

Their senses are correspondingly alien.

They know the world through temperature with the intimacy that wet creatures know touch. They read the age of a surface by its thermal lag. They sense buried hollows as coolness shaped in stone. They detect living bodies by their charge, their stress, the timing of clicks within their shells. Light matters, but not always as image. To many of them, the sun may be less a visible disk than a governing pressure, a directional command written into every warmed plate and shadow. Sound is not separate from touch. Distance is not always linear. A vent’s future pulse may be perceptible before its present exhalation arrives.

Their reproduction is equally dry.

Some bud mineral eggs in vent fields: hard nodules laid in warm cracks, where vapor infiltrates their porous shells and deposits the first scaffolding of a new body. Some shed plates that continue growing after abandonment, slowly developing legs along their undersides when the chemistry is right. Rolling herds exchange charge by locking together overnight; weeks later, tiny spheres detach from the joints between adults and scatter downslope with the first morning winds. The latticed grazers grow their young like internal frameworks, releasing them as folded tripods no taller than a person, already bearing miniature vanes that twitch toward heat.

Death does not return them to soil, because there is no soil.

Death returns them to process.

Shells crack, delaminate, and are worried apart by swarms. Vanes become glittering drifts. Ceramic bones are ground by wind into abrasive powder. Carbon-black enamel flakes collect in seam lips. Metallic tissues catalyze new colonies. A carcass may remain recognizable for years if it falls in an empty interval, or vanish in a day if it collapses near a migration front. Nothing rots. It oxidizes, vitrifies, sublimates, fractures, and is consumed grain by grain.

What passes for intelligence may be distributed.

No single species appears to rule the plateau. There are no cities because the landscape itself already performs the functions of city, factory, road, furnace, and archive. Yet the assemblies around vents, the spirals of herds, the synchronized opening of fault-throats, the way animals alter course in response to horn-calls from unseen distances—all suggest a coordination too large to fit inside one skull, if skulls are even present. The plateau may think in migrations. It may remember through polished fault lips and repeated paths. It may regulate itself through appetite, resonance, and extinction. Its inhabitants may be organs of a planetary mechanism that has no center because every seam, vent, herd, and thermal current is part of its nervous system.

They are ancient without being primitive.

Their forms are spare because waste was edited out ages ago. Their motions are slow because haste has little value where heat is abundant, water absent, and a feeding site may persist for ten thousand years. They are not unfinished imitations of animals from wetter worlds. They are complete answers to a different question.

What lives there are kiln-born herds and seismic grazers, rolling shells and needle-footed swarms, vent towers with mica lungs, crescent predators beneath ceramic crust, glass-bush colonies ticking in the heat, kite-things riding furnace air, and perhaps reflected leviathans moving in the false undersides of the flats.

They are the fauna of a dry engine.

They do not soften the desolation. They intensify it. They make the emptiness populous without making it welcoming, make the machinery biological without making it designed. They belong to a world where life never learned to be green, where hunger is a thermodynamic preference, where bodies click, ring, glow, and fracture, and where even an animal is partly a geological event that has learned to move.
