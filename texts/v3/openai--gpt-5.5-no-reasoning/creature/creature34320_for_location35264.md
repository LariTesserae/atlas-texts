They are a sparse people of wet stone and measured hands.

They live as though the town itself is older than they are, even if the mountains are not. Their bodies belong to terraces, steps, channels, roof edges, retaining walls. They move carefully not because they are timid, but because the ground teaches care. Every path may shine with thin water. Every wall may be listening to pressure from behind. Every basin has an order in the larger descent of things.

They are not loud. In a place without birds, insects, livestock, or running beasts, a human voice is too large unless lowered. So their speech is soft, close, practical. They call across courtyards only when necessary. Children learn early how far sound travels in mist. Laughter exists, but it is usually brief and covered by hands or tucked into doorways, like fire shielded from damp air.

They do not fill the town. They inhabit it in intervals.

One terrace may hold three families and a roofed work platform. The next may hold only a cistern, moss, and a tree whose leaves collect cloud-water. Another may have a small plastered shrine, a drying rack, and a channel that must never be stepped in. They are used to crossing emptiness between neighbors. A visit involves steps, slick paving stones, a little bridge of slabs, a turn beside a wall that weeps in two places. Distance inside the town is accepted. It gives everyone room to hear water.

Their skin would be accustomed to dampness, not to cold. Weather has softened them in some ways and toughened them in others. Their hands are broad, with thickened palms from stone, hoe handles, wet rope, clay lids, timber pegs. Their nails are kept short. Their feet are sure on uneven surfaces. Many go barefoot or in woven sandals when within their own district, feeling the slickness and temperature of the stone as one more kind of knowledge. In colder mist they wrap their calves and shoulders in coarse cloth treated with oil, wax, or smoke.

Their clothing is simple, layered, quick to lift clear of mud and water. Tunics of woven reed fiber, bark cloth, linen from some damp-tolerant plant, or trade-woven cloth from the lower valleys. Short cloaks for mist. Aprons for channel work. Headcloths against dripping roofs. Nothing trails if the person works near sluices. Dyes are muted because the air dims everything eventually: clay red, mineral yellow, soot black, reed brown, moss green, the grey-white of limewash. Bright color may be saved for indoor cords, ritual knots, children’s bands, the edge of a marriage cloth.

They smell faintly of damp wool or plant fiber, smoke, lime dust, crushed green stems, and wet clay.

Their houses are not private in the sealed sense, but neither are they open. Interiors are thick-walled and dry by discipline rather than by climate. Every dry corner is earned. Floors may be raised on stone pads or timber grids. Sleeping spaces are above storage when possible. Seeds, salt, dried roots, written tallies if they have writing, ceremonial objects, medicines, and fire-kindling are kept high. Ladders matter. Shelves matter. Hooks matter. A careless person who leaves a sack on a floor is not scolded dramatically; someone simply moves it, and later an elder explains why that must not happen again.

They build low because weight and water are always in negotiation. No one admires height for its own sake. A tall wall is not impressive; it is suspicious. Beauty is in a cleanly seated stone, a channel with the right fall, plaster that sheds seepage without cracking, a roof edge that drips into the jar and not beside it. Their craft has an inward elegance. Visitors from drier places might miss it at first. Then they would notice that no cistern overflows wastefully, that roof water enters three households before joining the garden run, that steps are angled just enough to spare ankles, that moss is removed in some joints and encouraged in others.

They are gardeners more than farmers, keepers more than conquerors.

Without animals, their agriculture has a particular quietness. No herding calls. No manure carts. No beehives. No chickens worrying the yard edges. Fertility comes from composted vegetation, human waste carefully aged and sealed, ash, silt from channels, crushed mineral, leaf mold gathered from groves. They know rot intimately. They know which pits heat well and which go sour. Clay lids over compost are not merely practical but moral objects: covers between the clean world and the becoming world.

Their food is wetland and terrace food: roots, tubers, broad leaves, sour greens, soft grains if any grain will grow there, sedge pith, cultivated mushrooms in shaded stone rooms, algae or water plants from clear basins, fruits from shrubs warmed against walls. Perhaps they grow small highland beans, not for abundance but for strength. They ferment much because drying is difficult. Sour pastes, leaf cakes, root beer, pickled stems, mineral salt from trade, smoked herbs hanging where smoke can reach but damp cannot. Their cuisine is probably warm, thick, earthy, tart. Bowls rather than plates. Stews kept low over banked embers. Cakes steamed between leaves. Food that accepts water instead of resisting it.

Fire is precious but not rare. Smoke does not rise easily in damp weather; every household knows how to coax it. Hearths are built with a draft-stone and a dry shelf for kindling. Children may be tasked with keeping splints wrapped and elevated. A house that lets its tinder mold is considered poorly governed.

Because there are no animals, their myths do not begin with beasts.

They do not say the raven brought light, or the frog called rain, or the goat opened the path. Their stories belong to stone, cloud, moss, spring, clay, echo, mist, seep. Ancestors may be compared to terraces: those who hold what comes from above so those below may live. A foolish person may be called “one who roofs a spring.” A dangerous one, “a wall without weep-holes.” A generous one, “a basin that clears after rain.”

They may believe that water is not owned, only delayed.

The central ethics of the town likely arise from this. To steal water is not simply to take a thing; it is to break sequence. It harms unknown people below you, gardens you cannot see, walls you did not build, future storage, perhaps the dead. A blocked channel is both practical emergency and spiritual offense. The whole town’s law could almost be derived from drainage.

Their leaders, if they have leaders, are not kings in high seats. They are water-readers, wall-setters, store-keepers, elders of terraces, people trusted to remember gradients and failures. Authority belongs to those who can say: this wall shifted in the year of long mist; this basin silts every third rain; do not plant roots there until the clay rests; the lower cistern tastes of iron because the pipe is cracked above the bend. Memory is infrastructure.

They might have councils arranged not by bloodline alone but by level: upper terraces, middle cistern yards, lower gardens, outer marsh shelves. Everyone depends on everyone else vertically. What the upper houses neglect, the lower houses receive. What the lower gardens overdraw, the upper storage must answer for later. Their politics is therefore hydrological. Disputes travel downhill.

Writing, if they possess it, may have begun as marks for water and storage: notches on plaster tablets, incised stones beside sluices, knotted cords kept dry in roof spaces. A symbol for “settle,” one for “do not draw,” one for “silt cleared,” one for “wall unsafe,” one for “seed dry.” Their script, if developed, may be angular like channels cut into stone, or flowing but interrupted by marks for gates and drops.

They are excellent listeners.

Not in the sentimental sense, but technically. They can hear when a roof gutter is clogged. They can hear when water falls into an empty basin instead of a half-full one. They know the sound of seepage behind a wall, the wrong hollow under a step, the dullness of saturated clay. After heavy mist or rain, people walk the lanes in near silence, heads tilted slightly, attending. Children are told to hush not because quiet is sacred in the abstract, but because danger and maintenance speak softly.

Their children play, but even play is shaped by the town. They float leaf boats down permitted channels and learn where each one emerges. They build tiny walls of clay and watch them fail. They race on dry days along terraces but not on polished steps. They memorize the names of pools. They are frightened, perhaps, not of wolves or snakes, but of sinkholes, sudden slips, cistern depths, and being caught outdoors when the mist makes every terrace edge vanish.

Since there are no animals, loneliness may enter them differently. They grow up without the companionable unpredictability of creatures. No dogs at thresholds, no birds stealing seed, no moths at lamps. The only other living presences are plants and people. This may make them attentive to vegetation in a way others reserve for animals. Individual trees are known almost as neighbors. Moss beds have histories. A vine trained by a grandmother is spoken of as still “hers.” A child may be given responsibility for a single basin-plant and judged by its condition.

Death is probably handled with great concern for water.

They may not bury bodies casually in wet ground. The dead cannot be allowed to foul springs or loosen slopes. Perhaps they are dried with smoke in special upper houses, wrapped in reed mats, and placed in stone chambers built into dry ridges. Perhaps bones are cleaned by time and mineral air, then stacked in lime-plastered niches. Perhaps ashes are mixed into clay for memorial tablets, never scattered into channels. Their dead remain above the water, watching the descent but not entering it.

Rituals would be quiet and procedural. Opening the first upper cistern after a dry interval. Clearing the main channel at the start of the wet season. Replastering a spring shrine. Setting a child’s first foot on the public steps. Carrying seed from high storage to prepared beds. Testing a new retaining wall by letting water stand behind it in measured stages. These acts may involve songs, but low ones, with long pauses, so that the song does not cover the sound of flow.

They may have music made from things that tolerate damp: stone chimes, wooden clappers kept indoors, reed flutes that must be dried carefully, skinless drums of hollow wood and struck boards. Rhythms imitate drip, settling, the alternating lift and scrape of clearing silt. Their songs may not soar; they descend, step by step.

They are not primitive in mind. Their intelligence is simply not metallic, explosive, or fast. They have refined patience. They understand load, slope, retention, seepage, timing, mildew, storage, germination, and social restraint. They know how to make a town persist where the mountain is still deciding its own shape.

Their fears are correspondingly slow.

A month of unusual dryness would disturb them deeply. Water absent from its usual lip. A channel silent at dawn. Moss whitening. Clay cracking on the south walls. Seeds failing to swell. They would not panic at first; they would measure. Then gates would close. Drawing rights would tighten. Meals would thin. People would walk farther down into the marsh flats and return with stories of exposed mud.

Too much rain would frighten them in another way. Not storm terror, but pressure terror. Walls sweating thickly. Doors swelling shut. Basins refusing to clear. The sound behind plaster. A terrace edge bowing outward by the width of a thumb. In such times no one sleeps deeply. Households take turns watching the walls with lamps shielded from mist.

They may be wary of outsiders from below, not because they hate strangers, but because strangers do not know how to step. They block channels with packs. They wash in settling basins. They speak too loudly near listening walks. They admire the wrong things. They ask where the market is when the town’s true center is the water sequence. But hospitality exists: a dry sleeping platform, a bowl of sour greens and root stew, a place near the hearth, instructions repeated gently until obeyed.

Trade, when it comes, comes on human backs. Salt, copper, bronze blades, hard dry wood, resin, perhaps cloth, pigments, medicinal bark from lower forests. In return the town offers lime, worked stone, preserved greens, highland herbs, clean water in drought years, knowledge of terraces, and maybe rare mineral deposits exposed in the young cliffs. Their traders are strong-legged and quiet, uncomfortable in noisy animal-filled lowlands where flies gather and birds scream in trees.

They might find lowland abundance vulgar and miraculous: the clutter of life, the waste, the smell of dung, the speed of markets, the disorder of rivers with fish in them. Returning home, they would pause at the first silent basin and feel the world become intelligible again.

Their faces, in imagination, are often turned slightly downward—not submissively, but in attention to footing and flow. They have weather-softened eyes from mist-light, not narrowed against snow or desert glare. They may age into stillness. Old people sit beneath eaves and watch water enter jars. They know from the sound whether the roof edge needs trimming.

Their houses may contain few decorations, but those few matter: polished stones with natural veins like watercourses; plaster reliefs of terraces and cloud bands; woven mats patterned in stepped lines; clay bowls glazed, if glaze is known, in mineral blue-white; ancestral handprints pressed into lime near doorways. Ornament follows structure. A beautiful object teaches descent, holding, release.

Their language would be rich in kinds of wetness. Words for mist that wets hair but not cloth. Rain that darkens plaster but does not fill channels. Seep from living rock versus seep from failed walls. Standing water safe for roots, standing water unsafe for storage. Clay that shines, clay that slips, clay that drinks, clay that has died. They may have fewer words for animals than other peoples, or use old borrowed terms almost mythically.

They probably do not think of silence as absence. To them silence is the normal body of the world. Into it come events: a footstep, a tool strike, a cough, a sluice opened, a child calling. Animal noise, if heard in travel, might seem excessive, almost like weather made nervous.

What lives there, then, is a human culture shaped around young geology and obedient water.

Also moss lives there, and perhaps moss is the second citizen.

Moss occupies the joints, roof edges, shaded stone backs, old mortar lines, unused terraces, rims of basins. It records neglect and tenderness alike. People read it. Too much moss in a channel is danger. Moss on a roof edge is acceptable until it redirects the drip. Moss on a memorial stone is allowed unless it hides the name. Children peel it where they should not and are made to press it back. Lovers may meet on a terrace known for its thick green quiet. The elderly may prefer moss-grown steps because they show where no one hurries.

Plants live there as opportunists, servants, and witnesses. Sedges in the drainage margins. Broad-leaved greens with rain cupped in their centers. Pale reeds clicking softly only when a human passes. Smooth-barked trees that stand apart from houses like calm relatives. Vines permitted to climb but not devour. Fruit shrubs trained to warm walls. Root crops swelling invisibly in damp beds. None of it wild in the ravenous sense. Everything negotiates with stone.

Water lives there most of all.

Not as a river god, not as spectacle, but as a distributed presence, a many-bodied resident. It sleeps in cisterns, walks in channels, breathes through wall cracks, gathers under flagstones, beads on leaf tips, stains plaster, polishes steps, feeds roots, loosens clay, remembers every error. The people do not master it. They host it. They slow it, divide it, listen to it, apologize to it through maintenance.

And beneath all of them lives the young mountain: not dead foundation, but a restless, cooling, settling creature. Its bones are close to the surface. Its scars have not healed. Its clay still surprises. The town survives by never forgetting that the mountain is still becoming.

So the inhabitants are not numerous, not hurried, not loud. They are keepers of gradients, gardeners of damp shelves, builders of low roofs and thick walls, listeners after rain. They live between seep and sky with the understanding that permanence is not something imposed on such a place. It is something requested daily, by hand, in silence.
