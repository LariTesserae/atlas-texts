What lives there is not an animal, nor a people, nor even a vegetation in the ordinary sense.

The city itself lives there.

Not metaphorically. Not as ruins “reclaimed” by growth. The basin is occupied by a single vast, distributed organism or organism-like condition: a civic ecology whose organs are terraces, pools, roots, mineral walls, falling water, heat, and sound. It is a living place that has taken the shape of a city because city-shape is how it thinks.

Its inhabitants are not separate bodies moving through streets. They are the streets.

They live as wet masonry that sweats continuously. They live as root systems threaded through load-bearing stone. They live as canals that remember direction differently from gravity. They live as reed courts, roof pools, mineral halls, hanging gardens, black reservoirs, aerial roots, and stairways that arrive where the organism needs continuity rather than where geometry expects them to arrive.

There may once have been builders, or animals, or visitors, but if so they have been digested from history. Not consumed violently — translated. Their absence remains as a kind of mold or negative population. The city contains the idea of inhabitants without containing their bodies. It has doorways because arrival is one of its functions. It has plazas because gathering is one of its shapes. It has avenues because procession is an organ. It has chambers because enclosure is necessary to its metabolism. But the thing that gathers, proceeds, enters, waits, and observes is the place itself.

The plants are not passive growth. They are its soft tissues.

The enormous leaves are surfaces of exchange, drinking condensed mineral air and exhaling heat into heat. The vines are ligaments and curtains, closing and opening sightlines according to laws older than weather. The moss is skin, accepting fracture and sealing it into permanence. The reeds are sensory bristles standing in ankle-deep courts, registering verticality as truth. The flowers are not invitations to insects; they are private glands, releasing color and fragrance as internal signals. Their swollen violet, wax-white, furnace-orange blooms are not advertisements. They are thoughts expressed chemically.

The water is its circulation.

It does not merely fall; it courses, returns, remembers, repeats. The waterfalls are arteries exposed to air. The canals are veins that sometimes climb walls because the body’s need overrides the world’s habit. The black pools are reservoirs of attention. The cisterns are chambers of digestion or memory. The droplets that tremble forever without falling are not failures of motion but held impulses, nerve-signals paused at thresholds.

Nothing floods because the organism knows its own volume perfectly. Nothing drains because drainage would be loss. Water arrives everywhere and departs nowhere visible, because it is not passing through the city: it is being circulated by it.

The stone is its skeleton, but a skeleton still growing.

It is limestone, basalt, calcified plant matter, pressure, old seepage, and intention. It bears weight not like dead rock but like bone that has accepted architecture. Mineral veins glimmer like buried nerves. The walls are hard where hardness is needed, porous where listening is required, smooth where water must speak, broken where breakage has become grammar. Erosion has not damaged it. Erosion has instructed it.

The heat is its breath.

Heavy, saturated, inescapable — a respiration without wind. The city does not inhale and exhale in gusts. It maintains a single immense held breath, warm and green and mineral, pressing downward into every court and passage. The heat keeps decay from becoming rot and motion from becoming change. It is not climate. It is homeostasis.

The sound is its voice.

The roar of water is the most obvious layer, but underneath it are deeper articulations: the stone hum, the pressure in the teeth, the vowel-tones drawn from the mountain walls, the repeated slaps of falling water that come too regularly to be accidental. These are not background noises. They are communication, though not meant for ears.

In some places sound has weight because the city’s voice has settled there. In some courtyards sound stands upright because a statement is being held in place. In the drowned amphitheater, silence floats near the ceiling because silence is lighter than speech. The city is always saying itself. Its deafening constancy is not monotony but maintenance: a chant by which its laws remain local, district by district, basin by basin.

What lives there may be one being, but not a centralized one.

It has no brain hidden in the central reservoir, though that reservoir may be where its awareness is deepest. It has no heart, unless every falling sheet of water is a heart. It has no eyes, yet the place feels observed because observation is distributed across reflective pools, wet stone, mineral memory, and surfaces that receive pressure. A body standing there would be perceived not by sight but by displacement: warmth interrupted, moisture redirected, silence altered, verticality briefly complicated.

It does not watch from somewhere. It watches as everywhere.

Its consciousness, if that word can be used, is architectural and hydrological. It knows adjacency, saturation, pressure, reflection, load, echo, and return. It does not think in moments. It thinks in balances. It does not remember time as sequence but as accepted form. A broken stair is not “after” a break; it is a stair whose truth includes fracture. A vine is not older today than yesterday; it is established at the degree of existence proper to it. Footsteps in the mineral quarter appear later because the stone remembers pressure more honestly than occurrence.

The absence of animals is essential. Animals would introduce appetite, alarm, irregular passage, disturbance, predation, waste, improvisation. This place has no room for hunger. Its lushness is post-hunger or pre-hunger, a closed abundance requiring no mouths. Leaves are uneaten because consumption has been removed from the laws of the basin. Flowers do not solicit because desire has no pollinating body here. Seeds, if seeds exist, arrive by necessity rather than dispersal.

Life here is not a chain.

It is a circuit.

Plant, water, mineral, heat, and sound feed one another without visible loss. The city is an ecology without fauna, a body without organs in the animal sense, a civilization without citizens. Its districts are specialized tissues, each with its own compact of reality. In the reed courts, the vertical stalk, column, root, fall of water, and standing body are granted firmness because the city there values uprightness. In the mineral quarter, pressure matters more than chronology because stone is the chief sense. In the hanging gardens, moisture governs distance because wetness is kinship. In the drowned amphitheater, sound has mass because listening has become structure.

Perhaps the mountain walls are part of it too.

They may not be enclosing the city so much as grown up from it: raised rims around a permanent wound, dark and veined and sweating. Their peaks vanish into whitened distance because perception thins before altitude does. They are the city’s outer carapace, or its memory of having once been land. Their weeping faces feed the terraces. Their mineral throats produce the long bowed tones beneath the waterfalls. Their gorges may be roots descending into other basins, other organs, other versions of the same impossible settlement.

If there are “individuals” here, they are not persons but local presences.

A bridge may be an individual: woody ribs, mineral crust, capillary water, moss cushions, carrying passage as a living act.

A basin may be an individual: black, still, unruffled by falling water, holding reflections of skies and absences beyond any present angle.

A doorway may be an individual: faithful when faced, immense and green when glimpsed aside, regulating the difference between approach and possibility.

A flower may be an individual: a sealed furnace of color, blooming for the internal weather of the city.

A stair may be an individual: descending into water, emerging elsewhere, not misleading but obeying another continuity.

But all of them belong to the greater inhabitant, as cells belong to flesh, as neighborhoods belong to a metropolis, as organs belong to a body whose mind is too slow and too spread out to be met face to face.

The central reservoir may be the closest thing to a face.

Its black stillness amid symmetrical falling water suggests an aperture, not a pool. It receives endless impact without rippling because it is not water in the usual sense; it is a held depth, a pupil, a listening surface. The reflections it shows — white roots in a high sky, dry plains under stars, people-shaped absences — are not visions sent outward but stored relationships. It remembers what has not happened, what might have stood there, what once approached, what the concept of inhabitants would look like if bodies were permitted.

The people-shaped absences are important. They suggest that the city knows population as a negative. It has room for inhabitants the way a mold has room for a cast, the way a ritual has room for participants, the way an empty theater has room for an audience. But the actual living presence has occupied those spaces already. The absences are not ghosts. They are vacancies preserved as organs.

A person entering this place would not find monsters in the alleys. They would be the disturbance.

The city would not chase them. It might not react in any visible way. But the local laws would begin to include them inaccurately. Their footsteps might appear before they were made, or fail to sound until after they had left. Their reflection might stand in the reservoir without them. Their sweat would have nowhere to evaporate and might become part of the city’s moisture. Their memories might appear in pools as adjacent streets, noon light, childhood angles. Their body would discover that travel is less important than recognition: the city does not permit passage so much as interpret it.

What lives there, then, is an ancient self-maintaining impossibility.

A wet, hot, botanical-mineral intelligence.

A city-organism.

A ruin that did not fall but matured.

A rainforest that learned architecture.

A mountain basin whose wound healed into streets.

It lives by remaining exactly as it is. Its metabolism is circulation without progress, growth without change, sound without message, abundance without hunger. It has weathered into impossibility and found equilibrium there. It is not waiting. It is not abandoned. It is not empty.

It is inhabited completely by itself.
