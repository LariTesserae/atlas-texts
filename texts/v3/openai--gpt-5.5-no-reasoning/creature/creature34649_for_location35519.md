They are small enough that the city is large to them.

Not tiny in the way insects are tiny, not hidden people of dust and fable, but a lean, low-built folk shaped by a place where height wastes heat, water, and risk. An adult can crouch beneath most of the ledges without striking the head, can sleep curled in a wall niche, can move along ladders meant to flex under little weight. Standing straight, the tallest among them might reach the chest of an ordinary plains human, though few stand straight for long. Their posture is a negotiation with overhangs, shade cloths, thorn-vines, nets, and the daily need to pass under things rather than through open space.

They are not one clean image at first glance. In the dimness they seem made of fragments: a narrow foot gripping a stone step; a shoulder dark with dust; a quick black eye under a brow of pale ash; fingers tying a knot in vine cord; teeth briefly visible as someone clicks a warning; the long line of a back vanishing behind a hide screen. Their bodies have the economy of drought. They are wiry, tendonous, ribbed without being weak. Their limbs are long enough for climbing but compact enough for burrows and doorways. Their hands are deft and strong, with broad thumb pads and nails kept short or sharpened according to trade. The soles of their feet are tough, almost hoof-hard at the heel and ball, but their toes remain nimble, capable of testing loose shale, hooking ladder rungs, pinning string, feeling hairline cracks in stone.

Their skin carries the plateau even in shade. It ranges through clay-brown, umber, grey-gold, ash-black, ochre, and the muted red of old pottery. Most are dusted deliberately with mineral powders: pale chalk around the eyes to cut glare, soot and grease along cheekbones against insects, ash in the creases of elbows and knees to dry sweat before it can be wasted or fouled. Children are often mottled from crawling through storage alcoves and root beds; elders have a smoother, polished look, their skin burnished by years of oil rationing, heat, and wind. Scars are common, not dramatic battle marks but small maps of living close to stone and animals: net burns, thorn scratches, bird bites, healed lizard nips, the round pale kisses of wasp stings.

Their hair is usually kept practical. Some shave it close with obsidian flakes and rub the scalp with ash against lice. Some bind it into tight cords stiffened with resin, beads, bone rings, or seed counters. Among plant tenders, hair may be wrapped entirely, because loose strands in a root tray are considered unclean and unlucky. Among hunters and foragers, hair is often veiled under pale cloth or hide, both for sun and concealment. Children’s hair is left in uneven tufts until they are old enough to keep their own hands from open vessels; then it is cut in a small public act, swept up, and saved for nests or cordage.

Their eyes are large-looking in the shade, though this may be because faces are narrow and light is scarce. They are people accustomed to seeing by fragments. They read motion before outline, shadow before form. They notice the twitch of a moth wall, the sudden stillness of ants, the wrong angle of a leaf, the weight of silence after a hawk-shadow. Many have a slight permanent squint from the rim glare; outside the city they wear slatted visors of reed, salt-bleached bone, or woven thorn stem. Inside, their eyes soften, dilate, become bright and watchful as beetle shells.

Their mouths are expressive but disciplined. Lips are often cracked, darkened with plant wax, or stained by bitter leaf pastes that encourage saliva without spending water. They do not speak idly during the hot hours. A conversation may pass in clicks, tongue taps, cheek pops, whistles drawn through the teeth, and knuckle rhythms knocked into railings. Voice is saved for teaching, bargaining, mourning, singing, and night. They can speak fully, and do so beautifully when there is reason, but their ordinary language is partly percussive, partly breathed, partly gestured. A hand held flat means shade-right. Two fingers crooked means loose stone. A thumb brushed across the lower lip means seal that vessel now. A palm tapped over the heart and then toward a plant means I take responsibility for this cutting.

They dress in layers of utility. Nothing hangs loose unless it is meant to catch air, shade, or insects. Their garments are made from cured skins as thin as paper, woven grass, reed fiber, bird down packed between cloths, softened bark strips from cultivated shrubs, and sometimes traded cloth so precious it is patched until more patch than original. Around the waist they wear belts crowded with small tools: bone awls, hooks, scraping stones, resin plugs, seed knives, tally cords, fire covers, beetle spoons, thorn tweezers. Children wear fewer clothes and more strings, each string marking which tier they are allowed to climb, which animals they may feed, whose hearth they belong to. Elders wear mantles patched with tokens of their former work: a strip of bird-tower wicker, a polished goat horn tip, a ladder rung, a salt shard, a dried vine knot.

They smell of the city because nothing here exists apart from anything else. Warm hide, ash, leaf bitterness, insect resin, fat smoke, powdered stone, animal breath. A goatherd smells different from a beetle keeper; a root guide different from a hunter; a lamp maker different from a corpse preparer. These distinctions matter. Dogs know them. Children know them. Mates know them. Plants, they claim, know them too.

They are not rulers of the hollow in the simple sense. They are its principal negotiators.

Everything living there makes demands, and the people survive by remembering all of them. They do not think of the city as built upon the rock but as entered into treaty with it. The ledges give shade, but only if not overloaded. The plants give food, fiber, scent, medicine, and night moisture, but only if fed, pruned, defended, and never insulted by careless cutting. The goats give dung, milk in rare seasons, hide, sinew, alarm, and trouble. The beetles turn rot into protein. The lizards keep larvae from eating stored seed. The bats clear the dusk air. The hawks take what they will, unless sometimes persuaded otherwise. Even ants are not merely pests; certain houses cultivate their routes, laying crumb tribute to draw them away from sleeping mats and toward refuse seams.

Their minds are ecological because there is no room for any other kind of intelligence. A person who sees only an object is considered childish. A jar is not a jar: it is clay debt, storage promise, shade occupant, inheritance risk, sealing obligation, possible burial vessel, possible water memory. A vine is owner, food, medicine, ladder hazard, goat temptation, roof anchor, ancestor cutting, and future cord. A dead bird is meal, feathers, fat, bone, mites, omen, and beetle ration. To forget any part of this is not immoral in the abstract; it is dangerous.

They count constantly. Not with written numbers alone, though there are marks scratched into plaster, bone, and salt. They count by beads sliding on cords, by knots in vine, by seed strings, by notches on ladder posts, by rhythm. They count eggs under birds, sealed jars, dung cakes drying, goats pregnant, beetle trays due for harvest, days since a forager departed, lamp portions for the cool shrine, mouths at a hearth, shadows until heat leaves the upper tier. Children learn arithmetic through hunger and husbandry. A common lesson is to give a child five seeds, let ants take one, a bird take one, a sibling beg one, and then ask how many belong to the planting basket. The correct answer is not always two.

Their families are not arranged only by blood. Blood matters, but waterlessness has thinned the usefulness of private lineage. Households are hearth-groups, work-groups, shade-groups, and obligation knots. A child may sleep in the niche of the mother who bore them, be fed by an aunt of no relation, apprenticed to a beetle uncle, disciplined by the owner of the ladder they damaged, and inherit from a plant tender whose vines they saved during wind. Parent words are many and specific: birth-mother, milk-giver, hand-teacher, shade-keeper, name-sponsor, waste-watcher, first rescuer. Orphans are rare not because death is rare, but because belonging is distributed.

Their old people are valuable in a way that gives them authority sharper than strength. Elders remember sealed cracks, failed root beds, bad wind years, which animals’ descendants bite, which salt panes hum before breaking, which strangers brought mites, which seed stores turned bitter after a certain ash was used. An elder may no longer climb above the second tier but can stop a building project with one cough if they remember that a buried chamber lies beneath. Some go almost blind and become listeners. They sit near crossroads, touching railings, naming who passes by footfall. Some become smell-keepers, able to tell whether a jar seal has failed before anyone else notices.

Their children are quick, dusty, and intensely employed. There is affection, but little sentimental idleness. Even toddlers carry husks to beetle troughs or shoo moths from drying racks. They are trained to freeze under hawk-shadow before they are trained to speak clearly. They learn the city vertically: first the floor, then the lower ladders, then safe ledges, then forbidden hot tiers, then the rim only after a ceremony in which they must name what can fall from above and what can be lost below. Games are rehearsals for catastrophe. They race to seal pretend vessels. They mimic wind-tie patterns. They steal one another’s bead counters and must reconstruct the missing number from memory. They build toy cities in dust, then release ants into them to see what routes fail.

They laugh often, but softly, or at night when mouths can afford it. Their humor is dry and exact. They joke about rain as a ridiculous animal that falls from the sky and dies on everything. They tease lovers by comparing them to badly tied vines. A boastful hunter may be told that even his shadow came back thirsty. Children are warned that if they leave jars open, the empty basin will climb out of the shrine and drink their names.

They fear waste more deeply than death. Death has a place. Waste is exile from meaning.

Their dead are handled with care and practicality that outsiders might mistake for coldness. Bodies are dried, wrapped, sung over, and divided according to custom, season, and need. Some bones remain with the household as tools, beads, or ancestor pieces. Some are placed in walls. Some are ground into root beds for lineage plants. Hair is saved. Teeth may become counting tokens. The dead are not imagined as vanished; they are redistributed. To be used well after death is a comfort. To rot unclaimed in open sun is a horror. To burn entirely is almost unthinkable except in plague.

They have priests, though priest is too large and separate a word. The basin-keepers tend the empty shrine and teach restraint. They do not ask for miracles. They preserve absence. Each morning one of them brushes the basin clean with a feather fan and inspects it for forbidden offerings. A drop of oil, a tear deliberately shed, a smear of fruit pulp—these would be sacrilege. The basin must remain empty, not because emptiness is loved, but because wanting must be given a shape that does not consume the city. The basin-keepers are often calm to the point of severity. During disputes over stores or breeding rights, they are called to sit silently beside the argument, reminding everyone of the thing no one possesses.

Near the rim are the feather-speakers, less serene. They maintain the bone shrine, read predator patterns, and decide when scraps must be offered. They know hawks as individuals: torn-wing, red-eye, high-circler, chick-thief, patient-mother. They do not believe the birds are merciful. They believe bargains can exist without trust. Their ceremonies are noisy, full of rattled bones and imitated wingbeats, meant as much to alert children as to appease the sky.

Their crafts are intimate and ingenious. Potters make vessels with narrowed throats and lids that can be sealed by one hand in darkness. Salt-cutters split translucent slabs thin enough to glow but thick enough to withstand pecking birds. Ladder-makers are respected because a bad rung kills more surely than a bad prayer. Root-guides train vines along walls with splints, weights, and whispered genealogies. Beetle keepers maintain warmth, rot, and timing with near-mathematical precision. Ash-handlers manage waste pits and are both avoided and honored; they know the dangerous border between filth and fertility.

Their healers work mostly with dryness, heat, pressure, bitterness, and time. They pack wounds with ash, powdered shell, resin, spider silk, and crushed leaves. They know which insect larvae clean dead flesh and which invade living. They stitch with sinew or cactus fiber. Fever is feared because it spends the body’s hidden water. Bleeding is frightening for the same reason. Tears are not forbidden, but mourners cover their faces with cloth so grief is not seen dripping. Sickrooms are shaded with special care, and the ill are given sour pastes, lizard broth, seed oils, or, in grave cases, stores that might otherwise have fed several. Such decisions leave long social shadows.

They do not lack tenderness. It appears as sealing another’s jar before one’s own. As shifting a sleeping child away from a wall warmed too much by the day. As chewing dry food first for an elder whose teeth have worn down. As placing one’s body between a plant bed and a goat. As spending oil on a lamp in the room of someone afraid of night insects. As sharing breath under a cloth during the coldest pre-dawn hour when plants open and the whole city smells green and bitter.

They are suspicious of openness. A wide plain unsettles them. A large room makes them whisper. Full sunlight is not freedom but exposure, expenditure, blindness. Visitors from wetter lands, if any ever arrive, seem monstrous in their looseness: sweating freely, speaking loudly, leaving cups uncovered, washing tools in water, throwing away peelings, building fires for comfort. The courtyard people would watch such strangers with a mixture of horror, envy, pity, and appetite for their goods.

And yet they are not merely deprived. They have pleasures particular to the hollow.

They enjoy the first opening of the night plants, when the day’s held breath releases and the leaves unfold one by one. They enjoy the brief passage of sun through salt panes, when interiors turn honey-white and even old scars glow. They savor roasted beetles crackling between the teeth, goat fat melted on seed cakes, bitter green scrapings from vine joints, the pepper smell of dry moss crushed into stews. They love complex textures: crisp husk over soft paste, powdered mineral on oily skin, the squeak of fresh cord, the coolness of shaded stone against the spine. Music is built from tapping, clicking, gourd rattles, birdbone flutes, and the city itself: ladders, rails, jars, hollow ledges. Their songs often have little melody but intricate rhythm, because rhythm travels through stone when voice fails.

Their dreams are crowded. They dream of roots entering rooms, of jars that will not seal, of hawks with human hands, of hidden chambers full of old seeds, of wind with teeth, of the empty basin brimming and everyone forbidden to drink. Some dream of rain though they have never seen it. In such dreams rain is rarely gentle. It is a catastrophe of abundance: walls melting, dung pits flooding, beetle troughs drowning, roots bursting, salt panes weeping, the city collapsing into mud. Children wake frightened from rain-dreams and are comforted by being shown dry dust.

Their stories say they came from elsewhere, but the elsewhere changes with the teller. Some say their ancestors were driven upward by floods and chose the dry crown of the world so water could never again take their homes. Some say they followed a black goat into the cracked plateau and found plants breathing in the stone. Some say they were once large people who shrank themselves by oath so their descendants could survive on less. Some say they were made by the empty basin from the first handful of dust brushed out of it. Practical people smile at these tales, then repeat them correctly to children.

They know of other pockets. Not many, not reliably, but enough that the world is not empty. There are cousin cities hidden in sinkholes, under basalt lips, inside collapsed bubbles of ancient lava, in gullies roofed by thorn and salt crust. Traders arrive in pairs or threes, never large caravans, because smell, noise, and need increase with numbers. They bring obsidian, dried medicines, strange seeds, bone flutes, shell fragments from impossible distances, news of disease, marriages, droughts, predators, collapses. Courtship often travels these routes. A person may leave their birth hollow and spend the rest of life learning the names of another city’s cracks.

Their politics are household politics enlarged to the scale of scarcity. Authority belongs to those who can keep systems from failing: storehouse stewards, ladder elders, plant-line owners, animal counters, ash-handlers, basin-keepers, and those with kin in other pockets. Decisions are made in layered councils because every act touches too much. To move a goat pen may shade a vine, stress a ledge, change dung routes, anger a household, expose a bird tower, and alter ant traffic. Arguments are patient until they are not. When anger breaks, it is sharp and brief, like a cracked pot. Violence inside the hollow is rare but not unknown; its punishment is usually restitution, labor, loss of climbing rights, or, in extreme cases, expulsion to the outer plateau at dawn with sealed food and no route markers.

Exile is almost death, but not always. There are stories of exiles finding other pockets, becoming traders, returning years later with one eye gone and a bag of impossible blue pigment. These stories are loved because they make mercy and terror share a doorway.

Their relationship to water is the deepest mystery in them. They do possess some, though almost never visibly: bound in plant flesh, sealed in bladders, hidden in stone-cooled jars, condensed in secret cloths during rare fog events, carried in the bodies of animals, stored as knowledge more than substance. They may taste actual liquid only during rites, sickness, infancy, or after extraordinary labor. A drop is administered with a thorn, a feather shaft, a hollow bone. To drink openly would feel obscene. To pour water would be madness. The word for generosity may come from the same root as “to moisten the tongue,” but it is used metaphorically more often than literally.

They do not worship water. Worship would be too simple. They fear what longing for it could make them do. So they honor restraint, condensation, containment, the sealed vessel, the closed mouth, the leaf that opens only in darkness.

If one watched them through an evening, the people would at first disappear into the city’s general rustle. They would seem only another species among goats, lizards, moths, birds, ants, bats, vines, and beetles. But gradually their pattern would emerge: the hand that redirects ants with a line of bitter powder; the child who sees the hawk-shadow and drops flat before the dogs bark; the old woman tasting dust from a crack and declaring it unsafe; the young man singing to keep rhythm while others haul an awning down; the ash-handler sealing a pit; the root-guide stroking a vine tendril away from a doorway; the basin-keeper brushing emptiness clean.

They are the city’s memory given hands.

Not masters of scarcity, because scarcity has no master. Not victims of it either. They are its descendants, its technicians, its poets, its accountants, its cooks, its mourners. They have become precise where the world is unforgiving, communal where solitude would kill, superstitious where pattern recognition saves lives, and tender in ways that leave almost no waste.

They live folded into the cracked crown of the plateau as lichen lives on stone: thinly, stubbornly, beautifully, making a world from surfaces others would call barren. Each night, when the plants open and the bats pass and the lamps glow behind salt, they move through their miniature city with the assurance of creatures who know every ledge by foot, every rule by scar, every mouth by obligation.

They endure not by escaping thirst, but by teaching thirst to take form: jar, shade, knot, law, song, shrine, seed, silence.
