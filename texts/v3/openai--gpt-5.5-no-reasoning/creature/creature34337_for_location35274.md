What lives there is not one creature so much as a whole cold guild of beginnings.

The largest of them are the pale mountain ungulates, built for a world that has not yet softened. They are heavy-shouldered and narrow-faced, with legs placed carefully under them as if every step is a decision. Their hooves are split wide and edged hard, good on wet slate, crusted snow, and loose basalt. From a distance they look like pieces of weathered stone set in motion: smoke-gray, ash-white, dun along the flanks, dark at the knees and muzzle where meltwater and mineral mud stain the fur.

They live in loose herds that are never quite still. Even when grazing, they drift. They do not crop meadows, because there are no true meadows; they work the land in fragments—salt crust at one tarn edge, a cushion of alpine grass in a crack, a reddish herb exposed by thaw, the damp mineral rind of a seep. Their heads are often low, lips searching the stone with a delicacy that seems at odds with their mass. Then one lifts its head, and all the others follow, ears forward, nostrils wet and black in the metallic air.

They know the plateau by moisture, shelter, and taste. Their paths are the first roads of the place, though no mind planned them: muddy braids between mineral licks, seep flats, lee slopes, and the shallow basins where the wind packs snow less deeply. Calves are born in pockets between rock shelves, where the ground is cold but not exposed, and they learn almost at once to stand among ticking stones and trembling ice. The herds do not belong to any one valley because the valleys are not yet proper valleys. They belong instead to a circuit of temporary offerings.

Among the talus live the small burrowers.

They are marmot-like, but less settled than marmots in older mountains. Their colonies are young because the land itself is young. They have not inherited deep ancestral tunnel systems; they make do with hollows under boulders, chambers where frost has loosened stone from stone, narrow runnels that can be stopped with grass, fur, and packed gravel. They are blunt-bodied and thick-pelted, the color of damp scree. Their claws are strong, but their burrows are cautious, shallow, provisional—like everything else here.

They emerge when warmth briefly touches the slope. At first only a nose appears between black stones, then whiskers shining with condensation, then the whole animal hunched low to the ground. They feed with urgency, gathering alpine stems, seeds blown in from lower country, lichens loosened from shaded faces, insect bodies caught near thaw pools. Their whistles are infrequent. In such open air, a warning travels too far; it informs predator and kin alike. More often they freeze, becoming another rounded stone, or vanish with startling speed into the broken slope.

They are the keepers of little stored warmth. Beneath the raw surface they make insulated pockets of breath and plant fiber. In a country of wet mineral cold, their dens are among the first true interiors.

There are smaller things as well: clawed, nervous, almost invisible. Pika-like gleaners move between grass pockets and rock cracks, cutting stems before frost hardens them, stacking tiny haypiles in sheltered crevices. Mice or vole-like creatures run through the rootless vegetation, feeding on seeds, insect larvae, and the softest parts of alpine herbs. Some are rarely seen except as tracks: delicate stitches in mud ash, vanishing beneath a slab.

The birds belong to different layers of the air.

High along the cliffs are the dark gliders. They use the weak thermals, the breath from rock faces, the uplift of wind against the raw shoulders of the mountains. Their wings are long and spare. Their bodies look black until they tilt and reveal iron, umber, or dull bronze in the feathers. They do not waste calls. Their silence suits the place. They watch the herds, the talus colonies, the newborn watercourses, the places where something slips, weakens, or dies.

Some are hunters. They hang almost motionless against the pewter sky, then fold themselves into a fall. A burrower too far from cover, a bird flushed from scree, a newborn ungulate separated on wet ground—these are taken quickly, without drama. Others are carrion birds, patient and practical, arriving after cold, gravity, hunger, or accident has done its work. Their beaks open the dead to the plateau. In a place where soil is only beginning, they are part of the making of soil.

Lower down are the small birds of stone and seep. They hop more than fly, appearing suddenly between slate fragments, then disappearing into colorless ground. Their backs are mottled gray-brown, their bellies pale, their legs fine and quick. They follow midges and other insects when thaw releases them. At the wet margins of tarns, they pick at larvae in mineral mud. In scree fields, they turn tiny flakes and probe between stones. Their lives are timed to hours rather than seasons: a brief softening of ice, a hatch of insects above a dark pool, a lull in wind.

In summer—or what passes for summer here—the air above the damp hollows trembles with insects. Midges rise in columns whenever the temperature permits. Mosquito-like forms gather where hoofprints fill with meltwater. Stoneflies, gnats, cold-adapted flies, and small beetles complete their lives with haste. Their abundance is sudden and almost unbelievable against the poverty of plant life. They are the plateau’s brief feast: food for birds, burrowers, spiders, amphibious larvae if any persist in the pools, and fishless waters full of small, transparent life.

The insects live because water is everywhere at the beginning of becoming water. Every seep, hoofprint, tarn skin, and under-ice trickle is a nursery. Their larvae cling beneath stones in icy films, feed on microbial slicks, mineral-stained algae, windblown organic dust, dung, and the faint beginnings of decay. They are among the first true harvesters of the mountain’s wetness.

There are predators on foot, though they are more often inferred than seen.

One is lean and gray, moving along ridgelines with the economy of hunger. It may be wolf-like, or something between wolf and fox, adapted to distances too open for ambush and too broken for easy pursuit. It travels with its head low, reading the ground: hoofprints softened by seep, the musk of a herd, the warm trace of burrow entrances beneath stone, feathers caught on a ledge. Its coat is not a single color but a shifting assembly of fog, gravel, old snow, and shadow.

It does not run unless it must. The land punishes waste. It quarters slopes, tests the wind, disappears behind tilted slabs, reappears much farther away than expected. Sometimes it hunts with others of its kind, spreading wide across a gravel pan to pressure the young, injured, or inattentive. More often it scavenges: a frozen calf, a bird kill, a burrower dug from talus by another predator, a carcass opened by ravens or eagle-like birds. Its success depends on knowing when the weather will fold visibility down to a few meters and when sound will carry too far.

Smaller scavengers come at dusk. Fox-sized, dark-footed, sharp-faced, they move along the margins of the herds’ paths after the grazers have left. They nose through dung for seeds and insects. They lick salt from stone. They steal eggs from ledges when they can reach them, take weakened chicks, cache scraps under loose rock, and vanish before the deeper cold settles. Their tracks are the padded ones around tarn edges, appearing overnight beside hoofmarks and bird prints.

There may be a cat-like hunter too, though almost no one would see it plainly. It would live in the broken border between cliff and basin, where vertical stone gives cover and the herd paths narrow. Broad paws for snow skins and scree. Thick tail for balance. Fur pale enough to be mistaken for old ice in morning light. It would be a hunter of waiting: lying above mineral licks, watching trails that the ungulates must use, measuring the hesitation of calves and the fatigue of old animals. Its presence would be known by sudden silence among the burrowers, by a scatter of feathers, by a drag mark crossing damp gravel before vanishing into rocks.

The cold waters hold their own small lives.

Not fish, perhaps—not yet, unless some ancient connection stocked the upper basins—but invertebrates, microbial mats, translucent larvae, tiny crustacean forms in the tarns, things that live beneath ice skins and in the first dark muds. They feed on mineral films, windblown pollen and dust, dung washed from hoofpaths, the bodies of insects that hatched too early or froze too late. Around vents and faintly warmed seams, microbial life gathers in rusty, black, or pale slicks. The mountain’s breath feeds them: sulfur, iron, dissolved salts, gases rising through new fractures.

These are not decorative lives. They are chemical workers. They stain stone, begin crusts, loosen minerals, trap silt, make the first slim continuities between geology and biology. Where a seep runs a few meters before freezing, a thin dark line of living film may follow it. Where gas bubbles through mud, the mud may hold invisible metabolisms older in style than plants, older than grazing, older than the idea of a meadow.

The plants that live there are almost animals in their stubbornness.

Cushion plants press themselves into the cracks as if gripping the mountain. Their surfaces are black-green, dense, and armored, made of thousands of tiny leaves packed so close that wind cannot easily enter. They create their own minute climates: a fraction warmer inside, a little less dry, a little more protected from ice abrasion. Seeds lodge in their edges. Insects shelter in them. Small grazers nibble them carefully, for too much damage kills what took decades to swell by a hand’s breadth.

Lichens are the patient colonists of the bare faces. Gray, yellow, rust, dull green, nearly black—they stipple the rock where exposure is tolerable. Many stones are still unmarked, too newly split, too often scoured by frost or sliding debris. The lichens reveal which surfaces have stayed still long enough to be found by life.

There are grasses too, but they are not meadow grasses. They stand in dry tawny tufts in pockets of grit and shallow soil, roots threaded through mineral fines and old dung. Reddish alpine herbs bloom low and close to the ground, often with leaves colored as much by cold as by growth. Their flowering must be quick. Their seeds must be ready for wind, mud, fur, or the passing gut of an ungulate.

Everything living there is shaped by interruption.

The grazers feed in pauses between storms. The insects hatch in pauses between freezes. The burrowers harvest in pauses between snow seals. The predators hunt in pauses between hunger and exhaustion. Even the microbes depend on pauses: water unfrozen long enough to move, chemistry exposed long enough to be used. Life does not spread evenly across the plateau. It flares at seep zones, mineral licks, tarn rims, sheltered talus, dung-enriched paths, carcass sites, and south-facing cracks that briefly take light.

What lives there, then, is a community of austerity and timing: smoke-colored herds, shallow-burrowing talus animals, silent cliff birds, sudden small birds, midges and cold flies, gray ridge predators, dusk scavengers, microbial slicks, lichens, cushion plants, wiry grasses, and low herbs red with endurance.

But more than any single organism, the resident presence is adaptation itself.

Everything is low, thick, watchful, efficient. Everything knows the value of a windbreak, a salt crust, a thaw hour, a stone warmed half a degree by hidden sun. Nothing assumes permanence. Burrows are made under unstable rock. Paths cross channels that may not exist next season. Grazing grounds appear where snow retreats and vanish beneath frost. Pools skin over, crack, drain, refill. The animals are not masters of the place; they are temporary agreements with it.

And beneath them all lives the mountain’s own unsettled body: venting, freezing, ticking, lifting, grinding, wetting itself with first waters. The creatures inhabiting it are like early workers in a factory still being built around them—feeding among the raw materials, nesting in the machinery, listening to the knocks and shudders of a world that has not finished deciding its shape.
