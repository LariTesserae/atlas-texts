What lives there is not a forest translated into another material, not a city’s usual ecology stripped of trees. It is something older in attitude and stranger in construction: a civilization of wet stone and patient flesh, where nearly every organism has learned to treat heat, mineral, and machinery as weather.

The inhabitants are divided less by species than by their relationship to water.

There are the ones who harvest it.

There are the ones who wait beneath it.

There are the ones who follow it through cracks.

And there are the builders—if they remain—who have made themselves part of the same system.

They are not abundant in the lively, noisy sense. They do not fill the streets with obvious motion. The place does not swarm until one learns how to look. At first, the city appears almost empty: white courts, wet gutters, black doorways, high towers combing vapor from the air. Then a shell shifts beside a wall. A seam of pavement darkens with hundreds of transparent bodies. A cable trembles, not from wind, but from small hooked feet passing along its underside. Something flat and pale opens its back to the sun and becomes almost invisible against the stone.

Life here is mostly low, armored, translucent, reflective, or still.

Many creatures have shells, not as ornament but as architecture. Their bodies are soft beneath mineral shields accreted over years, sometimes deliberately, sometimes because the city itself grows onto them. A grazer that spends its life against concrete may become indistinguishable from concrete. A gutter crab may wear a salt crust so thick that only its black joint-points reveal it as alive. Some animals do not shed cleanly; they let each old surface remain, building themselves outward in plates and rims until they resemble broken ceramic fittings or fragments of collapsed wall.

The smaller animals are everywhere. They live in the narrowest wetness: under pipe brackets, behind ceramic studs, inside the cooling jackets of machines, along the shaded lip of drains. They are pale, amber, black, or glass-clear. Some are no larger than a fingernail and shaped like flattened seeds, though there are no seeds here. They cling to damp stone and scrape microbial films with mouthparts that move in quiet rows. Others are jointed threads with hooked forelimbs, able to fold into cracks and reappear after rain in numbers impossible to reconcile with the empty surface seen moments before.

There are colonies that behave almost like stains. On shaded walls they form black, rust-red, or opalescent patches, each patch made of countless animalcules locked into a slow communal mat. They open microscopic mouths when water passes over them. They close in heat. They ripple if touched, not as slime would ripple, but as a crowded settlement might shift under threat. They are not plants, though from a distance they imitate lichen, moss, fungal bloom. They are little cities on the city, feeding from minerals, airborne particles, dissolved waste, and the thin organic slicks left by other creatures.

Around every source of steady condensate gather the grazers.

Some are broad and almost flat, built like living paving stones. Their translucent backs show pale bladders of stored water, blue-white organs, pulsing threads. They spread themselves over hot rock in the morning and darken slightly as internal fluids circulate. Their heads are nearly absent, tucked beneath the front rim of the shell, where combs and rasps work against stone. They leave behind clean arcs on walls and floors, polished trails where mineral-organic film has been removed.

Others are taller, slow quadrupeds or many-legged bodies with domed backs. These are the shell-backed grazers of the cistern yards. They look less like animals than pieces of old infrastructure given a careful, reluctant will. Their shells are white with salt, yellow at the edges, veined with rust and dark carbonate. When they rest against a wall, smaller creatures climb them without hesitation. They carry entire accessory ecologies: mites that clean their joints, sharp-legged parasites that drink from soft seams, tiny crabs that defend folds of skin in exchange for scraps, glass-flies that lay eggs in damp shell fissures.

The glass-flies are among the few creatures that seem delicate. Their wings are stiff transparent plates, not buzzing membranes. They do not hum. They click softly when they land. In sunlight they vanish except for little green or violet refractions at their joints. They feed at wet interfaces: the rim of a cistern, the mucus track of a grazer, the tears of sweating walls. They are prey for almost everything, and yet they persist in dazzling numbers after white rain, standing on the surface tension of shallow puddles like fragments of broken lenses.

In the drainage channels live the long-bodied animals.

Some resemble eels, but they are not fish exactly. Their fins are jointed, folding and unfolding like the ribs of fans. Their eyes are small or absent. They lie under warm silt with only sensory filaments exposed, waiting for flood pulses to bring them food. When they move, they do so with abrupt, efficient violence: a pale loop, a quick hinge of the body, then stillness again. Many have mineralized teeth or plates in the throat for grinding shells. Some possess side-pouches where water is stored during dry intervals, allowing them to travel short distances through slick pipework and flooded underpasses.

There are blind white crabs in old intake pipes, delicate scavengers in settlement tanks, red-mouthed filter worms inside ceramic throats. Reservoirs have their own stratified populations: surface skaters, suspended filterers, bottom-feeding armored things with heavy foreclaws. In the deep municipal cisterns, where light only arrives through maintenance slits and inspection shafts, live slower creatures that may be very old. They adhere to the walls as pale folded fans. When pumps stir the water, they open. Their bodies strain the flow for particles shed by the city above: skin dust, machine grease, microbial films, powdered shell, fragments of old insulation, the leavings of generations.

Above, in the hot air, are the heat-kites.

They are not birds. They have no calls, no feathers, no quick wingbeats. Their bodies are narrow knots suspended beneath great membranes, and their art is to spend almost nothing. They climb the thermal columns that rise between towers, drift outward, collapse part of a wing, turn, climb again. The membranes alter opacity in pulses: amber, white, smoky, clear. At certain hours a dozen may hang above the condenser quarter, all flashing softly to one another in no pattern a human eye can easily parse. Perhaps they signal weather. Perhaps mating readiness. Perhaps ownership of an updraft. Perhaps nothing as simple as speech.

They feed on airborne life: glass-flies, drifting larvae, mineral gnats, spores of animal mats, fragments lifted from drying basins. Their mouths are slits lined with wet cilia. When rain begins in sunlight, they descend through it in slow spirals, membranes beaded bright, drinking through their skin.

They are hunted by vertical predators.

These are thin animals, dark and still, built to resemble rails, posts, drain shadows. They cling to walls with many small gripping pads and may remain unmoving through the full violence of noon. Their heads are wedge-shaped. Their forelimbs unfold too far, too suddenly. When a heat-kite passes near a wall to drink condensation or rest beneath an awning, the hunter strikes from flatness into length. After feeding, it leaves brittle translucent molts stuck to the plaster or dropping into water below, where scavengers gather before the skin has finished sinking.

Outside the city, on the exposed flats, life becomes sparser but not simpler.

At mineral springs gather pale arthropods in loose arrangements, each keeping distance from the others by the rules of shade, size, and fluid need. Some are social only because the spring is there. Others coordinate by touch, arranging themselves in crescent patterns around seepage points so that each receives a portion of runoff. Their carapaces are semi-clear, and during the heat of day one can see the shimmer of stored water inside them. Predators read that shimmer. So the water-hoarding creatures often lie against salt patches or pale stone that confuses the eye.

In the marsh-like basins with no reeds, there are floating skins.

They appear at first to be slicks of oil, then mats, then perhaps sheets of living glass. They are colonial filter-creatures joined edge to edge. When undisturbed, they can cover entire warm pools in a taut, shining membrane. A foot placed on such a surface may not break water; it may press down into a living thing that grips, folds, and seals. Small animals cross them lightly. Larger ones avoid perfect smoothness. During white rain, the skins separate into plates, each plate flexing upward at the edges to catch falling droplets and trapped particles. By evening they rejoin.

There are also gelatinous drifters in those basins, warm-water creatures that inflate under sun and collapse at dusk. Some have mineral grains embedded in their tissues for ballast. Some are transparent sacks full of smaller commensal life. They detach when shadows pass over them, sinking away from heat-kites and surface-feeding mouths. When stranded at basin edges, they dry into tough films that scavengers rasp from salt.

The lizards of the abandoned machinery fields are among the more visibly familiar animals, though only at a distance. They bask on toppled towers, collapsed tanks, and the warm backs of old boilers. Their scales are reflective, not colorful exactly, but mirror-bright in patches, scattering heat and light. When still, they look like strips of polished foil caught on rust. When they move, the movement is low and liquid. Their tongues taste condensation, electrical residue, and the chemical trails of smaller prey. Some nest inside machinery housings where night moisture collects and predators too large to enter cannot reach.

Other inhabitants are harder to classify.

There are things between animal and device, or animals that have incorporated devices, or machines colonized so thoroughly by life that the distinction has become ceremonial. Along condenser towers, maintenance crawlers continue to move on fixed routes, cleaning mineral buildup from mesh and glass. Some are manufactured, with ceramic feet and articulated brass backs. Others appear to have been repaired so often with shell, tendon, salvaged chitin, and grown insulating tissue that they are now partly alive. Small filter-mouths open along their undersides. They pause to drink. They avoid predators. They respond to rain before the tower vents do.

In older districts, one may find wall-catchment guardians: squat, many-legged things that sit over private water channels marked by blue ceramic studs. Whether they are pets, municipal automata, symbiotic livestock, or legal persons depends on the district. Their shells are often engraved. Their faces are hidden behind polished masks of shell composite or grown enamel. They do not chase thieves. They simply block the channel, widen themselves, and release an alkaline bitterness into the stolen flow. Everyone knows not to take from a guarded wall.

And then there are the people, if people is the right word.

They are not numerous in the open. The city’s apparent emptiness is partly due to climate and partly to custom. Intelligent inhabitants live thickly but inwardly, embedded in shaded cells, cistern courts, underwalks, factory depths, and the cool backs of walls. They emerge according to pressure, light, labor cycle, and rain.

They are likely tall only by rumor. In the streets they appear wrapped, hooded, narrowed by heat: pale garments or shell-colored coverings, reflective veils, wetted cloths, joint guards, sandals with ceramic soles, gloves for railings and ladders. Their bodies may be human-derived, or not quite. One imagines skin adapted to salt and glare: dark, silvered, or translucent at the temples; eyelids heavy or doubled; nostrils that can close against mineral dust; hands calloused not from soil but from hot metal, wet stone, valve wheels, filter plates. Children learn early to read surfaces: which puddle is water, which is membrane, which wall is public, which shade is already occupied.

Their houses are not private boxes so much as moisture instruments. Sleeping rooms are deep in masonry, lined with ceramic and damp stone. Courtyards collect night condensation. Screens are angled to drink fog. A family’s wealth may be measured not by land or garden but by rights: to a wall-catchment, a share in a tower skin, a cooling shaft, a cistern depth, a shaded work bay, a reliable seam of stone that sweats before dawn.

They eat from the animal city around them. Not leaves, not grain, not fruit. Their food is cultivated in basins, trays, tanks, wall farms, and warm dark chambers: film mats enriched with mineral broth; shelled filter-creatures fattened in cistern outflows; jointed eels from drainage pens; translucent bladders of spring arthropods; cultured tissues grown on ceramic lattices; eggs harvested from heat-kite roost frames; salt-cured strips of grazer flesh; savory pastes made from scraped wall colonies. Flavor here is alkaline, briny, metallic, smoky from kiln heat, sour from fermentation, sweet only in rare condensed syrups produced by engineered animal mats.

Their domestic animals are quiet too.

Some are palm-sized condensation lappers that live along interior walls, keeping channels clear of film. Some are soft-bodied cistern cleaners with jeweled sensory knobs. Some are heat-sleeping companions carried under robes, storing warmth at night and releasing it slowly in cold underrooms. There may be messenger creatures that travel cable arrays, flat and sure-footed, bearing scent capsules or ceramic tags. There may be trained heat-kites whose wing pulses can be read across districts at dusk, though they are difficult to keep and impossible to truly own.

The people’s manners are shaped by scarcity without desperation. They do not waste motion at noon. They do not shout across glare. They gesture with wrapped hands, with reflected flashes from shell shutters, with touches on pipe and wall. Conversation in public shade is low, because sound carries strangely over wet stone and because pumps listen—if not intentionally, then structurally. Their laws are practical and old. Water law, shade law, basin law, maintenance law. Who cleans a gutter. Who may open a sluice. Who is responsible when a catchment animal poisons a shared channel. Which abandoned hall may be harvested and which must be left to its resident ecology because it stabilizes a district’s drainage.

They likely revere no forest, no field, no harvest moon over grain. Their sacred images, if they have them, are condensation, seepage, emergence: the first bead on a dry wall, the darkening of porous stone before rain, the filling of a cistern throat, the opening of tower vents in white light. A blessing might be given by touching damp ceramic to the eyelids. Mourning might involve returning a body not to soil but to mineral water, to be reduced by scavengers and filters, then lifted again as vapor through the city’s breath.

Their dead do not disappear into earth. There is little earth to receive them. Perhaps bones are cleaned by cistern animals and set into wall niches where they help regulate moisture. Perhaps shells of the dead—literal or ceremonial—are ground into porous tiles for new catchments. Perhaps names are inscribed in pavement that only appears after rain, hidden all dry season beneath pale dust and glare.

But the people are not necessarily the city’s masters anymore.

Some districts continue without them. Some systems open and close for no current resident. Some old industrial halls have become autonomous ecologies with legal status, taboo status, or simple danger enough to keep citizens out. A refinery that waters its own pool, lights its own galleries, and supports generations of animals is no longer merely abandoned. It is a living precinct. The inhabitants of the city, human or otherwise, understand that usefulness can outlast intention.

At the highest levels live maintenance castes or guilds: tower climbers, condenser readers, pressure auditors, glass-skin repairers. They move before dawn and after rain, when surfaces are wet but not yet burning. Their tools are ceramic hooks, shell mirrors, insulated clamps, moisture gauges, living sealants carried in jars. They know the moods of towers by touch: which hum means mineral clogging, which vibration means a trapped animal, which cold patch means productive condensation, which silence means danger.

Lower down are basin tenders, filter breeders, kiln workers, channel sweepers, causeway keepers, scavenger-right holders. Industry and husbandry overlap. A factory making porous stone may also maintain colonies of wall-scraping animals whose waste improves the material. A pump works not only by engineering but by agreements with the creatures that keep its intake clean. A drainage channel is infrastructure, pasture, trapline, nursery, and border.

The city’s predators include the people too, but they are careful predators. Overhunting a drain means fouling the drain. Taking too many shell-backed grazers means walls overgrow with choking film. Killing vertical hunters may produce a heat-kite bloom that clogs tower vents with membrane and droppings. So appetite is regulated by engineering, custom, and fear.

Nothing here lives outside the system. Even theft has an ecology.

A creature steals condensate from a pipe seam; another waits for it; a person seals the seam; a colony migrates to the new drip below; a grazer follows; its droppings enrich a film; the film darkens the wall, increasing heat absorption; the altered temperature changes condensation; a maintenance guild records the change as if noting weather.

The city’s life is therefore not lush, but exact.

Its inhabitants are creatures of boundaries: between wet and dry, shade and glare, machine and animal, ruin and function. They are quiet because noise does not help them. They are armored because softness must be hidden. They are pale or reflective because light is an enemy and a resource. They are patient because heat punishes haste. They are inventive because water is never simply present; it must be coaxed, caught, defended, shared, stolen, filtered, and returned.

What lives there is a whole civilization of condensation.

Shell-backed wall grazers and glass-winged flies. Blind crabs in pipes. Joint-finned eels in channels. Heat-kites pulsing silently above towers. Vertical hunters clinging to wet masonry. Floating skins stretched over warm basins. Reflective lizards on dead machinery. Animalcule crusts pretending to be fungus, pretending to be shadow, pretending to be nothing. Machines that behave like cliffs. Buildings that sweat like bodies. People who live inside thick walls and read rain in the darkening of stone.

No leaves. No grass. No trees.

Only heat, water, mineral, metal, flesh—and all the lives that have learned to bloom without green.
