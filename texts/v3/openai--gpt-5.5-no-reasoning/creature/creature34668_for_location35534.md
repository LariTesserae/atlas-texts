What lives there has learned not to distinguish too sharply between body, dwelling, and crust.

The people of the high plateau are not a loud people. They do not need to be. Sound travels badly there, or else too well in the wrong direction, down shafts and along pipes and into chambers where a careless shout may return as a misleading echo. Their voices tend toward low registers, close speech, gesture, tool-sign, chalk mark, lamp blink. Even their arguments often have the contained intensity of pressure gauges: the needle trembling, the casing intact.

They are a heat-wise species before they are anything else.

Not fire-worshippers, exactly. They know fire as the crude surface of a deeper condition. Flame is brief, wasteful, hungry for air. Heat is older, steadier, more civic. Heat cooks, warps, buckles, heals, distills, poisons, ripens, seals, lifts vapor, draws metal from fumes, opens stone, closes stone, decides where people may sleep. Their earliest lessons are tactile. A child learns to hold a palm near a wall before leaning against it. Learns which handrails can be gripped bare and which must be taken through cloth. Learns that a floor may look level and still be breathing. Learns the difference between ordinary warmth, useful warmth, warning warmth, and the dry, depthless warmth that means something below has changed.

Their bodies show this education.

They are lean, not from scarcity alone but from climbing: stairs, ladders, bridge ramps, service spines, switchbacks cut into hot basalt. Calves hard as cable. Shoulders rounded from carrying water vessels close to the body. Hands callused in layered ways—burn callus under rope callus under the fine cuts left by glass dust. Their skin is often weathered without being sun-browned in the ordinary sense; it has been polished by ash, dried by mineral air, reddened by radiated heat, stippled at the wrists and throat where grit collects under bindings. Hair is usually worn close, braided tight, wrapped, netted, or hidden beneath caps, because loose hair is a hazard around condensers and belt housings and static-charged dust.

They dress as though every garment is also a tool.

Outer clothes are pale or blackened depending on district and work: pale cloth to reject light on the upper decks, dark refractory coats below, white-lacquered masks in the Salt Quarter, glass-lensed hoods in the vent fields, shoulder aprons stiffened with ceramic scales where sparks and mineral slurries spatter. Nothing flutters unless meant to show wind. Sleeves close tight. Boots have replaceable soles, because glass fields and hot grates eat leather. Belts carry gauges, folded warning flags, heat chalk, small pry tools, waxed cord, ceramic whistles, and personal water seals no larger than a thumb.

Their jewelry, when they wear it, is often functional first and sentimental afterward: a pressure token from a parent’s first engine-house, a fused-glass bead from a completed bridge, a ring cut from retired conduit, a charm made from a gauge needle that survived a fracture. Shiny things dull quickly in that air, so brightness is not the main luxury. Clean edges are. A polished lens. A spotless cup. A bolt kept free of mineral bloom. A piece of uncracked porcelain passed through four generations.

They are not primitive. They live inside an old discipline of machines.

But their technology does not gleam with conquest. It squats, braces, listens, condenses, waits. The inhabitants resemble their machines in this way. They value timing over speed, maintenance over invention, continuity over display. A celebrated engineer is not the one whose tower rises tallest, but the one whose tower moves correctly when the ridge expands. A great builder leaves slack in the right cable, weakness in the right sacrificial joint, a route for steam to escape without taking a neighborhood with it.

They think in tolerances.

To outsiders this can look like fatalism, but it is not. They are intensely interventionist. They measure everything: seep, strain, crust thickness, cistern sweetness, pump delay, condensation yield, brine acidity, the slow lean of public stairs. They nail witness plates across cracks and date the break in careful script. They lower ceramic tongues into shafts to taste vapor. They keep wall needles beside family portraits. They wake before dawn to read the night’s deposition on exposed rods. The city is covered in little instruments, and the citizens read them the way another people might read clouds, birds, markets, or omens.

Perhaps they are reading omens. They would object to the word, then continue doing exactly that.

Their elders are not simply old people but survivors of particular ground. Someone may be introduced as “she who lived through the south tension release,” or “he from before the first Salt narrowing,” or “one of the children carried out of the Glass Wards when the panes softened.” Age is measured partly by remembered revisions of the city map. The oldest citizens know streets that no longer connect, wells whose names have changed twice, districts that became too hot, towers that were uncoupled and left standing with dinner tables still inside.

They do not romanticize permanence. In fact, a person too attached to an unaltered plan is considered childish, perhaps dangerous. Their virtues are adjustment, restraint, and keeping account.

Their families are extended less by blood than by shared systems. A household may include kin, apprentices, retired gauge-readers, a cistern clerk, three children born elsewhere, and an old pressure-mason whose hands no longer obey but whose memory of foundation sounds is treated as a civic resource. People belong to water-lines, scaffold cohorts, maintenance crews, culture chambers, bridge schedules. Marriage contracts include not only property but heat rights, cistern obligations, evacuation routes, and who inherits responsibility for unfinished work.

They have long memories for debts because debts often outlive the structures in which they were made.

A favor may be repaid in water during rain season, in labor during cable retensioning, in silence during a hearing, in the loan of a sheltered wall to sleep against during a cold maintenance interval. Clean water is never casual. To offer someone drinking water without remark can be more intimate than an embrace. To spill it carelessly is not merely rude; it suggests a broken relation to the city itself.

Their children are solemn in some ways and wild in others.

They play in scaffold neighborhoods, learning balance before caution fully develops. They race beads of condensation down tilted metal plates. They collect salt petals, glass crumbs, dead pipe-mice, flakes of gray gutter film, stamped ceramic warning tags broken loose by heat. They dare one another to touch walls they have already secretly tested. They memorize alarm patterns the way children elsewhere memorize songs. Their counting games use pressure intervals. Their skipping rhymes include rules about rain, cracks, and sweet-smelling cisterns.

They are told stories not of forests or seas unless someone’s family came from below, but of first surfaces: the first bridge to hold through a full thermal season, the first well that remembered water, the first time the lower engines paused and did not fail, the surveyor who slept against a wall and woke with the pattern of its heat burned into her back, the child who heard a silent pump room and saved an arcade.

The city’s animals are treated with a wary tenderness.

The blind pipe-mice are nearly household spirits in some quarters. They are not cute in the ordinary way—warm little knots of muscle, whisker, and heat-sense, with skin too pale under their sparse fur and feet clever enough to open insulation seams. But they hear poison before humans do, or smell it, or feel changes in vibration through their teeth. A pipe-mouse abandoning a duct can empty a workshop faster than an official alarm. Some are trapped and relocated. Some are fed. Some are named. In the Anchor District there are families who keep little wall-passages open generation after generation, claiming that a house without pipe-mice has lost its second hearing.

The fissure scavengers inspire less affection. After rain they rise: translucent, many-legged, almost headless things that seem assembled from wet glass and pale cartilage. They gather around brine pans and scum blooms, rasping mineral films with mouths hidden underneath. Children are forbidden to touch them, which ensures they will study them obsessively from platforms. Workers watch their movements for clues about new openings below. If the scavengers come up in unusual numbers, someone marks the fissure and sends for monitors.

The black-winged gliders are different again. They are rare enough to become communal events. When one crosses the thermal columns above the city, people pause without being told. It may be bird, reptile, machine, or some high-altitude remnant that has learned the invisible architecture of rising heat. It almost never flaps. Its body is a narrow mark against the brightness, wings rigid, making use of what the city releases. Some say they nest in unreachable warm cliffs beyond the vent fields. Some say they do not land at all except to die. Their feathers, if feathers they are, are prized only when found naturally fallen. To shoot one would be indecent.

Then there are the cultures.

These are not pets, not crops, not citizens, not tools—though they are treated at different times as all four. In warm sealed chambers, behind green-marked doors, the wet ceramic membranes pulse faintly with gray and opaline life. The cultures thicken on mineral salts, current, waste heat, and the patient feeding of technicians. They clarify certain brines. They foul others. They change conductivity. They produce films used in filters, medicines, sealants, perhaps food in emergencies, though no one speaks of that without distaste.

Their keepers are a peculiar class: part biologist, part priest, part janitor, part machinist. They wear no green except the required hazard marks. Their hands smell of wet stone and sour metal. They know how to coax a membrane back from dormancy with warmth and trace salts, how to starve a runaway bloom, how to read health in a surface too matte for most eyes. They speak to the cultures under their breath—not because they believe they understand, necessarily, but because quiet speech steadies the keeper’s pace.

Many citizens distrust the cultures. Many depend on them. Some do both.

The inhabitants’ spirituality, if it can be called that, is based on restraint before processes larger than intention. They do not pray for the ground to become kind. Kindness is not expected of basalt. They ask instead for alignment, for tolerable delay, for a crack to declare itself before night, for rain to come hard enough to fill the channels but not so hard that the low arcades drown in steam. Their shrines are small because humility is not symbolic there; it is practical. A thimble of sealed water left beside a warped bolt says: something held, something failed, something was spared.

Their dead are not buried in the ordinary way. There is little soil, and the ground is too active to be trusted with bodies without consequence. Practices vary by district. Some families cremate in civic kilns and press ash into ceramic memorial tiles set into interior walls that remain below dangerous heat. Some mix a pinch of ash into glass, making cloudy beads or panes that catch amber light. In the Salt Quarter, a few old lineages seal the dead’s names into white mineral plaques and let slow deposit obscure them over decades, so remembrance becomes a kind of deliberate burial by crust.

They fear suddenness.

Not danger itself; danger is continuous, almost domestic. They fear unannounced pressure, unexpected abundance, water where water should not be, silence where machinery should murmur, sweetness in a cistern, a cool floor over a hot chamber, a green stain in the wrong house. They fear negligence more than death. Their insults are structural: “unbraced,” “thin-walled,” “badly seated,” “all surface,” “cold-pour mind.” To call someone “careless with water” is severe. To call them “unreadable under heat” is nearly unforgivable.

Their humor is dry and technical. A courtship may begin with jokes about expansion rates. Political satire appears as fake public notices: IF YOUR REPRESENTATIVE GIVES THREE SHORT REPORTS, MOVE UPSLOPE. Children chalk false fracture marks on friends’ doorsteps and are punished mainly because someone must then inspect them. Workers name machines after stubborn relatives and relatives after stubborn machines.

Their food is shaped by altitude, industry, and absence.

There are no gardens in the city proper, no green markets spilling leaves into the street. Food comes by lift, caravan, pressure-sealed storehouse, mineral culture, fungal analogues from controlled chambers, and hardy livestock kept in lower, cooler terraces beyond the densest heat. Meals are compact, salted, smoked, steamed, fermented in ceramic jars, cooked over captured heat rather than flame. Bread, when available, is dense and treasured. Broths are carefully rationed. Mineral tang is everywhere. Children raised there can taste which cistern supplied the water in a soup.

Public baths exist, but bathing is not casual luxury. Steam-baths serve hygiene, medicine, and social negotiation. Condensed water is reclaimed. Bathhouses are among the few places where people loosen bindings, compare scars, trade information, and sit without tools in their hands. Even there, gauges tick on the walls.

Their politics are infrastructural.

Power belongs to those who control water offices, heat wells, bridge access, map revisions, maintenance crews, culture chambers, and the authority to declare a district uncoupled. There are councils, but also guilds, lineages, technical orders, scaffold assemblies, and emergency wardens whose temporary commands can override ordinary law when alarms sound. The fiercest disputes concern not ideology in the abstract, but allocation: which condenser is repaired first, which future terrace receives anchor rights, whose district must endure reduced flow, whether an unstable neighborhood is to be reinforced, evacuated, or allowed to become a monitored ruin.

Exile is rare and terrible because outside the city is not emptiness but exposure. A person cut off from water rights, bridge passage, and alarm networks becomes smaller than a scavenger. More common punishments are service in dangerous maintenance, loss of claim to future terraces, public accounting of wasted resources, or being made responsible for repairing what one’s negligence endangered.

They are not all native-born. Such a city draws people who can bear difficulty for the sake of work that outlasts them: engineers, fugitives, glassmakers, surveyors, ascetics of industry, mineral chemists, heat thieves, debtors seeking long contracts, families displaced from colder mountains or drowned lowlands, merchants who learn quickly that profit depends on respecting valves. Newcomers are easy to recognize at first. They look for shade in the wrong places. They distrust quiet machinery but ignore quiet walls. They drink too deeply after rain. They expect streets to remain streets.

If they survive long enough, they begin to pause with everyone else when the deep note comes from below.

That pause is perhaps the truest mark of belonging. No matter what one is carrying, arguing, buying, repairing, hiding, confessing—when the plateau gives its low report, the inhabitants still themselves. Heads tilt. Feet test ground. Hands go to railings, gauges, children’s shoulders. They listen not for meaning exactly, but for continuation: one note, or three; downward aftersound or lateral tremor; answering ticks from ceramic shutters; a change in steam.

Then, if the city has not altered too much, motion resumes.

There may also be beings below who belong to the place more completely than the people do.

The citizens do not speak of them directly in official settings. The maps name only heat wells, shafts, pressure corridors, sump galleries, brine locks. But workers in the lowest levels know that some sounds are not machinery, and some blockages are not mineral deposition. In red vapor, behind grates where no one should fit, there are sometimes patterned scrapes, slow rearrangements, a sense of attention moving through stone-warm dark.

Perhaps these are only colonies grown large in forgotten membranes, or fissure scavengers matured in depth, or pipe-mice myths inflated by heat and exhaustion. Perhaps they are mineral ecologies whose bodies are distributed through brine, ceramic, and current. Perhaps the plateau itself produces temporary organs where the city gives it shape: listening chambers, salt nerves, pressure lungs.

The people would not say the mountain is alive. That would be imprecise.

They might say the ground responds.

They might say some shafts should not be reopened after sealing, even if the instruments suggest profit.

They might say a building abandoned at the wrong time is not empty afterward.

And in the paused districts, where the stairs remain sharp and the painted numbers readable, something does seem to inhabit the waiting. Heat moves through rooms without occupants. Mineral fur grows around bolts like breath crystallized. Doorframes tick in the night. Condensation forms on the underside of tables. Pipe-mice avoid some floors and nest thickly in others. Gray film spreads where no water office admits water should be.

So what lives there?

People, certainly: patient, scarred, technically devout people who build by listening and survive by revision.

Blind warm animals in the ducts. Glass-pale scavengers in the rain cracks. Black gliders writing brief signatures in thermal air. Cultures pulsing behind green-marked doors. Films that wake with moisture. Machines so slow they seem geological. Abandoned districts not dead enough to be ruins. Contracts, warnings, gauges, habits. Water sealed in thimble vessels. Heat held under lids. Children who know that stone flows.

And beneath all of them, or among them, or through them, the unfinished ground: not a creature in any simple sense, but the largest resident, still deciding how much city it will allow itself to become.
