What lives there is mostly not *someone*, not in the quick, singular way that eyes and limbs suggest.

It is a population of patience.

The drowned highlands are inhabited first by plants that have forgotten the difference between tree and current. They do not stand so much as agree to remain. Their trunks braid themselves from many soft columns, each strand able to swell, loosen, root, or fray according to pressure. A ribbon-tree may be one organism, or a lineage, or a neighborhood of cooperating stems that have learned to remember one another chemically. Its crown is never truly a crown; it is a long green weather, a streaming architecture of leaves that trails for years in the same direction, then slowly relearns itself when the waters turn.

These trees breathe in increments. Their pores open like decisions. They gather dim light from the drowned day and dissolved minerals from the stone beneath them. Along their hair-fine leaves, gas beads form and tremble until they detach and wander upward into the pressure. If one listens very close, pressed against bark, one hears ticks and tiny knocks: sap shifting, trapped air migrating, colonies within the fibers answering warmth.

They are not silent because they are inert. They are silent because they are slow.

Around them live the lesser forests: moss columns suspended without ground, algae curtains folded in water, root-mats that have become drifting continents in miniature. Some are anchored to stone. Some to other plants. Some to nothing but density. There are seed-rafts that spend decades crossing a valley too slowly for any animal to notice; there are floating walls of root and silt that have accumulated so many generations of small growth that they possess an interior climate. Their outer surfaces are furred with feeding filaments. Their inner layers are dark, warm, bacterial, full of unspent spores.

The root-mats are almost creatures. They tighten before storms. They loosen when the warm vents exhale. They accept stones into themselves and carry them upward; they trap bones, ruins, bubbles, sleeping seeds. Sometimes two mats meet overhead and do not part, but begin exchanging fluids and seedlings until they become a single, larger drift. Whole gardens travel this way, slowly and without intention, bearing their own soil through the submerged sky.

On the stone live crusts that are older than many forests. They are microbial cities, lacquer-thin but layered with histories: mineral-eaters, light-drinkers, iron-binders, salt-weavers. They spread over basalt and limestone in black, green, and opalescent films. They taste the water’s chemistry before any larger organism knows a change has come. When a warm mineral plume shifts, they bloom first. When siltfall threatens to bury a ledge, they seal themselves under glassy skins and wait.

There are fungi too, though they are less like rot than persuasion. Pale antlered colonies grow beneath overhangs where the water is still and old light collects. They do not glow, exactly; they remember illumination. Brush them, and they answer with a delayed green afterimage, as though the touch has awakened yesterday. Their threads pass through roots, dead wood, old clay, fossil air pockets, and sometimes the mineralized posts of former structures. They digest little and negotiate much. A wounded root may become their passage. A buried trunk may become their archive. In anoxic mud, they keep things undecayed for centuries, holding them in a faint silver sleep.

The animals are fewer, and seem almost apologetic.

Small blind swimmers inhabit warm seams and vent gardens. They are translucent, with organs like pale knots and fins so fine they resemble torn skin. They gather where minerals rise, flickering through bacterial velvet and vanished the moment a shadow changes. Some live only long enough to follow one bloom. Some hatch inside sponge-caps on old posts, feed on drifting pollen, and die before leaving the clearing where they were born.

Tiny hunters drift among the spores. They are almost invisible until they feed: then their bodies fold around pollen, bacteria, or microscopic larvae, becoming brief bright knots in the water. They have no chase in them. They wait, open, close, and continue.

There are beetle-sized grazers, though they are not beetles and do not know air. They cling to leaves with many soft hooks, scraping films from the veins, riding the movements of the plant as though it were weather. During pressure storms they tuck beneath root lips and become indistinguishable from seeds. Their shells are flexible, amber-brown, mineral-dusted. When they die, epiphytes sprout from them before they reach the silt.

Farther off, in the dim troughs, the larger grazers move.

They are broad and flat, thin as shadows cast on water, with circular mouths on their undersides and bodies patterned like drowned leaves. They scrape mineral algae from old road-stone, from softened steps, from the backs of buried walls. Their eyes, if they have them, are not for images but for gradients of warmth and pressure. They may spend weeks crossing a single plateau. They arrive after blooms, when the water is sweet with reproductive dust, and pass through forests without urgency. Leaves brush over their backs. Roots settle after them. Sometimes one disappears beneath a root curtain and is not seen again, not because it has been killed dramatically, but because the place has accepted it into slower processes.

Rarer still are the long banner-finned creatures glimpsed between trunks.

They are not predators in the old, sharp sense. They nose among arches and suspended meadows, tasting currents. Their fins are ragged, dark, and translucent at the edges, trailing like torn ceremonial cloth. Their bodies are long enough that one may see the head pass and not recognize the tail when it follows later through another aisle of leaves. They feed on soft colonies, gas bladders, drifting seed clusters, and perhaps, sometimes, the sleeping young of the broad grazers. Their movements cause the forests to close behind them with an almost deliberate slowness.

Whether they are ancient survivors or newer inventions of the drowned world is impossible to tell. Here, evolution has no clean before and after. Catastrophe is too old to be origin anymore.

The vents have their own inhabitants.

Around the warm mineral plumes, life grows darker, thicker, more rubbery. Red-black leaves cup the heat. Bacterial velvet ripples though nothing touches it. Tubular colonies open and shut in the shimmering water, filtering iron, salt, and powdered stone. Some look like flowers and behave like mouths. Some look like wounds and are gardens. Bubbles collect beneath ledges in trembling ceilings, and within those bubbles live pale skinlike organisms that require both water and almost-air, spreading across the underside of trapped gas as if grazing an invisible pasture.

In the bubble chambers under roots and stone shelves, stranger things persist. Not true air-breathers, perhaps, but descendants of them: soft-bodied, lung-fringed, patient animals that cling upside down inside temporary atmospheres. They feed on spores caught at the air-water boundary. Their limbs are reduced to delicate braces. Their skins must remain wet, yet they seek the faint dryness of bubbles as if answering an ancestral habit. When pressure changes and their chamber collapses, they fold themselves into mucus and wait to be wetted back into motion.

There may also be people, or what became of people, but the place gives no certainty.

The structures suggest hands once moved here with purpose: stones placed in lines, steps carved into ridges, domes sealed under roots, rings of posts blackened and mineralized. If descendants of the builders remain, they do not live as builders now. They might dwell in the fossil air inside clay domes, pale and quiet, hearing the whole plateau through water pressing on the walls. They might have surrendered to the flooded ecology, becoming root-tenders, vent-listeners, keepers of membranes and pressure paths. Or they may be gone entirely, except as mineral habits that plants still follow: spirals around towers, clearings where no tree grows, openings that hum when currents pass through them.

The most convincing resident of that old intention is the tower-rib itself.

Water moves through its three rounded holes and makes a low tone, too steady to be speech but too shaped to be accident. Sponges cap nearby posts and open like eyes. Plants grow in a spiral around the base. Seeds passing through the tone alter course slightly. Small swimmers gather there during dim warm intervals and hang motionless in the vibration. The stone has become less a ruin than an organ the plateau uses to feel itself.

So what lives there is not ruled by animal hunger or quick alarm. It is a society of surfaces, attachments, blooms, films, spores, roots, vents, and enormous vegetal memory.

Its inhabitants communicate by pressure, chemistry, shade, warmth, and timing. A forest may flower because a tide presses from a continent away. A mat may tighten because a vent has changed flavor under the mountain. A fungus may release a root because the silt has grown too cold. A grazer may arrive weeks after a bloom by following sweetness diluted beyond any human measure. A ribbon-tree may bend for fifty years under one current and then, slowly, under another, preserving both directions in the twist of its trunk.

Nothing there needs to call out.

The drowned highland is alive in a manner closer to weather than to congregation. Its creatures are permitted by the water, slowed by it, enlarged into process. The plants are the citizens. The colonies are the language. The animals are brief thoughts moving through a mind made of wet stone and green pressure.

And beneath all of them lives the oldest inhabitant: the flood itself, no longer an event but a condition with habits. It carries pollen, bends forests, misplaces distance, gathers time in basins, loosens ruins into reefs, and teaches every living thing that survival is not resistance but accommodation. Everything there has learned to be broken gently, continuously, fruitfully.

It is a place crowded with life that has no interest in becoming visible all at once.
