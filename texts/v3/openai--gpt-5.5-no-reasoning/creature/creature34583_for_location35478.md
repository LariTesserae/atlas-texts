What lives there is not one creature, not exactly, though there are many creatures in it.

The bright hollow is inhabited by a community so complete and so fixed that it has become almost a single organism: a sealed littoral of stone, moisture, root, shell, gill, and sound. Its inhabitants are small, pale, damp-adapted things, but none of them seem accidental. They do not seem to have wandered in from the ordinary underground. They seem native to suspension itself.

Closest to the mineral skin live the snails.

They are numerous enough to make the walls look stippled. Their shells are not opaque but cloudy, crystalline, coiled like grains of salt that learned symmetry. Some are no larger than seeds; others are the size of a fingernail. Their bodies are translucent gray-white, with faint darker organs showing through like smoke behind vellum. Their eyestalks are shortened or absent. They do not need eyes in a place where light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Instead they attend with their whole soft bodies, each turned toward some private orientation in the brightness.

They rasp constantly. Their mouths travel over the mineral films, shaving away what cannot be seen: algae without color, luminous bacterial skins, powdered chalk, the invisible excess of the chamber. Their feeding is also maintenance. If they stopped, perhaps the walls would brighten too much, or glaze over, or begin to change. Their mouths are small clocks set into flesh. They keep the hollow from becoming something else.

Among them thread the rootlets, though to call the rootlets plant matter may be too simple. They behave less like growth than like stitching. They lace over stone, through clay, around nodules, under pools, into the seams between shale flakes. They enter cracks that seem too fine even for water. They bind without tightening. They drink without depleting. Their pale hairs tremble when the worms hum, and in that trembling there is a faint suggestion that the roots listen.

The visible plants are only the exposed syllables of a much larger hidden grammar. Thin stems, flat leaves, small coin-fronds, purple-edged straps—these are not a flora trying to spread. They are markers. Each plant occupies exactly the amount of space appointed to it, as if its final form had been decided before germination. They do not reach toward light, because light is already everywhere. They do not compete, because there is no surplus to compete for. They stand or cling or press themselves flat against the stone with a calm, emblematic patience.

Beneath leaves, in the undersides and folds, live the clear-bellied gnats.

They are wingless or nearly so, their wings reduced to glassy nubs, useless in a chamber where flight would only mean colliding with the ceiling. Their legs are long and delicate, their bodies faintly green from whatever they have fed on. Eggs cling in neat rows beneath the leaves, each egg rimmed by the same milk-white light that shines in the pools. The gnats do not swarm. They cluster. They arrange themselves along veins and edges as though punctuation has been placed on the plants.

In cracks too narrow to admit them, the pale crabs move.

They are not true crabs, perhaps, or not only crabs. Their bodies have the sideways grammar of crustaceans, but they are softened for stone: flattened, jointed, slick with permanent condensation. Their claws are tiny and precise, more like instruments of sorting than weapons. They pass through seams, emerge between mineral lobes, pause beside eggs or spores or pellets, and vanish again. They are the chamber’s hidden attendants. They rearrange what is nearly too small to matter: a shed skin, a grain of damp dust, a loosened flake, a dead gnat no one saw die.

If death happens here, it does not interrupt the pattern. It is incorporated immediately. Nothing rots in the dramatic sense. Nothing collapses into foulness. Dead bodies become pale scraps, then pellets, then films, then mineral memory. The crabs help this along. So do the worms. So do the snails. So, perhaps, does the light.

The worms hang from the ceiling in suspended curves, each one held by its own mucus thread. They are threadlike themselves, nearly indistinguishable from the strands that support them, except where their bodies thicken into faint knots. They do not climb, fall, hunt, or spin. They remain bowed in the air like living harp strings. When the chamber vibrates—through clicking legs, rasping mouths, the minute pressure of sealed earth—their bodies sing.

Their tones are thin but persistent. Some are too high to hear directly; others are felt in the teeth. Each worm holds one pitch. Together they give the hollow its chord, a tense shining sound that never resolves. They may be animals, but they are also tuned organs of the place. If one were removed, the light might seem slightly different. If one were added, the whole chamber might become unbearable.

In the basins lie the salamanders.

They are the largest obvious animals in the hollow, though still small enough to fit in two cupped hands. Their skin is pale rose, cream, or bluish white, almost luminous where the chamber-light rests on it. They have no eyes, or only sealed suggestions beneath the skin. Their limbs are delicate, their toes spread like little stars against the damp floor. Around their heads bloom external gills, red as exposed thread, branching in lace fans that pulse with terrifying gentleness.

They do not hunt in any visible way. They do not stalk the gnats or lunge at the crabs. They lie curled in shallow pools or damp basins, each positioned as if placed there in an ancient diagram. Their mouths open rarely. Their throats move. The gill-feathers pulse. They are the chamber’s deep animals, not because they dwell deepest, but because they seem to contain the most silence. Around them the clicking and humming continue, yet the salamanders make stillness legible.

Perhaps they are old. Perhaps age has no meaning here. A salamander may have lain in the same basin for a hundred years, or for the length of one held breath. Its pulse belongs to the hollow’s measure, not to ordinary time.

In the central bright patch stand the clicking things.

They are the most numerous and the most deliberate. Their bodies are compact, armored, low to the ground, like pill bugs or tiny trilobites, but they stand on many fine legs too delicate for such weighty little backs. Mineral grains pattern their shells in gray, ocher, and white-blue, so that each one seems partly made of the floor. Their antennae are short. Their heads are tucked low. They do not travel in lines, forage, scatter, or gather. They stand in place and click.

The clicking is not communication in the usual sense, because no message appears to pass and no behavior changes in response. It is more like enumeration. Each creature contributes a small hard sound to an overall pattern. Some click twice, some three times, some in broken clusters. Together their sounds interlock without becoming music. If counted long enough, the pattern would repeat exactly. It may be a census of the chamber, performed forever by bodies that are themselves included in the count.

These many-legged creatures are difficult to look at for too long. They give the impression of ritual without intention. They are not worshiping, because there is no object of worship. They are not waiting, because waiting implies a later. They are holding a place in a pattern, and that pattern may be what passes here for law.

There are others as well.

Small mites graze inside the root-mats, nearly transparent except for the white dust in their guts. Flat larvae cling to the undersides of stones, their bodies shaped like droplets that refuse to fall. Pale springtails gather around the rims of glowing pools and flex their forked tails without leaping. Tiny shrimp-shaped things are sealed in ceiling nodules, not fossilized but paused, curled in mineral wombs. Spores sleep in amber gel. Eggs remain eggs with the authority of finished beings. Shed skins lie folded in cracks, not discarded but archived.

The structures belong to the inhabitants, though perhaps no single species made them.

The shale columns may be the work of the pale crabs, each flake carried and set in place by claws too small to seem capable of architecture. Or they may be mineral growths encouraged by snail rasping. Or they may have been stacked by the clicking arthropods during some phase of their existence no longer visible. They stand with crude solemnity, waist-high only to an imagined miniature citizen, but imposing within the chamber’s scale.

The pebble ring around the glowing pool looks ceremonial, but it may also be practical. It may keep the pool from becoming shoreless. It may mark a boundary the salamanders do not cross. It may be the chamber’s idea of an eye.

The dried-stem platform between mineral knobs is just large enough for beetle-sized beings to gather. Perhaps the gnats crawl there during a stage when they briefly become something else. Perhaps the clicking creatures mount it one at a time, not to lead but to be sounded by the worms above. Perhaps nothing uses it now, and yet it remains essential, like a bone whose function has been forgotten by the animal that still needs it.

The scratched crescents and spirals on the ocher wall are the closest the hollow comes to speech.

They may have been made by crab claws, snail teeth, salamander toes, or the repeated brushing of root tips. But they have the unsettling quality of marks made in awareness. Rows of shallow crescents. Spirals. Clustered dots. Not language, perhaps, but pre-language: an urge to record before there is a story to record. In a place where nothing changes, writing cannot commemorate events. It can only affirm arrangement.

This lives here:
an arrangement.

A crowded, luminous arrangement of creatures that have no need for migration, ambition, concealment, weather, or future. They feed, but do not deplete. They reproduce, but do not increase. They die, if they die, without loss. They build, if they build, without progress. They sound continuously, but not to summon or warn. Every organism is both itself and a fixture of the chamber.

They are not trapped. Trapping would require a memory of elsewhere. These beings are interior by nature. Their bodies have been shaped not by darkness, exactly, but by sourceless brightness; not by scarcity, but by exactness; not by time, but by suspension.

Their senses must be strange. They likely feel the world through pressure, wetness, mineral taste, vibration, and contact. Touch is constant. No creature has the luxury of distance. A snail shell touches a root hair; a root hair touches a wet bead; the bead touches an insect’s antenna; the antenna quivers with the worm-chord; the chord trembles through a salamander’s gills; the salamander’s pulse enters the dust; the dust receives the clicking feet. To live here is to be continually adjacent. Privacy does not exist, but neither does intrusion. Contact is simply the medium.

If there is a ruling intelligence in the hollow, it is not centralized. It is distributed through repetition. The law of the place is enacted by every small mouth and leg and filament. The inhabitants maintain the brightness by occupying it. They keep the water from moving by drinking and exhaling it in perfect measure. They keep the stone young by preventing it from finishing itself. They keep sound from becoming silence and life from becoming history.

One could say the chamber lives in them as much as they live in it.

The snails are its patient teeth.

The rootlets are its nerves and sutures.

The worms are its strings.

The salamanders are its dreaming organs.

The clicking many-legged things are its counting heart.

The crabs are its hidden hands.

The gnats are its brief thoughts, clustered under leaves.

The spores, eggs, molts, and sealed embryos are its withheld futures.

And all of them together form a kind of buried unfinished animal: a bright, cool, crowded being below the skin of the ground, forever at the moment before growth becomes consequence.
