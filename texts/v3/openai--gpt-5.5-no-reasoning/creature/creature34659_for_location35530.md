What lives there is not a single creature at first.

It is easier, at first, to say that the district itself lives: not metaphorically, not exactly spiritually, but as a cold civic organism assembled from water pressure, rust, remnant electricity, trapped air, bacterial film, blind animals, and old work. It has no skin except its perimeter walls. It has no blood except the black water moving through storm drains, corridors, cracked pipework, and the canal gate. Its bones are gantries and tram rails. Its organs are boilers, switchboards, vats, refrigeration coils, culverts, sealed chambers, and silted basements. Its nerves are copper conduits that still sometimes remember current.

But inside that larger, drowned body, there are inhabitants.

They are few in the way lanterns are few in a mine. Each one matters. Each one has altered its habits until it belongs less to the world above than to this submerged quarter.

The most obvious are the blind fish.

They are small, silver-gray, translucent at the fins and belly, with eyes reduced to dark pinpricks under skin. They move in groups too loose to be schools and too deliberate to be random. Three pass through the gap between two warehouse doors. Five hover under tram wires. One turns sideways to slip through the smashed fan grille of the machine hall. They do not dart unless something changes in the pressure. Their normal motion is careful, almost bureaucratic, as if they are inspecting the drowned streets by district and schedule.

Their bodies have learned the map by current. They know the thin pull through the cooperage door, the colder thread from the refrigeration plant, the sour chemical avoidance near the battery works, the slow upward drift of pigment in the dye rooms. They feed on the almost nothing that lives on glass, brick, and old wood: bacterial lace, invisible films, the soft mouths of lesser things. In the blue pulses from the sleeping electrical system, they briefly appear as inscriptions written on water, then vanish back into blackness.

They do not own the place. Nothing owns it. But they are among its clerks.

The eel in the municipal baths is older than the fish seem to be.

It lives where people once learned to float. The tiled pool is cracked, green-black, and deepened by darkness beyond its original depth. The lane ropes hang slack and furred. The enamel signs remain on the wall: NO DIVING, WASH BEFORE ENTERING, CHILDREN’S HOURS. The eel coils in the deep end beneath a collapsed wooden bench, pale as peeled root, its eyes clouded but not useless. It has become thick in the body and narrow at the head, a thing of patience and delayed violence.

It does not often hunt by chase. It waits for mistakes: a fish disoriented by a pressure pulse, a worm shaken loose from the underside of a platform, a soft animal carried from the canal gate after storms. When the refrigeration plant exhales, the eel sinks lower, pressing its length against tile where old warmth remains stored in ceramic memory. When warm water comes from elsewhere, it rises but does not follow far. It has survived long enough to distrust generosity.

If the district has a hermit, it is the eel.

There are shelling things in the clocktower.

They are called limpets only because the mind wants a familiar word. They are pale, low, ridged colonies adhering to bell metal, clock faces, iron brackets, and the underside of the spiral stair. Their shells, if shells they are, have taken patterns from the tower: concentric rings like gear teeth, grooves like minute hands, ridges like cast lettering. They feed where vibration gathers. When the old bell shudders without striking, their bodies open by degrees, exposing milky fringes that tremble in the pressure wave.

Over many years they have made the bell softer. Not physically softer, perhaps, but less singular. Its metal no longer holds one clean note. Any vibration passing through it is caught, distributed, eaten, stored. The colonies have turned sound into sustenance. They are among the few inhabitants that respond to the clocktower as if it is still a clock.

They do not know hours. They know pulses.

There are worms in the silt.

Thin rust-gutted things, translucent except for the orange-brown thread of what they have swallowed. They live in the buried lanes, in gutters, around inspection covers, beneath the smooth silt floor of the central square. They make no visible tunnels unless light catches the faint disturbance of their passage. The smallest are like moving fibers. The largest, in the accountants’ basement and near the tannery lane, are long enough to be mistaken for wires until they withdraw.

They eat paper pulp, leather rot, bacterial mats, old glue, starch, softened wood, the dead skins of other organisms, and mineral-rich sediment sifted through mouths too small to see. They are the district’s slow digesters. Without them, the quarter would choke differently. With them, ledgers become mud, mud becomes film, film feeds fish, fish feed eel, eel dies someday and enters the same cold accounting.

Some have adapted to the chemical plumes. Near the dye vats they carry faint color: blue worms, yellow worms, a red-bodied strain that appears black except in electrical flashes. Near the battery works they are absent, or changed beyond recognition into stillness.

The bacterial clouds are almost weather.

They bloom from pipe mouths, vents, cracked gauges, warm seams, and places where old chemicals meet cold seepage. Some are white and granular. Some are brown and smoky. Some form standing veils in doorways, held in place by opposing currents. They do not look alive unless watched for a long time. Then one sees the edges thicken, fold, withdraw, extend; a colony answering invisible food.

They are the oldest citizens now, or the most numerous, if number still means anything at that scale. They occupy every flange, every gasket, every screw head, every ceramic insulator. They make lace on windows and velvet on submerged cloth. They cloud the old telephone exchange but leave the porcelain mouthpieces strangely clean. They have preferences. They flourish on ledger ink. They avoid certain copper salts. They gather around warm traces from below like people around a stove.

If the district thinks, it may be through them: distributed, chemical, without image.

At the air-water seams live the gray mats.

They grow in upper rooms where trapped air survives under sloping roofs. In the archives, they have colonized swollen paper and shelving. In the clerks’ office, they hang from ceiling beams in soft shelves, half fungus, half mineral appetite. Their upper surfaces breathe the stale pocket air. Their undersides trail into water. When pressure shifts, they tremble and release beads that rise, hesitate, or sink depending on what they have become.

They digest names.

Employee lists, shipping manifests, wage records, inspection certificates, disciplinary notices, maps of sewer repairs, municipal orders, inventories of lamp oil and bolts — all of it has passed into their gray flesh. Ink bleeds into them in brown-blue veins. Dates vanish first, then signatures, then printed headings. Sometimes, when an electrical pulse lights the room, one can see fragments suspended in the mats like thoughts not fully dissolved:

RECEIVED  
SHIFT B  
FROST DAMAGE  
UNACCOUNTED  
RETURNED TO STORES

The mats do not remember as a person remembers. Still, they preserve something. Their growth follows the bureaucratic layout of the shelves. Their lobes are arranged by ledger size. Their densest fruiting bodies occur where records of absence and death once lay. They are archives after ink.

There are organisms that imitate hardware.

Around valves and screw threads, white crusts grow in spirals too regular to be merely mineral and too rigid to be merely animal. They form collars on pipe mouths, knuckles on chains, small toothed reefs along cable trays. Some open when charged water passes through copper, exposing hairlike filaments that glow faintly green. Some close when touched by colder currents. They make the old machinery appear to be repairing itself in bone.

In the machine hall, entire clusters of these things have grown over pressure gauges. Their bodies follow the needles, encrusting the arcs of readings no one will ever take. At first glance the gauges seem frozen at old values. Closer inspection shows that the colonies have made new dials, new scales, new units based on seep, salinity, tremor, and cold.

They are not machines, but they have learned the grammar of machines.

There are roots in the administrative stairwell.

They come from a plane tree that is dead or nearly dead beyond the wall, above or beside the flood. The roots entered through stone when there was still soil to seek, then found water, then stayed. Underwater they hang black, fine, hairlike, and unnervingly articulate. They do not grow quickly. Perhaps they do not grow at all now. But they persist in a state between memory and hunger.

Small animals shelter among them. Silt collects in their forkings. Bacterial films silver their tips. When rain presses on the flood above, the roots move before anything else, sensing the change as if the drowned district still had a season.

They are the only inhabitants that clearly belong to two worlds: the drowned one and the one that may remain overhead.

There is also the animal that failed.

Its bones lie in the ticket office near the canal gate: seal, dog, or something made ambiguous by water and time. The skull is narrow and tilted upward, as if still searching for air. Ribs hold lacework deposits. Vertebrae are strung with algae. One forelimb is wedged beneath the rusted footrail of the ticket counter. Its fur, if any remains, has become a mineralized blur along the silt.

Yet even it lives now as habitat.

Worms work through the sediment around it. Pale crusts bead on the teeth. Fish sometimes shelter in the rib cage when pressure comes from the greater flood. Bacteria grow differently on bone than on brick. The failed visitor has been naturalized. The district did not let it leave, but neither did it waste it.

And then there is what lives in the refrigeration plant.

That is harder to name.

The plant is not inhabited by an animal in the ordinary sense, though things cling to its grilles and gather at its outflow. It is not conscious in the ordinary sense, though it responds. It is a machine continuing under impossible conditions, and life has arranged itself around its continuing until the distinction between mechanism and organism has become unhelpful.

Inside the plant, compressors shudder in the dark. Mineral-thick pipes carry temperature like a secret. A few gauges still glow faintly with radium or some exhausted industrial substitute. Cold brine circulates through ruptured loops. Somewhere below, a gradient is being harvested: metro warmth, city current, geothermal seep, old power, error. The plant exhales water so cold and clear that life slows almost to suspension. Around its vents, metal rings sharply. Silt falls out of the water. Fish avoid the first breath, then return to feed along the edge where slowed microbes collect.

The organisms nearest the outflow are glassy. Their tissues have become antifreeze and patience. Transparent fans grow on the grille bars, folding shut during the coldest exhalations. Beaded colonies line the brick like frozen spawn, though nothing freezes. White filamentous things extend into the clear flow and withdraw when the compressors tremble. They are not abundant, but they are exact. They live where most life is excluded, in the margin of a machine’s old command: maintain cold.

If the district has a heart, these are the heart’s parasites, priests, and sensors.

But beyond all these — fish, eel, worms, mats, crusts, roots, bacterial veils, shell colonies, failed mammal, cold-vent organisms — there is the larger resident that cannot be separated from them.

The drowned quarter has become a composite inhabitant of itself.

Its awareness, if it has one, is not centered. It is spread through relays that click once a month, bubbles that choose old stairwells, chemical snows falling upward, map paper influencing current, fish vanishing before cold, gauges quivering, colonies opening to vibration, and lamps arranging themselves into brief false constellations. It does not think in sentences. It computes in deposits. It remembers by corrosion. It answers disturbance with pressure.

A diver entering would not meet a monster immediately. They would meet rules.

The first touch of cold would be the district taking the temperature of them. The silt would receive their movement and store it. The blind fish would widen their routes. The gray mats would drink the skin cells and fabric fibers they left behind. The limpets in the clocktower would feel their tank strike a railing three buildings away. A typewriter key might depress in the drowned office, releasing a black ribbon of ink. The refrigeration plant might wait, then exhale.

Not hostility, exactly. Incorporation.

The place does not chase. It surrounds. It classifies by buoyancy, taste, warmth, vibration, and decay. It asks every arrival the same question the flood once asked the district: can you become part of this pressure?

Most cannot.

What lives there, then, is a society without speech and a machine without purpose, an ecology made from industrial leftovers, an archive that digests its own records, a cold animal whose organs are buildings.

It has citizens:

blind fish like moving filings of light,  
an eel in the baths, old and patient,  
bell-fed shell colonies in the clocktower,  
rust-bellied worms under the square,  
fungal archivists eating names,  
bacterial weather in rooms and pipes,  
hardware-shaped crusts growing new dials over old ones,  
roots persisting from a dead upper world,  
glass organisms at the cold vents,  
and the bones of those who entered wrongly.

But the true inhabitant is the drowned work itself.

It lives as aftermath lives when it does not end. It lives by continuing processes no one oversees. It lives in the conversion of labor into mineral, noise into pressure, streets into channels, documents into food, electricity into brief blue memory, and cold into law.

It is not dead.

It is still on shift.
