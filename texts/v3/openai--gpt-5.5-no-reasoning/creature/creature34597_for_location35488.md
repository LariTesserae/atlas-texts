What lives there are people who have learned to be less like conquerors of land than its attendants.

They are small-town people in the oldest sense: not numerous, not rich, not separate from the place that shelters them. They are compact in habit, economical in gesture, and saturated with the knowledge that everything upright is temporary unless touched again. Their bodies carry the town’s posture. Shoulders angled for narrow lanes. Knees accustomed to crouching. Fingers thickened by clay, cord, reed, hide, damp stone. They move sidelong without seeming to notice it, turning through alleys the way water turns through a channel.

Their skin is often chapped from wet and ash. Their palms are pale in the creases, darkened elsewhere by smoke, peat, clay, and lichen pigment. Hair is worn bound close, braided tight, wrapped in strips of reed fiber or softened hide, not for ornament alone but because loose hair catches damp, smoke, mold, and work. Beards, when kept, are short. Clothing is layered and practical: scraped hide, woven reed, felted plant fiber, patched fur, shell buttons, bone pins, slings and belts from which hang awls, wedges, plugs, little knives, measuring cords, charms against slumping.

They smell faintly of smoke even when wet, because smoke is one of the town’s preservatives and nearly everyone passes through it daily. Children smell of clay and sour milk. Elders of ash, old leather, and medicinal bitterness. Workers from the ditches carry the sharp mineral odor of fresh-cut earth.

They are not a loud people. They can shout when the bank gives way or a roof shifts, but ordinary speech is low, close, threaded through the constant dripping. A person here does not call across distance often because there is little distance. They murmur from doorways, from roof ledges, from below storage racks. Much is said by touch: a palm against a wall, two fingers to a damp patch, a knuckle tapped on a jar, a hand laid briefly on someone’s shoulder before passing in a narrow lane.

They live by noticing.

Their intelligence is not abstract first, but tactile, acoustic, seasonal, procedural. They know the difference between a healthy drip and a dangerous one. They can tell by the smell of a cistern whether the water has begun to turn. They know which walls may be leaned on, which roofs may be walked across, which floor mats must be lifted after rain, which children should not be sent down which lanes because their feet are too quick and careless. They know the sound of a full channel under planks. They know when silence means blockage.

To outsiders they might seem anxious, but they are not exactly fearful. Their alertness is communal discipline. Panic wastes hands. Carelessness costs houses. They are calm in emergencies because every day is a small rehearsal for collapse.

Their society is organized around maintenance more than command. Authority belongs to those who can read change early. The old woman who hears a cistern crack before anyone else believes it. The quiet man who remembers how the south bank behaved eleven rains ago. The child who notices that the shells in the clay have turned their mouths outward. The roof-mender whose feet know where weight may safely fall. Leadership moves with the problem.

There are named roles, though they overlap:

The seep-watchers rise before dawn and walk the basin wall with lamps or glow-coals shielded in shell cups. They press their cheeks close to the clay, taste moisture from a fingertip, mark new wetness with reed pins.

The cistern-keepers tend the buried vessels and covered water places. They are patient people, often older, trained to hear depth by echo and health by the behavior of the little blind crustaceans that live below.

The plug-makers weave willow, moss, reed, hair, clay, and ash into temporary answers. Their work is humble and vital. A good plug can save a wall for a season. A bad one merely teaches water a new path.

The floor-raisers are builders of layered ground. They work with shell, gravel, broken pottery, matting, clay, and memory. They know that every room is a stack of previous failures and try to make each failure useful to the next.

The smoke-tenders keep the kiln-house alive. They dry fish, harden tablets, cure cordage, warm infants, darken roof reeds, and prepare the black lichen pigment used for marks and seals.

The listeners sit on the six-step stair and at certain wall niches. They are not priests exactly, though outsiders would mistake them for such. They interpret clicks, bubbles, delayed whispers, sideways threads of water. Their records are scratched into bone strips, knotted into cords, pressed into clay tablets, and argued over in low voices.

The children are useful early. Not exploited thoughtlessly, but folded into the life of the place because small hands fit where adult hands cannot. They fetch stones for thatch, crawl under sleeping platforms to retrieve lost plugs, watch puddles for color change, carry messages across roof paths, gather exposed shells under supervision, and learn which kinds of mud may be stepped in and which must be skirted. Their games imitate repair: building miniature berms, damming trickles, racing droplets through scratched channels, making tiny houses and then deliberately flooding them to see which survive.

They do not have many animals. A few thin dogs live among them, quiet and serious, bred or selected less for hunting than for warning. These dogs do not bark often; they whine at pressure shifts, paw at covered drains, refuse certain floorboards. A good dog is considered almost a water-reader. There may be tethered birds in reed cages for eggs or alarm, but few. Livestock would churn the ground too much, eat too much stored reed, and sicken in the damp. The town cannot afford hooves.

They eat what the wet edge permits. Smoked fish when fish come. Eels or eel-like creatures from drainage pits, treated with caution and ritual because their arrival is never fully explained. Marsh roots, bitter greens, stored grains traded from firmer places, shellfish if nearby waters provide them, bird eggs, occasional meat from hunts beyond the unstable flats. Food is preserved with smoke, ash, drying, souring, and careful elevation above damp. A spoiled basket is not merely a loss; it is a warning that some part of the town’s arrangement has failed.

They make beautiful things, but beauty here is seldom separate from use. Measuring rods are carved with water signs and small repeated notches like rainfall. Plug handles are shaped into fish, hands, teeth, sleeping eyes. Bone needles bear family marks. Cistern lids are painted with charcoal rings and shell-white dots. Children wear strings of translucent spiral shells despite elder unease, and the strings glow faintly in gray light like captured bubbles.

Their ornaments tend to be small because space is small. A tooth at the throat. A polished pebble sewn into a sleeve. A spiral shell hidden in hair. A strip of dyed reed around the wrist indicating a person survived a wall-fall, descended into a cistern, bore a child during flood weather, or heard the listening wall speak clearly.

They are ritual people because repetition is survival. But their rituals are not grand performances under open sky. They are embedded in work.

A new wall receives palmprints while still damp, not as decoration only but as introduction: these are the hands that will return to you. A post is planted with a stone and tooth so it will understand firmness and appetite. A repaired path is walked by three generations if possible: child, adult, elder, so the path remembers beginning, labor, and duration. A cistern cleaned after sickness is left open to rain, then greeted with a whispered inventory of what it has held before: clean water, bad water, frog spawn, lost ring, one child’s reflection, three seasons of drought fear, ash from the kiln-house.

They do not believe matter is inert. Nor do they think everything is alive in the way a person is alive. Rather, everything has a tendency. Clay tends to slump. Reeds tend to lift and return to reed-bed. Water tends to remember lower places even before they exist. Tools tend to lose edges if insulted by being left point-down. Walls tend to forget straightness. The task of people is to remind things of their present agreements.

Their dead are not buried deep. Deep belongs to water. The dead may be placed in raised clay chambers, folded beneath floors only after careful sealing, set into bank niches lined with shell and reed, or reduced by smoke and bone-sorting in the kiln-house. Ancestors are not imagined as far away. They are underfoot in the practical sense: part of floor layers, wall temper, family thresholds, name cords. A house contains its dead the way it contains its older floors.

Memory is architectural. A family history may be recited as repairs: the year the east wall shone silver; the season the lower sleeping room breathed upward; the day grandmother heard the cistern mutter after rain; the flood that brought eels into the grain pit; the child who found shells where no shells had been.

Birth is treated carefully because infants and damp do not bargain fairly. Newborns are kept near smoke but not too near, wrapped in warmed reed fiber and soft hide, their sleeping shelves hung above floor level. The first outing of a baby is often not to the basin rim or a shrine, but to the water court, where the cistern lid is touched and the child’s foot is shown, not dipped, to the stored water. The meaning is simple: here is another who will need you; here is another who will help hold you.

They know outsiders, but outsiders are rare and uneasy here. Traders from firmer ground bring grain, timber, stone blanks, pigments, salt, stories of high dry places where one may dig without finding wetness. They leave with smoked goods, woven mats, medicinal roots, black lichen pigment, perhaps the town’s peculiar clay tablets, which dry hard and light and hold impressions well. Outsiders find the lanes oppressive, the quiet suspicious, the constant maintenance exhausting. They sometimes joke that the people are servants of mud. The people do not mind. Mud is more dependable than pride if properly handled.

They may appear poor, and by many measures they are. Little metal. Little surplus. Few beasts. No broad fields. No monumental structures. Yet they possess wealth of another kind: stored techniques, exact thresholds, communal responsiveness, intimate maps of unstable ground. Their riches are in things not happening: a wall not falling, grain not rotting, a child not sinking through a crusted path, a cistern not cracking through the cold months.

Their faces, when at rest, often seem inward-turned, listening. Even in conversation their attention may drift to a roof sound, a change in dripping, a dog’s low whine. This is not rudeness. In that town, the place is always also speaking.

There are other residents too, though fewer and less visible.

Blind cistern crustaceans, pale as bits of fingernail, move in the stored dark and are watched like omens. Midges rise when the damp warms. Moss grows on shaded stones, tolerated where useful, scraped where dangerous. White fan-fungi bloom beneath neglected mats like little soft warnings. Black lichen fattens on smoke-warmed stone and becomes pigment, medicine, perhaps poison in the wrong dose. Eel-things wait wherever underground water finds a secret path. Marsh birds step outside the berms with the long-legged caution of creatures who know the land is unfinished.

And beneath all of them lives, or almost lives, the hollow itself.

Not a beast exactly. Not a god exactly. A young depression with habits. A thumbprint still filling. A lake’s dream. A footprint kept from vanishing. It sweats, remembers, repeats whispers, rearranges shells in its banks, borrows sharpness from downward-facing tools, presses back against floors at night. It is not malicious. It is becoming. That is dangerous enough.

The people who live there are therefore not masters of a settlement but participants in a long negotiation. They wake each morning into a question: will this place remain a place today? Their answer is always physical. Hands smoothing clay. Stones carried. Reeds tied. Channels cleared. Water listened to. Names repeated.

They are the inhabitants of a town that survives by being continually re-made: damp, close-voiced, practical, superstitious in useful ways, tender with materials, suspicious of dryness, respectful of pressure, and patient beyond what people from stable ground can understand.

They live there because they know how.

And because, by now, the hollow knows them back.
