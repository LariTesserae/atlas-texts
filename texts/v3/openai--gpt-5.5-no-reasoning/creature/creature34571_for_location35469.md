They are the dry-city people—if “people” is still the right word for a population that has lived for generations among disciplined roots, mineral air, and buildings that answer to touch.

They are not plants, but no one here is simply animal either.

Their bodies have learned the place. They are narrow, economical beings, built less for strength than for conservation: long-limbed, spare, pale where covered, darkened or silvered where exposed to filtered light. Their skin has the matte dryness of seed husk or fine ceramic. It does not shine with sweat. Some lineages no longer sweat at all; others exude faint salts along the clavicle, wrists, and temples, which are brushed away and collected in the old domestic habit. Their lips are thin and often sealed behind breathing shells outdoors, not because the air is poisonous, but because every exhalation is resource and every inhalation must be filtered of pollen, mineral dust, fungal spores, and charged industrial fines.

They move quietly, even when there are many of them.

The city has taught them not to stamp, not to shout, not to waste sound in corridors where sound is used by machines, animals, and walls. Their footgear is soft-soled and segmented, gripping bark, resin, mineral dust, and tremoring walkways without bruising living surfaces. Children learn early to distinguish what may be stepped on, what must be skirted, what is dormant, what is feeding, what is load-bearing, and what merely looks like pavement. A careless footprint on young cambium can scar a street for years.

They are veiled more often than clothed in the surface sense. Their garments are layered against cold and abrasion: close underwraps of fiber felt, outer mantles of bark-cloth, mineral-dyed sashes, porous shoulder shells, tool-belts grown from flexible reed-composite. Clothing is not bright. It follows the city’s palette—ash white, lichen gray, old copper, resin black, dull green, oxidized blue. Color is used sparingly and legally: a saffron veil for pollination workers, a red-black collar for fire wardens, pale bands for those licensed to enter immature districts, ultraviolet-threaded hems for night-cycle navigators.

Most wear masks outside the sealed interiors.

Some masks are simple breathing veils, pleated and stiffened with dry leaf-fiber. Others are smooth, fitted shells covering nose, mouth, and jaw, with replaceable mineral filters and small side bladders that reclaim moisture from breath. Engineers and growers wear larger respiratory cowls, like the faces of patient insects, fitted with pollen gates, chemical tongues, and whisper-comms. Among elders, there are ornate masks grown rather than manufactured: thin root lattices trained over molds, lacquered with resin, the filters braided into living membranes that must be fed salts once a week.

Their eyes tend to be large, dark, and reflective, accustomed to even artificial light rather than sun. Many have a second translucent lid or wear clear lens-caps against dust. Their eyelashes are pale and dense. Their pupils narrow quickly under ultraviolet lamps. In pollination phase, when hidden patterns appear on leaves and animal skins, their own faces sometimes answer: faint bioluminescent freckles along the cheekbones, lineage marks under the skin, occupational tattoos that glow in coded rings.

They are quiet, but not somber.

They laugh softly, with shoulders more than mouths. They greet by touching the backs of fingers, or by briefly laying a palm against the nearest approved handrail so that the building registers the meeting. In crowded civic halls, greetings ripple through walls as much as through voices: pressure, warmth, identity salts, tiny electrical signatures. Old districts know their residents. A threshold may loosen when its keeper approaches. A bench may warm a degree for a habitual sitter. A stair may hold its angle for the limp of an elder.

They do not think of this as magic. It is maintenance, law, and courtesy.

Their society is organized around growth-rights, air-rights, mineral allocations, and structural maturity. A family does not own a house in the fixed sense; it holds tenancy in a living volume, with obligations to prune, feed, inspect, and report changes in grain, flex, disease, brittleness, unauthorized sprouting, and animal intrusion. Rooms are not renovated so much as persuaded. A nursery alcove is budded from a domestic trunk. A workroom is widened by months of pressure training. A doorway can be petitioned into existence if traffic justifies it and the local wall-organism is judged healthy enough to heal around the cut.

Architects here are partly gardeners, partly surgeons, partly civic theologians. They carry calipers, nutrient needles, legal tablets, and pruning saws with ceramic teeth. They read stress in bark. They can hear when a column is carrying too much by the pitch of its midnight creak. They know which arch-vines can be crossed with scaffold reeds, which fungal resins make a bridge too rigid, which leaf-skins photosynthesize well under lens-filtered light, which neighborhoods are beginning to close themselves around old habits.

Children aspire to become arch-trainers, salt-harvesters, pollination conductors, root advocates, mineral chemists, ventilation listeners, animal-route keepers, or silence inspectors.

The silence inspectors are not police exactly, though they can issue fines. They monitor vibration, machinery resonance, corridor noise, crowd behavior, and the stress responses of living streets. A festival drumbeat in the wrong avenue can startle sensory grasses, spook pruning herds, alter pollinator flight, and trigger defensive closure in young doorways. Sound is infrastructure. Silence is a public resource.

Water, to them, is not ordinary.

They know it chemically, industrially, ritually, medically. They know it as something bound, measured, reclaimed from breath, sealed in ampoules, traded under civic seal. Children see liquid water first in lessons: a bead trembling inside a transparent capsule, passed from hand to hand under supervision. They are told not to romanticize it, then grow up surrounded by songs, taboos, metaphors, and mourning customs that do exactly that.

To spill drinkable water is obscene. To weep openly is intimate beyond nakedness. Public tears are either tragedy or protest. Some people train themselves never to cry; others save the salt-streaked cloths from funerals in sealed family reliquaries. Lovers may exchange a captured breath through twin filters. Parents sometimes press a condensation bead from their own mask into the mouth of a newborn. The act is called first weather.

They eat from the city without imagining themselves separate from it.

Their food is dry, dense, mineral, aromatic: pressed leaf-protein wafers; cork-grain; salt-rib shavings; fungal cakes cultured in dark vats; pale tubers that grow in sealed nutrient sleeves; insects roasted in induction pans; lizard eggs cured in alkali ash; thin strips of herd-beast flesh dried until they can be snapped. Warm meals exist but are clustered around factory exhausts and domestic heat-throats. Steam is unknown except in sealed kitchens, and even there it is captured. Soups are ceremonial, not casual. A household that serves broth is making a declaration: of mourning, alliance, wealth, birth, or apology.

Their homes smell of resin, cloth dust, mineral spice, old filters, and the faint green bitterness of trained walls. They sleep in recessed chambers lined with cork-fiber, where the air is fractionally warmer and breathing condensers hum beside the beds. Infants are kept in cradle-pods grafted into domestic trunks, not because the trunks nurse them, exactly, but because the living walls buffer temperature and sound better than any machine. The pod records breath and movement. If a child stops breathing, the whole room tightens and rings.

They have pets, though the distinction between pet, pest, tool, and co-resident is fluid.

Small beetle swarms are kept by some households to polish domestic photoskins. Pale lizards are tolerated because they eat filter-gnats. Click-birds, blind and clever, nest near transit stations and learn human footfall patterns; some imitate family knocks or machine warnings. Children bond with long-legged dust rodents and hide them from municipal trappers until the animals chew a root-line and cause a district complaint. The pruning herds belong to the city, but individual grazer-beasts are named by the neighborhoods they serve. Predators are not named aloud unless one has seen them directly and lived without panic.

Their laws include animals. Not sentimentally; practically. A hunting corridor blocked by an illegal workshop will be opened by order of the ecological court. A nesting cavity sealed during construction must be replaced within a defined radius and height. Killing a predator without license is punishable if it results in grazer overpopulation and vine damage. Children recite the old civic balance: streets for passage, roots for load, beasts for correction, death for return.

Death is dry.

Bodies are not buried in wet earth; there is none. Nor are they burned except in rare criminal or plague contexts, fire being too feared and too wasteful. The dead are dried, named, measured, and taken to decomposition chambers, where carrion plants and mineral fungi unfold in darkness. Families may keep a small rib of crystallized salt from the process, set into a doorframe or family mask. To be incorporated into a public structure is an honor. Some elders petition to have their processed mineral share fed to a bridge, a school grove, a ventilation tower, or an immature district where children will one day walk.

They do not say the dead are gone into the ground. They say they have entered load.

Religion, where it persists, is architectural and respiratory. The oldest prayers are addressed not upward but outward—to air, load, lumen, root, and interval. Their sanctuaries are not lush gardens but disciplined groves of columnar trunks grown in perfect alignment, each one bearing the slight thickening of thousands of hands. Congregants stand in cool light while pressure lungs breathe through the hall. The sermon may be a reading of civic maintenance logs, genealogies of buildings, chemical allocations, and remembered failures: the mold year, the brittle bridge collapse, the pollen panic, the illegal cistern, the fire in the reed mills.

They fear fire more than violence. They fear uncontrolled moisture almost as much.

Too much water means swelling, rot, microbial bloom, structural confusion, old diseases waking. Their myths of apocalypse are not floods exactly, because most have never seen one; they imagine walls softening, stairs sagging, food stores blooming black, masks clogging with wet growth, mineral flats turning to sucking paste. In children’s stories, the monster is sometimes a shining thing that drips. It gives generously until everything dissolves.

Yet there are dissenters.

There are surface-dreamers who gather in unused conduit chambers to trade contraband images of rain, oceans, leaves broad as blankets, human skin beaded with sweat. There are moisture cults that keep illegal droplets in seed-glass, worshiping decay as liberation. There are engineers who argue the city has become too severe, that controlled wet zones could multiply food production. There are old-line dry traditionalists who call this rot-thinking. There are young builders who want more animal autonomy, more unassigned space, more darkness. There are factory syndicates that want faster growth, thinner safety margins, more aggressive mineral extraction.

And beneath all of them are the plants, which have politics of their own, though no one agrees whether this is metaphor.

A district overused by one faction may narrow its doors. A neglected administrative grove may flower out of phase, delaying a hearing. Unauthorized tendrils appear near disputed excavation faces. Public handrails thicken under protest marches, preserving the route in living tissue long after officials erase the pigment signs. The city edits itself according to habit, but habit includes dissent. Every movement that repeats becomes a request addressed to bark.

Their language reflects this. They do not say “build” when they mean the making of a place. They have separate verbs for seed-build, train-build, harden-build, cut-build, occupy-build, and remember-build. They do not say a road “goes” somewhere; it is “kept open toward” that place. A room that is rarely used is “withdrawing.” A popular market is “being believed by the floor.” A person who cannot adapt is “wet-minded” or “stone-minded,” depending on whether they are chaotic or rigid. To be wise is to be “well-ventilated.”

Their writing blooms.

Public text appears as pigment, raised grain, pruned notches, scent codes, conductive bead-lines, and ultraviolet markings. Announcements are grown on sign panels that flush with color and then fade as the event passes. Laws are carved only when they are meant to outlast a district’s harvest cycle. Personal letters are often written on shed bark slips, thin and aromatic. For secrecy, one writes chemically: invisible until breathed upon through the right filter.

Their economy is a circulation of obligations as much as goods.

Air workers are paid in warmth credits. Salt harvesters receive mineral allotments and early access to new domestic alcoves. Pollination crews are compensated with food privileges during high-allergen cycles. Families who maintain nesting cavities receive pest abatements. Those who live near ventilation towers accept constant vibration in exchange for better air, more reliable transit, and first claim on warmed public baths—not water baths, but dry heat chambers where bodies sit in mineral vapor and reclaimers drink the exhaled moisture from the walls.

They trade with somewhere else, presumably. Surface caravans, sealed elevators, deep rail lines, or neighboring hollows. But outsiders find the city difficult. They breathe too loudly, ask for water, touch immature walls, mistake living railings for decoration, panic when a door wilts shut behind them. The residents are polite in the distant way of people whose entire world can be damaged by ignorance. Visitors are assigned guides, masks, permitted paths, and sometimes mild sedatives during pollination phase.

There are also those who are not quite human residents.

Caretaker organisms move among them: ambulatory grafts, root-backed haulers, semi-animal maintenance bodies shaped by civic horticulture. Some resemble stooped figures made of pale bark and tendon, with tool sockets instead of hands. Others are wheeled or many-legged, carrying nutrient cartridges, pruning blades, sensor arrays, or young vines in moistless gel. They are not treated as citizens by all, but many households speak to them, name them, leave mineral offerings in their paths. A child may be rescued by one and grow up insisting it chose.

The boundary between engineered organism and laborer is politically dangerous. Some dry-city people carry symbiotic implants: lung filters grown along the ribs, mineral-processing gut flora, conductive nerve-thread used by ventilation listeners, photosensitive skin patches that warn of bad light. Some lineages were altered long ago to survive low humidity and oxygen discipline. If a person can photosense, reclaim breath-water, root a fingertip temporarily into a diagnostic port, and hear stress in wood, is that culture, medicine, or speciation?

The city does not answer. It accommodates function.

Their children are lively in the way of young things raised among hazards: controlled, watchful, inventive. They play at opening and closing doors by imitating wilt commands. They race beetles along inactive conduit ribs. They learn to click like blind birds and navigate blindfolded through echo courts. They plant forbidden seeds in cracks and argue that any sprout which survives municipal scraping has earned citizenship. They collect shed scales from pruning beasts, dead pollen from filters, curled leaf chips, mica dust, and tiny broken machine teeth.

Their elders become increasingly architectural. They walk slowly through districts they helped train, touching old decisions. This arch was once too narrow. This stair was petitioned after a strike. This wall still bears the scar of the fire wardens’ axes. This plaza was empty when they were young; now its shade-skins are mature enough to host markets. They remember when the cavern vault was raw over certain neighborhoods, before the lenses were installed and the light went steady.

The city is young, but they have already developed nostalgia.

Not for nature. For earlier phases of construction. For the first towers before they thickened. For the days when one could see across flats now occupied by administrative trunks. For the raw excavation face where they kissed someone through masks. For the old freight rail before the cork padding was replaced and its rumble could be felt in the sternum. For the first unauthorized green line in a crack, later declared the beginning of a district.

They live in a place that is always unfinished, and so they are unfinished too.

They do not imagine permanence as stone people do. Their civic ideal is not preservation unchanged, but correct succession: seed, train, harden, occupy, adapt, harvest, return, regrow. A beloved building is not expected to stand forever. Its value lies in whether it bore load well, shaped good habits, housed fair exchanges, and left viable cuttings. Even memory is propagated vegetatively: a story grafted onto a new hall, a dead engineer’s handprint trained into a public rail, a court decision encoded into the branching pattern of a legal grove.

At a distance, crossing a pale mineral flat under cool artificial light, they look like small dark motes moving between enormous patient organisms.

Up close, they are dusted with pollen, smelling of resin and ozone, their masks clicking softly as they breathe. Their hands are callused not from stone but from bark, clamps, filter screws, pruning blades, ceramic saws, and the habit of reading surfaces. They pause often—not from fear, but from attention. They feel the air change. They notice a street listening. They step aside before a herd appears. They touch a wall and know whether it is thirsty for salts, overburdened, asleep, diseased, or remembering too many hands.

They are citizens of a city that grows around them and because of them and in spite of them.

They are dry, careful, severe, communal, quietly tender, and difficult to separate from their infrastructure. They have made an urban life out of scarcity without making it barren. They have learned to love restraint, not as denial but as form. They are not gardeners living underground; they are the pollinators, inspectors, parasites, heirs, negotiators, and conscience of an industrial plant-city.

And when they stand beneath the light-wells during growth phase, veils pale in the filtered glow, while the towers breathe and the leaf-skins pulse with slow amber sap, they seem less like inhabitants placed inside an environment than one of its mobile organs: the thinking, arguing, remembering tissue of a dry underworld still deciding what kind of city it intends to become.
