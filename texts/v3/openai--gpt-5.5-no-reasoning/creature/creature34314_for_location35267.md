What lives there is not a creature, not exactly.

It is a colony only in the oldest and thinnest sense of that word: a life spread flat, life without limbs or hunger, life that has given up almost every drama associated with being alive. It lives as dark green adherence on the white mineral faces, in stains and crusts and brittle scales, not moving through the chamber but accepting the few places where the chamber permits it to be.

It is a photosynthetic film, or something analogous to one: a severe, reduced organism made for brightness, dryness, and duration. It has no body in the usual sense. No center. No mouth. No organs. No visible boundary that suggests will. Each patch is a little country of cells compressed into a skin, a matte black-green tarnish on salt and carbonate, darkening the pale architecture where chemistry has made the surface barely hospitable.

It lives in islands.

Some islands are no larger than a fleck of ash. Some spread into thumbprint-shaped plaques with irregular, fretted margins. Some follow cracks in hair-thin lines, making the seams look inked. Others form tiny rosettes, hard scales overlapping like the armor of a microscopic plant that has forgotten softness. Their green is not lush. It is old bottle glass, dried olive, the black underside of a leaf pressed too long in a book. In the brightest places the crusts are almost charcoal; in the shallow concavities they show a faint mineral sheen, as though they have absorbed a little of the white light and hardened it into pigment.

They do not drink. At least, they do not drink as plants drink. They wait for no rain. They lift no roots toward hidden water. Moisture, if it ever touched them in any abundance, might destroy them as much as feed them. Their metabolism is tuned to absence. They take from light, from salts, from traces locked in the stone, from infinitesimal exchanges too slow to count as nourishment by the standards of animals. They live on the edge between chemistry and biology, making a virtue of almost nothing.

Their time is immense and nearly motionless.

A human life would pass without seeing them grow. A century might only thicken a rim, darken a pore, extend a fringe by the width of a powdered grain. They do not bloom. They do not shed leaves. They do not turn toward morning, because there is no morning. They do not sleep, because there is no night. Their living is a maintained condition, a held note inside the louder mineral note of the chamber.

They are not lonely, because loneliness requires expectation. They have never known animals. Nothing grazes them. Nothing nests in them. Nothing carries them from wall to wall. Their dispersal, if it happens, must be internal and rare: a flake lifting during some microscopic relaxation of crystal stress; a cell waking along a fracture where the chemistry has become permissive; a border dividing so slowly that spreading and remaining are almost the same act.

They are disciplined by the place. They do not cover it. They cannot make a forest of it, cannot soften it, cannot green it into abundance. The mineral architecture is too complete, too strict, too dry. The crusts occupy only sanctioned ledges and seams: concave shelves where light pools without scorching, undersides that are not dark but slightly less exposed, shallow basins where the salt is not immediately lethal, rims where ancient accretion has left a usable skin.

In this way they seem less like invaders than inscriptions.

They are the chamber’s only living punctuation: dark commas in the white script of fins and ribs, green pauses among the packed mineral sentences. They give scale to the place. Without them the chamber might be purely geological, a sealed architecture of salt and silica. With them, it becomes inhabited—not animated, not softened, but inhabited in the most austere possible manner.

They have no eyes for the light that sustains them. No ears for the roar that surrounds them. The chamber’s unending tone passes through and around their crusts, perhaps even through the brittle bodies of their colonies, but they do not listen. The pressure, the brightness, the coolness, the dryness: these are not sensations to them. They are the terms of existence.

If they possess anything like behavior, it is restraint.

They persist.

They hold to the mineral faces.

They darken by imperceptible increments.

They endure conditions that would be emptiness to almost any other life.

And perhaps, at their edges, they make more of themselves: not with urgency, not with appetite, but with the slow confidence of something that has never been required to hurry. A cell divides under a glaze of salt. A pigment deepens to protect what little chemistry is still tender. A scale curls almost invisibly away from the stone, cupping the cool light. A green-black margin advances into a pore left by ancient mineral stress.

That is the local animal, the local forest, the local civilization.

A crust.

A stain.

A living mineral afterimage.

And beyond it, if “living there” may include what is not alive but nevertheless dominates the place, then the chamber itself lives more forcefully than its inhabitants. The white fins and ribs seem to possess the memory of growth. They behave like a fossilized organism whose final act was to become environment. The shelves, combs, flutes, and buttresses are not alive now, but they have the authority of something once generated rather than assembled. They are dead only if death can apply to a structure that may never have had flesh.

The green films are therefore not masters of this place. They are tenants on the surface of an older, greater body.

They live on its ribs.

They line its healed fractures.

They blacken the permitted hollows of its salt-white skeleton.

They are the faint biological remainder inside a world that has mostly chosen mineral permanence.
