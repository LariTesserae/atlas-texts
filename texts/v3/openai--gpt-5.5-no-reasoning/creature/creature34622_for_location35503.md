What lives there is not a single creature, though at a distance one might first mistake it for one.

The plateau itself has become the first resident: a many-bodied thing spread thin and wide over the buried mountain, breathing through leaves, drinking through roots, remembering in wood. It is not conscious in the way an animal is conscious, but it behaves as though it has preferences. It prefers damp hollows sealed from sun. It prefers repetition. It takes footpaths and turns them into polished root. It takes fallen trunks and makes them into bridges. It takes abandoned shelters and enters through the lashings. It takes bones, tools, dung, feathers, seed husks, and charcoal, and folds them slowly into its skin.

The place lives by layering. Nothing here simply dies and leaves. A tree becomes a tower, then a garden, then a den, then a ridge. A carcass becomes beetle heat, cat scent, bone salt, fungus bloom, root food. A human basket becomes a nest, then a trellis, then a lump in the green. Even absence has inhabitants: hollows full of vapor, tunnels full of listening, broad open fern basins watched from every edge.

The most numerous residents are the small ones, the true citizens of the plateau.

They live in cups of water held by plants, in seams of bark, in rotting root chambers, in the wet undersides of leaves, in the dark felt between living ground and stone. Mosquito larvae tremble in brown flower-pools. Beetles polish tunnels through deadwood that is not quite dead. Ants move along vines in military ropes, farming pale scale-insects beneath leaf shelters. Termites boil out of cracks after rain, briefly becoming weather, a soft-winged upward snow instantly harvested by mouths. Grubs fatten inside fallen seed pods. Mites graze on fungus films. Spiders stitch leaf to leaf and wait in the green shade with legs spread like hooked thorns.

The insects own the hours by sound. They are the plateau’s machinery: raspers, sawyers, drillers, fiddlers, knockers, whiners, pulse-makers. Their noise is not background but occupation. It announces that every stem is being eaten, cleaned, bored, pollinated, defended, courted, and dismantled at once. Without them the place would seem merely overgrown. With them it becomes inhabited down to the smallest wet chamber.

Among them live the bright amphibians, small as thumb bones, slick as fruit skin. They are creatures of borrowed water. They never need ponds in the ordinary sense. A frog may spend its entire life inside the lifted basin of a bromeliad, calling from the throat of a plant while rain beads on waxy leaves above. Some breed in hoofprints before the prints spring back. Some glue their eggs to the shaded inner walls of flowers. Some are striped like warning, blue-black and yellow, or red-limbed, or green enough to vanish until they blink.

Their voices rise after rain as if the plant mat has begun to simmer. They answer one another from root-holes and leaf-cups and hollow stems. Many are poisonous, not dramatically, not mythically, but in the practical way of this place: bitter skin, numbing water, a sickness that teaches a predator after one mistake. Birds know which ones to turn over and which ones to leave untouched. Children, if children are raised here, learn the same lesson early.

Lizards occupy the exposed bones of the mountain. They flatten themselves on warm granite domes in the morning, ribs expanding, throats pulsing. Their skins shift with sun and temper: gray on stone, green among fern, black along the flanks when another male comes too near. Some are quick, needle-bodied things that vanish into cracks. Others are heavy-headed ambush hunters that wait beside flower-cups for frogs, tongues flicking at the chemical news of the air. There are geckos in the hollow buttresses, pale and nearly translucent, clicking softly while moths gather around fungus glow or the dim light of a human ember.

Snakes live where the ground has two surfaces: the visible mat above and the warm root-dark below. Some move like water through fern shade, taking eggs and nestlings. Some coil in the woven ceilings of root tunnels, patient near the runs of rodents. Some climb, draped along vines with the looseness of dead growth until they tighten into life. The people know them less by sight than by pattern: the shed skin snagged on thorn, the sudden silence of birds, the cool muscular withdrawal underfoot.

The birds are the most public inhabitants, though many remain unseen.

Small green flocks comb the fern plains, leaf-colored until they turn together and reveal yellow throats, blue eye-rings, flashes of white underwing. They travel like thoughts through grass, rising all at once when a canid appears, then settling again a little farther off, scolding as if the land belongs to them by volume of complaint.

There are heavy-beaked fruit birds in the rare great trees, birds that swallow figs whole and drop seeds with audible thuds into the living ground. Their calls are hollow and wooden, carrying across basins at dawn. They are partly gardeners, partly thieves. Around them grow the next generation of canopy islands.

Long-legged runners pick across mossy openings, stabbing for grubs and beetles. They are wary, deliberate, beautifully ungainly, with high knees and alert crests. In the breeding season the males clear little circles in the cushion plants and decorate them with pale petals, beetle wings, chips of quartz, anything bright enough to matter under hard light.

Raptors ride the plateau rim. Their lives are made of air and patience. They hang over updrafts rising from the lower valleys, watching the open green backs of the highland for movement: a rodent in a gap, a young bird flushed from cover, a lizard on stone, a newborn browser unsteady on the springing mat. Their cries fall into the valleys and return changed by cloud.

At night the birds withdraw into hidden roosts, and other winged things take possession. Bats pour from hollows in the old buttresses, some fine-faced and insect-hunting, some long-tongued and flower-drinking, some larger and fruit-eating, carrying seeds over ridges in their bellies. They know every corridor of grafted branch and wind-pruned grove. They skim root-bridges inches above the polished surface, pass under hanging curtains, vanish into white mist without slowing.

The mammals are fewer in number, but they give the plateau its weight.

There are small ones everywhere: rodents with soft gripping feet, shrews that hunt through moss, possum-like climbers with naked tails coiled around stems, striped burrowers that live in the warm gap between plant mat and stone. Many are more often heard than seen: a scratch in a hollow trunk, a chewing under bark, a sudden patter across a shelter roof at night. They raid granaries, steal eggs, gnaw cords, vanish with seeds. They are the reason no stored food is ever merely stored; it is defended, suspended, smoked, sealed, watched.

The highland browsers are the great steady presence of the open basins. They move in files, because the ground teaches file movement: one weight testing, others following. Their feet are wide, soft, and splayed, made for living ground rather than soil. They do not strike the earth so much as press it, waiting for the spring, judging what holds. Calves learn by stepping where their mothers stepped.

Some carry horns that sweep backward along the skull, good for pushing through low branch corridors without snagging. Others have blunt, bony shields along the neck and shoulders, armor against thorn and tooth. Their coats are dark in rain, tawny or moss-gray when dry. They browse cushion plants, young shoots, fern crowns, and the bark of saplings. They visit the same water-vines at dawn, biting carefully to open them, licking the wound before insects find it.

Their herds are never wholly relaxed. Even while feeding, ears turn independently. Nostrils widen at changes in wind. The insect roar hides much, so they listen through their hooves as well as their ears: the pressure of a running animal on a root-bridge, the underground crackle of something large moving beneath the mat, the abrupt stilling of frogs in a nearby hollow.

Among them move smaller deerlike animals, quick and high-ankled, with coats broken by pale spots that mimic leaf glare. They use the fern basins dangerously, depending on speed and confusion. Fawns lie in root-shadow, scentless and rigid, while flies walk over their eyes.

The predators are not rulers, exactly. Nothing rules here for long. But they are constant editors.

The spotted cats belong to the buttresses and the half-open places. Their coats are not simple spots but moss maps: olive, gold, charcoal, lichen-white along the face. A resting cat can disappear against a vertical garden of bark and fern. They descend silently from old trunks, hunt along root corridors, and use the hollow interiors of ancient trees as nurseries. Their claws are curved for climbing, their tails long and heavy for balance. They kill by nearness. Most animals never hear them until impact.

The long-bodied canids are more visible and less silent. They work in pairs or family bands, russet-gray, narrow-faced, with black legs wet to the elbow from fern dew. They trot with heads low, reading the pressed moss and dung heat. In open basins they separate, one moving plainly, the other hidden along a wind-pruned wall, turning birds, hares, and small deer toward each other. Their calls are yelps, whistles, and coughing barks that carry strangely under cloud.

The climbing bears are slower, larger, and more destructive. They are not numerous, but everyone knows where one has passed. A bear opens the plateau. It tears apart bromeliad towers for larvae, rips honey nests from hollow trunks, peels bark from living columns, overturns root mats to expose grubs and fungi. Its claws leave pale commas in wood. Its fur holds twigs, pollen, burrs, and the sour smell of fermented fruit. It can climb with astonishing patience, hugging the old buttresses, pausing often to breathe and sniff. When angered, it does not rush at first; it considers, and then removes the thing in its way.

Beneath the surface lives the animal people speak of indirectly.

Perhaps there is more than one kind. Perhaps stories join several burrowers, predators, and old fears into one presence. But something large does move in the warm, half-rotted underworld between mountain stone and living mat. It uses old root channels, collapsed stream roofs, and the softened cavities left by dead trunks. At night it can be heard before it is seen: a distributed pressure, a muffled cracking, a shift underfoot that makes sleepers sit up without knowing why.

When it emerges, it does so from places that seemed solid. A fern hummock opens. A mat tears. A dark head, plated or furred or mud-slick, pushes through hanging roots. It feeds on grubs, roots, eggs, carrion, perhaps unwary animals bedded too warmly on the ground. People do not sleep directly on the mat because of it. They build raised platforms, use old buttresses, hang hammocks from living ribs. They teach children to feel through the soles of the feet for motion that is not wind.

Then there are the cliff-dwellers.

Monkey-like creatures, or cousins of monkeys shaped by vines and height, live along the frayed rim where the plateau begins to fall away. They have long arms, gripping tails or tail-like limbs, narrow clever faces, and voices like cracked gourds. They move vertically with no drama, crossing spaces that would empty a human stomach. Their trails are not on ground but in tension: vine to root to ledge to hanging branch. They raid bird nests, fruiting orchids, human drying racks, and one another’s infants if hunger is bad. They watch people with bright, appraising resentment, always from just beyond sling range.

Near the cliffs also nest birds that never cross far inland, white-bellied and black-winged, sleeping on ledges veiled by plants. Their guano feeds hanging gardens. Their eggs are taken only by the daring or desperate.

And human beings live there too, but lightly, as if by permission that may be withdrawn.

They are not masters of the plateau. They are a careful, listening kind of animal among louder and older arrangements. Their settlements are small and often seasonal: a cluster of bark shelters under a living wall, a repaired platform inside a hollow buttress, granaries on root stilts, a smoke-blackened stone hollow used by generations and then left to moss. A family may occupy a place through the dry weeks, then move when insects thicken, when browsers change their routes, when fruiting shifts, when a predator begins to use the same paths.

Their bodies would be shaped by balance and attention. Wide-soled feet toughened by root and wet bark. Hands scarred by fiber cord, thorn, stone edges, resin burns. Shoulders used to baskets, nets, children, bundles of fern bedding. Their movements are economical. No one crashes through vegetation unless fleeing or driving game. Branches are tied aside rather than hacked away. Vines are cut at an angle, sealed with clay, resin, or chewed leaf paste. A careless cut becomes a smell, a smell becomes insects, insects become birds, birds become commotion, commotion becomes teeth.

They know water as a dispersed thing. They drink dawn from leaves, cut swollen stems, collect rain in cup-marked stones, set bark troughs below dripping roots. They know which plant-cups are safe, which belong to frogs, which will sour the stomach, which hold mosquito larvae fit for bait or food. Children learn to read red rims on flowers before they learn stories.

Their tools are suited to a world that grows over everything. Stone blades are kept sharp because dullness wastes effort and makes wounds messy. Bone awls pierce bark, hide, and basket reed. Digging sticks open tuber mats and pry grubs from rotted wood. Nets catch birds at corridor mouths. Slings keep monkeys from granaries. Thornwood hooks take fish from the rare clear runnels and pull down fruiting branches. A metal knife, if one exists, is inherited like a name. It is not left lying on the ground, because the ground here accepts gifts too eagerly.

They probably do not build monuments unless one counts maintenance as monument. Their standing stones are modest because modesty lasts longer here. A stone half-swallowed by moss, with a cup carved on top to catch rain, may matter more than a tower. It says: this ridge is remembered. This crossing has a rule. Someone died here. Someone waited here for weather. Do not pass after dark. Leave water. Turn back if the birds are quiet.

Their speech would contain many words for conditions of ground: mat that holds, mat that floats, mat that hides water, mat that has been opened by bear, mat that sounds hollow, mat that is safe after hooves have crossed. They would name winds by smell. They would know clouds from below and clouds from above. They would distrust sudden ease.

They likely tell stories in which the old mountain is not dead but covered, sleeping under the green weight. The great trees may be elders, survivors, or former people who rooted themselves to hold water for their descendants. The under-mat animal may be hunger without eyes, or the mountain’s tongue, or a warning against sleeping carelessly. Fire may be treated almost as a captive predator: useful, fed sparingly, never allowed to wander.

Their dead might not be buried deeply; deep burial is difficult where ground is alive and stone waits underneath. Some may be placed in hollow trunks, wrapped in bark and cord, given to insects, roots, and time. Some bones may be cleaned and stored in high dry chambers, then later set beneath standing stones. Some may simply disappear into the plateau’s ordinary appetite, becoming another mineral trace in the root-dark.

But they are not the only thinking things, if thinking is allowed to mean memory, planning, manipulation, and social cunning. The monkeys at the rim know who carries slings. The canids remember traps. The bears return to sealed granaries after tasting them once. The birds learn the timing of human meals and rains. The plateau itself, by taking every path and repair into its growth, becomes a kind of distributed record of use. To live there is to enter a conversation already underway among roots, hooves, claws, and weather.

No inhabitant is separate from the architecture.

The browsers make the root-bridges trustworthy by crossing them. Their dung feeds beetles, beetles feed birds, birds scatter seeds into the dung. The cats use the old living buttresses, and their kills nourish the same roots that hide them. Frogs poison water-cups, teaching mouths and hands to discriminate. Bears wound the plant towers, opening light for thorny growth. Humans clip corridors and tie branches; animals use the cleared ways; plants graft around the ties. Every act becomes habitat for something else.

So what lives there?

A high, warm, roaring society of interdependence and suspicion.

A skin of plants pretending to be ground.

A buried mountain carrying forests that have forgotten they are forests.

Insects in such numbers they become an atmosphere.

Birds that speak for every hour of the day.

Frogs in flowers, snakes in the seams, lizards on the stone bones.

Soft-footed herds moving carefully over a world that may give way.

Cats like patches of moss with eyes.

Canids like lean weather crossing the fern.

Bears that dismantle gardens.

Something large below, felt before named.

Cliff-climbers with clever hands and bad tempers.

And people: small, skillful, temporary, returning; not owners, not intruders exactly, but negotiators. They live by knowing that the plateau is never empty, even when it looks vast and open under ordinary blue light. They live by listening through the din for the one sound that does not belong, or the sudden absence of sound that belongs too much to fear.

Everything there lives on something else, inside something else, above something older.

And under all of them, patient and almost forgotten, the mountain lives as foundation: flattened, warm, root-pierced, holding up its impossible garden while the garden eats the sky.
