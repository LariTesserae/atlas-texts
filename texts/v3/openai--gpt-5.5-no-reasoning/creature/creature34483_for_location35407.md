What lives there is not a creature, not in the way a creature is usually meant.

It is a vegetation, or something that has accepted the role of vegetation because there is no better local category for it: a standing, rooted, light-drinking population of mineral bodies that grow from stone and behave less like plants than like slow instruments of separation, refinement, and charge.

They are the plateau’s inhabitants: rigid groves of dry life.

They live without water. Not by conserving it, not by hiding it in tubers or waxy skins, but by having never entered into the bargain of water at all. Their chemistry is founded elsewhere. Their tissues are not wet tissues. They are sealed composites: translucent ceramics, conductive glass, flexible mineral films, enamel-like skins, carbon lattices, metallic veins, resinous plates that do not soften. If broken, they do not bleed. They chip, delaminate, crack, flash faintly with released charge, or shed powder from the wound. A damaged frond may slowly seal over with a glossy scab of silica and metal oxides, taking decades to complete what an animal body would attempt in minutes.

They are rooted in ore seams rather than soil. Their roots are not seeking moisture but difference: electrical potential, reactive surfaces, pressure, heat, exposed compounds. Beneath each colony, a buried architecture spreads through cracks and conductive beds — branching filaments, plates, sockets, nodal mats, and mineral membranes pressed tightly against the stone. These structures taste the plateau in the only way they can: by contact, charge, chemical tension, microscopic fracture. They insinuate themselves into unfinished geology and persuade it to yield.

The visible parts are only the outward appliances of that buried life.

Some rise as fan-colonies: tall, open curtains of green-gold translucent blades, each blade striated with copper-dark veins. They stand in nested ranks, all angled slightly differently, not to move with the sun but to divide it, filter it, trap particular bands of light in stacked internal layers. From far away they look like frozen flames or immense mineral ferns. Up close they are intricate and hard, each edge keen as ceramic, each surface faintly waxed, not to prevent evaporation but to maintain separation between inner chemistry and the dead, dry air.

Others grow as black columns. These are older, lonelier organisms, ribbed and hollow, thirty meters high in places, their fluted bodies drilled by vertical slits. They are not trunks in the familiar sense. They are resonant chimneys, heat exchangers, charge towers, and reproductive organs combined. In the day they drink heat through their dark skins and conduct it downward into buried strata. At night, when the air cools, they release stored gradients slowly, producing faint internal ticks and a pressure-like murmur. When rare wind passes over their slits, they sound a single tone, deep and dry, less a call than an accidental consequence of their design.

There are low radial things too, spread close to the ground in mats of overlapping scales. These are the most numerous where the crust is thin and electrically active. Their scales are dull green, graphite, or blue-black, edged in pale mineral growth. They lie arranged with unnerving regularity, each mat separated from the next by exact distances determined by repulsion fields and root competition. They gather sunlight on their upper faces and ground-current on their lower ones. They resemble manufactured arrays abandoned on the plain, but they are alive: slowly thickening, slowly sorting metals, slowly laying down halos of waste.

Some species are glassy and pale, nearly invisible until the angle of light catches them. These grow as combs in the shadows of black ridges, where lightning scars or tectonic stress have activated the stone. They consist of filaments finer than hair, bearing little reflective plates like cooling fins. Their function is delicate and strange. They harvest charge from pressure in the rock. They are nourished by strain. A long still century of compressed geology may feed them better than any rainstorm could feed a forest.

Their reproduction is local, patient, and almost motionless.

They do not flower for insects, because no insect has ever learned them. They do not make fruit, because there is no mouth to tempt. They do not cast seeds to the wind, because the air is too uninterested and the world too selective. Instead they accumulate readiness.

At certain joints or along the undersides of blades, mica-like flakes develop over years or centuries. These are spores only by analogy: thin packets of dormant architecture, mineral memory, catalytic surfaces, and encoded growth tendencies. They loosen after enough charge has passed through the parent body. A faint click, a slight relaxation of a seam, and a few fall. Most land in the colored residue beneath the parent and remain inert indefinitely, glittering among old dusts. A few slide into cracks. Fewer still touch a seam of the right composition. Then, if the temperature gradient is favorable and the stone can be persuaded open, they begin.

Beginning is not germination as much as installation.

The spore fuses to rock. It etches a pit beneath itself. It grows a film. The film thickens into a contact organ. The first aboveground sign may be no more than a dark varnish spreading along a crack, then a bead of green glass, then a rigid blade the size of a fingernail after many seasons. The young do not compete by speed. There is no speed here worth having. They compete by suitability: by being the right chemistry in the right seam under the right light.

They do not fear being eaten. Nothing eats them. Nothing browses their edges, bores their trunks, lays eggs in their hollows, nests between their blades, or carries their fragments away. Because of this, their bodies lack the anxious vocabulary common to most living things. They have no thorns. No bitter warning colors. No toxins meant for mouths. No sudden closures. No camouflage. No lures. No tenderness hidden behind armor. They are not defenseless exactly; they are simply unaddressed by predation. Their hardness is not protection from animals but a requirement of their own chemistry.

They live in isolation from one another, but not entirely without relation.

A grove may occupy an acre and be internally dense: hundreds of blades, coils, tubes, mats, fans, nodules, hollow stems, and buried filaments all drawing from a single reactive node. Beyond it may lie miles of lifeless crust. Another grove on a ridge may be connected not by roots in the simple sense, but by the conductive habits of the plateau itself. Charge moves through beds below. Heat moves through stone. Stress travels along plates. Atmospheric gases pass uniformly over all. When evening comes and stored energy releases in slow pulses, distant colonies may flicker in patterns that look like response.

It may not be communication. It may be regulation. It may not say anything. It may merely be the same vast circuit finding balance through widely spaced organs.

If the plateau is one organism, then these groves are its exposed glands, leaves, radiators, and manufacturing stacks. The barren intervals are not empty but functional: insulation, spacing, reservoirs, silent compartments where reactions too slow to observe proceed beneath sealed crust. The apparent loneliness of each colony would then be misleading. They would be features of a single buried being whose body is measured in ridgelines and basins, whose metabolism is tectonic, whose thoughts, if any, would be indistinguishable from chemical equilibrium.

If they are separate organisms, then they are still bound by a common law: nothing circulates freely. Everything must be held, touched, conducted, plated, vitrified. Their world has no streams, so they cannot afford extravagance. Their wastes fall directly beneath them and harden in rings. Their dead remain standing or lying for millennia, slowly bleaching, sometimes incorporated by younger growth if contact is useful. Death does not enrich the ground in a soft way. It leaves architecture, shadow, surface area, trace metals, and brittle inheritance.

A dead grove is not a ruin so much as a deactivated machine.

Its fans go colorless. Its copper veins tarnish to black-green lines. Its hollow towers split from crown to base as thermal stress works along old seams. Its resin sheets peel and stack like discarded transparent skins. Its metal nodules remain polished in the dust. Around it, the residue rings persist: violet, sulfur-yellow, rust, blue-black. New growth may use them, but slowly, with discrimination. There is no rot to hurry the return.

Their senses, if the word applies, are distributed and impersonal.

They know light as intensity and angle, not as sight. They know pressure in the ground. They know charge accumulation, gas composition, heat, mineral exposure, the presence of neighboring bodies through field interference. A nearby organism is not perceived as an object but as a distortion in available current, a shadow in useful wavelengths, a chemical signature settling in dust. They do not turn toward one another. They do not reach in gestures. They adjust growth over decades. They space themselves. They thicken on one side. They close a slit or open a fin. Their behavior is morphology.

Their time is almost geological.

A human could sit beside a blade-forest for days and see nothing happen except the sun moving through it. The inhabitants would appear completed, fixed, sculptural. But across centuries they would be busy. A tube would extend one more rib. A mat would add another ring of scales. A fan would alter its translucency as inclusions migrated through it. A buried filament would split a silicate grain and draw out what it needed. A colony would exhaust one seam and lean, almost imperceptibly, toward another.

Their lushness is real, but it is not generous.

They make no shade that comforts for long. They give off no edible fruit, no fragrance meant to travel, no coolness, no softness. Their abundance is internal: chambers, veins, lattices, catalytic skins, layered panes, charged cavities, cooling fins, powder vents, silent ducts. They are elaborate because their metabolism is elaborate, not because they invite a surrounding community. They are ecosystems with almost no consumers. Gardens without insects. Forests without birds. Factories without workers. Bodies without hunger in the animal sense.

They do hunger, though.

They hunger for exposed mineral, for voltage, for sunlight, for carbon in the warm air, for pressure differentials, for rare lightning, for the exact contact between root and seam. Their hunger has no urgency. It does not chase. It waits in contact with the world until the world yields molecule by molecule. It is a patient appetite, dry and exact.

What lives there, then, are the dry engines of the plateau.

Not machines, because they grow, reproduce, die, inherit, and alter their surroundings.

Not plants, because they have no sap, no thirst, no soil, no flowers, no soft green commerce with animals and rain.

Not stones, because they choose, in their slow way, among possibilities; they build themselves against entropy; they maintain boundaries; they store light and release charge; they make more of their kind.

They are mineral flora: glass-leaved, ore-rooted, sealed against the air, luxuriant in structure and barren in companionship. They stand in widely separated colonies across a warm high world that has never learned rivers. They make residue instead of loam, pulses instead of song, flakes instead of seeds, husks instead of compost. They are alive at the speed of crust.

And beneath them, perhaps, something larger also lives: a buried plateau-mind without nerves, a factory-organism spread through conductive strata, expressing itself as groves wherever the stone permits. Or perhaps there is no mind at all, only many separate lineages of austere life, each one solving the same impossible problem in parallel.

Either way, the inhabitants do not notice absence as absence. They do not miss animals, water, weather, rot, or sound. They have no concept of barrenness. To them, the world is full: full of light, charge, hard surfaces, locked compounds, stress, warmth, and time.

They live there by being almost still.
