What lives there is not one kind of being, but a whole way of being alive.

The city is inhabited by things that have learned not to separate shelter from food, street from current, house from garden, body from water. Everything that lives there is, in some sense, a resident of a shared lungless architecture: fish in windows, roots in masonry, pale crustaceans under steps, sleeping rays in courts, turtles among roofs, people perhaps in dark chambers behind breathing curtains. The place has made citizens of plants and animals alike.

At the smallest scale live the quiet maintainers.

Fine algae fur every surface, not merely as growth but as skin. It covers lintels, tiles, carved faces, stair edges, old doorposts, the inside of abandoned bowls. It softens the stone and feeds armies of grazers. Snails with heavy fist-sized shells move over inscriptions and thresholds, polishing the past away one thin meal at a time. Pale shrimp and crustaceans work beneath steps and in wall cracks, picking at decay, cleaning fronds, stirring silt. Transparent worms extend crowns into the passing water, withdrawing instantly when a shadow thickens. Tiny armored door-creatures live in hinges and sockets, where old joinery has become reef, and burst into bitter ink when pried loose.

These are the custodians no one appointed. They rasp, filter, prune, digest, seal, and open. Without them the city would clog, rot, or vanish beneath its own abundance.

Then there are the fish that use the city as birds once used trees.

Small bright fish occupy windowsills and beam-holes. They hover in the blue squares of former rooms, slipping in and out through curtains of weed. Schools gather in alleys, then turn all at once through openings too narrow for anything larger. Some are nearly transparent except for a thread of silver gut and a dark bead of an eye; others flash yellow, blue, or red when shafts of light reach them. Fry shelter in mossy paving, rising like shaken dust when disturbed.

There are climbing fish with sucker fins and blunt rasping mouths. They cling to vertical walls, old civic carvings, shrine mouths, and the underside of bridges, patiently shaving algae from stone. In another place they would seem peculiar; here they are as ordinary as sparrows. They grip, scrape, release, drift a handspan, grip again. Their young are born in the cold spring corridors and grow up learning the taste of different neighborhoods: mineral, moss, shrine-stone, leaf-rot, silt.

Long transparent eels live deeper in the rootwork. They thread through vine walls like thoughts passing through sleep. Most of the time they are glimpsed only as a wavering line, a mouth, a ripple in a bladder-plant’s glow. They know every hollow inside the woven architecture. They pass through rooms no human hand can now enter, following routes made by roots, collapsed beams, and the old intentions of doorways.

Blind freshwater rays sleep in courtyards. They settle beneath a dusting of silt and become part of the floor: a gray cloth, a fallen mat, a soft patch of shadow. When they rise, the whole plaza seems to breathe. They lift from stillness, wing themselves slowly over the slabs, and leave behind a clean oval where their bodies were.

Larger fish come and go like weather. Silver pike enter from open water, all blade and patience. Heavy catfish rise from the drowned river below, nosing along the lower road, their whiskers reading the city’s edges. At dusk a ribbon-bodied hunter coils around posts and bridge supports, becoming a dark spiral among the vines until some careless school passes too close. These predators are not truly citizens. They are visitors to the gates, outer terraces, and dim lanes. The smaller life knows their signatures in the water and folds itself away before sight confirms danger.

The plants are perhaps the city’s truest inhabitants.

They do not merely grow in the place; they conduct it. Long ribbon-leaves pour from parapets and balconies, indicating the deep current better than any flag. Brown-green vines descend from upper rooms to lower streets and root there, making pillars of themselves. Pale bladder-plants cling to lintels and gateposts, glowing faintly when brushed, so that a passing turtle or child might leave a brief wake of greenish lamps. Hair-algae makes velvet of stone. White fern-mats grow in cold spring-water, blue-veined leaves hold stiffly in the current, and black-green mosses thicken in sheltered hollows.

Some plants have the bearing of domestic things. They grow in rows too even to be wholly wild. They form arches where a frame once guided them. They make square mats in market stalls, hollow fruits at hand height, corridors whose walls flex but do not close. Others are older, wilder, less obedient. Past the outer wall they stream downslope in long unpruned masses, giving shelter to larger animals and obscuring the drowned road.

Many of the plants seem almost architectural in temperament. They wedge cracked stone. They hold roofs together. They bind terraces to the mountain. A bridge may stand because tubeworm colonies stiffen its base and roots lace its span. A doorway may remain open because crabs prune it. A wall may still be a wall because generations of stems have thickened where hands once tied them.

The animals and plants do not live beside each other so much as through each other. Fish carry pollen in mucus on their flanks. Seedpods are shaped to catch briefly in gills, hitching rides without killing their carriers. Crabs maintain root passages by eating just enough. Grazers keep household gardens from closing. Turtles browse the upper kelp like goats in an orchard, and in doing so they shape the roofs.

The turtles are among the most visible large residents.

Long-necked and deliberate, they move through roof gardens, high ledges, and planted balconies, biting leaves with the calm entitlement of livestock. Some may be wild; some may descend from herded animals. They know the routes between feeding grounds, the warm courts where they rest, the ringing stones whose tones mean food or danger if anyone remains to sound them. Their shells are often gardens themselves: moss, thread algae, small limpets, pale barnacle-like freshwater growths. Younger fish follow them for dislodged scraps. Children, if there are children, probably learn to swim by holding their shell rims and feeling the slow power of their strokes.

There are also shelled grazers that climb the drowned road from below. They take weeks to reach the plaza, scraping and browsing as they go, their heavy bodies making processions out of appetite. They are not hurried by anything except perhaps seasonal hunger or breeding memory. Behind them gather fish, seed, silt, and small scavengers. By the time they pass through the lower gate, the city has already felt them coming as a tremor and a change in the feeding schools.

If people still live there, they are a quiet, weighted people.

They would not be numerous. The city is too small, too balanced, too full of other claims. They would inhabit pockets of air trapped in rock chambers, or rooms lined with plants that release breath, or sealed bells maintained with great care. They would be lean from swimming, broad-handed from gripping, slow in motion because speed wastes warmth and strength. Their hair would be bound close or allowed to drift in disciplined cords. Their clothing would be woven from leaf-fiber, skin, and netting, practical rather than decorative, hung with stone or shell weights at the waist, ankles, shoulders, and wrists.

They would move as if listening with their entire bodies. A hand on a wall would tell them who struck a signal stone three lanes away. A cheek turned toward a current would tell them whether a door-passage was safe. Children would be taught not “left” and “right” first, but updraft, cold pull, gate-drift, shrine-stillness, turtle-road, spring-thread. They would know which vines can be grasped, which sting, which tear free, which belong to a household. They would read silt the way dryland people read footprints.

Their tools would be simple and treasured: shell knives, coral rasps, bone needles, hooked poles, stone weights, thorn hooks, dull bronze blades passed down like heirlooms. They would make little that depends on fire. Instead they would transform things by pressure, fermentation, scraping, soaking, resin, mineral water, sunlight, and time. Food would be cultivated as much as gathered: edible leaves in old stalls, larvae in baskets, fish in net courts, pale stems from spring gardens, turtle-grazed orchards, cured pods, mineral-sealed pottery filled with preserved softness.

Their craft would be gardening, but gardening enlarged into civilization.

They would feed walls. Comb bridges. Trim doorways before they flower shut. Train kelp ribs over frames. Tie living beams to stone pins. Maintain boundary nets not as fences against enemies only, but as agreements with current, seed, fish, and neighbor. A neglected house would not simply fall apart; it would become overgrown, overclosed, too alive in the wrong ways. Maintenance would be a form of conversation with growth.

Their ceremonies would be tactile and resonant. Bells would be struck not to ring through air but to move through bone. News would arrive in the teeth and sternum: danger at the western gate, a birth in the spring chambers, the arrival of grazers on the lower road, a death, a migration, a summons to prune the central plaza. Shrines would have open mouths not for flame-smoke, but for cold spring exhalations, drifting offerings, strings of shell, seed bladders, and the touch of hands on vibrating stone.

Their dead might be placed in cliff chambers where mineral water issues clear and cold, or given to root gardens, or weighted beneath living arches until plant and bone become scaffold together. In such a city, burial and construction would not be entirely separate arts.

If no people remain, then what lives there is memory made biological.

The domestic plants continue their trained gestures. Market gardens still produce rows. Door-curtains still slow passage. Boundary nets still divide old plots because roots have learned the pattern. Signal stones still hang, and turtles or currents strike them by accident, sending meaningless but solemn tones through the water. The city would still appear inhabited because cultivation has outlived the cultivators. Its habits would have become hereditary in stems, routes, grazer behavior, and fish migrations.

In that case, the residents are descendants of use: feral orchards, trained root-walls, fish that spawn in sleeping cells, crabs that prune ancestral doorways, turtles that answer vibrations no one intends anymore. The city’s culture would persist as ecology.

What lives there, most deeply, is an agreement.

Stone accepts root. Root accepts stone. Animals accept corridors shaped by vanished hands. Currents accept streets. Gardens accept being walls. Houses accept being reefs. Predators accept the outer edge. Small life accepts the inner chambers. People, if they remain, accept that they are not masters of the city but one maintenance species among many.

The inhabitants are therefore slow, sensitive, interdependent beings: grazers and pruners, clingers and drifters, bell-feelers, current-readers, wall-feeders, algae-raspers, seed-carriers, roof-browsers. They live by touch more than sight, by patience more than force. None of them owns the city completely. Each holds open some part of it.

And together they make the drowned hamlet seem not abandoned, but occupied by a civilization wider than people: a cool, green, murmuring society of plants, fish, shells, turtles, roots, silt, signal stones, and perhaps human hands moving carefully among them.
