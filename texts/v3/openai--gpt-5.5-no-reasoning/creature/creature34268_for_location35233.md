They are not a people of houses.

They live in pauses.

In the lee of stone shelves, under scalloped overhangs, in fern-choked clefts where the wind breaks its teeth before reaching the ground, they keep their small belongings wrapped in oiled leaves and woven fiber. They do not make settlements large enough to be seen from a distance. A stranger crossing the open plateau might pass within thirty paces of one of their shelters and notice only a dark line beneath a rock lip, a faint ash-smell, a clay bowl catching slow water from a sweating wall.

They are human, or near enough that the word still holds: upright walkers, tool-makers, fire-keepers, children carried against the chest, old ones consulted in storms. But the plateau has altered them in habit, body, and attention until they seem less like inhabitants than a manner the place has learned to take.

They are lean and compact, with strong hands and feet hardened by stone. Their skin is weathered into deep browns, umbers, olive-black, reddish tan—colors that match wet basalt, sunwarmed sandstone, bark after rain. Their hair is usually bound tight or cut in wind-safe ways, braided close to the skull, wrapped in cord, tucked beneath caps of felted plant fiber. Loose hair is a nuisance here; the wind makes ropes of it, fills it with grit, lashes it into the eyes at the wrong moment. Their clothes are similarly close and practical: layered wraps of bark cloth, hide, woven sedge, and tough leaves treated with waxes and oils from highland plants. They tie everything down. Sleeves bind at the wrist. Cloaks knot under the arm. Tool rolls are lashed twice. Even children learn that what is not fastened belongs to the wind.

Their faces often have a narrowed, listening quality. Not suspicious exactly, but tuned. Their eyes flick to mist, bird-flight, the darkening of moss, the motion of seeds in the air. They read the plateau less by sight alone than by change: pressure in the ears, tremor under the heel, the smell of lichen before rain, a sudden low note from a hidden tube in the rock. They speak softly when close together, because shouting is usually useless. Across distance they use signs, reed whistles, stone taps, mirror-flashes when the sun allows, and sometimes long cords with knots and shells that rattle in distinct patterns when shaken.

Their language has many words for wind.

Not poetic excess, but necessity. Wind that dries. Wind that pushes rain upward. Wind that comes before falling stone. Wind that smells of far pools. Wind that lies low and invisible in slots, waiting to knock a person from a ledge. Wind that carries pollen, wind that carries rot, wind that means birds will not fly, wind that means the frogs will breed tonight. They name storms by behavior rather than size. A nine-minute white rain is not the same thing as a crawling mist-storm, nor a black-rim squall, nor a thunder that lives mostly inside the stone.

They are sometimes called the Hollow People by lowlanders, when lowlanders know of them at all. That name is only partly wrong. They do not live in the open backs of the plateau, though they cross them. Their real lives thicken in cracks: cliff-mouth shelters, root-hung basins, wet amphitheaters, hidden ledges behind temporary falls. Each family or band keeps a circuit of such places, returning with the seasons, the storms, the flowering of certain plants, the movement of night animals between pools.

Their homes are not owned in the lowland sense. A shelter belongs first to the stone, then to the water that visits it, then to those who maintain it. A family may have used the same overhang for generations, but if another group arrives half-frozen from a night storm, the fire-space is shared. Refusing refuge is one of the few acts they consider truly monstrous. The plateau itself refuses enough.

Their architecture is slight because it must be. They build low crescent walls to turn gusts aside. They roof with reed mats weighted by flat stones. They weave root screens across cave mouths to break rain spray. They carve drainage grooves in shelter floors so sudden runoff does not drown the fire. They hang sleeping platforms from pegs driven into cracks, because the ground may become a stream while one sleeps. Their best craft is not grandeur but adjustment: a wall reset after every wet season, a ladder that can be pulled up before wind takes it, a bridge light enough to replace, a water basin placed precisely where dawn condensation gathers.

They travel in small numbers. Three is good. Five is safer. More than eight becomes difficult unless the weather is known to be calm, and calm weather is never trusted for long. They move from hollow to hollow with a patient, angular gait, crouching low when crossing exposed slabs, spreading arms when gusts strike, placing their feet not where the rock is smoothest but where old lichen gives grip. They carry hooked staffs of hard wood or bone-tipped cane, useful for testing pools, catching roots, hooking pack-straps, and arresting a slide. Many wear cord belts from which hang wedges, scrapers, whistles, gourds, dried tubers, coils of vine-fiber, fire-stones, little packets of salt or bitter lichen.

Children are taught early not to follow sound.

A waterfall may lie. A voice may rebound from three cliffs and arrive from beneath the feet. Thunder may seem to call from a safe hollow when in fact the path between is slick with new algae and broken by runoff. Instead, children learn cairns, scent, slope, the lean of wind-bent shrubs, the feel of familiar stone under bare soles. Games are lessons disguised: finding hidden clay cups by condensation trails; identifying pools by taste; lying flat to feel whether a distant fall is strengthening; listening to two echoes and naming which one is false.

Their dead are not buried deeply, because the plateau does not offer much soil. Nor are they burned whenever wood is scarce or rain relentless. Instead, they are given to cracks, caves, and root chambers. Bodies are wrapped in woven fern rib and bark cloth, painted with mineral ochres, and placed in narrow stone hollows sealed with slabs. In wet places, moss grows over the seams until the grave vanishes. In dry amphitheaters, cairns are built low and broad so the wind passes over without toppling them. The oldest dead are not remembered by name but by shelter: Grandmother of the Three Drips, the Child Under the Split Tree, the Singer in the Blue Gorge.

They believe sound is not merely sound.

To them, the plateau is full of old breath. Not a single god speaking with intent, but many throats left in the stone by vanished ages. Wind through tubes, rain in cracks, thunder under shelves, frogs in black pools, reeds clattering in ravines—these are not messages exactly, yet they are not meaningless. Their ritual specialists are listeners: men, women, and others who have trained themselves to distinguish weather from warning, ordinary echo from the rare wrongness that precedes collapse or flood. They sit with teeth touching a stone staff, feeling tones through bone. They place palms on slabs during thunder. They sing into cave mouths and wait for the land to answer in its own shape.

Their songs are often low, pulsed, repetitive, meant to fit beneath the wind rather than rise above it. A shelter-song may last an hour and contain only a few tones. A crossing-song keeps walkers breathing together while moving through fog. A grief-song is sung facing stone, not sky, so the dead can enter the plateau through vibration. They have flutes made from hollow reeds, drums stretched with thin hide, rattles of seed pods and beetle shells, but the greatest instrument is always the place itself. Certain hollows are visited because they return a voice changed and enlarged. Certain caves are never sung into after dusk.

Their diet is scattered like the land. They dig water-holding roots from cracks, roast tubers in ash, harvest fern fiddleheads, sedge seeds, bitter greens, mushrooms, waxy flowers, cliff honey when they can find it. They trap lizards with looped fiber and catch translucent frogs in wet months, though some frog pools are taboo because those frogs are thought to call rain into being. They gather beetle grubs from rotten wood and smoke them for travel. They follow the night-browsers’ tracks to fruiting shrubs and seed-heavy grasses. When the three-toed animals come to the basins, the people may hunt one with pit traps and stone-tipped darts, but such kills are rare and heavily shared.

They waste almost nothing. Bone becomes needle, hook, bead, awl. Hide becomes water bag, sling, drumhead. Sinew becomes binding. Horn or hoof becomes scraper. Feathers become markers tied to cairns, not for ornament only but to show wind direction at a glance. Even ash is saved, mixed with plant gum for sealing, rubbed into cords to resist rot, scattered at certain thresholds to see whether hidden drafts move through them.

Metal is mythic and practical at once. A bronze pin may pass through six generations, not as treasure kept idle but as a thing used carefully and spoken to before use. Copper hooks are wrapped when not needed. A sliver of iron, if one exists, may belong to an entire band. Lowland traders sometimes climb partway up with salt, beads, pots, bright cloth, and metal scraps, but few remain long. They find the wind maddening, the distances empty, the paths unreliable. The plateau people trade dried medicinals, resins, rare lichens, feathers, echo-stones, and knowledge of storm seasons. They do not sell maps. A drawn path is a dangerous lie in a place where water invents and erases roads.

There are others here besides the human bands.

In the wet clefts live small, pale amphibians with nearly transparent skin, their organs faintly visible like shadows behind milk-glass. They cling to stone beside seep-lines, silent most of the day, then pulse with tiny calls when pressure drops. The people watch them closely. If the glass-frogs climb above the moss line, a flood may come through the cleft. If they vanish entirely, something has poisoned the water upstream.

On warm slabs bask lizards the color of old bronze, flat-bodied and quick, with throat fans that flash blue-green before storms. Some are no longer than a finger. Others, older and scarred, grow as long as a human forearm and carry ticks beneath their jaw folds. Children hunt the small ones; the large ones are respected as guardians of dry crossings, partly because they often flee minutes before rockfall.

High above ride the crescent birds. They are dark, narrow-winged, almost never landing where they can be seen. They nest in undercut cliff pockets and feed on airborne insects, wind-stunned seeds, perhaps small creatures caught in updrafts. Their cries are thin and cutting, though often snatched away by gusts. A feather from one is valuable, not because it grants luck but because it proves a person climbed somewhere few survive.

The three-toed browsers are real, though rarely observed. They move at night between green basins, long-legged, narrow-faced, with pale coats that catch moonlight and vanish among mist. Their feet are soft and split into three delicate toes, good for slick stone and moss. They browse on sedges, low leaves, and mineral crusts. They are shy, almost soundless, but in mating season they drum their forefeet on hollow roots, making a distant, wooden knocking that can be mistaken for human signals. The plateau people hunt them only in need. To overhunt such an animal would be foolishness bordering on sacrilege; the basins would lose a carrier of seeds, and the hidden agreements would fray.

There are also things less easily named.

In some mist basins live colonies of threadlike organisms—not plant, not fungus in any simple sense—that hang from roots and rock lips in pale veils. When the air is wet they swell and glisten, catching pollen, spores, insects, even mineral dust. When dry they shrink into brittle gray cords. The people harvest them sparingly to make wound dressings and water filters. Too much cutting makes the basin “angry,” which in practice means the fog no longer pools correctly there, the seep changes course, or insects bloom in biting swarms.

Certain mosses react to breath. A traveler bending too close will see a dark handprint spread across them before slowly fading. Children dare one another to whisper secrets into such moss, then run before elders notice. The listeners use it more seriously. In caves, breath-darkening moss reveals hidden drafts, cracks, and sometimes the presence of an animal sleeping in darkness.

The low, sideways trees are communities unto themselves: orchids, ants, beetles, fungi, mites, small root-frogs, lichen crusts, moisture-catching vines. Their bark peels in storms, translucent strips rattling wildly. In heavy wind, whole groves sound like applause or dry rain. The people collect fallen bark sheets for window screens and signal rattles but avoid stripping living trees. A tree rooted in cliff stone may be older than any lineage. Its roots hold the path together.

If there is a dominant creature here, it is not large. It may be the wind-borne seed.

The plateau breeds plants that gamble violently. Seeds with hooks, wings, hairs, bladders, mineral skins. Seeds that wait decades in cracks. Seeds that germinate after nine minutes of rain and complete half a life before noon. Seeds that require vibration, thunder, frog-call, the grinding of stone, the passage through a browser’s gut. The people know dozens by touch. Some are food. Some are poison. Some are medicine only during the hour after sprouting. Some must be burned to wake. Some must be sung over—not from superstition alone, but because the low vibration shakes them loose from their husks.

The plateau people’s calendar is not a circle of months but a sequence of events:

When the black lichens sharpen their smell.

When the fog lies heavy enough to pour.

When the glass-frogs climb.

When the reed ravines clatter all night.

When the pale browsers knock.

When the old south tubes begin to hum.

When the copper flowers open after thunder.

When the dry flats grow seed-dust.

When the shelters must be retied.

When the children are first allowed to cross the wide stone.

Their society is loose, because anything too centralized would be absurd here. Bands meet at certain hollows during reliable seasons to exchange mates, stories, tools, warnings, and names for newborns. These gatherings are brief and crowded by plateau standards: thirty, fifty, perhaps eighty people in a large basin loud with talk and cooking and reed music. For a few days the place seems almost festive. Fires burn beneath rock lips. Meat smokes. Children from different circuits teach one another games. Elders compare storm memories. Young adults vanish into fern paths and return with wet hair and secretive faces.

Then clouds gather wrongly, or water stores run low, or the agreed time simply ends. The gathering scatters. Within a morning the basin is nearly empty again, except for ash, trampled sedge, repaired cairns, and a few offerings tucked into root hollows.

They do not worship permanence. They honor return.

A thing need not remain to be faithful. Rain returns. Mist returns. Birds return to the same invisible nests. Families return to shelters repaired by grandparents. Songs return to the same cave mouths. Even the dead return as names given to places, as warnings attached to paths, as habits of hand and ear. The plateau teaches them that gathering is brief, but patterns endure.

Their myths say that once the world was lower and louder in a different way. Some tell of an inland sea whose waves struck the ancestors’ cliffs, leaving shells in stone. Some tell of ice that lay over the high backs like a sleeping animal. Some tell that the plateau was once a mountain with a head so tall it tore the sky, until the wind sang it down grain by grain. No one argues over which story is true. Truth here is not singular. A stone can have been seabed, mountain, shelter, grave, and drum. A person can be child, hunter, listener, ancestor, and path.

The rare outsiders who encounter them often mistake their caution for fear and their light-built camps for poverty. Both judgments fail. These people possess little because little can be held. They build small because small things survive. They speak softly because the world is already speaking. They move often because stillness in the wrong place kills.

They are not masters of the plateau. Nothing is.

But they are among its most careful students.

Their bodies know where to crouch before the gust arrives. Their hands know which moss can be wrung for water and which burns the skin. Their ears know when a cave note has deepened because rain is filling a chamber below. Their children can sleep through thunder inside stone but wake instantly at the small, wet crack of a loosening roof slab. Their elders can smell whether a pool has held too long and gone bad. Their singers can call a band through fog by striking two stones in a rhythm that imitates falling water but is not mistaken for it.

They live in the high green emptiness as scattered warmth: a coal under ash, a footprint beside a pool, a tied roof surviving one more night of wind. They are brief compared with the stone and fragile compared with the weather, yet they belong because they have accepted the rule of the place.

Nothing gathers in the open for long.

So they gather in hollows, in memory, in sound.
