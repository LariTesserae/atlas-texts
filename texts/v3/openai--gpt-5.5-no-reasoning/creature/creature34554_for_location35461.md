What lives there is not one species, and not quite an ecosystem in the familiar sense. It is a whole assembly of mineral patience: dry forests, listening crusts, stone-born membranes, human remnants, and perhaps a few surviving people who have learned to make themselves quiet enough for the plateau to tolerate them.

The dominant life is the forest-that-is-not-wood.

Each standing “tree” is less an individual than an agreement between mineral, fungus, crystal, and atmospheric charge. It has no sapwood heart, no green leaves, no thirst in the animal or plant sense. Its trunk is a bundled column of capillaries: glassy fibers, chalk tubes, blackened carbon threads, pale fungal cords hardened into something like ceramic sinew. These columns grow by accretion rather than swelling. Dust adheres. Vapor reacts. Pressure leaves deposits. Invisible elements condense along charged strands. A trunk becomes taller because the air slowly confesses matter to it.

Inside, there is motion, but almost none that the eye can catch. Charge travels along the fibers in faint pulses. Heat is stored in lattices and released inward. Mineral salts migrate by electrical persuasion. The organism drinks gradients: temperature differences, tension in stone, pressure in sound, the chemical trace of exposed strata, perhaps even the instability of local reality itself. Where other life would open pores to water, these beings open themselves to discrepancy.

Their crowns are harvesting instruments.

Some crown-forms spread into antlers of translucent mineral, each tine fringed with smaller tines, all tuned to the roar. Some grow stacks of shallow cups, angled not upward exactly but toward whatever direction the sky is most permeable that century. Others produce mica-thin fronds arranged in overlapping fans. These fronds can close during periods of dangerous light or open when the world loosens. On their inner faces are branching diagrams like veins, though they carry no blood or sap. They conduct resonance. They gather faint charges from the constant pressure-roar and convert sound into structure.

A grove of them does not rustle. It ticks, rings, settles, and remembers.

They reproduce rarely and indirectly. A mature crown may shed a brittle antler, not as a seed but as a proposition. If the fragment lands in a compatible shadow, on crust of the correct mineral tension, it begins to anchor. It does not sprout roots downward so much as negotiate with the ground. Threads pass into cracks. Plates unfold. Powder nodules form along its base. Over decades, perhaps centuries, the fallen piece becomes a new congress.

Spores also exist, but they are dry, ash-like, and uncertain. They collect in cups and hollows, sometimes for generations. They are not released by wind, since there is little wind to trust. Instead they respond to fracture, echo, gravitational inconsistency, and the brief presence of future conditions. When a fault sings in the right way, a cup may open and let its spores become almost-patterns in the air. They do not drift far. They hover, diagrammatic and half-material, until some surface gives them permission to be matter.

There are smaller lives too.

The salt flats are not bare. Their crusts are inhabited by thin films that look like mineral bloom: blue-grey, white, faint green, sometimes violet-black along cracks. These are slow mats of dry metabolism, colonies flattened into the surface itself. They feed on chemical exhalations from below and on the static charge that accumulates across the broad flats. Some are photosynthetic in a dim, reluctant way, using tarnished light filtered through the broken sky. Others are lithovoric, digesting the youngest stone molecule by molecule. Still others seem to live on repetition: they thicken in places where ground has appeared twice, where a ledge repeats down a ravine, where the world has contradicted itself and left a seam.

These crust-colonies are the plateau’s first skin. When new ridges begin to exist, the films arrive almost immediately, sometimes before the stone has fully hardened into the present. They form hairline green threads in fresh cracks, chalk-white powder on raised seams, dark stippling under overhangs. They stabilize surfaces. They teach raw mineral how to remain.

In the faults and ravines live the listeners.

They may not be plants at all. They appear as pendant organs clinging to sheer walls: folded membranes, curled shells, nodules on filaments, little rows of cup-like mineral ears. Their bodies are pale, often translucent, and layered like dried kelp or thin horn. They face the roar between alternatives. Their growth rings do not count years but versions. In ravines where the same ledge appears five times, a listener will grow five chambers, each tuned to a slightly different ledge.

These organisms feed on resonance. They collect pressure from sound and turn it into mass. Around them the stone is often smoother, as if the noise has been combed out of it. Their dead tissue becomes hard, shell-like plates, useful to the plateau’s human visitors for scrapers and small blades. But harvesting them is dangerous. Cut too deeply and the wall may forget which version of itself was wounded.

Around the great distant spires, life is richer.

The spires are either hosts, parents, predators, or organs of the land itself. At their bases grow dense rings of the plateau’s most elaborate vegetation: emerald-black fans, violet cages of thorn, amber lattices, white powder-corals, and narrow towers of stacked translucent cups. These colonies are darker and more vigorous than the scattered groves. Their structures lean inward toward the spires, though the spires also appear subtly bent toward them, as if they are exchanging nourishment.

There, the organisms are less solitary. They interlock without crowding. A black fan may shade a powder bloom with a shadow that has measurable weight. Amber fibers may bridge from one plant to another, carrying pulse-signals when the roar deepens. Thorn-cages trap not animals but unstable light. In their interiors, small flakes of sky-defect collect like shed skin. The plants digest these flakes slowly, building from them impossible geometries: branching forms that should intersect themselves but do not, leaves with only one side, seed-cups larger inside than outside.

No mouth eats them. No hoof breaks them. No insect drills their stems. Their pressures are geological and metaphysical, not predatory. Their enemies are misalignment, over-silence, defective gravity, and being named wrongly.

Because there are no animals, there is no quickness. The fastest native movement may be the closing of a crown-plate during a dark interval, or the slight recoil of a filament when struck by a harmful sound. Most life moves at the speed of mineral decision. A grove may take a lifetime to alter its silhouette. A salt film may spend a century crossing a stone. A spire-ring colony may grow backward from a condition that has not happened yet, becoming denser before the nourishment that causes it arrives.

Yet the place is not unconscious.

Much of what lives there listens.

The groves listen through their crowns. The crusts listen through vibration. The ravine organs listen through repeated space. The spire colonies listen through light that has failed to travel properly. Together they form a distributed awareness, not centralized, not animal, but responsive. A traveler crossing the flats may feel watched despite the absence of eyes. More accurately, the traveler is being measured: by weight, by heat, by footfall rhythm, by the pressure-shadow of a body, by the way their presence causes possibilities to collapse around them.

A spoken word may cling to a plant because the plant has captured the sound-pressure of it. A name uttered carelessly might be incorporated into a mineral frond, causing that frond to answer to the wrong identity for decades. This is why the human inhabitants, if they remain, do not speak openly.

The people who live or lived there are austere, intermittent, and deeply adapted to uncertainty.

They are not numerous. The plateau cannot support villages in the ordinary sense. It permits shelters, pairs of shelters, small seasonal stations, and routes marked more by caution than possession. These people are likely migratory across stable intervals rather than across seasons of warmth. They travel when weight is dependable, when paths remain attached to destinations, when harvested fiber keeps its shape, when shadows behave closely enough to be read.

Their bodies would be lean, scarred by dryness, protected by layered garments made from mineral reed, cured fungal membrane, powdered fiber, and hide if they bring hide from elsewhere. Their faces would be veiled not against sand but against abrasive sound and chemical bloom. Their hands would be calloused from stonework and from handling brittle living structures that can cut like glass. They would carry staffs inscribed with route-signs, gravity warnings, and silent grammar.

Their language would be largely nonvocal: knots, cords, hand-signs, stones arranged in relational patterns, marks pressed into dust and sealed beneath clear crust. Speech would be intimate, used only mouth-to-ear, and even then with caution. Public language would be physical. Meaning would be placed, tied, carved, raked, aligned. A sentence might be a row of pebbles beside a shelter. A prayer might be a bundle of green filaments suspended under an upward-pointing plumb line. A warning might be a spiral cut into a boulder where the ground repeats.

They would not think of tools as inert. A scraper made from listener-shell would have a direction in which it prefers to cut. A mineral reed basket might hold some substances only during quiet intervals. A stone hammer taken from the wrong terrace might become too heavy to lift near a fault. Nothing would be fully owned, because objects belong partly to places.

Their shelters are low and thick because dwelling is an act of insistence. To stack salt-slate into walls is to ask reality to stay stacked. To sleep under fungal membrane is to trust a dead colony to remember enclosure. Doorways face away from abysses not only because of wind or danger, but because ravines contain too many versions of departure.

If these people still survive, they are probably fewer than their ruins suggest. They may live beyond the richer spire fields, or in calmer folds where the roar thins. They may come to the abandoned shelters only during certain permissions, to harvest crown-fronds, collect crust salts, cut reed-bundles, measure shadows, or leave offerings that are also instruments. Their rituals and sciences would be indistinguishable because the plateau does not separate obedience from measurement. To know a thing there is to avoid angering it.

They may believe the plateau is unfinished, not ruined. They may regard themselves as caretakers of becoming: witnesses who help ridges choose to stand, who bind filaments around new stone seams, who rake circles into powder to stabilize the center of a room. Their cairns and aligned standing stones are not memorials alone. They are negotiations. They hold shadows steady. They pin routes to the present. They persuade places to remain adjacent.

But the deepest inhabitants are not the people.

The true native life is the plateau’s dry abundance itself: mineral organisms that do not soften the land but annotate it; forests that grow from charge and contradiction; crusts that teach newborn stone permanence; ravine-listeners that fatten on the roar between possible worlds; spire gardens that exchange nourishment with broken geometry.

They have no blood, no hunger, no eyes. Their version of desire is orientation. Toward sound. Toward charge. Toward stable shadow. Toward the moment when a possible form becomes real enough to inhabit.

They live without water by drinking law.

And in that high, unfinished country, to live is to become structure slowly, to endure the roar, to open only when the world is permissive, and to fold inside oneself the outline of another sky.
