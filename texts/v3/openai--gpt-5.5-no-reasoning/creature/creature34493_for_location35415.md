What lives there is not one creature at first, but a tenancy: moss, machinery, seep-water, heat, and the first stubborn forms of life that arrive before a place has decided what it is.

The true inhabitant is the wet itself.

It lives in every seam of the new black stone, insinuating upward through fractures too sharp to have names yet. It beads, gathers, trembles, falls. It has no single body, but it repeats itself everywhere: on the underside of pipes, in the felted backs of ferns, under the bowed iron grating, in the orange-lipped puddles and the lime-white terraces and the fogged glass of gauges that still try to report on conditions no one reads. It is not passive water. It is industrious, territorial. It claims objects by softening them. It changes metal into bloom, brick into sponge, wood into dark swollen fiber. Anything placed here is accepted only after it has been made damp enough to belong.

The plants are its most visible citizens.

They are young plants, quick and shameless, not yet dignified by age. Ferns unfold from cracks in the basalt with a lacquered brightness, each frond too green against the raw stone, as if painted there moments ago. Moss cushions crowd the floor in yellow and emerald masses, rounding the angularity of the hollow without quite erasing it. Liverworts make reddish slicks on the shadowed stones, like small colonies of blood-colored tongues. Sedges stand in clumps where gravel has trapped enough mineral silt to pretend at soil. Algae sheet the runnels and tremble when drops strike them.

They do not look settled. They look opportunistic. They grip, spread, test, and return. Their roots are exploratory rather than deep, threads and fingers pushed into brand-new cracks, into rust flakes, into mortar joints, into the edges of mineral crust. They live as if every surface is temporary but worth taking.

There may be no old trees here. Nothing with a trunk broad enough to cast judgment. The hollow has no patience yet for grandeur. Its vegetation is intimate and multiplying: fronds, mats, beads, tongues, hairs, rootlets, films. Life here is close to the surface. It advances by covering.

The pump-house also lives there.

Not like an animal, not exactly, but more than a ruin. It is the hollow’s heart or lung, depending on where one stands. Its brick skin sweats. Its windows go blind with inner condensation. Behind them something rotates, compresses, opens, closes, hesitates, and resumes. The heavy chuff comes through the ground. The deeper knock arrives every half-minute or so, a thought in an iron body. Belts move in the dark. Valves complain. Pipes shiver. Hidden water hisses through old routes.

No one tends it, but it has not accepted abandonment. It continues by habit, perhaps from a flywheel’s ancient confidence, perhaps from pressure, perhaps from some half-failed governing system that has mistaken persistence for duty. The machine is not intelligent in the human sense, but it has temperament. It dislikes dryness. It likes rhythm. It answers the mountain’s seepage with its own mechanical breathing.

Condensation runs down its walls like sweat down a sleeping face.

Inside, the machinery is likely furred and mineralized, a creature of pistons and calcite. Its gauges have cataracts. Its joints are rimmed with rust. Its bolts are no longer purely metal but iron hosting moss, mineral, and bacterial stain. The pump-house is becoming geological from the outside inward and organic from the inside out. It is being digested very slowly by the place it once managed.

The pipes live there too, though in a more ambiguous condition.

Some still carry water. Some carry only echoes. Some are blocked with lime or ocher sludge. Some have split and now leak in fine shining threads. The working pipes possess a tense, narrow life: vibration, pressure, warmth. The dead ones are becoming habitat. Moss takes their upper surfaces. Algae slicks their mouths. Insects shelter beneath them. Rootlets enter hairline cracks and follow the darkness inside. Tiny bubbles cling to submerged sections and release one at a time, as if the pipes are still exhaling after death.

The iron stanchions stand like a sparse artificial reedbed. The walkway is a spine laid across softness. The cracked ceramic insulators hum faintly when wet, keeping a little electrical ghost in their porcelain bodies. The whole abandoned system has not disappeared into nature; it has become one of nature’s available shapes.

Among the green and the iron, small animals come and go, but they are not the rulers of the basin.

There are water-striders or something like them: dark, jointed, impossibly delicate things that skate across puddles and read the world through vibrations on the surface. They know the pump’s deep knock before the air does. When the ring travels through the central pool, they pause, then resume their scribbled patrols.

There are pale moths tucked beneath the rusted railings, their wings dampened at the edges, holding still through the wet daylight. At night they may stir toward the indicator lamps, circling the amber and green glows behind dirty glass, mistaking old warning lights for moons. Some perish there, softening on the sill, becoming part of the mineral film.

There are lizards on the warm stones near the pump-house, dark-backed and patient, making use of the impossible climate: alpine sun above, geothermal warmth below. They bask on black rock until they resemble little cast pieces of the same material, then vanish into cracks when a shadow passes. Their presence feels provisional, as though each one has discovered the basin separately and has not told the others.

There are likely larvae in the puddles, transparent-bodied and quick. Midges rise in brief, wavering columns when the air is still. Beetles hide beneath wet bark scraps or under metal lips. Pale worms thread through the moss mats. Snails may arrive eventually, if they have not already: small, dark-shelled, patient grazers of algae, perfectly suited to a world that never dries.

But animal life remains sparse. It has the quality of visitation, of scouts and accidents. The hollow belongs more deeply to organisms that do not flee.

The unseen life is probably the most abundant.

Bacteria bloom in orange and white along mineral runnels. Heat-loving films slick the mouths of springs. Algae make green shadows in the shallow water. Fungal threads lace the swelling wood and old cloth, if any cloth remains. Lichens begin their slow negotiations on the highest stones where noon can dry an edge for an hour. Microbial colonies write themselves in color: rust, sulfur-yellow, blue-green, milk-white, black.

These are the first scribes of the hollow. They translate stone, heat, iron, and water into soil.

They live in the pump’s splash zone, in the gravel at the bottom of the clear pool, in the orange rims of puddles, inside the pipes where light never reaches. Some feed on minerals. Some on decay. Some on the residues of oil. Some exist in thin, trembling alliances with the moss and the algae, binding the place together molecule by molecule. If the hollow has a future forest in it, that forest begins with these films.

And beneath all this lives the mountain’s unfinished heat.

It is not visible except by consequence. It warms the floor. It keeps the air soft. It persuades tropical ferns to unfurl beneath a knife-edge ridge where the wind would otherwise strip moisture from the eye. It rises through faults and new fractures, carrying dissolved minerals, feeding the pool, fogging the pump-house windows. It makes the hollow an exception to its altitude.

This heat is older than the pump and younger than the mountain’s deeper fire. It is residual, but not spent. It is the memory of making. The rock around it is new enough to look wounded, and the heat below it is new enough to keep the wound from scabbing over.

If there is a presiding creature here, it is a composite one.

It has a brick chest and iron ribs. It has pipes for veins, moss for fur, mineral deposits for teeth, warm seep-water for blood. Its breathing is mechanical, but its skin grows. Its voice is the chuff, hiss, drip, knock, ping, and fall of water on different substances. Its thoughts are slow rings widening across the clear central pool. Its dreams are condensation.

At night, this creature is easiest to believe in.

The ridge goes black against the stars. The outer mountain freezes into silence. But below the lip, the hollow remains awake. Indicator lamps glow through the pump-house windows: amber, green, red. The red one pulses so slowly that it seems less like a signal than a closed eyelid lit from within. Mist crawls at ground level. Ferns become silhouettes. Drips strike metal with small exact sounds. The machine knocks once, waits, knocks again.

Then the whole basin seems to be sleeping lightly, not unconscious but inwardly occupied.

No human lives there now, though humans are everywhere implied. Their hands set the stanchions, laid the pipes, bolted the gauges, built the little brick house, strung the cables, installed the valves. But the hollow has outlived their attention. Human intention remains only as architecture and rhythm. The purpose has drained away; the operation continues.

That absence is important. It gives the place its peculiar mood: not abandonment, but succession.

The human-built things are not being erased quickly. They are being recruited. The walkway becomes a trellis. The pipes become channels, burrows, roots, perches, dams. The pump-house becomes cliff, cave, warm wall, resonant shell. The machinery’s waste heat becomes climate. Rust becomes nutrient. Leakage becomes irrigation. Even malfunction becomes a kind of ecology.

What lives there is everything that can make use of an unfinished boundary.

Alpine and tropical. Natural and industrial. New stone and old brick. Living root and dead pipe. Cold wind one pace above, warm marsh one pace below. Nothing in the hollow belongs cleanly to one category, so its inhabitants are all adapters, opportunists, and hybrids.

The moss does not care whether it grows on basalt or iron.
The water does not care whether a channel was carved by erosion or threaded by a pipefitter.
The pump does not care whether anyone remembers its purpose.
The ferns do not care that they are too lush for the altitude.
The bacteria do not care that the mountain is young.

They all accept the contradiction and elaborate it.

Over time, the place will grow more itself. The plants will thicken. The pump will either stop or become stranger in continuing. Pipes will clog, split, and become indistinguishable from roots. Roots will enter pipes and make use of their darkness. The central pool will cloud, terrace, seal, reopen. New mats will spread over the old ones. The pump-house will slump into its wet foundation, not collapsing all at once but yielding by increments.

More things may come: frogs if there is a route for them, birds nesting in the warmer cracks, spiders stringing webs beneath the grating, shrubs finding purchase in gravel pockets, perhaps eventually a stunted tree leaning in from the least impossible wall. But even then, the first inhabitants will remain dominant: water, warmth, green film, iron pulse.

So the answer is this:

A young marsh-machine lives at the top of the mountain.

It is inhabited by mosses, ferns, liverworts, algae, sedges, insects, lizards, microbes, rust, mineral water, and a pump that has forgotten the distinction between work and breathing. It is not haunted by a ghost so much as animated by continuation. Everything there is either seeping, corroding, rooting, condensing, or listening to the next deep knock from inside the brick house.

It is a place where life has not settled into form yet.

It is still arriving.
