What lives there is not a people, not in any clear sense. It is a population of plants that has become almost a place-organ: a settled, luminous, self-roofing flora inhabiting the buried basin so completely that individual lives are difficult to separate from the whole.

The dominant inhabitants are the light-bearing plants. They are thick, old, and slow beyond animal measure, though the ground beneath them seems young. They do not merely grow in the chamber; they maintain it. Their tissues make the ceiling. Their roots bind the tender stone before it can crack or slump. Their mats soften the floor. Their stems form corridors, screens, buttresses, cisterns, ladders, partitions, and low enclosures. They are architecture without builders.

They are warm-bodied in a plant sense. Their sap carries light and heat together: a pale yellow-white radiance stored in ribs, bladders, bulbs, leaf-undersides, hanging sacs, and translucent veinwork. The glow is steady, not flashing or seasonal. It is not display. It is metabolism made visible. The plants have no need to reach upward toward a sun; they have internalized sunlight, or something like it, and now live by circulating it through themselves. Their leaves are not arranged to catch light from above but to distribute it through depth, around pillars, beneath mats, into hollows and cisterns. They illuminate their own roots. They light the creatures that hide among them. They erase night.

There are many kinds of them, though they interweave until classification becomes difficult.

There are roof-plants: broad, fused growths spread overhead in a continuous membrane, thick as earth and alive throughout. Their lower surfaces are waxy and faintly translucent, crossed with glowing ribs. From them hang bladder-clusters full of bright sap, root-cords, pendulous cisterns, and thin curtains of hairlike leaves. Some roof sections sag into great shallow bowls, collecting condensation until they release it drop by drop to the same places below. Others swell upward out of sight, as if forming sealed chambers inside themselves.

There are pillar-plants: immense descending roots or stems, glossy, braided, and almost muscular. They join ceiling to floor without seeming to branch, though their surfaces reveal a history of many tendrils fused together while soft. They are load-bearing but alive, faintly warm, and sometimes ridged with channels that carry water downward or light upward. Around their bases, other plants gather in rings, forming miniature ecologies: moss-collars, pale fungi-like pads, red-edged leaves, tiny still pools.

There are floor-plants: compressed mats of root, stem, and absorbed decay. They are springy, hot, fibrous in layers, and sensitive to pressure over long periods. Where something regularly passes, they compact into smoother ways. Where water drips, they thicken into dark green lips and mossy basins. Where no weight falls, they rise slowly into ridges, cushions, or sealed swellings. Fallen leaves do not rot openly there; they are received, softened, and incorporated, as if the floor digests its own shed skin.

There are cistern-plants: cupped leaves, green urns, clear sacs, root-held bowls, and hollow stems that store the basin’s rationed water. They are among the liveliest centers of the place. Their margins are crowded with fine white root hairs, purple stems, black-green blades, and films of pollen. Some hold clear water. Some hold sweet mineral fluid. Some contain small blind animals. Others appear empty but tremble slightly when touched, as though their contents are held under tension.

There are curtain-plants: countless narrow leaves or tendrils hanging in still sheets from roof and arch. They barely move, even in the passage of a body, and seem to close behind disturbance by relaxation rather than motion. They filter air, catch spores, bead moisture, and make thresholds between one green district and another. Passing through them may leave a faint resin on the skin.

There are blade-fields, pad-districts, fern-slopes, organ-gardens: neighborhoods of specialized growth. Waxy blue pads lie packed like scales. Yellow upright blades form silent ranks. Fernlike fronds cover slopes without quivering. Huge folded fans resemble lungs. Root tunnels curve like intestines. Heart-shaped chambers open inside masses of red-veined leaves. Hexagonal mats repeat with geometric exactness, not from design but from pressure, heat, moisture, and some patient vegetal rule.

If there is a central intelligence here, it is distributed through these plants. It does not think in images or plans. It does not decide, speak, remember as animals remember, or intend in the way a toolmaker intends. But the place behaves as though it knows balance. It allots water. It preserves warmth. It routes drops to waiting roots. It thickens where future weight may come. It leaves corridors through density, not for travelers exactly, but because circulation requires them: air, spores, creeping animals, root pressure, the release of heat. It builds repetitions that resemble shrines and dwellings because living matter, given enough time and constraint, discovers form.

The small pale structures are likely old growth-forms rather than ruins. They are the hardened residues of previous arrangements: root-rings, mineralized fiber, abandoned cistern rims, nursery arches, seed-hollows, pressure frames, perhaps the casings of organs the larger plant-system no longer needed. They resemble huts, platforms, low walls, or ritual enclosures only because the mind looks for makers. The actual maker is recurrence. A root curled around empty space, hardened, was overgrown, and the shape remained. A circle collected water for long enough that plants reinforced its edge. A row of knobs marks where an old tendril-line once anchored itself. They are botanical fossils still embedded in living tissue.

The animals that live there are few, quiet, and subordinate.

They are mostly pale, flattened, blind, translucent, or slow. They have adapted not to disturb the equilibrium. A loud creature would be absurd there; a swift one would be wasteful.

On the undersides of glowing leaves cling thin insects like flakes of milk-glass, almost two-dimensional, their legs spread wide along veins of light. They feed by piercing luminous tissue delicately, taking sap without killing the leaf. Their bodies are so translucent that the yellow-white glow passes through them, making them look less like animals than flaws in the leaf. They may remain fixed in one place for days, digesting warmth.

In the cisterns live small blind swimmers: larval things, thread-bodied crustaceans, soft-mouthed pale tadpole forms without true eyes. They rise only to disturb the film on the water, making brief rings beneath pollen and luminous dust. Some graze on the inner skins of water-holding plants. Others eat spores that fall from above. Their lives are circular, confined to one cup, one root-basin, one suspended bladder. To cross from pool to pool may require generations: eggs carried in condensation drops, larvae transferred by a creeping insect’s foot, cysts absorbed and later released by plant tissue.

Under the mats move burrowers. They are not miners; they do not excavate with violence. They part the warm root layers slowly, pressing through seams that close behind them. They may be soft-bodied, noseless, nearly eyeless, with sensitive sides that read heat and pressure. Their passage produces only a low displacement, a breathing of the floor. They feed on shed fibers, softened leaf matter, or nutrient nodules grown by the plants themselves. They are less predators than internal fauna, like worms in fruit or gut-life in a body.

High in the roof growth live the winged still creatures: translucent, jointed beings with limbs folded close and wings like thin wet membranes. They do not swarm. One may hang alone for a very long time, warmed by the plant-light, its body scarcely distinguishable from a pendant bladder. Perhaps they fly only rarely, when some chemical signal passes through the roof-plants. Perhaps their wings are for drifting from one lit vault to another during reproductive phases separated by years. Their stillness is not sleep exactly, but a low-burning existence.

There may also be mites in the leaf fur, pale snails on the cistern rims, root-lice clustered in warm seams, and tiny transparent predators no larger than seeds. But none of them command the scene. They are tenants, pollinators, cleaners, parasites, and messengers. They live inside the plant-world’s permissions.

Decay-organisms must exist, though they do not announce themselves as rot. Fungal threads, bacterial films, soft white digesters, and saprophytic veils probably lace the floor-mats and fallen leaves. They work without smell because nothing is left exposed long enough to sour. Death is handled internally. A leaf falls, lies briefly, then darkens into the mat. A dead insect becomes a pale fleck, then a stain, then food. A failed root is sealed by new tissue. The ecosystem has no refuse, only reabsorption.

The beings there have little use for time as surface life knows it. Without day, night, season, drought, storm, or cold, their rhythms are chemical and positional. A drop falls every so often from the same rib. A cistern reaches a certain fullness. A vein brightens imperceptibly. A spore ripens in constant warmth. A burrower’s pressure triggers a root to thicken over months. A pale insect molts after feeding from one vein for half a year. Events do happen, but the place stretches them until they resemble conditions.

What lives there, then, is an underground garden that is almost an animal, or an animal so dispersed and rooted that it has become a garden.

Its inhabitants do not seek escape. They are not trapped in darkness, because they have made brightness. They are not waiting for weather, because weather has been replaced by circulation. They are not progressing toward a forest, a city, or a ruin. They have achieved a paused abundance: total occupation without hurry, metabolism without drama, growth held at the edge of change.

If one wanted to name the true resident, it would be the basin’s vegetative body itself: a luminous, heat-keeping, water-rationing underworld flora, older in habit than the young stone that carries it, patient enough to seem motionless, alive enough to make the whole buried valley glow.
