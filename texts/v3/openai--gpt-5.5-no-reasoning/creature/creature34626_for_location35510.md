What lives there is not an animal, and not a people, though it has inherited the vacancy of both.

It is the plateau itself, or something distributed through it: a dry, botanical industry spread across miles of dark height. It does not have a single body. It has neighborhoods, organs, districts, reflexes. Its limbs are root-cables and gantries. Its lungs are chimney-groves. Its mouths are vents and dust-flowers. Its memory is not held in thought but in rings, scars, grafts, repeated structures, stress marks, preserved instrument housings, and the habitual routes of heat.

It lives as a civilization might live after forgetting citizens.

There are no inhabitants separate from the place. The inhabitants are the wall-trunks, the pipe-vines, the furnace blossoms, the seed vaults, the pressure ferns, the conveyor roots, the ambulatory trellises, the heat petals, the ceramic-barked columns, the dormant pavements waiting to grow. Each seems like a part of an enormous organism, but none is merely passive tissue. They behave like institutions. They regulate, store, negotiate, consume, replace, archive. A grove exhales according to buried pressure. A plaza sorts minerals. A tower breeds chambers of seeds. A wall records thermal history in thorn and pigment. A gantry moves shade and tension through the region like a slow official.

The life there is plant life that has passed beyond pasture, forest, garden, and farm. It has become infrastructure.

It is adapted to the absence of water so completely that wetness would be disaster. Its living matter is dry, mineralized, resinous, carbonized, conductive. It drinks what other ecologies would die among: furnace heat, vapor metals, salts, static, infrared leakage, exhaust, pressure, abrasive dust. It does not bloom for bees or fruit for mouths. Its reproduction is mechanical and sealed: pollen hammered through ducts, spores pressurized into storms, seed capsules conveyed along root-rails, flowers opening like valves toward one another in the dark.

Its colors are the colors of burned usefulness: black, rust, sulfur, violet bruise, dull silver, bone-char matte. Its leaves are not tender surfaces but tools: radiators, shutters, membranes, fans, armored vanes. Its trunks are not simply trunks but columns, chimneys, tanks, pillars, memory cores. Its roots are miners and engineers. They do not seek soil; they make soil unnecessary. They eat substrate directly: slag, glass, carbon, brick, machine shell, dead growth, old scaffold, prior city.

It is old enough that origin no longer matters.

If people made it, they are gone. If machines made it, the machines have become botanical. If plants overtook a city, the city has become anatomy. The life of the plateau contains the ghosts of builders only as useful forms: doorway-shapes repurposed as vents, console cavities serving as cooling chambers, collars swallowed into bark, rails become root-guides, balconies carrying sap-cables. The former inhabitants are not remembered sentimentally. They are pattern, mineral, habit. Their designs survive only where they help the plateau regulate heat, dryness, pressure, and growth.

The creatures of this place are therefore structures with appetites.

A root-factory lives by digestion and redistribution. It clamps into the hard body of the plateau and draws out salts, metals, old carbon, residual charge. It grows not upward like a tree seeking sky, but laterally and downward and around, strengthening what it consumes as it consumes it. It does not destroy a ruin; it metabolizes ruin into continuity.

A chimney-cactus lives by breath. Tall, ribbed, crowded among its own kind, it inhales and exhales hot gases in controlled pulses. Its pores are tiny furnace-stomata. Its flesh is armored against the very heat it needs. Around its base, fan-ferns open and flatten in response, like hands, like gauges, like subordinate lungs.

A furnace-blossom lives by emergency. It remains a sealed black seam until heat exceeds tolerance; then it unfolds house-sized petals and shows a white interior hotter than ordinary flame. It is beautiful only accidentally. Its beauty is the visible part of a pressure protocol.

A seed-tower lives by sorting futures. Inside it are chambers filled with hard dry embryos classified by density, charge, mineral hunger, heat tolerance, perhaps by memory. Its seeds are not soft promises but cast components, waiting for the right abrasion, pulse, pressure, or catastrophe.

A gantry-tree lives by movement without wandering. It rolls or drags along root-rails, a skeletal trellis of trunk and black steel, lubricated by mosslike tissue. It carries hooks, pods, pollen sacs, cable-vines. Plants lean away from it or offer themselves to be clipped and tensioned. It is gardener and plant, tool and growth, laborer and limb.

A pavement-field lives by latency. What seems like flooring may be compressed stems, hardened seed-pods, leaf-metal scales, root velvet sharp as fiberglass. It waits as ground until conditions require it to vent, split, sprout, seal, hinge, or harden. No surface is inert. Bareness is not permitted.

The whole ecology is governed by a few severe hungers.

It must capture heat without being consumed by it.  
It must preserve dryness.  
It must turn death into structure.  
It must fill emptiness.  
It must make every surface functional.  
It must transform catastrophe into maintenance.  

Its instincts are architectural. Where an animal might flee, it seals. Where a forest might rot, it carbonizes. Where a city might repair, it regrows. Where a machine might fail, it flowers into another configuration.

It has no voice, but it communicates constantly.

Pressure shifts through ducts. Roots click beneath the floor. Electrical charge branches along leaves. Thermal petals snap shut in unison. Pollen is sent through sealed channels. Pigment scars and thorn rows repeat across walls like script. Whether these are messages, reflexes, or developmental patterns may be impossible to say. In this place, instruction and anatomy are the same. A command is something grown. A memory is something reinforced. A law is a vascular habit that has survived enough heat to become permanent.

Its storms are not weather but collective action.

When pressure wrongness rises from below, the entire plateau prepares. Vanes fold. Vaults seal. Whip-vines tighten between towers. The air fills with mineral dust, charged pollen, powdered carbon, metallic spores. In the opacity of the storm, the inhabitants work upon themselves: drilling, molting, grafting, shedding, re-routing, feeding slag into bark-cups. Afterward, the changes are visible. A hedge has become a wall. A wall has sprouted needles. An avenue has closed. A tower has divided. This is how the place moves through time: not by seasons, but by rearrangements under stress.

If there is a mind there, it is not centralized.

It would not think in images or words. It would think in gradients: heat rising, charge gathering, mineral depletion, pressure loss, structural fatigue, reproductive timing, dryness threatened, surface exposed. Its attention would be slow but not dull. It would notice a wet object instantly, as an animal notices blood or fire. It would reorient miles of root toward it. It would not be curious in a human way. Its curiosity would be absorption.

It is not hostile because hostility implies an outside category called enemy. But it is absolute. Anything entering it becomes condition, obstruction, nutrient, heat source, contamination, scaffold, shade-caster, or seedbed. A traveler would not be hunted. They would be processed by proximity. The plateau’s responses would be impersonal and exact: seal the moisture, wick the salts, carbonize the remains, grow around the hard parts, archive the shape if useful.

The inhabitants, then, are dry gods of function and survival, though godhood here has no grandeur, only persistence.

They are plants that have made a world where animals are unnecessary. Plants that learned industry not as imitation but as evolution. Plants that took machinery into their bodies until machine and body ceased to be different. They are factories with sap, buildings with metabolism, forests with maintenance schedules, engines that reproduce by seed.

They live in darkness, but not in deprivation. Darkness is their medium. Heat is their weather. Dust is their rain. Death is their timber. Ruin is their loam.

What lives there is an ancient, self-maintaining, plant-machine ecology: an overgrown civilization of dry vegetal mechanisms, endlessly rebuilding itself from furnace breath and mineral dust, high above a lower world it has already forgotten.
