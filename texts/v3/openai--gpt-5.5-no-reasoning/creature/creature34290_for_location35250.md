What lives there is not a single species so much as a way of enduring.

The beings of that drowned, high basin are interval-creatures: sparse, pressure-taught, unfinished. They do not fill the place. They punctuate it. One in a cleft. One beneath a ledge. One rooted where two violences cancel each other for a moment. Their lives are arranged the way the chamber arranges its ruins—partly deliberate, partly accidental, always with more emptiness around them than comfort would require.

Most are small, or appear small, because largeness is difficult to keep intact there.

They are made to bend, flatten, vanish, return.

The most common inhabitants are not animals in the usual sense but films, mats, stains of living chemistry spread thin over hot stone. Copper, black-green, rust-orange, gray like wet ash. They live where the vents breathe and the water has not yet decided to scour them away. They are the basin’s first handwriting: slim microbial skins taking down dictation from heat, sulfur, iron, pressure, lightning, powdered basalt. They have no faces and no haste. They bloom in silence beneath the roar, weaving themselves bead by bead over newborn mineral crusts, then retracting, hardening, or dying into stone when the current sharpens.

Some of them are not merely passive. In the warmer phases they darken and thicken, raising cilia-like hairs into the flow. These hairs comb the water for minerals and drifting organics, but also for changes—temperature, pressure, electrical charge, perhaps even memory. When lightning roots through the descending water above, the mats answer with dim internal pulses, one colony after another, until the basin floor resembles an extinguished city trying to relight itself under mud.

They are eaten by nearly everything that can eat.

The plantlike colonies live farther apart.

They are anchored in contradiction: not in calm water, because calm betrays, and not in the strongest current, because force has no patience. They choose confusion. The lee of a spire. The underside of a slanted shelf. The pocket behind a slab where three currents argue and none wins completely.

Their stems are dark and fibrous, more ligament than stalk. They knot themselves into cracks and mineral seams with root-threads that can loosen instantly if the stone begins to close. Above these anchors rise frayed ribbons, gas bladders, hair-leaves, black translucent fans. Some are wine-colored, some amber-brown, some a green so diluted it seems remembered rather than present. They do not reach upward toward light; upward is not reliable. Instead they turn toward warmth, toward chemical flavor, toward vibration. A vent pulse opens them. A thunderclap folds them shut. A distant collapse makes their bladders tremble.

They store air when the water remembers air. They store heat when the vents breathe strongly. They store minerals, yes, but also patterns: the intervals between booms, the direction of recurring surges, the taste of a crack before it opens into somewhere else. Their bodies are maps written in bruise colors and torn membranes.

In the amber phase they unfold the most. Not fully—nothing here trusts fullness—but enough to reveal inner surfaces furred with black velvet. These surfaces drink warmth and perhaps sound. When the slab-holes moan continuously, the plants turn their narrow leaves toward that chord, arranging themselves like listeners.

Some live a long time by becoming less alive. Their outer tissues mineralize in layers until they resemble coral, shell, weathered bone. Within that brittle armor, a thin dark thread remains living, waiting for the next phase in which softness is possible.

The animals are rarer.

They arrive like corrections to a page already being erased.

The pale eels are the most visible, though even they are usually only glimpses: cloth-flat bodies slipping from cracks, trembling in the current without seeming to swim. They have no true eyes, or else their eyes have migrated under the skin to avoid being torn away by grit. Their mouths are soft oval openings ringed with minute hooks. They scrape microbial film, sift vent-plume particles, and feed on the larvae that rain down from above or rise from below.

Their bodies are nearly transparent except where minerals have collected in bands along the spine. Some have amber streaks, some black flecks, some blue-white knots that flare faintly when they pass through colder water. They do not fight the current head-on. They become banners. They let the water stretch them thin, then use sudden muscular ripples to fold themselves into eddies and cracks. A frightened eel can disappear by flattening so completely against basalt that it becomes only a pale seam.

They are solitary except when the black phase deepens. Then, drawn by low sound, several may emerge from different clefts and hang together around the slab line, all aligned toward the holes as if listening. Their mouths open and close. Their throats pulse. They may be feeding on the vibration itself, or communicating through it, or being tuned by it into some necessary reproductive state. Eggs, when released, are not laid in nests but glued singly inside bubble rows on grooved stone. Many are scraped away. A few hatch into threadlike larvae that drift for an unknown distance before finding cracks of their own.

Under the ledges live the crusted ones.

They are small, communal, and built like shut doors. Each is a hinged mineral lump with legs tucked beneath it, shell roughened to match the underside of stone. Colonies of them cluster where the roar is slightly broken, hundreds of tiny armored bodies arranged in crescents and fans. When the water booms, they close together in unison, and the ledge briefly looks as though it has grown scales.

They feed by opening hair-fine nets into the passing flow. These nets are nearly invisible until they catch bubbles, then the whole colony glitters with trembling white beads. They are cautious but not slow. If a vent stutters wrong or a crack opens nearby, the colony can detach as one and tumble away in a compact ball, rolling through the water until it strikes another sheltered surface. Many die this way. Enough survive that the species persists.

Their shells record the basin’s phases. Amber layers, black layers, white-disrupted layers where growth has doubled or reversed. The oldest individuals are unreadable spirals of accreted catastrophe.

There are also bell-bodied drifters.

They come rarely through the rain-columns: translucent, soft, cold-lit things with trailing threads that flash blue where the water cuts them. Whether they are native to the basin or merely pass through it is unclear. They seem too delicate for the place until one notices that their delicacy is evasive. They tear and reseal. Their bells collapse and reinflate. Their threads break into sparks, each spark wriggling briefly before dissolving.

They feed on electrical difference, on larvae, on powdered organic matter, perhaps on moments when reality thins. In the white phase they become numerous for a little while, appearing in duplicated groups, one bell drifting where three should be, three shadows following one body. Then they vanish into the open plunge or the rain-ceiling, leaving only a cold afterglow in the water.

Deeper down live the large shadows.

No one sees them clearly, because clarity is not one of the permissions granted there. They rise during black phases, when distance briefly lengthens and the plunge shows terraces or teeth or roofs below. Their bodies move with a deliberation unlike falling stone. Broad-backed, pale-bellied, jointless from afar. Or ridged. Or many-limbed. Their outlines change when rain passes before them.

They browse on mineral skins, tearing away whole plates of microbial crust with slow mouths. They nose through pumice rafts and root tangles. Sometimes one turns beneath the basin and the pressure changes everywhere at once, drawing plants flat and making the slabs sing. Their presence does not feel predatory exactly. More like weather given hunger.

They may be animals of the larger atmospheric ocean, visiting this chamber as grazers visit a shallow reef. They may be future inhabitants seen through the western crack. They may be pieces of drowned architecture learning to move. The smaller creatures respond to them with absolute caution. Eels vanish. Crusted colonies seal. Plant bladders go dark.

Yet after the shadows pass, the mats grow thickly in their wake.

There are larvae everywhere at times.

Not in abundance, never that, but in sudden clouds: commas, needles, curled flecks, translucent seeds with beating rims. They emerge from vents, from cracks, from beneath ledges, from nowhere. Some fall with the dense rain from the ceiling. Some rise from the abyss. Some appear inside air-pockets and perish when those pockets collapse. Many are not the young of anything visible in the basin.

They are experiments with appetite.

One has a shell before it has a mouth. One is all mouth and no body. One drifts as a ribbon until it touches hot stone, then becomes a stain. One has tiny mineral teeth that gnaw basalt for an hour before dissolving. Their mortality is immense and impersonal. The chamber samples them. It keeps a few.

The inhabitants that seem most like people are not people.

They are the arrangements.

The three slabs with circular holes are occupied, though not by flesh. Something lives in their tone. When currents pass through the openings and the bass note shudders out, the surrounding organisms alter themselves. Eels gather. Plants turn. Crusted colonies close in patterns more complex than simple fear. Microbial mats pulse. Even suspended grit arranges into faint bands. The slabs may be habitat for a vibrational ecology: resonant creatures made not of tissue but of standing waves, pressure knots, recurring chords.

These sound-beings would have no bodies except when water grants them one. They live in hollows, in holes, in the throats of arches, in the six-step stair that descends from nowhere. They reproduce when structures appear in the white phase—new steps, new cups, new rings around vents—each fresh shape offering a chamber in which a tone can stabilize. They die when a lintel cracks or a hole fills with accretion.

Their intelligence, if that word applies, would be distributed through recurrence. They remember by vibrating stone in the same way twice. They speak by altering current. They are why the architecture feels interrupted rather than abandoned: it is less a ruin than a body still trying to choose its bones.

There may also be things that live in the air-pockets.

Briefly.

Whenever the water remembers being air, warm trembling spheres appear among the rain-columns, smelling of hot rock and mist. In those globes, tiny motes wake. They cling to the inner membrane, bead-like and frantic, absorbing oxygen, heat, mineral vapor. Some unfold vanes. Some sprout legs. Some sing too high to hear until the pocket begins to fail.

Their lives may last seconds, but seconds behave strangely there. Inside the pocket, perhaps an entire generation flowers and breeds against the membrane while outside only a pulse of thunder passes. When the pocket collapses, most are crushed or drowned; a few encyst into grains that drift until the next remembered-air opens.

They are inhabitants of interruption itself.

Then there are the revision-creatures.

These are harder to separate from the place.

Near the largest vent, where pebbles orbit into a dark ring and a hand can return briefly older, living things sometimes enter and emerge changed in ways that are not injury. An eel passes through and comes out with shell-plates along one side. A plant-root threads into the spiral and returns forked into three temporal versions, each responding to a different current. A crusted animal caught in the ring may become fossilized before it dies, then open again later with soft new legs beneath a stone shell older than the chamber.

Some beings have adapted to this. They seek the unstable zones not by accident but as part of their life cycle. Their bodies require revision. They are born incomplete and must pass through altered pressure, altered time, altered gravity to acquire organs. A larva may enter the heat spiral as a translucent loop and leave with a digestive tract. A flat eel may brush the western crack and acquire a memory of the blue-lit chamber, using that impossible memory to navigate cracks that do not yet exist.

For them, mutation is not disaster. It is weather.

The place’s most enduring resident, however, may be the basin itself—not as metaphor, but as organism or nearly-organism.

It breathes through vents. It grows mineral towers and sheds them. It opens mouths in the western wall. It remembers air. It hums through slabs. It arranges sparse structures at survivable intervals. It punishes crowding. It preserves emptiness. It invites life, abrades life, revises life, and incorporates what fails into stone.

The chamber may be young stone learning landscape, but it also behaves like a body learning what organs are.

The arch could be a jaw or doorway or rib because those distinctions have not hardened yet. The stairway may be an appendage forming. The shallow basin in the ledge may be an eye that has chosen overflow instead of sight. The grooves on the wall, gathering bubbles in rows, may be gills practicing script.

Its inhabitants are therefore not merely living in it. They are being tested as possible features of it.

A plant may become a current-sensor. A microbial mat may become skin. A colony of crusted animals may teach the underside of ledges how to close. Eels may be the basin’s first thoughts of motion. Bell-drifters its experiments with nerve-light. The great shadows below its dreams of appetite on a scale it has not yet earned.

Anything that remains too separate is eventually torn away.

Anything that yields may be remembered.

So what lives there?

Heat-fed films. Solitary torn plants. Blind flat eels. Armored colonies under ledges. Blue-flashing bell drifters. Larvae from phases without names. Huge uncertain grazers rising from below. Brief air-breathers in the remembered bubbles. Resonances inhabiting holes and arches. Revision-born organisms that use broken physics as anatomy.

And beneath and through them all, a young drowned chamber that may itself be alive, or becoming alive, or assembling the conditions under which life and architecture and erosion will no longer be different categories.

None of them flourish in the lush sense.

They persist.

They are sparse because the water prefers intervals. They are strange because the rules are temporary. They are soft where yielding saves them, mineral where softness would be erased, and unfinished because completion attracts destruction.

They live as the place lives: by being made and unmade in the same gesture.
