What lives there is old in the way a habit is old.

Not ancient as an individual, perhaps—most bodies on that high plain are brief, frangible, eaten quickly, dried quickly, replaced before the stone has altered enough to notice—but ancient as a manner of being. The inhabitants of the plateau are creatures of permission and restraint. They do not belong to abundance as forest animals do, nor to stark deprivation as true desert things might. They live in the thin interval between dryness and damp, exposure and concealment, warmth and the memory of cold. They are low-built, patient, economical, secretive. They do not so much occupy the land as fit into it.

Many of them are colored like the ground: tawny, ash-brown, old yellow, basalt gray, salt-pale, the reddish dun of rubbed soil. Even the bright ones are bright only when seen closely—the lacquer-black of an insect’s shell, the green varnish of algae after rain, the blue flash at a lizard’s throat, the wet amber of an eye under a stone. From a distance almost everything disappears. The plateau teaches vanishing as a first law.

There are the grazers, the most visible of the larger lives: compact, high-shouldered animals with heavy necks and narrow faces made for pulling nourishment from poor growth. Their legs are short and certain. Their hooves are hard, splayed slightly for stone, worn smooth on the old paths. They have no extravagance of horn or mane; if they bear horns at all, they are swept close to the skull, ridged and dulled by rubbing against rock. Their hides carry the plateau’s whole vocabulary of color—dust, smoke, dried blood, ochre clay, rain-darkened stone. Calves are born already silent, already the color of shadow under a shrub.

They move as if listening through their feet. A herd does not surge unless death is near. It loosens and gathers, loosens and gathers, each animal cropping, pausing, lifting its blunt head to test the air. Their trails are older than any living memory, shallow braided lines worn across stone and soil, sometimes splitting around termite towers or lichen rings or the fallen slabs that no hoof will cross. They know the swales where the grass fattens after rain. They know which hollows hold dampness longest. They know the mineral crusts worth licking, the clay that steadies the stomach, the basins where predators wait.

Alongside them live smaller attendants. Thin birds step between their hooves, picking ticks and dust-colored flies from their legs. Muzzle-shy rodents follow the herd’s passage to gather shattered seed heads and insects startled from grass. Dark beetles arrive at dung almost before the steam leaves it, rolling, cutting, burying, making of each dropping a neighborhood, a nursery, a contested estate. The grazers carry worlds in their fur and bellies: mites, seeds, larvae, gut flora descended from lineages that may have known the plateau when it still had sharper bones.

There are predators, but they are not theatrical rulers. The open land does not favor dramatic ambush. Its hunters are measured and low.

Some are long-bodied, stone-backed creatures that move through cracks and grass like spilled shadow. They have wide paws, flexible spines, and faces narrow enough to enter burrows. By day they sleep in the old cool beneath flat rocks or in abandoned termite galleries. At dusk they come out and travel with their noses almost touching the ground, reading the day’s inscriptions: lizard track, beetle acid, fledgling feather, frog-skin damp, blood no larger than a seed. They kill quickly and carry what they can below.

Others hunt from stillness. Blunt-headed reptiles lie along warm stone seams, indistinguishable from the mineral ribs until something crosses the line of their mouths. Their eyes are polished and lidless. Their patience is not thought but climate made flesh. They can wait through heat, through cloud, through the passing of a herd, through the ticking rain. A careless bird, a young grazer’s fetlock, a winged night-thing fallen to ground—these are enough.

Overhead, scavengers are more common than killers. Broad-winged birds turn in the lifted warmth, gray and brown against a pale sky. They are not impatient. Hunger can circle. Their necks are bare or sparsely feathered; their eyes are exact. They know the look of a herd that has left something behind. They know which pools trap the weak in drying mud. When they descend, they do so with rough dignity, folding their wings like old cloaks. On the ground they quarrel in hoarse whispers and clicks, not wasting the voice on grandeur.

The birds of the plateau are mostly small and practical. Branches are scarce, so they have learned stone.

Some nest in crevices, lining them with hair, grass fiber, beetle wing, and the pale stems of dead shrubs. Some tuck eggs into the lee of rocks, relying on speckling and stillness. Their feet are strong, their bodies close-feathered against wind. They do not sing long songs. A note, a query, a warning, a small dry trill: enough. Sound travels too far here, and listeners are many.

There are prying birds with curved beaks, patient as old women with needles, levering flakes of lichen to find the sleeping softness underneath. There are ground walkers with cryptic backs and pale bellies, freezing into pebbles when shadow passes. There are little parasite-pickers that attend the grazers almost ceremonially, landing on backs and shoulders, stepping carefully through the hide’s ridges, vanishing beneath lifted ears. Some birds time their breeding not to season as such, but to the first decisive rains. A pool appears, gnats hatch, frogs call once, and suddenly there are eggs.

The insects are the true population.

They live in such number and intricacy that the plateau, seen properly, is not empty stone with insects upon it but an insect nation thinly covered by rock. Ants own the ridges. They have roads too small to notice until the light catches them: braided black lines over tawny soil, disappearing under pebbles, emerging around salt pans, forking toward seeds, carrion, fungus, moisture. They excavate below the old crust. They farm, raid, tend, dismantle. A dead lizard becomes a moving velvet of bodies, then a clean arrangement of bones.

The termite-like builders are stranger and more architectural. Their towers rise along cracks where damp breath comes from below. Some are no taller than a fist, others as high as a crouching animal, fluted, hardened, leaning all together as if acknowledging a pressure no other creature feels. Inside are galleries of controlled air, nurseries, fungus chambers, royal hollows, walls repaired grain by grain from clay and saliva. Their lives are nearly invisible except when winged forms emerge after rain and the air briefly fills with fragile bodies seeking new cracks in the world.

Beetles travel like thoughts repeated over centuries. Long-legged ones cross the stone in deliberate lines, lifting themselves high from the heat. Armored ones wedge under dung and dead stems. Jewel-dark ones appear after rain, polished with dew, then disappear before the surface dries. Some harvest lichen. Some drink condensation from their own backs. Some bury themselves upright in the dust and wait with only their antennae exposed, tasting tremors and humidity.

There are moths that sleep beneath shrubs, wing-veins aligned with dry leaves. There are flies that rise in columns over damp hollows and vanish by noon. There are mantis-like hunters made of straw and angles. There are wasps that know the exact burrow of a spider under stone. There are tiny parasitic things whose whole lives unfold in an egg, a larva, a drop of blood, a single hidden chamber under a seed husk.

The reptiles belong beautifully to the place. Small lizards are everywhere once the eye learns them. They are flat, fast, and patterned with impossible precision: mica fleck, basalt seam, salt crust, lichen stain. They bask wide against stone, ribcages fluttering, toes spread, eyes half-lidded in the warmth. Then they are gone—the movement less like running than ignition.

Some have translucent throats they flash only for an instant. Some carry blunt tails heavy with stored fat. Some live almost entirely among the termite towers, stealing workers and eggs. Others specialize in the wet pockets, appearing after rain to gorge on gnats, then retreating to fissures where their skin dulls and their hearts slow.

Snakes, if they can be called snakes, are often short and thick, made for entering the layered ground. Their scales hold dust in the seams. They hunt blind worms, nestlings, frogs, the pink young of burrowers. A few longer ones travel at night, warm from the stones beneath them, leaving cursive marks in fine soil that the morning wind edits but does not erase.

In the damp hollows, the plateau remembers another world.

There live the brief-water creatures: frogs no bigger than fingernails, their backs pebble-brown, their bellies pale and soft as fungus. They sleep sealed in cracks or mud, wrapped in themselves, waiting for the chemical announcement of rain. Then they emerge by the dozen, by the hundred in good years, each male knocking once or twice into the wet dark, each call small and wooden and urgent. They mate in puddles no wider than a footprint. Their eggs become commas, then flickers, then tiny limbed things in a race against evaporation. Many fail. Enough do not.

Transparent worms thread the mud. Minute crustaceans bloom from dust as if invented by water on the spot. Flat armored things—descendants of an older arrangement of life—stir under silt, unfold their plated bodies, and begin at once to eat, mate, molt, and lay the future back into drying earth. They are less like animals than recurring events. Their eggs can endure heat, thirst, trampling, years of nothing. When water returns, so do they, intact in pattern if not in body, an ancient answer repeated.

Dragonflies come to the pools as if they have been waiting just beyond visibility. Their larvae are monsters at the scale of the basin, hinged jaws opening beneath the water’s brown mirror. The adults patrol with jeweled severity, claiming a courtyard of air above a puddle that will be gone in days. They gleam briefly in the mild light, lay eggs into vanishing water, and trust the old gamble.

There are mammals smaller than hands, though they are seldom seen. They live under the low shrubs, in abandoned burrows, in the soft accumulations around stone rings. Some are seed-eaters with cheek pouches and long hind feet, emerging at night to glean the day’s fallen abundance. Some are insectivores, sharp-snouted and tremulous, pushing through rootlets and dung crusts for larvae. Their fur is fine and dust-colored, their ears thin, their eyes black and wet. They cache seeds in chambers that also feed beetles, fungi, ants, and the roots of plants. Nothing stored here belongs to one creature for long.

And at dusk, from the cracks, come the pale winged animals.

They are common, yet never ordinary. Not quite bats, not quite gliders, not quite anything easily named. Their bodies are small, furred in gray-white or leaf-brown, with membranes stretched from elongated fingers or ribs or some stranger support. Their faces are folded and seedlike, with dark nostrils and petal-thin ears. They rise without fuss from fissures that looked too narrow to hold a breath. One by one they loosen themselves into the warm evening and drift low over the ground, silent as shed bark.

They feed on gnats above damp hollows, on moths under shrubs, on the delicate winged termites that emerge after rain. Their flight is not frantic; it is a soft browsing of the air. Nothing fears them much because they are too small for terror and too familiar for omen. Yet in their faces there is something unfinished, as if the plateau made them in a season of experiment and then, finding them sufficient, declined to refine them further.

The plants are inhabitants too, though they live at the edge of refusal. They are not generous. They are leathery, wiry, padded, thornless in some places and thorned in others, resinous, mineral-tasting, close to the ground. Their roots go sideways more often than down, netting the hidden damp beneath stones. Some drink dew through hairs. Some seal themselves during long dryness and return from apparent death with a few hours of wet. Lichens are among the plateau’s elders: crusts, rings, maps, stains, green-gray scabs that eat light and stone at a pace almost indistinguishable from geology. Some form circles so precise they seem intentional, though perhaps intention at that timescale looks exactly like growth.

The burrowers shape everything below notice. They loosen soil around roots. They hide from heat in tunnels inherited from other species. They make chambers where moisture lingers, where eggs survive, where blind white things feed on fungus and hair and shed skin. Many creatures use the burrows without having dug them. A lizard sleeps in a rodent’s side passage. A frog seals itself in a beetle chamber. A snake follows both. A plant root enters after them all.

What lives there, then, is not a single creature but a civilization of small survivals.

It is a place of bodies made humble by exposure. A place where intelligence often looks like stillness. Where abundance hides in pores, under plates, inside eggs, in the bellies of grazers, in the memory of mud. Where a rainstorm is not weather but a summons. Where death is rapid, useful, and thoroughly attended. Where nothing grows tall except stone, and even stone has been lowered by time.

If the plateau itself has a resident spirit, it would not be a giant or a god. It would be something low to the ground and difficult to distinguish from its surroundings: many-eyed, many-legged, furred in places, scaled in others, carrying seeds in one cheek and silt under its armor, with lichen on its back and beetles in its shadow. It would move very little. It would wait without impatience. It would wake fully only when the rain darkened the old rock.

And when the water dried, it would fold itself away again—not gone, not sleeping exactly, but held in readiness beneath the final face of stone.
