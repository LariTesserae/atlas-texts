What lives there is not one species, not one people, not even quite a community in the tidy sense. It is an undercity of damp-adapted life: a whole citizenry of things that do not consider themselves citizens, arranged by seep, shadow, hunger, shelter, nesting season, and the old downward habit of water.

The inhabitants are mostly small.

They are the bead-eyed, whiskered, soft-footed ones who know the passages under the passages: water voles, shrews, mice with dark wet fur, little burrowing mammals whose bodies are built for tunnels no hand ever dug. They live in the warmest part of the cold, which is to say inside matted roots, under old sedge platforms, in hollows of dead trunks where fungus has softened the grain but not yet collapsed it. Their nests are lined with shredded grass, seed fluff, feathers, and pale peeled bark. They keep chambers above the highest ordinary seep-line, but they also have escape runs climbing into root walls, because rain changes the map. A tunnel that is home in the morning may be a drain by night.

They move through the basin almost invisibly. A trembling of moss. A reed twitching though there is no wind. Two black seeds of eyes at the lip of a burrow. Their teeth worry at sedge stems, roots, fallen seeds, insect cases, fungal flesh. Their paws leave tiny paired punctuation marks in the mud, later rimmed by frost and then blurred by drip. They are numerous enough to feed many hungers and cautious enough that the city never seems overrun by them. They are everywhere, and yet the place keeps their secret.

There are the reed-dwellers, too: frogs slow as thoughts in cold water, newts tucked under stones, salamanders patterned like wet bark, their skin drinking the basin directly. They live where the seep-pools hold a little warmth and where the mud is deep enough to hide in. In winter-cold hours they become almost mineral, pressed under leaves, barely breathing. In gentler stretches they climb among moss and root hair, taking gnats, mites, soft beetles, larvae, the small meat the wet city continually produces. Their voices are not the loud evening choirs of warm marshes. A frog here calls once, low and wooden, as if testing whether sound can survive the air. Another answers from under a ledge. Then silence folds over them again.

In the blacker channels live fish, pale below and dark above, quick as torn shadows. Some are ordinary stream fish that descended through cut-open underground water and remained. Others have become basin-shaped over generations: narrow-bodied, large-finned, comfortable in dimness, alert to vibration. They nose among submerged roots, nibble larvae from stone, vanish under peat lips when herons pass overhead. In the deeper flooded vaults beneath the central mound-city are smaller, paler things: blind amphipods, threadlike worms, translucent crustaceans, the inhabitants of gravel, silt, and hidden cold water. They are the basement population, older in habit than the basin itself, revealed and given new rooms when the ceiling fell.

The insects are the most populous residents, though they are less seen than felt as texture.

Midges rise in faint smoke where light shafts touch standing water. Mosquitoes persist but slowly, their bodies chilled into deliberation. Caddisfly larvae drag their little cases through clear runs, wearing architecture as clothing: grains of sand, splinters of reed, crumbs of shell. Stonefly and mayfly nymphs flatten themselves under clean current. Beetles walk on water skin, dive through it, bore in soaked wood, tick in hollow stems, polish dead leaves down into lace. Their grubs occupy rot, mud, fungus, dung, carcass, seed pod, root pith. The city is full of nurseries so small they appear to be stains.

Snails live here in great patience. Some have amber shells, some gray, some almost transparent, as if made from condensed mist. They graze algae from stone lips and leave shining script over roots. Slugs, dark and yellow and mottled, come out when the air is thickest. They cross fungal plates, wet bark, old feathers, the undersides of leaves. They are eaten often and replaced often. Their eggs sit in clusters like tiny pearls beneath rotting wood and in pockets of moss.

Spiders are among the basin’s quiet engineers. They take the already woven place and weave it finer. In sheltered root alleys they cast small nets between fern stems. Under overhangs they string traps that catch mist until each thread beads with water, making their invisible work briefly luminous. Some are long-legged and patient, clinging to ceilings of root and mineral crust. Some hunt freely on the moss surface, colored like peat and lichen. They know where gnats rise, where beetles climb, where the air moves most gently. Their webs are maps of narrow appetite.

Birds occupy the vertical city.

Swifts and swallows use the rifts and broken shafts, entering from the upper world and vanishing into pockets of the underworld with hardly a wingbeat wasted. Their nests cling under ledges in rows and clotted colonies, built of mud, moss fibers, spit, and hair stolen from deer rubbing against bark. They hunt the bright columns of air where insects gather. When they turn, the whole shaft seems to flicker.

Bats sleep deeper in cracks that daylight barely finds. They hang in old ceiling wounds and limestone pockets, wrapped in themselves like damp black leaves. At dusk, when the gray light flattens and the white-throated birds quiet, the bats begin to write over the basin in quick broken lines. They take midges above the pools, moths from the reed tops, beetles clumsily rising from rotted trunks. Their bodies make almost no sound, but their presence changes the air. The undercity at evening belongs partly to them.

Water birds claim the ledges, pools, reed margins, and mud fans. Small white-throated birds nest in shelves of stone and root, leaving bright droppings that feed moss and algae below. Herons stand in the reed quarter, so still that sedge grows around their ankles in the imagination. They are tall, cold-eyed, nearly architectural themselves: living pilings with spears folded into their faces. They strike fish, frogs, voles incautious at the waterline. Rails and crakes, secretive and narrow-bodied, thread the reed mazes. Their calls are brief, cracked, and quickly swallowed.

Owls occupy the higher dark. They are not many, but the place makes them powerful. A small owl can command a whole terrace of mouse-runs. A larger one may sit in a broken cliff hollow with water dripping behind it, its facial disk pale as fungus in the gloom. They hunt by listening into the muffled world, sorting the difference between drip, beetle, vole, settling root, and the careless scratch of a living foot. When one drops, it is less a flight than an interruption.

There are mammals large enough to be glimpsed rather than noticed by their tracks alone.

Deer come down from above, especially in early light. They are not creatures of the basin alone; they are visitors who have learned its ramps, its edible shelves, its safe intervals. Their coats grow thick and dull in the damp cold, guard hairs beaded with water. Their legs seem too delicate for the root-streets until they move, and then they reveal exact knowledge. They step where peat will bear, where stone will not slide, where root bridges are sound. They browse willow shoots, pale grasses, fern edges, mineral-salted leaves. Sometimes one stands behind a curtain of hanging roots, only an eye and a rib of antler visible. Their presence gives scale to the city: suddenly the root towers are towers, the reed quarter a district, the central mound a crowded hill.

Otters, or something close to otters, are among the basin’s livelier inhabitants. They use the channels as roads and the roads as channels when rain allows. Their bodies are sleek, dark, and blunt-muzzled, built for water that would numb a less insulated animal. They leave wet slides over low banks, prints like opened flowers in mud, heaps of cracked shells and fish bones under protected ledges. They den in bank hollows reinforced by roots, with underwater entries and dry inner chambers lined with reed. They are playful only in the sense that quick intelligence looks like play: rolling, diving, testing currents, vanishing under a mat and appearing where no opening seemed to be.

Foxlike predators patrol the upper terraces. They are thick-furred, narrow-faced, broad-eared despite the cold, perhaps because sound matters more than distance here. Their paws are soft and splayed enough for peat. Their tails stay low to avoid dripping branches. They know the root parapets, the squirrel runs, the nests built too near access holes. Their hunting is not dramatic. They wait beside vole paths. They nose under moss roofs. They pause for whole minutes at the entrance to a burrow while water gathers on their whiskers and falls. Their color depends on the district: rust where the iron bands stain the stone, gray-brown among old trunks, almost black when wet.

Martens and weasels live in the mid-level labyrinth, the part of the city between bird height and flooded tunnel. They slip through hollow logs, root halls, squirrel galleries, abandoned nests. Their bodies are shaped like decisions already made. They hunt eggs, sleeping birds, mice, frogs, insects, anything warm enough to betray itself. One may appear in a door-shaped opening beneath braided trunks, look out with a bright triangular face, and disappear upward inside the wall.

Squirrels, if present, are not the loud scolding kind of open woods. They are muted, moss-colored, damp-tailed, moving along upper root bridges and cliff trees. They store seeds and fungus in cavities where the air is merely cool rather than wet. Their forgotten caches become saplings when light changes. They are among the unintentional builders of future neighborhoods.

Beaverlike animals are among the closest things the place has to civil engineers, though they have no city in mind. They thicken channels with damwork, lodge walls, chewed stems, packed mud, and stones small enough to shove. Their work slows water, raises pools, drowns paths, creates reed beds, redirects fish, floods burrows, saves other burrows, and begins whole new districts. They are dense-bodied, cold-water animals with orange or dark teeth and paws that remember mud. Their lodges are not isolated huts but absorbed into the greater architecture: one wall a root mass, one wall a mineralized old dam, the roof sprouting sedge, the entry hidden under black water.

There are also the inhabitants that hardly move yet dominate everything.

The plants are not scenery here. They are the greater population, the long-lived majority, the ones who hold territory by persistence rather than patrol. Mosses occupy every moist claim. They make roofs, carpets, bandages, filters, nurseries. They soften sharp stone and conceal danger. They keep footprints. They drink mist. In places they pile on themselves until they become land, and then other things trust them and fall through.

Ferns crowd the walls and balconies: small spleenworts, tongue ferns, maidenhair-like fronds where spray is constant, tougher shield ferns on colder shelves. Their fiddleheads emerge like green fists from cracks no larger than a knuckle. Sedges and rushes stand in the low districts, their roots binding mud into temporary certainty. Dwarf willow, alder, and other water-loving shrubs lace the channels. Their roots descend into moving water and come back holding silt, bones, leaves, larvae, the beginnings of embankment.

The trees are stranger because they are partly of the upper world and partly of this one. Some grow sideways from basin walls, their crowns leaning toward rifts of sky while their roots pour downward like frozen waterfalls. Some have braided together over generations, trunks touching, rubbing, grafting, thickening into walls with living grain. Some are dead but still inhabited from root to crown: fungus shelves on their sides, beetle galleries within, vole corridors under loosened bark, bird nests in forks, water threading their hollow hearts. A tree does not cease to be a resident when it dies. It changes profession. It becomes housing, bridge, food, sponge, archive.

Fungi are the quiet clerks of the undercity, recording and converting everything. White mycelium stitches soaked wood to soil. Orange cups open in leaf litter like small listening mouths. Gray caps line old root beams. Brackets stack on trunks in damp stairways. Pale fungal plates armor animal mounds and old burrow-cities. Some passageways hold because fungal threads have bound rot into a flexible wall. Some chambers smell of mushroom, wet bark, and animal sleep. The fungi do not rule the basin, but without them much of it would fall too quickly or remain too hard to enter.

Then there are the mineral lives, if life can be stretched to include the slow behavior of stone in water.

Calcite teeth grow under ledges. Limestone crusts preserve the ghosts of stems. Iron seeps stain roots red-brown. Clay slumps and seals and opens. Shale flakes into thin shelters for eggs and larvae. These are not alive, but the living things treat them as participants. A mineral curtain becomes a nesting shade. A crusted reed bed becomes a terrace. A young stalactite changes the drip pattern below it; moss answers; gnats gather; spiders follow. In this place, even stone behaves socially because water introduces everything to everything else.

No single creature owns the basin. Ownership here would be absurd. The basin belongs temporarily to whoever can use a surface before it changes.

A ledge may belong in the morning to swallows, at noon to basking insects, in rain to runoff, in evening to bats, in winter to ice, in spring to roots. A hollow log is a beetle nursery, then a vole corridor, then a fox ambush site, then a fungal chamber, then a collapsed bridge, then a dam. A pool is a sky mirror, a larval cradle, a fish road, an otter pantry, a drowning hazard, a sediment trap, a future meadow. Every address has many occupants, most of them unaware of one another except as scent, vibration, obstruction, food, warmth, or threat.

The closest thing to a people of the place is this layered behavior: the shared discipline of dampness.

They all know, in their own forms of knowing, that height matters. Eggs go under lips but above flood marks. Nests are tucked where drip misses by a feather’s width. Burrows climb. Food is cached in places that breathe but do not soak. Sleep happens in chambers with more than one exit. Paths are chosen not by shortest distance but by texture: root over mud, moss over sharp stone, reed tunnel over exposed bank, current over stagnant pool, stillness over display.

They all know that sound carries poorly but nearness carries intensely. A vole can ignore a distant rockfall but freeze at the touch of a shadow. A heron can stand through dripping and mist but strike at the smallest ring in water. A fox hears the private rasp of claws inside a wall of roots. A bat reads the air between fern and stone. A spider learns the difference between mist weight and wing impact. The whole population lives in close quarters without becoming noisy because noise here is wasteful. The basin favors touch, scent, tremor, waterline, and memory.

They all know that the city is temporary, though none would phrase it that way. Their generations are built around revisions. Flood comes: climb. Frost comes: seal. Stone falls: avoid, then investigate, then colonize. A dam rises: nest higher. A pool appears: spawn, hunt, drink, drown, feed. A tree falls: cross it, enter it, hollow it, root through it. Disaster is not exceptional here. It is one of the forms of construction.

So what lives there?

A cold, wet multitude.

Moss lives there like a skin with memory.  
Roots live there like roads and ribs.  
Fungi live there like slow white weather inside wood.  
Larvae live there in every cup of water.  
Snails write there.  
Spiders map there.  
Fish flicker through drowned corridors.  
Frogs wait under leaf-dark glass.  
Birds stitch the vertical light.  
Bats inherit the dusk.  
Voles and shrews keep the hidden floor moving.  
Otters use the canals with sleek entitlement.  
Deer descend like visitors from a brighter country.  
Foxes and owls measure the whole settlement in hunger and silence.  
Beaverlike builders alter the drainage and accidentally found neighborhoods.

And beneath all of them, through all of them, around all of them, water lives there most of all: not as a creature, but as the common law. It decides the streets, edits the walls, opens rooms, closes tunnels, carries seed and scent and cold. Every resident is, in some way, an answer to it.
