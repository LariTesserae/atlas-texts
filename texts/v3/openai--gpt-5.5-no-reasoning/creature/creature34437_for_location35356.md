What lives there is not an animal, and not one thing.

It is a drowned husbandry: a whole way of tending that has survived the tenders.

The inhabitants are the groves, the roof-gardens, the moss-pelts, the bladder-plants, the root-cords, the pale crops inside clay vessels, the cold grasses combing the terraces, and the long listening structures they have made of the ruins. They live without eyes, without hunger as animals know hunger, without pursuit or fear. They occupy the basin by pressure, by touch, by leaning, by division, by memory. Their bodies are slow enough that a generation might look like weather. Their conversations take place through current and strain.

They are plants, but not merely plants. They are what plants become in a world where no mouth ever comes to crop them, where no hoof bruises the bed, where no wing carries pollen, where no sun commands leaf and flower upward. They have had to invent other loyalties.

Some live as banners.

These are the kelp-trees rooted in the old orchards, dark flexible columns rising where dry trunks once stood or where their idea remains. They do not grow toward light so much as toward motion. Their crowns are long, translucent, storm-colored leaves, blue-green and black-green, silver-veined, turning every current into a visible thought. In stillness they hang like drowned hair from a giant head. In storms they flatten all one way, obedient and immense, then slowly recover, not springing back but remembering their former shape.

They are probably the oldest visible beings in the basin. Their lower trunks are almost stone, wrapped in generations of their own discarded sheaths. Around their roots lie circles of softened terrace rock, eaten and gripped and gently changed. They seem solitary at a distance, but underground — or under-silt — they are joined. Their roots pass messages by tightening, loosening, tasting minerals, altering the flow of cold through mud. An orchard here is not a collection of trees. It is a single distributed animal that never became an animal.

Some live as hands.

These are the pale plants in the longhouse vessels, the small curled stems that spill over clay rims like fingers feeling for the edge of a table. They are tender-looking, almost domestic. They grow in rows because someone placed their first ancestors in rows, and they have kept the arrangement with a fidelity more ritual than botanical. Their roots fill the vessels, wind around the ballast stones, press into thumb marks in the fired clay. When the valley drones, their stems tremble before the rest of the room does, as if they hear through the pot.

They do not flower in any usual sense. Instead, at certain tones, their curled tips uncup and release a faint meal of spores, so fine it looks like milk breathed into water. The spores do not scatter randomly. They drift toward other vessels, toward roof-beams, toward hearthstones. Many die on surfaces they refuse to inhabit. A few settle in prepared silt. The row continues.

If anything here can be called cultivated, it is them. Yet the cultivator may now be the house itself: the shape of the walls, the direction of currents through the doorway, the drone of suspended rings, the black hearth that still gathers a slightly different cold. The longhouse tends its garden by being shaped the way it is.

Some live as lanterns.

The bladder-plants cling to beams, posts, lintels, the undersides of fallen carts. Each is a cluster of swollen sacs, translucent and pearly, holding not air but brightness: a dim internal suspension that waxes when pressure drops and fades when the deep cold season hardens the water. They do not illuminate so much as mark themselves. In darkness they appear as small, patient organs.

They keep upright by buoyancy. A broken beam thick with them may try to rise for decades, checked by roots and stone. Whole roofs are held in strange tensions by them: wood remembering verticality, plants remembering ascent, water insisting downward and around.

They pulse when touched by certain currents. Not all at once. A tremor of light can pass across a ruin, bladder to bladder, as though a message has moved over the roof and down a wall. Whether this is communication or only reaction hardly matters. Here, reaction repeated for centuries has become a language.

Some live as fur.

The mosses cover nearly everything that has stopped resisting. They are thick, drowned pelts, gray-green, white-green, sometimes black as wet wool. They make walls soft and stones ambiguous. They blunt carved marks but do not erase them; rather, they preserve the pressure-difference of carving beneath themselves, growing thinner in grooves and thicker on raised edges. They are archivists by accident or instinct.

Under still conditions, faint constellations show beneath them on certain stones. The lights are not produced by the moss alone, nor by the stone alone, but by the two in contact: mineral, cold, memory, and living film completing a circuit. The moss draws something from old masonry that ordinary soil could never have offered. It knows placed stone from fallen stone. It grows differently on lintels than on field walls, differently on shrines than on cottages, differently on the smooth empty square where it will not grow at all.

Where it refuses, one feels the presence of an older rule.

Some live as nerves.

The roots are the most active inhabitants, though most of their activity cannot be seen at once. They cross thresholds and descend wells. They enter handles, baskets, wall gaps, sockets where pegs have loosened. They bind collapsed carts to the earth as if preventing them from drifting away into forgetfulness. They braid around fence posts and read the carved marks there, not as writing but as changes in resistance, mineral concentration, old human pressure.

They avoid some thresholds. Not because of poison. Not because of lack of silt. Because the interior beyond has a shape they will not enter.

There are houses the vines refuse.

There are rooms kept clear by no visible guardian.

In such places the silt lies fine and even, and sound changes when it crosses the doorway. The roots gather outside, thick as waiting fingers, and turn away.

The root systems are probably what passes here for society. A grove leans toward a shrine because its roots have found older nourishment there: copper salts, bone ash, hand-oils fixed in clay, the faint warmth of thumb marks, the acoustics of stacked slate. Root mats thicken around standing stones because those stones hum, and the hum alters growth. A plant here may have no brain, but the basin itself supplies pattern. Current is nerve. Pressure is weather and command. Sound is pollination, warning, calendar.

There are also the sleepers.

These are not animals either, though one might mistake them for corpses if one were determined to find bodies. In the silt below terraces, inside collapsed storage pits, beneath the smooth square where nothing grows, lie dense mats of dormant crowns and seeds, black nodules no larger than teeth, pale coiled rhizomes, folded leaf-embryos sealed in mineral skins. They wait through seasons that may last many human years. They open only when a particular convergence arrives: colder upper water, a certain undertone from the ridge slabs, darkness collected in cup-stones and released as sinking dust.

Then a meadow may begin somewhere it has not grown for centuries.

Not sprout suddenly. Nothing here is sudden except snapping wood and released gas. The meadow appears first as a paleness under silt. Then threads. Then a nap of grass. Then, after years, a ribbon-field combed by current, flowing one way for hours, reversing all at once when the ridge water descends.

These migrations are the nearest thing to herds the place possesses.

There may once have been people here. There may not. But something like them remains distributed through the habits of the living basin.

The eyeless masks in the doorways are not inhabited in a simple ghostly way. No faces look through them. Yet the plants treat them as significant. A doorway with a smooth mask may remain clear beneath it, vines stopping just short of the hanging wood. A mask with a mouth-groove may accumulate pale threads along the groove until it seems to be whispering rootlets. When currents move through, the masks knock softly against lintels, and nearby bladder-plants dim.

Perhaps the last people made masks without eyes because eyes had become useless. Perhaps they learned to live as the basin now lives: by pressure, echo, touch. Perhaps, before vanishing, they selected plants that could hear bells and obey cold. Perhaps they taught roots to hold walls upright, moss to seal roofs, bladder-plants to glow in depth, vessel-crops to seed by tone.

Or perhaps the plants taught them.

Either way, the distinction has thinned. The human-made world has become a trellis for a nonhuman continuity. Walls, posts, cairns, benches, bowls, bells, and masks are now organs in the larger life of the place. A shrine is not a dead building covered in growth. It is a resonating skeleton used by moss and vine. A fence post is not a boundary marker. It is a rooted instrument. A longhouse is not empty. It is a rib cage full of cultivated pale hands.

The deepest inhabitant is harder to name.

Beyond the ridge, where marker stones descend into ink and the sound becomes a single vast undertone, something continues the logic of the basin into darkness. It may be only water moving through a chasm. It may be a forest too deep and lightless to resemble vegetation. It may be the old road’s destination: a pressure-field, a root-sea, a submerged gate made not of doors but of gradients.

The basin listens to it.

The standing slabs on the high ridge hum in sympathy. The groves lean, minutely, over generations, toward the drop. Spores released in the turbid season drift that way if no cross-current interrupts them. The carved posts seem to mark not property but approach: a road into more depth, each stone smaller, each interval longer, until measurement fails.

If the basin has a mind, it is not centered in the longhouse or the square or the orchard. It is strung between the ridge stones and that undertone below. It thinks by delay. It remembers by growth. It answers disturbance by alteration of root and leaf.

It has no eyes because it does not need to separate itself from what it perceives.

It has no voice because the whole valley is already sounding.

The lives here are therefore vegetal, architectural, and acoustic at once. They do not move from place to place as creatures do, but they change the meaning of place. They make a roof into a hanging garden, a hearth into a cold altar, a fence into a scale of depth, a road into a current-channel, a ruin into a body. They bloom for pressure. They seed by tone. They recoil from forbidden thresholds. They gather around hand-shaped things as if hands were a kind of mineral.

They are lush without abundance in the animal sense. Nothing feeds on them; so their struggle is not against teeth but against collapse, burial, forgetting, and the wrong kind of stillness. Their predators are silt, brittle stone, pressure fractures, storms from above, warmth from below, and perhaps the empty square where no spore can take.

They are patient, but not passive.

When storms descend, they flatten and preserve themselves. When currents return, they rise. When stone shifts, roots tighten. When wood tries to stand, bladder-plants assist or restrain it. When bells drone, vessel-crops uncurl. When darkness collects in cup-stones, some buried season begins counting.

Whoever lives there is the drowned upland itself, alive through many green-black bodies.

It is an eyeless commons.

A cold, submerged garden-civilization.

A people of root, moss, bladder, frond, tone, clay, and remembered hands, inhabiting ruins so completely that ruin has become their form of shelter, their ancestry, and their language.
