What lives there is not an animal, and not quite a plant, though it wears plants the way a body wears hair.

It is the cleft itself, alive in the manner of something too young to have become separate from its surroundings. Its life is distributed through moss, seep, root, pressure, spore, fracture, and vapor. It has no face, no den, no lair hidden behind a waterfall, no watchful eyes under leaves. If it senses, it senses as wet stone senses weather: through contact, weight, tension, and the loosening of what was once fixed.

It lives by taking hold.

Everywhere it has fingers. Root-fingers in cracks. Moss-fingers laid flat over slate. Fern-fingers uncurling in the dark. Threads of fungus drawn through soaked litter and powdered rock. Water-fingers slipping down seams so narrow they look dry until they shine. None of these is the creature alone, but together they make its grasp. It grips the new mountain with thousands of small green attempts, loses purchase, grips again. A ledge falls; it grows over the wound. A slab shears away; it sends wet threads into the exposed face. Gravel pours down; it receives it, mats it, binds it, makes it disappear under fronds.

Its body is close-packed and layered. The upper hide is black leaf and wind-twisted branch, a low canopy dragged sideways by weather. Beneath that is a lung of ferns, always collapsing and inflating under gusts. Beneath that, a pelt of moss and liverwort, thick, cold, and saturated, pressed over broken stone. Deeper still are the hard organs: ribs of slate, pale bone of granite, iron seams staining red where the creature bleeds mineral water. There is no one place where it begins. Cut into stone and you find moisture. Lift moss and you find root. Break root and you find grit. Follow grit and you find the mountain still grinding itself down.

It does not move from the cleft because the cleft is its shape. But it is never still.

It heaves in weather. It tightens in cold. It loosens during rain. When wind enters from above, the whole being shudders and sets up a roar through its chambers. Its leaves thrash like cilia. Its trunks creak like strained tendons. Its wet mats slap and tremble. Its stones chatter in their sockets. It makes sound the way other creatures make heat: continuously, involuntarily, as proof that pressure is passing through it.

In storms it becomes almost mechanical. Then its hidden parts begin to work audibly. Water knocks in fissures. Drips fall with metronomic insistence into hollow stone. Gravel descends by chutes and shelves. Vapors vent upward from dark pockets. The cleft pumps, grinds, exhales, intakes. It is a factory with no maker and no product except more of itself: more moss, more loosened rock, more green occupation, more wetness carried from one surface to another.

Its metabolism is collapse and repair.

It eats weather. It eats exposed mineral. It eats the brief cleanliness of newly broken stone. Rain softens the edges; roots widen the cracks; frost and pressure pry; falling rubble creates new beds; spores settle; moss thickens; seedlings lodge; seepage undermines; another portion slumps. The creature does not resist destruction as an enemy. Destruction is one of its organs. Every fall feeds it. Every injury increases its surface. Its wounds are not closed with scars but colonized.

It has no need of animals yet. No wing, hoof, claw, or mouth has been admitted into its system. Flowers open without expectation. Seeds arrive violently, not gently, hurled by gust and lodged by accident. Spores replace bees. Water replaces traffic. Wind replaces the passing of bodies. The cleft’s life is pre-animal, or post-animal, or simply indifferent to that whole quick kingdom of hunger and pursuit. Here the drama belongs to rooted things that cannot flee and stone that cannot choose not to fall.

If it has a mind, that mind is dark, damp, and immediate. It does not remember in stories. It remembers in stains, in root scars, in tilted saplings, in moss thickness, in the angle at which gravel has come to rest. It knows recentness everywhere. Its memories are not old enough to be buried. Fresh breaks still smell of cold mineral dust. Young roots still grope through debris not yet settled. Trees lean because the ground beneath them has already changed since they began growing.

It is young enough to be crowded by its own becoming.

There is a kind of impatience in it: every available surface seized, every crack wetted, every pocket filled. It does not wait for soil to form properly. It makes do with crumbs. It grows from cups, shelves, splinters, vertical seams, rotting needles, crushed fern-stems, and mud caught behind stones. It builds gardens in places that may not survive the next rain. A shrub roots in a temporary ledge. A fern opens from moss on a wall. A seedling stands in the damp crown of another plant. Nothing here asks permanence before beginning.

The creature’s skin is cold to the touch. Glossy in places, velvet in others, blade-sharp where the green has not yet covered rock. It smells of rain driven into slate, of bruised fern, resin, wet iron, fresh breakage, and the clean sourness of young rot. Its breath is mist, rising and falling in short cycles. Its blood is seepage, not flowing in rivers but appearing everywhere in small insistences: beads, threads, stains, drops gathering under stone lips before falling into hidden basins.

It is loudest when unseen.

Because light barely enters, its presence is first known by contact and sound. Slickness underfoot. A sudden plunge into water hidden under leaves. The wet give of moss over angular stone. The rasp of grit moving somewhere nearby. The turbine moan of wind in cracks overhead. The tick and knock and hollow pulse of water traveling through the mountain’s dark interior. The creature surrounds before it reveals. It is less something looked at than something entered.

Its temperament is not hostile, but it is without accommodation. It has no paths because no creature has persuaded it to leave any space unfilled. It has no clearings because emptiness is immediately occupied. It does not attack, but it gives way underfoot. It drops stones. It hides edges. It slicks every surface. It overgrows and undermines in the same motion. To enter it is to become, briefly, another loose object in its working: something to be wetted, slowed, tripped, bruised, perhaps kept.

And yet there is tenderness of a severe kind in its growth. Moss softens knives of rock. Ferns catch falling grit. Roots lace rubble into temporary coherence. Water gathers in tiny cups as if the place were saving every possible drink. After each tearing, the green returns not grandly but intimately, fiber by fiber, film by film. Its repairs are minute, relentless, and almost affectionate toward ruin.

What lives there is a young mountain learning to have a skin.

A cold, green, industrial organism of weathering.

A basin-creature with stone bones, moss flesh, fern lungs, root nerves, and rain for pulse.

It is not waiting to be inhabited. It is already inhabited by its own making.
