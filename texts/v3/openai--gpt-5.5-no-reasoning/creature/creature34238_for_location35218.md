What lives there is not one creature first, but a pressure of creatures. Life there does not stand out from the place; it crowds out of it, sweats from it, hatches in it, wears it as hide and shell and leaf. The inhabitants are the basin learning locomotion.

At the smallest scale live the first true owners: films, spores, threads, eggs, larvae, motes with hunger. Every wet surface is grazed or farmed or pierced. Pale fungal nets run through mudstone cracks and rotting wood, glowing faintly where they breach the dark, knitting soil before roots can claim it. Algae sleep dry in curled black-green skins and wake within minutes of rain. Bacteria bloom in mineral seepages, painting stones rust, sulfur yellow, oil-blue. Pools that seem empty are packed with commas and needles and trembling glass bodies: larvae with transparent guts, rotifers turning like hidden gears, tadpoles with no pigment yet, little crustaceans rowing through warm brown water, bloodworms standing upright in silt and waving to whatever passes for breath.

Above them, insects and their kin are everywhere, not as ornaments but as weather. There are long-legged mosquitoes with banded bodies and mouthparts delicate as needles of smoke. Thick midges rise from pools in columns and are taken by bats, birds, frogs, flowers. Beetles occupy every possible profession: diving beetles in temporary water, bark beetles in softened deadwood, luminous beetles whose abdomens flash under leaves, carrion beetles that arrive at death almost before dying finishes. Termites build no permanent towers here, because the ground shifts too quickly; instead they make covered galleries along roots and branches, dismantling wood from within until trunks collapse like emptied reeds. Antlike colonies cross water on living bridges and raid nests, fungus, eggs, anything still soft enough to divide.

Some of the insects are nearly plants in habit. Stick creatures hang in thorn tangles, sprouting moss and lichen along their backs. Leaf-mimics walk with the slow sway of foliage, then fold themselves around prey. There are caterpillars that carry tiny gardens of fungus to mask their scent; mantis-like hunters that imitate orchid roots; moths with furred antennae broad enough to comb pollen from the air. At night, fat pale pollinators blunder from flower to flower, dusted yellow, red, and black, drunk on heated nectar that smells half of fruit and half of meat.

The amphibians are numerous beyond counting. This is their republic. Frogs live in pools, in flowers, in pitcher leaves, in hollows in bark, in shells abandoned by crabs, in water held between the clasped bases of leaves. Some are no larger than fingernails and call with voices like dripping water. Some are heavy-bodied and warty, sitting in root forks like knots of living mud, swallowing anything that mistakes them for stillness. There are tree frogs with transparent bellies through which eggs and organs faintly show; reed frogs that change color with the wetness of the air; burrowing frogs that emerge only when pressure drops, their blunt heads already slick with soil. Armored amphibians push through the muck like small, deliberate tanks, plates ridged with algae, tiny orchids, mites, and fungal pins. They do not seem to notice their passengers except when birds come to harvest them.

The reptiles are quick and abundant, warmed by a sun they seldom see directly. Narrow-headed lizards flick through the understory in bright, broken patterns, appearing and vanishing like thrown strips of leaf-shadow. Some run across pool surfaces for two or three miraculous strides before sinking into roots. Others are vertical citizens, sleeping head-down on trunks in clusters, bellies distended from beetles and frogs. Snakes are everywhere but rarely fully seen: green vine snakes looped among lianas, thick water snakes under duckweed, little blind burrowers nosing through soft clay, patterned constrictors whose markings resemble fungus shelves and wet bark. A few glide between trees, flattening their bodies into ribbons and falling sideways through the humid dark.

There are larger reptiles too, though the land does not yet favor giants. In the blacker pools wait broad-mouthed ambush animals, low and armored, mud over their eyes, nostrils just breaking the surface film. They move seldom, but when they do the whole pool changes shape. Along fresh banks smaller crocodilian things drag themselves from one temporary channel to another before the water leaves them behind. They know the basin as a sequence of smells and vibrations rather than places.

The crab-things may be crustaceans, or may only have learned the crab solution because it works. They come armored in blues, greens, bruised purples, clay reds. During dry intervals they vanish into dens plugged with mud and leaves. When rain returns they erupt by the hundreds, scraping mineral crusts, seizing larvae, clipping seedlings, battling in shallow rivulets with raised claws. Some carry mats of moss on their backs. Some cultivate threads of edible fungus in their burrow walls. Their young pass through puddles in cloudy swarms, eaten by nearly everything and still too many.

Birds fill the middle dark. They are not graceful in the open-sky sense; this is not a place for long wings and clean flight. They are short-winged, explosive, loud, built for bursting between trunks. Nut-crackers with blunt stone-colored beaks hammer green pods against branches until pulp rains down for rodents and beetles below. Reedbirds nest in sagging platforms over water and scream at anything with eyes. Glossy black flocking birds move like spilled ink from one fruiting tree to another. Pale nightjars lie lengthwise on branches, invisible until their eyes open. Tiny nectar birds hover before fleshy flowers and leave with faces powdered in luminous pollen. There are herons adapted to darkness, crouched low in root pools, stabbing at frogs by the sound of their calls.

Bats are among the true powers of the canopy. Fruit bats hang in quarreling curtains, wings wrapped around red-brown bodies, jaws working constantly. At dusk they pour out through canopy wounds and follow scent trails to flowering trees, to fermenting fruit, to heat-breathing blossoms. Smaller bats hunt in the insect roar, their ears huge, their faces folded and leafed. Some roost inside hollow trunks softened by fungus. Some carry seeds glued to their fur by sticky fruits that seem designed less to feed them than to ride them. When they return, they decorate their roosts with droppings, seeds, parasites, mites, and the beginnings of gardens.

Mammals exist here in many small, clever forms, but few seem settled into anything final. Bands of wet-nosed, hot-blooded scavengers flow along root buttresses, too restless to count. They have gripping paws, flexible backs, shining eyes, and social tempers: chittering, grooming, stealing, warning, fighting, reconciling. They turn leaves, raid nests, crack shellfruit against favored stones, eat beetles, eggs, fungi, fallen flowers, carrion, one another’s young if hunger presses. Some have prehensile tails; some have cheek pouches; some carry infants clamped to their bellies while they climb. They are intelligent in a bodily, opportunistic way, remembering which vines hold, which pools bite, which flowers drug, which stones split pods cleanly.

There are gliders with long tails and skin stretched between wrist and ankle. They launch from trunks under the low canopy, not flying but passing through the dark in controlled falls, their membranes briefly visible as blacker shapes against greenish light. They feed on fruit, sap, insects, bird eggs, and warm flower nectar. When alarmed they freeze flat to bark, and fungus-colored fur makes them nearly part of the trunk.

There are heavier browsers too, though the ground gives them little peace. Low, piglike animals with long snouts root through mud and fern litter, followed by birds and biting flies. Small deerlike creatures with splayed toes step through soft ground, browsing on new shoots, always listening. Their young are hidden in root hollows and often found by snakes. Something like a tapir, but lower and more flexible, pushes tunnels through reed walls and leaves polished mud where its flanks pass. These animals do not make roads, exactly, but their repeated panic and hunger create the temporary grammar of passage.

The predators are not rare, because prey is not rare. Predation here is ordinary weather.

In the pools, mouths wait. In the reeds, long-bodied hunters slide low, whiskered or scaled, taking frogs and nestlings. In the canopy, forward-eyed mammals move with terrible softness, their paws broad and silent, their tails balancing them as they stalk roosting birds or sleeping gliders. There are cats, perhaps, or things near cats: spotted, vine-shadowed, patient, able to become a branch until they are falling. There are civetlike raiders with narrow muzzles and musk glands, fierce enough to enter nests of stinging insects for larvae. Owls or owl-equivalents hunt by sound in the vegetal night, round-faced and damp-feathered, swallowing luminous beetles so their pellets glow faintly afterward.

Some predators are collective. Driver ants, or their cousins, pour through the understory in living rivers, and everything small enough flees upward or dies. Social wasps build paper chambers under giant leaves and reduce intruders to pain and heat. Leeches wait on wet leaves, thin as threads until blood swells them. Parasitic flies lay eggs in frog foam, bird nostrils, mammal wounds. Fungi take insects and lift their bodies into position before blooming from them. Certain flowers are not quite carnivorous, not in any theatrical way, but small animals vanish inside them often enough that the distinction may not matter.

Then there are the ambiguous inhabitants, the ones that belong especially to this young and undecided country.

There are root-backed crawlers: slow animals, perhaps amphibian, perhaps something stranger, whose hides are ridged with grooves where seeds lodge. Moss, seedlings, and fungal threads grow across them until they resemble pieces of wandering bank. They graze in the dark, and where they sleep too long their living gardens root into the soil; when they rise, they tear free with soft ripping sounds and carry part of one place into another.

There are rain-callers, small throat-sac creatures hidden in leaf cups and bark seams. Their calls imitate the first scattered drops of a storm so precisely that other animals respond before clouds do. Frogs begin. Flowers open. Insects rise. Sometimes real rain follows, though whether summoned, predicted, or merely joined is impossible to say.

There are mud-anticipators: pale, nearly blind quadrupeds that travel regular routes beneath the surface crust, pressing upward just before they emerge. Their tunnels leave dents that look like footprints arriving early. Predators have learned this. So have certain plants, whose roots thicken near those dents before the animal passes, ready to drink from the disturbed wetness.

There are vine-handlers among the clever mammals, not tool-makers in any lasting sense, but habitual shapers. They swing, pull, knot, and worry certain flexible vines until loops remain. Generations of grasping paws train the plants into ladders and snares and sleeping nets. Whether the animals intend this, or the vines do, or both have simply discovered each other, is unclear.

Some trees are inhabited so thoroughly they are less individuals than apartment bodies. Their roots hold frogs; their bark holds beetles; their hollows hold bats; their crown holds birds; their leaves host eggs; their flowers heat moths; their fallen fruit feeds mammals; their deadwood becomes fungus, termites, orchids, and new roots. Other trees are almost animal in their responsiveness. Touch sets off tightening spirals in their tendrils. Repeated vibration changes the direction of growth. One species lowers rootlets toward nightly trails, tasting the warm carbon breath of passing herds.

Fungi are among the most intelligent-seeming lives here. Not intelligent like a face, but like a network of delayed decisions. They signal through rotting logs, fruit overnight around carcasses, harden mud with white-threaded mats, open blue-green seams of light under the darkest canopy, and raise temporary lattices on which seedlings climb before the structure is eaten. Some make animals return to them. A mammal that eats their bitter orange fruiting bodies later seeks mineral crusts with obsessive focus, depositing spores exactly where fungal threads prosper. Beetles tunnel through their shelves and become dusted with spores. Frogs hide beneath them and keep them wet.

The plants are not passive background. They are the dominant animals of another tempo. They climb, grip, raft, lean, remember pressure, and exploit bodies. Reeds grow in armies. Broad-leaved herbs smother openings within days. Vines bridge treefall wounds and convert dead trunks into green tunnels. Floating mats carry shrubs, nests, eggs, snakes, crabs, and whole quarrelsome communities from one bank to another. Some fruits are hooked. Some are adhesive. Some ferment quickly, making mammals reckless and mobile. Some seedpods burst at the pressure of passing feet, firing grains into fur and feathers.

Life here is therefore mostly transport. Everyone carries everyone else.

Birds carry seeds and mites. Bats carry pollen and fever. Crabs carry moss. Amphibians carry algae. Mammals carry burrs, larvae, lice, memory. Floating islands carry groves. Flood pulses carry eggs into pools where their parents have never been. Predators carry spores in their claws from carcass to branch. Even death moves rapidly: a fallen body is first a silence, then a crowd, then a stain, then a garden, then nothing recognizable.

If there are people, they are peripheral and temporary. The basin does not yet know how to hold a village. It would accept a person as it accepts a treefall: disturbance, opportunity, surface. A human who stayed would have to become partly arboreal, partly amphibious, forever remaking paths, drying tools over smoky failures of fire, sleeping above mud and below bats, learning which noises mean rain and which mean imitation. They would leave few monuments. A cracked nut-stone, a lost blade, a reed probe, a scar on bark already swelling shut. The place would not remember them separately for long.

The true inhabitant is the crowded beginning itself: a composite creature made of fern vaults, temporary water, fungal light, eggs in every hollow, wings in every gap, mouths under every film. Its blood is brown runoff. Its nerves are roots and mycelium. Its breath is steam, pollen, rot, and the chorusing of frogs. Its skin is never dry. It has no stable outline, only appetites and exchanges.

Whoever lives there lives by arriving, clinging, breeding, carrying, eating, being eaten, and becoming ground for the next thing. Nothing belongs by permanence. Everything belongs by occupation.
