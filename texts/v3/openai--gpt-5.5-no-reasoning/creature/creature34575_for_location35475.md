What lives there is not a people, and not exactly a single organism, though from far enough away either answer would seem plausible.

It is a plateau-biome so old and so completely interlocked that its inhabitants are less like separate species than recurring gestures in one immense body. Nothing there lives privately. Every creature is also a valve, a cleaner, a counterweight, a pulse in the arithmetic by which the drowned canopy keeps itself unchanged.

The dominant life is the vegetation, though “vegetation” is too passive a word for it. The great plants are the place’s architecture and its metabolism: floating leaf-continents, tower-trunks, siphon groves, bladder-stems, root curtains, turbine hollows, seed-drums, conductive sapwood nerves. They do not merely grow in the water. They route it, warm it, cool it, strain it, pressurize it, oxygenate it, mineralize it, and set it moving through channels as wide as rivers and capillaries finer than hair.

They are not intelligent in any quick, animal sense. They do not decide, hunt, remember, or plan. But they regulate. Their lives are made of pressure gradients, mineral hungers, electrical rhythms, gas balances, buoyancy, the opening and closing of millions of mouths. They maintain the plateau the way a heart maintains a body, without needing to know what a body is.

The oldest among them are the root-pillars: vast descending structures that reach from the high clear water down into blue-black drowned stone. They are woody, mineral-hardened, and sleeved with generations of younger tissues. Their surfaces are furred with filamentous life, eggs, mites, bacterial veils, grazing scars, pale nodules, and small voltage-bright organs that pulse in the dimness. They drink the buried mountain slowly, drawing salts and metals upward into the green world. Around their lower tips gather blind animals, copper-jawed worms, soft mineral-fed colonies, and flowerlike parasites growing from the backs of hosts.

Above them are the working plants.

The compressor groves live in districts, each trunk swollen and muscular, each one flexing with the others in intervals older than memory. Their bark is porous and cool. They inhale water through vast hidden surfaces, alter it inside dark chambers, and exhale it through root-pipes in slow enriched tides. Small crabs live in the pores and seams of these trunks, pruning clogs, eating starch leaks, fighting over valve-lips, and being eaten in turn by needle-mouthed fish that know the exact timing of each expulsion.

The siphon forests are taller and hollower. Their internal spaces ring like organs. Falling water passes through grown vanes and spiral gutters, turning soft turbines of lignin and horn. In their tissues runs a weak, constant electricity: not lightning, not thought, but a shimmer of exchanged ions carried through wet wood. Some animals navigate by it. Some feed from it. Some lay their eggs only where the current hums at the correct faint pitch.

There are also the seed-drums, enormous rotating cylinders suspended in open water from cables of living fiber. They are not reproductive in the way ordinary seedpods are. They produce possibility and then restrain it. Inside them, embryos form, pause, are sampled by grazers, harden, soften, and are reabsorbed unless some exact vacancy appears elsewhere in the plateau. Their thunder is the sound of abundance refusing to become excess.

The broad floating leaves are perhaps the most visible inhabitants. They are great green plates, overlapping for kilometers, their undersides stippled with valves, air pockets, rootlets, sucker scars, egg-clusters, and grazing paths. Their rims curl upward into basins, and in those basins live small communities in shallow suspended water: transparent larvae, moss-snails, pollen-feeding shrimp, skin-thin fish, and mats of photosynthetic slime that gleam like powdered jade.

Beneath those leaves live the air-chamber creatures.

The leaf-lungs are among the strangest habitats in the plateau: long trapped atmospheres held under vegetation, silver-roofed by water, humid, dim, smelling of sap and iodine. In them dwell fern-bats with thin translucent wings and gripping hind claws. They sleep in green-brown clusters against fibrous ceilings, wrapped in their own damp membranes, breathing air made and remade by the plants. They feed on insects that skate upside down along the waterline, on soft-bodied mothlike things that never know open sky, and on the sugar beads secreted by certain root hairs.

There are warm-blooded small animals there too, though they are quiet and secretive. They are low, sleek, whiskered things, something like otters reduced to the size of hands and adapted to ceilings rather than ground. Their paws spread wide on wet fiber. Their fur sheds water in silver sheets. They nose along the leaf-lungs, licking mineral condensation, stealing eggs, caching nothing because nothing dries and nothing keeps. Their young are born in woven root hollows warmed by microbial mats.

In the open water move the grazers.

Some are long and translucent, ribbon-bodied, almost without thickness. They drift edgewise to the current, flexing only when necessary. Their mouths are combs. They pass over bark, root, and submerged leaf, shaving off algae, bacterial velvet, and young fungal films. Their organs are faintly visible: a green gut line, a row of pearl bladders, a pulsing thread of blood or saplike fluid. They exhale bubbles in strings, and those bubbles become habitat. Mites cling to them. Spores wait in them. Tiny fish nip them free when they are needed elsewhere.

The upside-down walkers live beneath the leaf mats. They are slow finned ungulates, calm-eyed, heavy-bodied, with soft suction hooves and flexible necks. They browse the ceilings of the world. Their backs hang toward the depths; their bellies nearly brush the leaf undersides. When startled, they do not flee quickly. Instead they loosen all four hooves at once and sink, turning as they descend, fins spreading like drowned banners. Predators take them only rarely, because their flesh is sour with plant alkaloids and their hides host defensive colonies of stinging filament-worms.

Needlefish hold in shafts of light. They are almost invisible until they move, then they become brief silver strokes. They feed on larvae, small crustaceans, and the shed reproductive dust of the industrial plants. In some regions they school by sound rather than sight, arranging themselves in the pressure shadows of booming trunks.

The great filter-beasts hang in the open halls.

They are among the largest animals there, but they are not rulers. Nothing rules. They suspend themselves vertically from gas bladders grown along their spines, their bodies dangling like pale bells or sacks of living gauze. Their mouths remain open for hours, sometimes days, while manufactured currents pass through combs, baleens, and ciliated chambers. They consume pollen, microbes, soft eggs, mineral flakes, and the constant edible fog produced by plant labor. Cleaner crabs and translucent eel-things live on them. Their scars are gardens. Their excrement falls in slow black threads that feed root fans far below.

Predators exist, but they are not abundant.

In the trembling silences between root veils hang white-jawed animals shaped for stillness. Their bodies resemble torn leaves or strips of pale bark. They wait with fins spread and mouths sealed, tasting vibration through their skins. When prey crosses the correct invisible boundary, they open with astonishing speed. The strike is not a chase but a door suddenly becoming teeth. Then stillness resumes. Another grazer somewhere is born, another clutch survives, another sickness removes an excess elsewhere. The total never changes.

There are crabs in the joints of everything.

Some are pale and blind, living inside valve chambers, feeding on starch and trapped organisms. Some are green-backed and territorial, defending a patch of root no larger than a hand with absurd solemnity. Some are almost symbiotic: their claws are shaped to scrape particular plant pores, and the plants grow ridges that only those claws can clean. If the crab is removed, the pore clogs; if the plant dies, the crab cannot molt properly. Neither relationship is affectionate. It is older than affection.

There are worms with metal in their jaws. There are shell-less snails that move like beads of mucus over submerged bark. There are mites inside air bubbles. There are fish that spend their juvenile lives inside the hollow thorns of vines and emerge only when their bones have hardened. There are amphibious birds that never touch dry land because dry land does not exist.

The birds are glass-voiced and narrow-bodied. They nest in hanging root tangles above stable air pockets, weaving fibers, shed bat wings, and buoyant seed floss into wet cradles. They swim as well as they fly, though flight in that mist-heavy air is more like rowing through vapor. Their calls are high, clear, and repeating, part of the acoustic map of the plateau. Some follow the compressor pulses, snatching organisms stunned by pressure changes. Others feed on insects from the leaf-lungs. Their eggs are gelatinous-shelled and must remain damp; dryness would kill them instantly, but dryness is only a myth here.

The smallest lives may be the most important.

Every root hair is inhabited. Every strand of submerged fiber carries colonies: translucent beads, bacterial films, fungal threads, ciliated cups, red-brown mineral fixers, tiny pulsing sacs that blur the line between animal and plant. They clean toxins before toxins can accumulate. They digest wounds before rot can spread. They lock excess bloom into edible forms. They release gases at the exact rate needed to maintain the trapped atmospheres beneath the leaves. They are the reason death never becomes catastrophe.

There are parasites, but none free enough to become plague. There are sicknesses, but each is braided into counter-sickness. A fungus hollows the gills of a grazer; a shrimp eats the fungus; a worm eats the shrimp when shrimp become too numerous; the worm’s larvae feed a fish whose migrations distribute spores to the compressor groves. No line of life is independent. Nothing can succeed too well.

If there is anything like a mind in this place, it is distributed through balance.

Not consciousness, perhaps, but recurrence. A pattern that includes every pulse, appetite, larva, valve, wound, birth, and pressure wave. The plateau does not think, yet disturbances vanish into it as if answered. A torn leaf becomes food, shelter, silt, gas, signal, and replacement. A dead beast becomes a vertical rain of nutrients already anticipated by the open mouths below. A new growth does not expand the world; it fills a vacancy shaped exactly like itself.

So whoever lives there lives without history.

They do not migrate with seasons. They do not wait for drought, thaw, fire, fruiting, storm, or night. Their lives have events, but the place does not. A predator may remember a successful root veil. A bat may know a warm chamber. A crab may defend a valve for the whole span of its small life. But above and around them, the old green machinery continues, unchanged by their urgency.

They are inhabitants of a completed ecology:  
plants like engines, animals like maintenance notes, microbes like laws, all suspended in cold clear water at the top of the world.

They live inside equilibrium so vast it has become geography.
