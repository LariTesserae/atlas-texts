What lives there are citizens of dryness.

They are not one species, nor a single hidden people, but a whole under-earth society assembled from roots, wings, mandibles, fur, scale, spore, and patient hunger. Everything that lives in that buried city has been shaped by three laws: take little, lose less, and move when the wind permits.

The plants are the first inhabitants, though they do not look like forest plants. They are more like pale cables, mats, knots, and listening organs. Their roots are not underground so much as everywhere: descending from ceilings, pressing through stone seams, threading mineral cracks too small for sight. Some hang in long gray curtains down the mineral avenues, braided together by age, thick at the top and frayed into fine searching fibers near the floor. Some have grown until they resemble columns themselves, root-trunks twisting from ceiling fissure to dust basin, their surfaces ridged and polished by passing bodies.

Their leaves are small and economical. They fold in wind. They seal themselves against the dry air. Their color is never bright green, but sage, ash, old silver, the dull green of something alive under restraint. In sheltered recesses, velvety wall-growths spread in low nap over limestone and salt crust, gathering weak light and mineral breath. There are brittle shrubs too, rooted in guano-rich pockets and old dust, their branches jointed and pale, bearing papery pods that rattle whenever the wind strengthens.

Their flowers are rare and modest. A flower may open for only an hour, and only when the air carries scent far enough to make the effort worthwhile. They are small, waxen, often almost colorless except for a faint stain of violet, yellow, or bruised green at the throat. Their perfumes are dry and sharp: resin, chalk, pepper, old hay. Pollinators find them by chemistry more than sight.

Around these plants live the small multitudes.

There are pale beetles in disciplined processions, their bodies flattened and satin-dull, the color of gypsum, salt, or old bone. Some browse the wall mats. Some harvest fungal crust. Some cut dead root fiber into manageable lengths and drag it back through cracks. Others are undertakers, dismantling bodies before rot can happen. They know the city by taste. Their roads are invisible but exact, looping around stones, through root shadows, along the edges of wind-scoured plazas. When the drafts rise, the processions vanish almost instantly into pinholes and seams.

Blind crickets cling to the root curtains and mineral ribs. They are long-legged, translucent beige, with antennae far longer than their bodies. Their eyes remain as dark specks or not at all. They sing by scraping their limbs, but their music is never delicate down here; the cave takes each thin rasp and flings it outward until it becomes part of the roar. They feed on lichen, tender root skin, shed leaves, and one another when hunger narrows the law.

There are mites, springtails, waxy grubs, dryland worms that live deep in the cooler powder, and silver moths whose larvae chew through papery seed pods. There are flies that do not need open water, living briefly on breath-moisture, dung, wounds, and flowering intervals. Some insects carry spores in special hairs. Some plants seem to depend on them entirely.

The lizards are nearly the color of the walls. Dust-gray, salt-white, iron-brown, depending on their district. They are thin-bodied and broad-toed, able to lie so flat against stone that only the blink of an eye gives them away. Many have translucent eyelids or reduced eyes; others see well in the dimmest green lichen-glow. They hunt by vibration, waiting beside root curtains where crickets descend, or near beetle roads where traffic thickens after a lull in the wind. Their tails break easily and regrow short and blunt. Their skins shed in pale flakes that beetles eat by morning.

Higher up live the flyers.

They are bat-like, though not necessarily bats as the upper world knows them. Some are true bats that entered ages ago and became smaller, dustier, quieter. Some may be something older or merely stranger through adaptation: narrow-winged cave flyers with folded faces, large listening ears, and claws made for mineral eaves. They roost in dense colonies beneath ledges and false balconies, hanging like dark fruit in the dim heights. When asleep they are nearly silent except for the faint ticking of claws and the soft compression of many bodies breathing together.

When they rise, the city changes.

A single flyer is almost nothing: a scrap of leather passing between shafts of weak light. A colony is weather. Thousands lift at once from a mineral facade, and the air becomes muscular with wings. They feed on insects, night-blooming scents, windborne seeds, and in some species, the nectar of those small pale flowers that open in the moving air. Their droppings fall beneath their roosts in dark islands, and those islands become gardens. Shrubs grow thicker there. Beetles gather. Grazers visit. Predators learn the paths. Whole neighborhoods of the undercity are founded on the sleep of flyers.

In the dust floors live soft-furred burrowers.

They are small mammals, cool-bodied, round-backed, and pale underneath, with dense fur that holds moisture close to the skin. Their eyes are reduced or dark and beadlike. Their forefeet are strong for digging through powder and mineral loam. They make sealed chambers below root groves where the air is calmer and warmer with bodies. At slack-wind hours they emerge to strip bark, gather seeds, nibble fungus, and harvest dry leaves. They carry food underground and plug their entrances behind them.

Their colonies are careful. They do not chatter much in the open. They communicate by foot-drumming through dust, by whisker touch, by musk laid on root and stone. They maintain seed caches in hollow ledges. Forgotten caches sprout after rare favorable seasons, making the burrowers accidental gardeners. Their dead are quickly taken apart unless they die deep in sealed chambers, where they dry into curled husks that may remain for generations.

There are grazers too.

They are larger than seems possible in such a place, but not numerous. Their bodies are built for thrift. Wide padded feet spread their weight over powder and salt crust. Their legs are jointed to climb mineral terraces slowly, without panic. Their muzzles are blunt and tough-lipped for stripping root bark, brittle shrub stems, and waxy leaves. Some have pale hides and sparse hair; others are shaggy along the spine where dust gathers and makes them look like moving stone. Their ears are broad but often held flat in the wind. Their nostrils can close to slits.

They move between feeding grounds as if following old civic routes. A root grove here, a guano garden there, a basin where shrubs seed every few years. They know sheltered walls and pressure shifts. They can stand with their heads lowered for hours, waiting for the wind to become passable. Calves are born in lee chambers dense with roots, where the air is full of animal smell and softened by living bodies. The young learn early not to run blindly. In that city, panic wastes water.

Some grazers browse upward. They rear on hind legs against stone buttresses to pull down hanging roots with flat teeth. Their tongues are rough enough to rasp lichen from mineral faces. Where they feed often, root curtains are trimmed into arches and high ragged fringes, giving the illusion of doorways used by generations.

And where there are grazers, burrowers, flyers, and lizards, there are hunters.

The predators are seldom seen whole. They belong to edges. A long shape beneath a ledge. A gray shoulder passing between mineral columns. A scrape on salt. A warm musk moving through cold air after the body has gone. Some are catlike, low and sinewed, with padded feet and whiskers long enough to read the walls. Their eyes do not shine brightly; instead they catch light like damp stone, dull and depthless. Others are mustelid or foxlike, narrow enough to enter root thickets and burrow mouths. There may be larger ambush hunters in the deepest avenues, creatures that wait where wind and shadow confuse hearing.

They hunt by patience rather than speed. Speed belongs to open country; down here, speed breaks bones against stone and spends moisture. A predator waits beside a beetle-rich lizard wall, a burrow entrance, a root grove used by grazers. It learns the rhythm of drafts. It moves when the roar covers footfall. It freezes when antennae lift, when ears turn, when dust shifts wrong.

Carrion is never wasted. If a grazer dies in a plaza, the first visitors may be flies and beetles, then small mammals, then larger scavengers, then fungi and salt. Hide tightens. Tendons dry. Bones bleach slowly in the dimness. Horn, tooth, claw, and husk remain along corridor margins for so long that the city seems to keep records in remains.

There are also the nearly invisible inhabitants: lichens, fungi, bacteria, mineral-eating films, colonies that live by chemistry more than light. They occupy salt blooms, iron seams, guano beds, old bones, the undersides of ledges where breath condenses invisibly and disappears. They are the city’s slow digestive system. They turn dung to root food, corpses to crust, dust to thin fertility. Some glow faintly green in recesses, not enough to illuminate, only enough to stain darkness with the fact of metabolism.

Nothing there is lavish. Even abundance is rationed.

A root curtain may support crickets, beetles, mites, lizards, nesting flyers, seed moths, fungi, burrower colonies, and the occasional browsing grazer, but it does so by slowness. Every gain is held. Every scrap passes through many mouths. A fallen leaf becomes food, shelter, egg-case, mold-bed, mineral trace. A dead insect feeds beetles, whose droppings feed lichen, which feeds crickets, which feed lizards, which feed hunters, whose bones harbor salts, whose salts feed roots. The cycle is dry, quiet in its chemistry, loud only because the city itself gives everything a voice.

The inhabitants have learned the wind as another kind of terrain.

They know leeward corners the way surface animals know water holes. They know which plazas become lethal to insects when pressure rises, which galleries boom but do not scour, which root masses remain still at their cores. Many bodies are shaped to close: leaves fold, nostrils seal, ears flatten, wing membranes tuck tight, beetle shells clamp against dust. During the great drafts, life withdraws into the city’s pockets and seams. The place may look abandoned then, except for the thrashing root tips and the enormous sound.

When the wind slackens, the population returns by degrees. First antennae. Then beetles. Then crickets begin again in the roots. Lizards unflatten from walls. Burrowers nose open their sealed doors. Flyers drop from eaves and skim the avenues. Grazers step out from behind stone masses, chewing already. Predators follow later, or were present all along.

No one rules the city. There is no central species, no builder, no keeper of halls. But there are neighborhoods.

The guano districts beneath flyer roosts are dark, rich, crowded, and dangerous. Plants grow thickest there; insects swarm; lizards and small predators patrol constantly. The root avenues are vertical forests turned upside down, full of clinging life and swinging shelter. The dust plazas are open crossings, burial grounds, hunting grounds, and seed corridors. The low crawl districts belong mostly to insects, reptiles, fungi, and the smallest mammals. The deep regular chambers, with their false masonry and aligned cracks, host sparse but old communities—slow shrubs, pale grazers, predators that know long routes, and wall-mats that may have been spreading for centuries.

The creatures are not bizarre for the sake of strangeness. They are believable descendants of surface life, cave life, desert life, all pressed together by time beneath stone. They have become pale where color costs too much, quiet where quiet saves them, loud where the cavern magnifies them against their will. They are careful without being timid. They are ancient without being unchanged.

If one were to name them collectively, they would not be monsters or spirits or a hidden civilization.

They are an ecology that resembles a populace.

Root-trees as towers and granaries. Flyers as commuters of the dim air. Beetles as street cleaners. Fungi as archives and undertakers. Burrowers as miners and accidental planters. Grazers as slow citizens of the avenues. Predators as the law of edges. Lichens as the low green memory on the walls.

They live in a city no one built, and their lives make it more citylike every generation. They wear paths into dust. They darken roosts into neighborhoods. They trim roots into doorways. They leave bones along margins like old monuments. They move by districts, gather at resources, retreat during storms, return when the avenues open. Without hands, without speech, without intention, they inhabit the architecture until habitation itself becomes the builder.

What lives there is life reduced to essentials and then elaborated again: pale, dry, persistent, listening through its feet, drinking from vapor and flesh, flowering in brief hours, singing into a roar so vast that even the smallest cricket seems part of a crowd.
