What lives there are not simply animals and not simply plants, but provisional citizens of an unfinished world: bodies adapted not to a stable environment, but to revision itself.

They are creatures with a sense for pressure, moisture, tremor, and possibility. Many of them are quiet because noise is less useful than listening. Sound travels strangely there; vibration tells the truth sooner. The largest herds move by feeling the mountain’s pulse through the plates of their feet and bellies. Their skulls are low and shielded, their eyes set wide under mineral brows, their backs armored in dark overlapping scutes that bead with condensation at dawn. Steam lifts from them as from cooling metal. They graze on lichen crusts and blade-grasses, but also on salts, warm clay, pollen films, and the edible skins of new stone. When geothermal rhythms alter beneath them, the entire herd turns as one—not by command, not even by fear, but by bodily arithmetic.

Their young are soft for only a few hours.

A newborn upland grazer drops wet onto hot basalt, unfolds four or six trembling limbs, and hardens visibly as the air touches it. Its first meal is often its birth steam: mineral-rich condensation gathered along the mother’s plates. By evening it can run. By the next local storm it may already have developed the ridge-sense that keeps its kind from stepping onto ground that has not finished deciding its angle.

Among the cliffs live long-limbed wall-browsers, narrow-bodied and patient, with toes that spread like damp leaves against vertical stone. Their hides are mottled with iron red, fungus green, and black glass gray, so that a whole family can be mistaken for mineral staining until they move. They feed on hanging forests: copper fronds, vent moss, swollen cliff-fruit, root-tips exposed by fractures. Some have flexible necks that can fold backward over the spine; some carry their young in pouches sealed by skin-flaps against ash and vapor. They are almost silent except when the stone under them cracks. Then they hiss through throat-slits, not as warning but as balance—a release of pressure, a counter-vibration.

Gliders inhabit the ferned scarps and vapor shelves. They are not birds, though some have beaks; not bats, though some have membranous sails; not insects, though some fold themselves into lacquered cases when the air dries. Their wings are made for broken weather. A true wind is rare and dangerous, so they ride exhalations: vent-breath, ravine fog, the upward fall of mist. Their nests hang beneath basalt fins where condensation gathers in threads. The eggs are translucent, veined with faint electrical blue, and must be turned toward different kinds of dimness to develop properly. In the wrong shadow the young hatch with stone-heavy wings and crawl into the walls.

In the ravines, life becomes more intimate and less easily separated into kinds.

There are six-limbed canopy-climbers with faces like damp seeds and hands delicate enough to peel spores from the underside of a leaf. They are social but nearly voiceless. Instead they communicate by altering the color of throat skin, by drumming lightly on hollow vines, by arranging shed leaves in branching patterns that last until the next rain revises them. They know which pools are mouths. They know which flowers are listening. They sometimes carry glowing larvae pressed into their fur as ornaments, parasites, lamps, or inherited relatives.

Blind fish climb the wet rock there, swimming upward through films of water thinner than their bodies should permit. Their fins have become soft fingers. Their mouths rasp algae and mineral bloom from the stone. During dry hours they seal themselves inside mucus lenses and wait, suspended like thoughts in glass. When rain falls upward out of a sinkhole, they ascend with it, flash pale in the dimness, and vanish into crevices halfway up the wall.

The amphibians are almost transparent. Their organs are colored by what they most recently drank: green from fern-water, yellow from sulfur seep, black from deep pools, silver from suspended rain. They gather in condensation basins and sing without sound, inflating sacs that vibrate the surface of the water into patterns. Mates are chosen by ripple geometry. Predators learn to read those patterns too.

Some predators are patient enough to be landscape.

A bark-hunter may spend three days as an apparent wound on a tree trunk, a vertical seam of brown and gray tissue with moss growing across its shoulders. Then a moth-stack stirs, or a vine-primate reaches too close, and the seam unfolds. It has too many joints because joints are useful in a place where direction is negotiable. Its limbs can bend around branches, into cracks, across surfaces at improbable angles. Its mouth opens sideways first, then forward. It kills quickly and almost without disturbance, folding prey into a pouch of enzyme-warm darkness while the surrounding leaves continue their slow coordinated turning.

Other predators are brief events. Luminous swarms gather around heat signatures in the fog: pinpricks, then threads, then a pulsing veil. They feed on warmth, salt, breath, fear, perhaps memory. A large animal caught in them does not fall. It fades in increments, first losing steam, then color, then certainty. When the swarm disperses, it may leave bones, or mineral casts, or a living but altered creature that no longer casts the correct shadow.

There are burrowers under the ash fields: soft-bodied, warm, blind things that move through grit and young soil like thoughts through sleep. Their passage leaves heated trails that certain seeds require before sprouting. They harvest cooled crust from lava tongues and digest it with fungal symbionts, excreting loam so new it smokes in the morning air. Many plants depend on them. Many predators listen for them. When the ground-hum deepens, they surface and climb, adhering to trunks in pale clusters until the pressure front passes beneath.

The plants are inhabitants too, and in this place they are more active than plants elsewhere would dare to be.

Vines bridge fissures before soil has a chance to gather. They taste the air for future moisture and grow toward rain that has not yet happened. Their roots secrete acids, sugars, and persuasive electrical pulses into the rock, asking it to soften, fracture, feed. Some vines form temporary ladders used by animals during storms; afterward the vines absorb the oils and skin flakes left by passage, thickening around the memory of feet.

Fern-trees cluster around steam vents like congregations. Their copper fronds open and close with the vent cycle, harvesting heat as much as light. At night they glow faintly along their ribs. Their spores are not dust but tiny folded mechanisms, each waiting for a specific combination of mineral vapor, animal breath, and atmospheric uncertainty. Released in the wrong weather, they become mold. Released in the right revision-storm, they become whole groves before dawn.

The grove-islands of the plateaus are almost communal organisms. Their low canopies interlace so tightly that the interior has its own breath. The leaves are glossy, warm, and dark; touch one and others nearby slowly reorient toward the pressure of your hand. Within each grove live insects, mites, root-fish, hanging larvae, small sleeping mammals or mammal-analogues, fungal nets, and pools no larger than an eye. The grove regulates them all imperfectly. It exhales vapor at night. It hoards shade. It listens through roots. When conditions change, a grove may migrate, not by walking all at once, but by abandoning itself from one side and growing urgently from the other, dragging its damp center downslope over the course of days.

Some plants spend part of their lives as animals.

Rain-woken walkers begin as rooted bulbs clinging to mineral faces. In dry periods they resemble swollen black-green tubers with folded limbs embedded in barky sheaths. When a certain kind of rain hangs in the air without falling, they split open, pull their root-tendrils from the stone, and move. Slowly at first. Then with insect certainty. They cross exposed ground in groups, seeking new cracks, new heat, new chemical rumor. During this wandering phase they feed on small organisms, carrion, and each other. When the rain ends they root again, often in entirely different shapes. The next generation may inherit both the place they settled and the memory of the path.

There are mineral organisms too, or things close enough to life that the distinction is decorative.

Crystal ticks grow in fog along vent lines, transparent at first, then smoky, then bright with trapped electrical charge. They attach to the legs of passing animals and ride until they find a region of compatible stone. There they drop, root, and begin ticking softly as they grow, each pulse matching some distant geothermal engine.

The basalt grazers rasp lichen, but the lichen rasps them back over generations, tinting their plates, altering the mineral content of their bones, guiding herds toward places where the lichen needs hooves to crack the crust. In this highland, many relationships are mutualism, predation, agriculture, infection, and weather all at once.

The most successful inhabitants are those that can notice wrongness before it becomes lethal.

They pause before shadows that do not match their owners. They avoid over-perfect pools. They distrust stones too smooth for their age. They know the taste of a revision-storm before it arrives: metallic sweetness, leaf-undersides turning pale, insects aligning along invisible bands. A herd will lower itself flat to the ground when distance begins to flex. A glider will fold its wings and cling upside down when rain stops in midair. The six-limbed canopy people bind themselves into knots of vine and one another when the local time-texture loosens, so that no individual ages too quickly or is left behind in an hour that lasts all day.

Memory here is not only in brains. It is stored in routes, in spoor, in root scars, in mineral accretions around old nests. A species may have instincts for cliffs that have not yet risen because, in some nearby basin, they already did. Fossils of unborn animals are occasionally licked for salt by their living descendants. Eggs are laid in hollows that will become safe yesterday. No creature finds this mystical. It is simply part of the terrain.

Above all, the inhabitants are unfinished without being primitive.

They are specialized for instability, elegant in their provisionality. Many bodies have redundant limbs, backup senses, reversible organs, seasonal skeletons. Some hatch with multiple possible anatomies and settle into one only after tasting the local air. Some never settle. Their skins are maps of pressure and moisture. Their bones conduct tremor. Their blood may carry spores, magnetite, dissolved silica, or symbiotic threads that stiffen during seismic events. Their eyes, when they have eyes, often see heat, vapor density, electrical charge, and the credibility of edges.

They do not build roads because roads would be arguments with a world that will not keep them. They do not make walls because enclosure is unreliable. They make nests that can be abandoned, burrows that can flood upward, hanging cradles, mineral cocoons, living bridges, social habits, chemical warnings, migratory loops. Their civilizations, if that word can be used, are ecological and rhythmic rather than architectural. A shift change at the vent plains; a breeding silence in the suspended rain; a dawn crossing of plated herds over grass that may be stone by noon.

The largest things living there may not move in any ordinary sense.

A ravine dense with ferns, pools, moths, climbing fish, root-light, and warm fog may be a single organism spread across incompatible categories. It feeds on rainfall, animal passage, mineral seep, and time irregularities. It reproduces when a landslide tears away a portion of its wall and carries a packed fragment of its ecology into another basin. A plateau grove may dream through its fungal underlayers. A vent field may cultivate its own belts of vegetation and animals as part of its breathing apparatus. The highland itself may be alive, though not as an animal is alive: more like an engine becoming a jungle becoming a weather system becoming a body.

So what lives there?

Herds like armored steam.

Gliders with storm-colored membranes.

Transparent basin-drinkers and stone-climbing fish.

Six-limbed, near-primate vine dwellers with silent languages of color and touch.

Predators that unfold from bark.

Swarms that behave like hungry mist.

Plants that walk when rain remembers them.

Fungi that illuminate ravines from beneath.

Mineral ticks, heat ferns, root-cables, lichen crusts that farm the mouths that eat them.

And beneath or among all of them, something broader: a young world’s metabolism, crowded with creatures that have learned not permanence but response. They live by listening to pressure, drinking from imperfect water, breeding in pockets of moisture, hardening quickly, moving softly, and accepting that by morning the valley, their bodies, and the rules between them may have changed.
