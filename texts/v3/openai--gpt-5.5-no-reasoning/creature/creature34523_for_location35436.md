They are not a people who fill a city. They inhabit it the way water inhabits stone: by contact, by patience, by choosing the seams.

The residents of the high city are sparse, deliberate, and lightly distributed across an enormous civic body. They live in towers and shelf-neighborhoods that may hold only a few thousand people where an older lowland city would have pressed in ten times as many. Whole districts can feel unoccupied at noon: windows dimmed against glare, walkways empty except for frost and the prints of birds, transit platforms holding only wind, a repair drone, one bundled figure waiting without impatience. But absence is misleading. The inhabitants are there, behind regulated walls and under planted roofs, in laboratories sunk into basalt, in warm communal kitchens suspended above marsh basins, in observatories where cloud forms on the glass.

They are adapted less to cold than to change.

Their culture begins with the assumption that ground is temporary. Children learn early that stone can breathe, that ice can lift a building by a finger’s width, that a dry basin may one day become a wetland, that a path useful in one decade may be surrendered in the next to migrating animals or to water. Their maps are not fixed diagrams but forecasts, layered with probabilities: seep emergence, frost heave, sedge advance, nesting exclusion, heat budget, safe crossing, silence zones. A resident does not ask simply where something is. They ask when it is, and under what conditions.

They are tall or short, broad or slight, whatever their ancestry has made them, but most carry themselves with a particular economy. Movements are measured in a place where careless heat, careless noise, careless pressure all have consequences. They walk as if the floor may be listening. Their clothing is close-layered and adaptive, matte in color, often grey, black-green, bone, rust, or the dark blue of evening ice. Outer fabrics bead water instantly, stiffen in wind, soften indoors. Hoods are common, as are face veils, not for secrecy but for the constant fine moisture, the mineral glare, the sudden knives of cold. Gloves are almost ceremonial in their variety: work gloves with sensor pads, reed-cutting gloves, transit gloves, falconer-like gloves for handling semi-wild service birds, thin social gloves worn in warm rooms as a courtesy to shared surfaces.

Their faces often have the look of people accustomed to distance. They focus far away even in conversation. They notice cloud edges, bird movement, the pitch of a drainage grate, the smell of thawing algae under ice. A visitor might mistake them for solemn, but their humor exists; it is simply dry, low-voiced, and often geological. They make jokes about foundations courting basins, about districts sulking under bad pressure, about a person being “built too permanent” when stubborn.

They dislike haste, but they are not slow.

When needed, they act with extraordinary coordination. A seep becomes chemically wrong; within minutes a wetland team changes a heat schedule, a transit line lifts to a quieter height, schoolchildren are rerouted from an outdoor path, drones descend to sample moss, and three districts downstream dim their waste warmth. A nesting cliff shifts; bridge traffic alters before the birds return. A pressure crack opens beneath an old plinth; no alarm screams, but rooms gently wake, walls unlock their flexure, floor lights guide sleepers to alternate chambers, and by morning the building has given some of its weight elsewhere.

They are engineers, ecologists, hydrologists, animal-keepers, meteorologists, marsh surgeons, cold farmers, transit listeners, stone-readers, and civic negotiators. Even those whose work seems unrelated—musicians, teachers, cooks, archivists, makers of fabric or games—know the practical grammar of the plateau. Everyone understands heat rationing. Everyone can read the public seep boards. Everyone knows which amber lights mean silence for migrating flocks and which mean do not cross because the ice skin is forming thin.

They do not divide nature from infrastructure cleanly. To them, a reedbed that filters mineral runoff is as much a civic organ as a pump station. A foxlike predator that keeps tunnel rodents from overbreeding is part of maintenance until it learns doors. Birds are not “wildlife near the city”; they are upper-air residents with protected corridors. Long-backed grazers are not picturesque herds but pressure-footed participants in keeping sedge plains open. Amphibians asleep in the urban marshes are watched with the respect given to old instruments. If their bodies warm too soon, something is wrong. If they abandon a basin, something has already happened.

So the people share governance with signs that are not human.

Their councils include models, sensors, migrations, water tables, microbial blooms, and seasonal animal behavior. They may argue fiercely over a proposed wall, not because of its cost or appearance alone, but because it would disturb the downhill movement of cold air, or gather snow in a way that starves a marsh, or shine faintly upward during the week when needle-birds navigate by a particular dark. The oldest civic oath is not loyalty to the city but restraint toward the unfinished ground beneath it.

They build beautifully, though beauty is rarely their first word for it. Their rooms are warm without being lush. Interior walls curve slightly to shed condensation. Floors have subtle give, responding to load and tremor. Windows are deep-set, often with layers that frost at the outer edge while remaining clear within. Many homes contain a “wet threshold,” a transitional chamber where snow, mineral water, tools, feathers, samples, and outdoor garments are received before anyone enters the living warmth. In these thresholds one finds the real household clutter: boot racks, moss scrapers, folded transit skins, seed capsules, thaw charts, children’s stone collections, tagged feathers, heat-credit tokens, bone-white cups drying upside down.

Communal life gathers around warmth, but cautiously. Heat is too important to waste privately, so there are shared bathing halls, kitchens, repair atria, nurseries, and winter rooms where multiple households eat together during the coldest dark. These spaces are not bright in the old urban way. Light is amber, lateral, shielded. Sound is damped by hanging textiles and moss-backed panels. People speak quietly, not out of repression, but because quiet is the native courtesy of a place where birds sleep on towers and ice is always listening for vibration.

Their food is compact, mineral, and fragrant. Much comes from sealed gardens: dark grains, cold-tolerant greens, fungal loaves, root threads grown in vertical racks, cultured oils, pale fruit from low-light orchards. Some comes from marsh and reservoir under strict seasonal rules: fish with translucent flesh, reed shoots, algae cakes, eggs taken only from managed colonies, milk or blood from the long-backed grazers when herds pass through agreements older than some districts. They flavor food with smoke sparingly, with brine often, with sour ferments, bitter herbs, and the metallic tang of upland salts. Hot drinks are central. People carry them in sealed vessels, exchanging sips at crossings and terraces as a small act of trust.

Children grow up with great freedom and many boundaries. They roam between districts in supervised bands, learning animal corridors before traffic laws, seep etiquette before formal geometry. They are taught to sit still near marshes until amphibian eyes reappear. They learn to identify towers by the clouds they gather. They build small temporary dams only in approved channels, then dismantle them and record what changed. Their games involve prediction: where frost will first form, which basin will skin over by night, when a flock will lift. A child who can cross a wet flat without breaking the ice crust unnecessarily is praised. A child who wastes heat is corrected with gravity.

The elderly are valued as memory-keepers of ground behavior. Since the land is young and still forming, firsthand recollection matters. An elder may remember when a district was dry gravel, before the seep rose and sedges came. Another may know which retaining spine was printed too rigidly in the early years and now requires seasonal loosening. Their stories are not nostalgic in the usual sense; they are warnings, baselines, calibrations. “Here the water first found us,” one might say, pointing at a quiet plaza of ceramic scales. “Here we thought basalt meant certainty. We were wrong.”

They have celebrations, but few explosions of light or sound. Festivals follow hydrological and atmospheric events: the first stable ice skin, the opening of melt channels, the return of high flocks, the week when tower-rime falls in glittering sheets, the brief flowering of engineered heath in the warm hollows. During the Rimefall, people gather beneath catch nets and watch the upper city shed winter. Children collect permitted fragments in insulated bowls before they melt. Musicians play instruments tuned to the acoustics of wet stone: low flutes, struck ceramic plates, stringed frames that hum like bridge cables in wind. Dancers move in arcs based on drainage patterns, separating, joining, vanishing behind veils of vapor.

Their art is full of intervals. They leave blank space deliberately. Paintings may be mostly grey plane with one dark bird. Songs may include long rests in which the actual city—drips, wind, distant transit—becomes part of the composition. Sculpture is often temporary: frost lattices encouraged on wire, mineral deposits guided across porous forms, moss inscriptions that grow legible only in certain months. Permanence is considered a little vulgar unless it is earned by service.

They do not worship the land exactly, though many forms of reverence have grown there. Some residents maintain shrines to pressure, seep, thaw, and distance: not gods with faces, but forces given bowls, heat, silence, or carefully placed stones. Others are secular and speak only of systems. The difference between them may be mostly vocabulary. Both will remove a glove before touching exposed bedrock inside an old foundation hall. Both will lower their voice near a basin where birds are gathering for migration.

They are wary of lowland visitors, not because they hate outsiders, but because outsiders often arrive too warm, too loud, too certain of straight roads and permanent walls. Visitors want to know where the city center is. Residents answer with weather reports. Visitors ask why so much land lies empty. Residents say it is not empty; it is occupied by future water, animal passage, frost drainage, and the city’s right to survive. Visitors ask how long the city will last. Residents say: if we keep listening, longer than its first mistakes.

There are also nonhuman inhabitants who belong to the city so thoroughly that no description of its people is complete without them.

The long-backed grazers are among the most visible. They move in loose herds through the sedge flats and under the high bridges, deliberate and mild-eyed, with heavy shoulders, low heads, and hooves that spread like opened hands on saturated ground. Their coats change with the season from ash-brown to pale smoke, often crusted with frost along the spine by morning. They know the warm vents and the safe crossings. Some wear light sensor bands, though many do not need to; their migration is tracked by tower cameras, hoofprint moisture, and old habit. They are not domesticated exactly. They are negotiated with.

The foxlike predators are thinner than lowland foxes, high-legged, with dense fur and dark reflective eyes. They travel along maintenance corridors and thermal seams. Their intelligence is inconvenient. They learn schedules, locks, scent patterns, and which human food stores are guarded by machines rather than people. The rule says they are tolerated unless they learn doors. Some inevitably learn doors. Then specialized crews relocate them far across the plateau, where, if stories are true, they sometimes teach other predators what hinges mean.

The small furred seam-dwellers are everywhere and almost nowhere. They live under conduits, in warmed joints, near rootlike anchors and ceramic paving. They eat moss, insects, grain dust, microbial films, and the nutrient paste that sometimes leaks from garden freight. Children adore them. Engineers respect them as pests and indicators. A sudden absence of seam-dwellers in a transit plinth can shut a line down faster than a formal alarm.

The amphibious marsh-sleepers are older in manner than everything around them. Slow, armored in slick skin or pebbled hide, they rest half-submerged with their eyes above ice film and their bodies in mud warmed by exchange arrays. Their blood chemistry is watched closely. They are patient enough to seem like stones considering movement. When they surface in numbers, people come quietly to the railings, not to cheer, but to witness a basin declare itself healthy.

Birds are the most sovereign. White-winged scavengers ride tower thermals and clean the high ledges. Needle-birds nest in antenna groves and produce thin, metallic calls that seem to come from the cables themselves. Heavy waterfowl gather where waste heat enters marsh mud, their dark plumage absorbing what light there is. Speckled flocks lift from gravel flats in sudden sheets, turn together, and become weather for a moment. The city dims itself for them. It bends transit. It alters schedules. It accepts droppings, nests, blocked vents, and seasonal inconvenience as part of the treaty.

There are machines too, and though they are not alive in the same way, residents treat many of them as persistent local beings. Maintenance drones hover like seeds in fog, patient and almost shy. Slope-lacers crawl over unstable ground, threading anchors into wet stone. Scale-fields in plazas shiver and tilt as if covered in metallic feathers. Foundations murmur below hearing, redistributing burden in their sleep. Children name neighborhood machines, but adults pretend not to, except in emergencies, when even the most formal engineer may pat a frost-crusted drone casing and say, “Good. Stay with it.”

If there is a dominant temperament among all who live there, human and otherwise, it is attentiveness.

They listen downward, outward, and ahead. They know that the plateau is not scenery but a young force, still rising, still changing its mind. They know their city is not a victory over that force. It is a set of requests, renewed daily: may we stand here, may we warm this much, may we cross there, may we borrow this water before returning it.

They live under tired weather, among unfinished mountains, in a city that has chosen not to be dense, loud, or triumphant. They are a highland people of engineers and marsh-keepers, quiet children and far-looking elders, heat-sharers, bird-dimmers, ice-negotiators, builders of flexible foundations and temporary certainties. Around them move herds, predators, seam-creatures, fish, birds, amphibians, drones, mosses, and slow water.

None of them own the place.

They are all, in different ways, learning how to remain.
