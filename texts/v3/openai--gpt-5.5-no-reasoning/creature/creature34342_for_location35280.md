What lives there is not one creature first, but a compact society of warmth: a whole damp intelligence divided into roots, throats, eyes, feelers, spores, skins, and hunger. The hollow is inhabited by things that have accepted closeness as a law. Nothing there has the extravagance of open country. Even the largest lives by folding itself, flattening itself, waiting in seams.

Its true resident is the undergrowth made animal.

Ferns live there like green weather slowed almost to stillness. They are not background. They are the chamber’s many hands, layered and lifted, drinking dimness, catching mineral sweat, sheltering eggs and mites and beadlike frogs. Their fronds make roofs under the roof, rooms under rooms. Beneath them, mosses take the shape of every fallen thing and soften it into sleep. Fungi live in the dead wood, but also seem to be thinking through it: pale shelves, violet-gray ears, rust-colored tongues, little midnight umbrellas that appear after rain and dissolve almost at once. The place is full of mouths too small to see.

The roots overhead are inhabitants as much as intrusions. They hang down from the world above like the nerves of trees dreaming underground. Some are wiry and dry at their tips, some slick and pale, some furred with rootlets fine as mold. They drink from air. They collect drops and release them one by one. A few possess a nearly animal caution, withdrawing slowly when touched, not fleeing exactly but reconsidering their nearness. They are patient feeders, ceiling-creatures, suspended vines that have mistaken the cave for an inward sky.

In the water live the pale and the delicate.

Blind fish turn in the deeper basins, their bodies the color of peeled seeds or old milk glass. They are not many, and they do not dart unless startled. Their movement is corrective, minimal, as though they are always adjusting to invisible pressures in the dark water. Transparent shrimp pick at the softened leaves below them, carrying the chamber’s decay in tiny claws. Tadpoles graze films of algae from stone cups and flooded grooves, becoming temporary citizens of rainwater before the pools shrink. Worms thread the mud. Small larvae breathe at the surface and vanish when shadows pass.

The frogs are among the hollow’s most obvious people, if one learns how to look.

They live in every size that moisture can support. There are thumbnail frogs no brighter than flecks of wet lichen, lodged in moss hollows with eyes like black seeds. There are narrow, leaf-colored frogs folded along fern stems, throats pulsing so faintly that the motion seems borrowed from the plant. There are broad warty frogs sunk in the mud, old-looking from birth, their backs carrying grains of silt and fungal dust. Some cling to the ribbed wet wall with spread, translucent toes. Some gather on the warm stones of the old wall as if those stones give off a message only amphibian skin can read.

They do not sing loudly. Their voices are rationed: a chirr, a rubbery click, a note cut short before it can become a chorus. After rain they grow bolder. They come down to the linked pools, lay clear ropes or clustered beads of eggs, and sit afterward as if stunned by their own necessity. Their young darken the puddles. Their enemies know the schedule.

The snakes know.

They are not rulers, but they are conclusions. One lies along a root curtain in a pattern of damp rope and shadow, present only when it moves or when its tongue tests the air. Another keeps to the old stone wall, using the human-made curve as a hunting line. It waits where frogs must pass between seep and basin. Its body is narrow enough to become a crack, heavy enough to change the mood of the mud. There may be a larger one beyond the green-choked back passage, leaving that smooth pressed track and faint musk: not monstrous, perhaps, but old, warm, and accustomed to being avoided.

Geckos and lizards take the ledges. The geckos are ash-colored, translucent in the throat, with bellies soft as damp paper. They cling upside down in the low places and hunt the middle air, snapping gnats and moths with movements too fast for the chamber’s general slowness. Small lizards scrape over stone, pause, pulse, vanish. Some have tails regrown bluntly. Some wear mottled skins so exact that when they stop, they become mineral again.

There are bats in the folds above. They sleep in a dark crease of the ceiling, wrapped in themselves like old leaves. Their presence falls before it is seen: dry pellets, ammonia dust, the faint mammal smell that mingles with wet limestone. At certain hours they stir with leathery whispers, exchanging positions, quarreling softly, grooming wing membrane with needle teeth. Their droppings feed a pale fungal garden below, so that even their sleep becomes part of the chamber’s agriculture.

The shrews under the worn steps are among the few truly urgent things there. They are hot sparks in fur, quick-snouted, always near starvation because their bodies burn too fast for stillness. Their nest is tucked beneath the ghost of the stair, in a cavity lined with dry fern ribs, root fibers, and the softened remains of leaves. They emerge when the green-brown light has thinned to its lowest level. They hunt beetle larvae, roach nymphs, worms, fallen moths. They nose along the old treads where human feet or other feet once descended, inheriting the architecture without knowing it as architecture.

Insects occupy the hollow most completely.

Gnats live in the light shafts. They rise and lower as if suspended by invisible breath. Mosquitoes drift singly, patient and uncertain, finding blood when blood enters but otherwise living on the chamber’s general sweetness. Long-legged flies write on water with their feet. Roaches move under leaves with a papery hush, their bodies polished by darkness. Beetles wedge under bark, tunnel into fungus, shear wet wood with minute jaws. Some are lacquer-black. Some are brown as old seeds. Some carry mites beneath their wing cases like hidden passengers.

Ants make the roots into roads. They cross the ceiling curtains, the stone wall, the fallen wood, the edges of pools. Their trails are braided and purposeful, shifting after rain, breaking and reforming when water cuts a path. They carry white eggs to safer chambers, green scraps to fungus beds, dead insects home in sections. They know the hollow as a map of temperature, dryness, and risk. Termites own the dead beam so thoroughly that the wood has become a memory of wood around a warm paper city. Inside it are galleries, nurseries, damp chambers, blind workers, soldiers with heads like sealed doors.

The mites, springtails, nematodes, larvae, and pale worms are the chamber’s hidden multitude. They live in the leaf mold, in frog spawn, under stones, in bat dung, in the softening muscle of dead things. They turn endings into material. They are too many to name and too important to omit. Without them, the hollow would choke on its own abundance. With them, every fallen fruit, shed skin, drowned insect, dead frog, and rotting root becomes passage rather than waste.

And there are moths.

Large ones pass close to the face in the dimness, broad-winged and powder-soft, colored like bark, bruised leaves, wet ash, old ivory. They come through roof cracks or hatch from pupae tucked in the fern shade. Their wingbeats briefly disturb the air with a soft, blind insistence. Some visit pale flowers that open under leaves and smell faintly of fermenting fruit. Others do not feed at all as adults; they are only the winged moment of a life mostly spent chewing in secrecy.

What lives there, too, is memory—but not memory in a human form.

The old wall is inhabited. Moss lives in its seams. Frogs sit on its warm stones. Ants use its top as a causeway. A snake hunts along its curve. Fine roots enter the spaces between fitted blocks and widen them with the steady innocence of growth. The three leaning slabs under the root fall have become a niche for damp, shadow, webbing, and occasional eggs. Whatever hand placed them there has been replaced by occupancy. The chamber has not preserved the builders. It has digested their gesture.

If anything like a presiding creature belongs to the place, it would not be a visible monarch. It would be something assembled from recurrence: the watcher behind the fungal sheen, the slow recoil of root, the brightness in impossible pool-reflections, the frogs arranged on warm stones as though attending instruction. Perhaps there is an old amphibian somewhere in the rear passage, broad-headed, blind in one eye, living longer than such a body ought to live. Perhaps there is a colony of roots and fungus sharing signals through wet stone. Perhaps the hollow itself is only unusually good at making attention feel reciprocal.

Its inhabitants have certain traits in common.

They are soft-footed or footless. They are damp-skinned, jointed, furred, scaled, shelled, or filamentous. They have widened senses for darkness: whisker, toe pad, lateral line, antenna, tongue, skin. Many are nearly blind; many are all eyes. They do not waste sound. They do not waste motion. They know how to become part of a surface and then detach from it at the necessary instant. Their colors are those of things half-hidden: lichen-gray, mud-black, fern-green, root-white, copper underside, bruised violet, wet bark brown, the pale cream of mineral deposits and larvae.

They live by waiting for arrivals.

Rain arrives, and eggs hatch. Fruit drops from an unseen tree, and flies gather, then beetles, then ants, then fungus. A bat dies, and the floor receives a feast. A branch falls through a crack and becomes architecture, then food, then soil. Light arrives in broken columns, and gnats convene. Heat arrives not as event but as condition, held in stone, keeping blood quick and rot active.

No inhabitant is separate for long. The shrew eats the beetle. The snake eats the frog. The mites eat the snake’s shed skin. The fungus enters the fallen moth. The fern roots drink from the softened place where something died. Even fear is shared and recycled: a sudden stillness moves outward from one body to another, frog to fly to gecko to shrew, until the chamber holds its breath; then the tiny sounds resume.

So whoever lives there is a compressed underworld community: frogs like wet stones with eyes, blind fish like thoughts in dark water, snakes like ropes taught patience, bats folded into the ceiling, shrews burning beneath old stairs, insects making nations out of rot, fungi listening from dead wood, roots descending as slow animals from the daylit world above.

And beneath all of them, or around all of them, lives the hollow’s own appetite: quiet, warm, green, and continuous.
