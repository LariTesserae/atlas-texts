What lives there is not a single creature so much as a citizenship of rooted things, pressed so closely together that individual bodies have become a public anatomy.

They are plants, mostly, but not plants in the meadow sense: not separate stems rising politely from soil, each with its own allotment of light and air. They are fused, opportunistic, communal. They live by contact. Root into root, bark against moss, lichen over wood, fungus through peat, reed through shrub through dwarf tree. Their lives overlap until it becomes difficult to say where one organism ends and another has merely taken up the same intention.

The inhabitants are the city’s materials and its builders.

There are dwarf conifers with black-green needles, no taller than a person in the most sheltered places and much lower on the cold edge. They do not grow straight so much as lean, brace, and interlock. Their trunks are thin, resinous, and tense, always pulled slightly by the weight of vines, snowmelt, and the plants that have climbed them. Many have two or three crowns, or no clear crown at all, only a web of needled limbs absorbed into the overhead roof. They are patient, hard, slow-blooded things, drinking minerals from the young rock through root systems far larger than their visible bodies.

Among them grow woody reeds, sedges, and cold shrubs that behave almost like masonry. Their stems thicken where pressure falls on them. They bend until they meet neighboring growth, then fuse or become bound in living rope. Some rise as bundled columns, each one made of dozens or hundreds of small lives that have accepted the same vertical task. Others spread low, making floor, curb, mat, threshold. They have no concept of street or chamber, but they recognize weight, wetness, shadow, and gap; in answering those conditions, they make architecture.

Mosses are everywhere. They are the majority by surface, perhaps by soul. Fat cushion mosses swell over old wood and stone. Frost-moss on the upper terraces grows in pale, tight nap, silver when rimed, blue-green when thawed. Dark bog mosses pack the lower hollows, storing water until they become almost animal in their spring and give. Filamentous moss trails from roofs like wet hair. Some of it is so fine that it seems less like a plant than a texture of cold air made visible. The mosses are the city’s skin, insulation, sponge, memory. They bury everything, but they also preserve everything by taking it in.

There are lichens like old paint, like ash, like green glass, like parchment. They live on exposed bark, on dead trunks, on the rare stone surfaces that have not yet been covered by thicker growth. Some form crusts over schist and mica seams, patiently eating light and mineral. Some hang in pale fans from the shadowed roofs, translucent at the edges, catching dim brightness where leaves cannot. They are among the first colonists of fresh surfaces and among the last visible signs before stone disappears into the green mass.

Fungi inhabit the undercity.

They are not merely mushrooms, though small fruiting bodies appear along the root stairs: white pins, brown cups, black tongues, waxy little knobs like beads of frozen fat. The true fungal life is inside the city’s body. Threads run through peat, old cambium, dead leaves, drowned wood. They bind decay to nourishment. They decide, invisibly, which fallen trunk becomes a wall, which mat collapses into a hollow, which root mass can feed another generation. Pale fungal fans light the lowest chambers with no light of their own, only a faint presence, a suggestion of color where color has nearly been abandoned.

There are also plants that seem almost amphibious: thick translucent leaves around the dark pools, inflated with air bubbles, veined faintly green as if reluctant to choose between water and leaf. They grow half-submerged, with stems descending into the black cold below. Their roots are not anchored in soil but in woven rot and drowned wood. They breathe through skins that look like ice. When the pool rims freeze, they are not killed; they wait under the film, holding their little pockets of air like thoughts.

In the lowest chambers live the pale ones.

They are not numerous, but they are distinctive: ivory shoots, waxen membranes, fungal-partnered stems, plants reduced almost to gestures. They cannot compete above. They do not seek open sky. They live from rot, seepage, reflected gray, and the charity of the upper layers. Some may bloom rarely, if blooming is the right word—small colorless cups opening in darkness, receiving no bee, no breeze, only condensation. Their life is an economy of almost nothing. A mineral trace, a thread of sugar traded through root or fungus, a season of thaw measured drop by drop.

The place also contains dead inhabitants that have not ceased to belong.

The dead trunks are citizens still. Hollowed spires, broken towers, buried beams, drowned roots, old leaves pressed into black floors: all are occupied, reinforced, repurposed. Death here is not vacancy. A fallen stem becomes an avenue. A rotted core becomes a room. A split trunk becomes a cistern. Dead matter does not leave the city; it changes profession.

The dominant life-form, then, may be the mat itself: an aggregate organism, not genetically one, but behaviorally one. It senses through moisture and pressure. It responds through swelling, root growth, frost-heave, rot, and repair. It tightens around stone. It seals wounds with resin and moss. It diverts water away from one hollow and toward another. It consumes its own ruins. It expands where shelter permits and recoils from the scouring wind. It is not intelligent in the manner of an animal, but it has a kind of distributed insistence. It knows, through every growing tip, that nothing bare may remain.

Animals live there only as punctuation.

A few black beetles move through the lichen and bark seams, armored against cold, slow enough to seem carved until they change direction. Their legs negotiate entire neighborhoods of moss. They feed on fungus, softened wood, perhaps each other’s eggs. They are not abundant. A beetle crossing an avenue might be the only animal motion for an hour.

Pale moths cling to bark and dead leaves, their wings colored like ash or old frost. They fly rarely and badly in the dense air, making short, ghostlike transfers from trunk to trunk. Their caterpillars, if present, must be hidden in the mats, feeding in the safest folds of the city’s fabric.

In the pools are transparent larvae, glassy and threadlike, suspended among submerged stems. They barely disturb the water. They are almost indistinguishable from bubbles, root hairs, or scratches of light. Some will become midges or small flies, but never in enough number to make a swarm. The cold edits them down.

A single thin bird may pass through: needle-billed, quiet-footed, made for gleaning. It is not truly resident in the way the moss is resident. It visits the seedheads, bark cracks, and roof edges, taking what small animal life it can find before vanishing. Its presence proves that the city is legible to outsiders, but not hospitable to many. It is a passerby in a nation of plants.

There may be mites in the peat, springtails under bark, nematodes in films of water, rotifers in the moss cups, microbial multitudes everywhere moisture holds. These are the hidden mobile citizens: the small processors, grazers, dissolvers, swimmers in droplets. They live at scales where a bead of water is a pond and a decomposing leaf is a district. If the larger city speaks in drips, they are part of the soundless grammar beneath that speech.

But the principal resident is the vegetal collective: a cold-grown, high-altitude urban organism made of many species bound into one civic behavior.

It lives by slowing things.

It slows water until water becomes structure.  
It slows decay until decay becomes foundation.  
It slows growth until growth becomes pressure.  
It slows sound until the place seems secret.  
It slows death until nothing quite departs.

Its hunger is not predatory, but it is absolute. It wants surfaces. It wants fractures. It wants seepage, shade, and the brief gray light. It wants the exposed young rock not as a view but as food, anchor, and future floor. It advances in films and cushions, in root tips and lichen crusts, in sagging roofs and tightening mats. It does not invade quickly. It occupies completely.

If one had to name what lives there, one might call it a saddle-bog forest, or an alpine root-city, or a young cold green reef. But those are outside names. From within, it is less a forest than a body with streets in it: moss for skin, trunks for bones, roots for muscles, water for blood, fungi for memory, deadwood for ancestry, leaves for roofs, pools for eyes.

It has no ruler and no plan. Yet every inhabitant obeys the same severe laws:

hold warmth,  
hold water,  
hold fast,  
waste nothing,  
cover the wound of stone.

And so what lives there is a crowded republic of cold plants, fungi, seep-water, and minute animals, all gathered into one dense, dim, dripping intelligence of growth.
