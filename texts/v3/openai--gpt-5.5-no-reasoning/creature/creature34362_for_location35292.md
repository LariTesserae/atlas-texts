What lives there is not a people, though people have come close enough to leave arguments in stone.

It is not one creature either, not in the way a deer is one creature, or a bird, or a salamander under a shelf. The hollow is inhabited by something more like a condition given appetite. A wet intelligence. A firstness. A local will of water and unfinished gravity, clothed in rain because rain is the only body it has learned to keep.

It lives in the central pool most visibly, but it is not contained there. The pool is its dark eye, or mouth, or navel; the rain its skin; the crawling threads up the wall its fingers. When the tides lift and fall without a sea, that is its breathing. When the shelves empty and refill, that is the flex of its ribs. When fresh stone breaks and accepts water immediately, that is not damage but growth. The thing is not eroding the hollow from outside. It is growing the hollow from within.

It may be very young.

It may be older than anything around it.

Both seem true because it has never completed itself enough for age to settle.

If you watched long enough from one of the stacked platforms, soaked past fear, deafened past language, you would begin to see how the place’s motions are not random. The upward rain comes after certain deep booms below the pool. The small tides follow no moon, but they do answer the clicking shells. The silt fans are made, erased, and made again in nearly the same shapes, each time more exact, as if something is practicing deltas before it is trusted with a continent. Pebbles rise and turn not because they are weightless, but because they are being inspected.

The inhabitant handles the world clumsily and with immense patience.

It tries out riverbeds the width of a thumb. It invents shores, then drowns them. It lifts a mound of water above the pool and holds it trembling, perhaps imagining a hill, perhaps remembering one. It draws threads upward along the walls, learning roots from the moss and reversing them. It studies the black shrubs because they survive being struck. It studies shells because they speak without breath. It studies abandoned tools because they are proof that hands once negotiated matter successfully.

The bowl on the platform is for it.

Or was.

Those who climbed here knew enough not to worship the rain as weather. They understood that the hollow was occupied, though perhaps they never agreed on what by. They came with things the mountain could not make by itself: drilled weights, braided reed, a chipped blade, a bone awl, sea-shells carried impossibly high. Not luxuries. Lessons. Small demonstrations of form.

A cord says: length can be made from twisting.

A blade says: fracture can be chosen.

A bowl says: hollowing can hold.

A shell says: the sea has made chambers before you.

The inhabitant has kept them all, but not safely. It has soaked them, crusted them, softened them, rung them, hidden them under water and revealed them again. It does not preserve as a shrine preserves. It learns by weathering.

The nearest thing to its face appears only sometimes.

During the briefest thinning of rain, when the far wall leaps into clarity and the shell line jerks white against the dark pool, a pale form will rise beneath the surface. It is broad and slow, too smooth to be stone, too unfinished to be animal. It has no head one can point to. It presses upward from below like a question under skin. Rain hammers the water above it flat, but the shape does not break the surface. It waits just beneath, touching the boundary between air and depth with the delicacy of a tongue against teeth.

There may be limbs folded under it.

There may be none.

Some have seen branching whiteness there, like fingers made of drowned roots. Others would swear it was a back, a shoulder, the flank of something curled around the mountain’s hidden heart. Once, during a surge, a ring of openings appeared just below the surface, evenly spaced, each drinking rain without ripple. Gills, perhaps. Or drains. Or the first draft of gills, copied from no animal yet living.

It does not hunt. There is almost nothing to hunt.

The tiny crustaceans in the pool may be its lice, or its thoughts, or simply creatures that have learned to live in the folds of a god not yet certain it is a god. The eyeless salamander is closer to a priest than prey. It comes out when the rain strengthens and opens its mouth to the impacts, taking into itself whatever minute nourishment falls from the inhabitant’s body: minerals, spores, broken light, a taste of impossible pressure. Its translucent skin shows threads of silver in its blood. Its bones are faintly green. It knows the hollows beneath the shelves better than any visitor could, and it is never washed away.

The black shrubs are also residents, though unwilling ones at first. They live by refusing to be persuaded. Their roots enter cracks that open, close, and fill; their leaves shed water so violently each drop leaves like a thrown bead. They are bent daily into submission and daily forget to remain bowed. The inhabitant has learned stubbornness from them. That is why some tides come back after withdrawing. That is why the central pool rises again and again into a mound: the water is practicing refusal.

The moss is older in manner, if not in years. It clings where the rain is less a beating and more a continuous pressure. It accepts everything, compacts itself, brightens. It does not argue with the place. It is the hollow’s memory laid flat.

The lichens high on the walls are its calendar, though not a calendar of days. They mark experiments: how high the water stood when it tried to become a lake; where spray lingered when the rain briefly fell sideways for a month; which seams in the rock bled lime, iron, copper, salt. The blue lichen that glows at an angle is not decoration. It is the place thinking in a form slow enough to remain visible.

And there are the human traces.

Not inhabitants now, but not gone.

The people who came here must have been small against the noise, wrapped in soaked hide, hair plastered down, eyes narrowed against the spray. They would have moved by touch: hand on shoulder, fist against chest, palm to stone. Perhaps they came in pairs because no one alone could hold enough courage. Perhaps they sent the young, because the young can still believe a place may answer. Perhaps they sent the old, because the old already know that answers seldom arrive as speech.

They could not have slept here. They could barely have breathed here. So they visited, worked quickly, left offerings that were also instruments, and descended changed.

Some came to ask for water below in dry seasons.

Some came to ask the mountain not to split further.

Some came to hear the enormous syllable and carry its rhythm back in their bones.

Some came because children had dreamed of shells clicking on a summit where no shell should be.

The inhabitant did not bless them. It had no such category. But it noticed the repeated pattern of their return. It learned that beings may leave and still belong to a place by coming back. The shell line remains because of this. In the thickest roar, when the cord snaps taut and the shells clack with their thin dry underwater teeth, the hollow remembers visitors.

Not their names.

Their interval.

There may be dead among the pool’s depths. Not corpses recognizable as human; the place alters whatever falls into it. A hand would become a limestone fan. A tooth might become a white pebble with a dark center. Hair might feed the pale algae. A bone needle might return years before it was dropped. The central pool has no malice, but it is not a grave in any ordinary sense. It does not keep endings intact. It drafts them into other beginnings.

The true resident, then, is a beginning that has failed to choose its final shape.

It is a sea-seed.

A rain-womb.

A mountain’s unfinished organ.

A small god of hydrology before hydrology has agreed to be a science.

It lives by impact. It feeds on falling. It thinks through pressure and response. It knows the taste of limestone, shale, volcanic glass, bark fiber, bone dust, shell calcium, moss-spore, clay cloud, high air. It does not know warmth except as absence. It does not know silence except as something it prevents. It does not understand sky, though it receives sky constantly. It does not understand visitors, but it has learned the outline of a hand from the things hands leave behind.

If it ever fully wakes, the mountain may change.

Not catastrophically, perhaps. Not with fire or avalanche. More likely the laws will settle in one direction. A spring will burst from the summit and run down both sides forever. Or the pool will find the ancient sea it has been answering and open a vertical road to it. Or the rain will stop all at once, leaving a wet stone room full of tools, shells, moss, and a single dark basin reflecting a clear sky like an eye that has just closed.

But for now it remains in practice.

It lifts water.

Drops it.

Calls stone soft.

Calls falling a kind of standing.

Calls the small bowl full, though everything is full.

Calls the shells to speak.

Calls the salamander out to feed.

Calls the shrubs to bow and rise.

It is alone only if one believes a place must contain a body to be inhabited. But the hollow is crowded with one presence, spread thin and forceful through every falling line. The resident has no doorway because the rain is all doorway. It has no voice because the roar is before voice. It has no face because the pool has not yet decided which side of itself should look outward.

What lives there is the storm’s child and the mountain’s doubt.

It is still becoming.

And because it is still becoming, everything around it must become also.
