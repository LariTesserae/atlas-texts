The people who live there are small in the way cave-things become small—not stunted, not diminished, but folded inward by generations of doorways that require bowing and ladders that reward lightness. They are spare, long-fingered, narrow-shouldered, with strong calves and toughened soles. Their bodies know ledges before level ground. Even standing still, they seem braced for vibration: knees soft, weight spread, one hand near a wall, a rail, a cord, a neighbor.

They have the pallor of people who live in light but not open sun. Not ghost-white exactly; their skin ranges from warm beige to ocher-brown to ash-gray, but most carry an undercast of bone-light, as if the ivory glow has entered them and never quite left. Their faces are lined early around the eyes from squinting through glare. Their brows are often marked with soot paste or matte mineral dust to cut reflection. Children wear dark smears under their eyes before they are old enough to understand vanity.

Their ears are shaped by the city, not outwardly perhaps, but in habit. Many bind soft pads or shell cups over them at midday. Older people often hear poorly in the ordinary sense, yet perceive more than outsiders do: the change in a ladder’s buzz, the thickening of air in a vent, the faint wrongness in a garden membrane, the pressure-drop before a still storm. They do not ask, “Did you hear that?” They touch the wall and ask, “Did you feel it turn?”

They are not a silent people, though they are not a speaking people in the usual way.

Their language lives in several bodies at once.

There is the close language: murmured directly into the ear, lips almost touching skin, used for affection, curses, secrets, prayer, bargaining too delicate for witnesses.

There is the hand language: quick, complex, beautiful, capable of argument, gossip, poetry, insult, law. Children learn it first as play, then grammar, then inheritance. Outsiders mistake it for frantic gesturing until they realize half a conversation may be happening behind someone’s back, under a table, across a market trough.

There is the foot language: stamps, heel-drags, toe taps, weight-drops on resonant planks. It is used for distance within the city, for warnings, for work coordination. Different floors answer differently. A message sent along a bridge may not mean the same thing when sent through the central rib.

There is the color language: strips of dyed cloth tied at vents, doorways, baskets, ladders. Red-black for heat danger. Pale blue for root-work underway. Yellow ocher for opened crack. White knots for death rites. Three fluttering greenish silver ribbons at an entry mean: do not bring salt-spice within; the plants are feeding.

And then there is the city-language, which no single person speaks. It consists of shutters, reed stops, hanging baffles, chimes, wall-taps, open holes, plugged pores, polished ribs. Gardeners, vent-keepers, builders, and elders converse with the bone itself, altering what the city says to its crops, its dead, its sleeping infants.

They call themselves something that outsiders might translate as the Housed-in-Breath, or the Bone-Held, or the People of the Inner Tone. Their own word is less a noun than a gesture: two fingers touched to the throat, then opened toward the wall.

They do not think of the bone as merely shelter.

It is ancestor, instrument, weather, boundary, law. Not a god exactly, because gods may be begged and blamed, while the bone simply is. But shrines are set into its quieter hollows. Offerings are made at old stress cracks. Babies are held against warm satin wall within their first day of life so they may “learn the body that holds them.” Lovers press their palms to the same patch of polished curve and feel, through vibration, whether the other is laughing.

They do not know what creature left the bone. They have stories, of course. Too many.

One says the world was once filled with walking mountains made of meat, and this bone belonged to the last of them, who lay down in mercy to become a house for the thirsty.

One says the bone is not dead at all, only sleeping through a season longer than history, and that the city’s noise is its dreaming breath.

One says the first inhabitants were not people but plants, and humans arrived later as servants to carry sound where roots could not.

Children prefer the version in which the giant swallowed the sea, walked inland, died of thirst, and left its rib full of trapped songs.

The people dress in layered fibers, tight where fabric might catch on ladders, loose where air must move. Their everyday clothes are made of split leaf, softened hide, woven root-hair, dust-cloth rubbed with wax. Their garments are practical and densely repaired. Patches are not hidden; they are often arranged in patterns marking family, trade, or district. Broad visors are common, as are neck-wraps that can be pulled over the mouth during dust gusts. At festivals, they wear glossy plant lacquer, shell disks, copper beads, moth-wing veils, carved bone pendants that knock lightly at the collarbone.

Jewelry is often audible. Not loud—nothing personal can compete with the city—but intimate. Tiny clicking beads at the wrist. A pendant tuned to hum against the sternum. An ankle ring that marks the gait of a dancer or marriageable youth. Mourning ornaments are deliberately mute: matte cords, soft ash-thread, seed pods emptied so they make no sound.

Their hair is usually braided, wrapped, or cut close. Loose hair is a hazard near resin lamps, spinning wheels, and vibrating reed shafts. Many oil their hair with seed wax and dust it with pale mineral powder. Gardeners often shave the sides of the head so they can press their temples to wall or stem and read vibrations clearly.

Their food is dry, dense, and strongly flavored. They eat curled sheets of leaf softened in oil, cakes of pollen and ground seed, salt-bright crystals with edible kernels trapped inside, bitter resins scraped thin and mixed with ash-spice, flour-tree shavings baked on hot stone, pale tubers grown in humming chambers, moth paste, beetle meal, rare strips of sun-dried scavenger meat traded from the flats. Moist foods are ceremonial. A mouthful of gel-fruit at a birth feast can make an old person weep. To waste liquid by letting it drip down the chin is obscene unless done ritually for the dead.

They do not drink casually. Moisture is taken in bulbs, sealed skins, softened paste, root-gel, or vapor captured in cloth and sucked slowly. A guest offered unbound water would understand either profound honor or reckless madness.

They have professions shaped by the city’s needs.

The vent-keepers are among the most respected. They rise before dawn and climb to the slit mouths and high pores to read the first wind. Their work is dangerous: a mistake may starve a garden, poison a nursery, or crack an old wall. They carry wax seals, shell knives, warning ribbons, plugs of fiber, and hollow tubes for testing breath.

The gardeners are not quiet pastoral figures but acousticians, chemists, climbers, midwives. They know the moods of lung-vines and bell creepers, which leaves prefer market-roar, which roots must be fed ancestral ash, which pods swell best under low night purr. They tune growth with mallets, whistles, shutters, and their own voices pressed into plant cups.

The scaffolders and bonewrights maintain the city’s skeleton within the skeleton: ladders, bridges, pegs, braces, shelves, suspended rooms. They can smell when a lashing has dried too far. They read stress lines in old bone the way others read letters.

The hush-tenders serve the Wall of Names. They prepare the dead, seal the niches, trim the roots that enter them, and keep that curve as quiet as possible. Their hands are stained gray with ash and resin. They speak gently, even in sign. Children half fear them and half seek them out, because they tell the oldest stories.

There are potters who work with salt-clay and ash temper, making vessels small-mouthed by necessity. There are soot-makers, glare-cloth weavers, shell polishers, seed grinders, chime carvers, moth netters, lizard watchers, insect sweepers, traders, sign-teachers, memory keepers, and those who travel outside.

The outside-travelers are strange even to their own people. They have darker skin, thicker veils, scarred lips, and a habit of pausing too long when they return, overwhelmed by walls after horizon. They cross the flats to meet caravans or harvest rare salt crusts, vent minerals, shells from ancient beds, scavenger sinew, copper beads, stories. Some claim the open plain has its own sound, too large and low for city ears.

Their social life is dense, unavoidable, interdependent. Privacy exists, but it is made by custom rather than distance. A sleeping cell may be divided from a walkway by only woven screen and hanging roots, yet to look through without permission is a serious violation. Lovers learn to recognize the difference between concealment and secrecy. Arguments become public quickly if foot-stamps enter the floor. Children are raised by families, neighbors, trade-clusters, and whoever happens to be within grabbing distance when they start climbing somewhere foolish.

They are tender with children and severe about carelessness. A child may be indulged in noise, movement, questions, mimicry, but never in leaving a vessel unsealed, blocking a vent, striking an untuned rib, wasting gel, or entering a breath-garden with the wrong dust on their hands. Their first toys are safe rattles, soft chimes, miniature shutters, hand-sign games, carved models of vents and cracks. Their first lessons are: hold the wall, cover the mouth, tie the warning strip, never trust quiet.

Their festivals are not escapes from labor but intensifications of it.

At the Festival of Full Roar, when the season’s fiercest winds arrive, the city opens selected pores and lets the bone become almost unbearable. Everyone wears ear pads and chest wraps. The gardens tremble visibly. Market pods shed sweet dust. Dancers stamp on reinforced floors, not to make music—music is already everywhere—but to shape the roar into patterns the body can survive. Children are lifted high to feel the strongest tones through their ribs.

At the Night of Soft Bone, vents are narrowed and baffles hung until the city falls into its gentlest purr. People speak aloud then, extravagantly, even those whose voices are cracked from disuse. Old songs are sung close and low. The Wall of Names is visited. The dead are told what has changed: new cracks, new babies, failed crops, good trades, marriages, quarrels resolved or not.

Their religion, if called that, is practical and intimate. They pray by maintenance. They polish a resonant rib as devotion. They clear dust from a star-hole for remembrance. They feed ancestral ash to a root with thanks but without sentimentality. They believe neglect is a kind of blasphemy. They believe sound carries obligation: if you feel warning through the wall and do nothing, the fault enters your bones.

They fear stillness most.

Not silence as rest—the city has softer hours—but true stillness, the dead absence of wind, the plants hanging slack, the ladders no longer trembling, the bone giving no answer under the palm. Stories of still-air storms are told in flattened gestures. During such times, poisonous breath may gather from below, crops may close their mouths, children may panic because their cradles stop humming. The city becomes not peaceful but blind.

They also fear open wetness. Outsiders imagine they must dream of rain, but many find the idea horrifying: water falling everywhere, soaking fiber, swelling doors, rotting ancestor niches, drowning roots that breathe through air, turning precious dust to paste. Rain belongs to myth because myth can contain terror safely. A traveler who once saw a coastal storm is treated with fascination and pity.

They are not primitive in mind, though their tools are ancient. Their knowledge is precise, empirical, inherited through hand, ear, skin, and ritual repetition. They can diagnose a crop by the tremor of a leaf edge. They can calculate whether a crack must be sealed or sung open. They can tell which caravan arrived by the rhythm of its animals outside the slit. They possess maps not only of space but of resonance: diagrams carved into tablets showing chambers by pitch, vents by seasonal behavior, plantings by required tone.

Their art is everywhere because bare utility would be unbearable in such closeness. Screens are woven with patterns that seem abstract until wind moves them and their shadows flutter like remembered water. Door lintels are carved with curled lines representing old tones. Shell tiles are set where morning light will ignite them for only a few breaths. Stories are painted in soot and mineral on the undersides of walkways, visible to children climbing below. Dancers perform with hands, feet, shoulders, and gaze, their bodies speaking against the city’s thunder.

Their music is not melody as outsiders understand it. It is tuning, interruption, sympathy. A skilled performer may stand in a corridor with reed stops and a palm drum, altering the existing roar until hidden overtones bloom. Audiences listen with palms on floorboards, backs against walls, teeth lightly touching. Applause is a ripple of hand-sign and heel taps.

Outsiders struggle there. They arrive too loud in the wrong ways and too deaf in the necessary ones. They leave vessels open, shout when they should touch, flinch at harmless tremors, miss danger in quiet corners. The Bone-Held are not cruel to them, but they are watchful. Hospitality is real and bounded: a guest is assigned a guide, a sleeping niche away from delicate gardens, a sealed drinking bulb, ear pads, and a list of prohibitions repeated until understood.

Some outsiders never leave. A caravan child orphaned by salt fever. A fugitive who learns to tend moth cages. A trader who marries a vent-keeper. These adopted people are known by their first long season of mistakes. They may become beloved, but their descendants will still be teased for “flat-walking” if they stomp without listening.

The people are not numerous. Perhaps a few hundred live within the bone, perhaps fewer, depending on drought, crop tone, and caravan luck. But they think of themselves as a city, not a village, because they contain specialization, memory, law, trade, districts, strangers, markets, shrines, public works, and old arguments nobody can fully end. Their world is compact, but its inner life is urban: layered, negotiated, crowded with expertise.

They have names for every part of their home. The Dawn Throat. The Bitter Stair. The Mother Hum. The Cracked Ear. The Market Marrow. The Blue Pore. The Low Sleep. The Wall of Names. The Lizard Door. The Chamber That Sickened Three Children. The New Note. The Place Where Dust Turns Sweet.

They live in a dead giant’s bone, but they are not morbid people. Death surrounds them as material fact: bone shelter, ancestral ash, dried bodies, extinct marrow, old calcium feeding pale roots. Yet their daily existence is busy, funny, skilled, affectionate, irritated, hungry, inventive. They laugh with shoulders when the roar is too high for sound. They gossip in flashing fingers. They flirt by matching another’s foot rhythm on a bridge. They mourn by muting ornaments and feeding the gardens. They measure wealth in sealed moisture, tuned vents, healthy roots, trustworthy neighbors, and children who know when not to strike the central rib.

What lives there is a people who have made a civilization out of insufficiency.

They have taken no rivers, no fields, no shade trees, no rain, almost no animals, almost no metal, and from light, salt, breath, bone, and deafening wind they have made streets, harvests, customs, instruments, cemeteries, jokes, markets, festivals, and lullabies.

They are the inhabitants of an ivory hollow that never stops singing.

And when they sleep, they sleep as their first ancestors must have learned to sleep: suspended in warm brightness, rocked by the enormous voice of the bone, while above them the pale gardens open their listening mouths and feed on the city’s breath.
