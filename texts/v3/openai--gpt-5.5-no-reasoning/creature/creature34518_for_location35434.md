What lives there is not one people, not one dominant form, but a whole society of exposure: creatures made low, slick, armored, pale, and loud, all adapted to a world with no softness except flesh.

The place is inhabited by things that have learned to make a life between stone and water.

Most of them are close to the ground. They do not rise unless they must. Height is dangerous in a flat land where wind has nothing to break against and every shape is visible for miles. Bodies here tend toward flattening: bellies wide, backs low, limbs jointed outward, heads narrow, tails forked or dragging, skins tight and wet-looking. Even the creatures that fly seem as if they came reluctantly from crawling ancestors, as if the sky were only another surface they have learned to scrape.

There are the waders first: pale, deliberate hunters with long splayed feet and knees that bend too sharply backward. They move through the shallows like tools being operated by hidden hands. Their bodies are narrow and hunched, their backs the color of wet bone or diluted clay. Their heads are chisel-shaped, almost faceless until they strike; then a seam opens, a hard bill snaps downward, and something translucent is lifted writhing from a crack.

They are not graceful in the usual sense. Their grace is mechanical. They pause for minutes at a time, feeling tiny movements through the water with their feet. The joints of their legs lock and unlock with dry clicks. When several hunt together, they spread out in a loose arc and drive small prey toward mineral seams, communicating with bill-clacks and low throat pulses that travel through the shallow water.

Their eyes are set high, glossy and dark, protected by a cloudy inner lid. When rain hardens, they do not hide. They simply lower their heads, angle their bills into the weather, and wait until the world becomes legible again.

Below them, in the broader pools, live the shell-creatures.

They are ancient-looking even if they are newly hatched. Each is a dark, rounded shield with a rim of small working limbs underneath, a living stone that scrapes, churns, and grinds. Their shells are layered with mineral growths, scars, pale rings of old attachment, places where others have rasped them clean. Some carry whole communities on their backs: eggs glued into grooves, smaller scavengers tucked beneath rear plates, thin parasites like white threads waving from joints.

They move slowly until disturbed. Then the shield lifts, the many legs beat, and the creature scuttles with horrible speed through inches of water, vanishing under silt it has stirred itself. Their mouths are underneath, hidden and industrious. They rasp mineral films from stone, scrape the softened edges of clay shelves, consume stranded larvae, and occasionally grind down the dead.

Their sound is part of the place: a steady, communal abrasion, like wet knives sharpened in another room.

There are also the shoals.

They are flat, quick, slate-colored swimmers, so thin that from above they seem almost like shadows detaching from the bottom. In the channels they pack together by the hundreds, turning as one body through narrow seams and flooded cracks. Their sides flash dull silver when they twist. Their mouths open and close constantly, sieving the water for microscopic animal life, gas-bladder larvae, powdered shell, perhaps even the mineral richness itself.

They know the pressure of the underground water before it rises. A shoal may sleep in a basin one moment and pour away the next, vanishing through a crack no wider than a hand. When trapped by hunters, they leap together in a single convulsion: hundreds of slate bodies snapping into the air and falling back like thrown gravel. The leap is not panic alone. It confuses eyes, ears, and surface tension. For an instant the pool becomes all bodies and noise, and some survive because no predator can choose quickly enough.

In cracks, vents, and under stone lips live smaller things: blind soft-bodied feeders, pale cords with mouths at both ends, translucent larvae held upright by gas bladders, little hinged scavengers that fold shut when shadow passes over them. Some never see open water except during rises. Some live in stone pores, emerging only when pressure pushes them out like seeds from a pod. They are the hidden abundance of the plain, the reason the larger creatures gather where nothing visible should sustain them.

There are predators that resemble mineral accidents.

A stain on black rock may be a body. A silted ridge beneath a pool may have eyes. Some hunters flatten themselves so completely against stone that water runs over them in an unbroken sheet. Their hides are mottled gray, black, and dull blue, textured like clay crust or wet sediment. They wait with mouths slightly open, sensing vibration through jawbones and belly plates. When prey comes close, the strike is less a lunge than a collapse of the landscape: stone becomes animal, mud becomes teeth.

Others hunt by using sound. In a world so loud, silence is camouflage but so is imitation. There are low-throated animals that can copy the bubbling of a rising seep or the distress clicks of trapped shell-creatures. They call from behind stone shelves, drawing waders or scavengers close enough to seize. Some juveniles of these predators shelter in old drilled holes and abandoned basins, their bodies no larger than a handspan, already practicing the noises that will feed them.

Above all this wheel the membrane-winged colonies.

They are not birds, though distance might forgive the mistake. Up close they are stranger: long rigid heads, narrow bodies, wings stretched on fingerlike spars, forked tails used as rudders in the gusting air. Their skins are gray, ash-white, black-blue, or the color of old shell. Their bones must be light, but their voices are enormous.

They occupy every ruin that rises even slightly above the plain. Roofless rings become nesting bowls. Wall gaps become brood shelves. Holes bored by vanished builders become amplifying chambers. They beat their wing membranes against stone hollows and shout into them, turning abandoned structures into instruments. Their colonies do not merely live in ruins; they perform them.

Their social life is loud, quarrelsome, and precise. They recognize one another by rattles, barks, shrieks, and throat buzzes. They court by drumming on stone. They warn of rising water with one call, gas vents with another, large submerged things with a third that can empty a basin of life in seconds. Eggs are glued high in cracks with a chalky secretion. Young cling head-down to damp stone and scream constantly, fed on regurgitated swimmers, larvae, scraps of membrane, and the soft parts of cracked shell-creatures.

They are scavengers, hunters, thieves, weather-watchers. When hundreds lift at once from a distant pool, everything nearby understands that something worth fearing or eating has arrived.

And there are the large things.

They are not common, or perhaps they are common but mostly unseen. They belong to the permanent channels, the deep-connected basins, the places where the plain dips just enough to remember being sea.

Some are immense flat-backed grazers, if grazing is the right word in a world without plants. They move under the water’s skin, scraping bottom and back at once, their broad bodies stirring dark wakes through water that should be too shallow to hold them. Their heads are wide, their mouths placed low, their feeding surfaces hard and ridged. They plane over submerged stone, grinding up mineral crusts, colonies of small animals, eggs, larvae, anything soft enough to be taken with the sediment. After they pass, smaller creatures pour into their trails to feed on what has been exposed.

Others are predators of the same shape but different patience. A whole pool may seem empty except for the slow drift of fog. Then the water wrinkles against the wind. Shell-creatures stop rasping. Waders lift their heads. A wake moves through water no deeper than a forearm, and something vast slides beneath the surface by forcing its body through mud and liquid together. Its back may break the water only once: a dark plate, scarred and ridged, shining like newly wetted stone.

The local animals know them by pressure before sight. The winged colonies rise. Shoals vanish. Stain-predators abandon their disguises and crawl for cracks. What cannot flee becomes food or becomes still enough to be mistaken for stone.

The ruins are inhabited too.

Not only occupied, but digested into the ecology.

Under fallen slabs live squat slick-bodied den animals with blunt heads and clever forefeet. They collect shell fragments, bone splinters, polished stones, and sometimes the drilled weights of ancient nets. Their nests are dry only by local standards. They line them with shed membranes, scraped hide, and powdered clay that absorbs moisture until it must be carried out and replaced. They are omnivorous, nocturnal or fog-active, and intensely territorial. Their warning call is a wet cough that echoes under platforms.

In old storage pits sleep clusters of small amphibious creatures, pressed together for warmth, steaming faintly in the cold mornings. Along causeways move long-legged scavengers that pick delicately through cracks with hooked toes. In the cup-marks of carved stones, eggs hatch in rainwater. In the blackened shelters, where fires once sulked, modern animals lick mineral salts from old ash and grease stains.

The built places have become cliffs, burrows, nurseries, traps, echo chambers, perches, and dens. Whatever made them is gone, diminished, hiding, or transformed into something that no longer builds so high.

But perhaps something handlike still lives there.

If it does, it is not a city-maker in the grand sense. It would be a crouching, weather-wise people, low to the ground like the rest of the fauna, wrapped in hides or membranes that never fully dry. Their hands would be strong and blunt-fingered, good for prying slabs, knotting gut cord, drilling stone with grit, scraping fat, setting traps in shallow channels. They would know fire as a difficult animal: fed with rendered fat, dried shell, bone, and rare drifted matter; guarded under stone lips; coaxed rather than commanded.

They would not measure wealth in fields or timber, but in routes, timings, shelters, and remembered water.

They would know which stones darken before rain. Which pools breathe together underground. Which calls mean a pressure-rise is coming. Which low platforms stay above the black water. Which shell-creatures are safe to crack and which carry poison. Which ruin will sing when the basin fills, and whether that song means warning, gathering, mourning, or prayer.

Their dwellings would be small because the world does not reward height. Their paths would be polished stepping stones. Their tools would be stone flakes, shell rasps, bone wedges, sinew nets weighted with pierced rock. Their records, if the marked slabs are records, would not be separate from weather. They would be grooves placed where water can complete them, cup-marks that hold bubbles during flood, pecked bands that become legible only when submerged.

Their knowledge would be amphibious. Nothing important would exist only dry.

They may be few. They may move between ruins rather than possess them. They may share shelters with animals uneasily, driving off winged colonies during one season and yielding the same walls to them in another. They may eat swimmers, larvae, shell-meat, rendered fat, and the pale blind things stranded when waters withdraw. They may listen more than they speak, because in that roaring place the ear is a survival organ.

Or they may be gone, leaving only arrangements that other lives have misunderstood usefully.

The dominant resident, then, is not a single creature but a condition: life stripped of greenness and forced into mineral intimacy.

Everything there listens through water and stone. Feet are wide to read mud. Bellies are sensitive to vibration. Shells are shaped against rain. Voices are sharpened by echoes. Colors are muted because brightness is an invitation. Nests are glued, not woven. Fuel is fat, not wood. Memory belongs to paths that vanish under inches of water and return slick with new danger.

The inhabitants are harsh but not barren. They are abundant in pulses, gathering wherever the plain briefly offers food: a draining pool, a pressure tide, a cracked basin, a carcass, a singing ruin. Then they scatter again into the enormous wet openness.

They live in a world without leaves, so nothing rustles.

Instead, everything clicks, rasps, slaps, shrieks, bubbles, grinds, and drips.

The life of that place is a cold chorus of shells and throats, low bodies and sudden wings, ancient water moving through ancient stone, and animals that have learned to make shelter out of timing because the land offers almost nothing else.
