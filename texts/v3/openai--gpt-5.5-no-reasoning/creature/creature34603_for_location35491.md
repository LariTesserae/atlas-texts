What lives there is not a species so much as a citizenship.

It is made of creatures that have learned to inhabit intention after the makers of that intention forgot it. They do not live in ruins. They live in the uses the ruins still suggest: corridor, stall, duct, nursery, threshold, cistern, platform, shaft. Their bodies have accepted the old city plan as an inherited skeleton.

There are the small ones first, because they are everywhere and because everything larger is partly built out of their labor.

In the warm cracks live mites colored like brick dust, pollen-gold, iron-red, mold-white. They herd spores along damp seams and vanish when the fans begin. Beetles occupy the bolted joints of the old girders, each species devoted to a different corrosion: one rasping orange rust into powder, another polishing blackened metal until it conducts well enough for certain vines. Silver larvae chew insulation into nesting floss. Blind springtails graze the pale lawns that grow behind switch boxes. Moths hang in ranked colonies on the heated conduits, wings closed over bodies soft as stored breath; when disturbed, they lift all at once and the air fills with labels, warnings, false eyes, little municipal maps printed in dust.

Ants, or something with the discipline of ants, own the dry tiles. Their bodies are translucent brown, full of greenish threads. They build covered roads from shredded paper, glandular resin, and filings of copper stolen from cable wounds. They farm a fungus that tastes of oil. Their soldiers have heads plated with flakes of old enamel paint, so that a patrol advancing through liverwort looks briefly like a procession of chipped signage.

There are rats, though not only rats.

They are long-faced, deft-handed, sleepless, fur darkened by generations of soot and condensation. Some have whiskers sensitive to current. Some carry seeds in cheek pouches and tuck them into warm cabinets where the black-leaved vines will sprout. They know the safe rails and the killing rails. They nest in ticket offices, in pipe elbows, inside vending machines softened by fungus. Their social lives are elaborate, scented, litigious. They trade in marrow, fruit, copper beads, dried grubs, fragments of salt that bloom on masonry. They steal from flowers and are punished by flowers. They bury their dead in dry substations, where roots eventually find them and raise small red leaves.

There are geckos pale as candle grease, with organs visible through the skin when they pass before a lamp. They hunt upside down on ceilings filmed with algae, pausing over puddles to drink falling drops before those drops touch the floor. Their eggs are glued to the backs of warning placards. When predators come too near, they flatten themselves against cracked tile and arrange their spots into the semblance of old mold.

Crabs live in the drains, not descended from any sea anyone remembers, but faithful to the idea of crabs: sidewise, armored, practical. Their shells are mottled with rust and algae. They prune the channels. They cut away roots that block flow and carry the pieces to their burrows. In the cisterns live white fish with no eyes and mouths like soft holes, feeding on larvae and falling spores. Some grow too large for their basins and spend their lives curved around the walls, turning slowly, dreaming in pressure and vibration.

Birds live there too, but they are not sky creatures anymore.

They are platform birds, girder birds, shaft birds. Their wings are short and loud. They build nests from wire, fiber, shed vine-skin, foil, hair, molted insect shell. The burrowing kind tunnels into fungal panels and lines the chambers with dry moss. The bright-throated kind nests in the housings of mechanical arms and screams when the old servos wake, then settles again as if machinery were weather. Some birds migrate only from one end of a conveyor belt to the other, following the trays as they pass through warm zones, wet zones, larval zones. Their songs imitate compressors, dripping valves, the soft alarm-chirp of failing relays. A whole flock can sound like a machine remembering its instructions incorrectly.

The mammals in the kiosk row are thin and deliberate, with narrow faces, small black eyes, and tongues long enough to vanish to the root in a flower’s throat. Their fur is dust-colored, except around the mouth, where nectar and soot stain it dark. They work in shifts. They know which blossoms open to vibration, which to heat, which to the chemical breath of passing herds. They do not waste motion. They enter a kiosk through a broken service hatch, cross rafters furred with moss, drink from seven hanging flowers, defecate into a slit where seedlings wait, and depart by a route used by their mothers and their mothers’ mothers. Around their dens are heaps of rejected seeds, splinters of chitin, paper scraps bitten into bedding. They look fragile until something threatens the nursery; then thirty of them arrive in silence with their needle teeth exposed.

Larger grazers move through the lanes with the assurance of commuters.

They are low-backed, shell-ridged animals with blunt mouths and hooves split wide for root-mats. Their hides carry colonies of moss and small flies. They browse the swelling leaves that grow after the sweating hour, scrape mineral bloom from walls, chew softened signage, lick salt from handrails. Their routes are older than any one animal. The vegetation knows them. Corridors remain open because their bodies pass through. Certain roots lift slightly at their approach; certain seedpods wait for the pressure pattern of their hooves before clicking open and lodging burrs in the animals’ flank moss. Calves are hidden in abandoned stairwells where dry vines make a thorny cage.

Behind the hanging curtains, behind membranes that seem to be walls until they breathe, are the large shy things.

One is velvet-backed, almost deerlike until it turns and shows the lateral fold of its jaw, the soft plates along the throat, the double set of nostrils for steam and air. Its antlers may be antlers, or antennae, or fungal growths tended by blood. They brush the bell-shaped mushrooms and make them ring: one note for passing, one for alarm, one for mating. It drinks at sumps with extreme care, lowering its head between electrical roots that would stiffen at a careless touch.

One lives in the boiler tank. Amphibious, patient, old. Its skin is dark green-black and pebbled with mineral deposits. It sleeps in warm water among blind fish, its back mistaken for a submerged stone until the brief rains begin. Then it rises, pushes open a hatch with its forehead, and hauls itself into the lanes to feed on swollen leaves. Smaller animals climb onto it to eat parasites. Vines trail across its back and sometimes flower there. When the fans engage and the drying wind comes hard, it returns to the tank, leaving a path of crushed stems and wet prints that fill quickly with insects.

In the ticket hall lives the many-legged mother.

Not spider, not centipede, not machine, but using parts of all three arguments. She has jointed limbs polished by movement, a velvet abdomen, mandibles fine enough to peel larvae from moss, and a carapace patterned like dirty brass. She keeps her young inside the turnstiles. They cling to the bars in clusters, pale and bead-eyed, learning the rhythm of passing bodies. The mother maintains order in the hall. She removes the sick, eats the unwary, tolerates the careful. Her webbing is not silk alone but root fiber, stripped wire, fungal cord. It binds cracked machines to columns and columns to the ceiling, so that her territory is also a repair.

Above, along the high pipes, are the hunters.

They have glassy eyes and whiskered limbs, or featherless wings, or paws that feel magnetism through rust. Some are lean and catlike, with translucent teeth and tails used for balance among cable bundles. Some hang folded from pipe brackets and drop on prey passing below. Some hunt by current, sensing the little storms in nerves and hearts. At electrical night their pupils vanish and their faces become dark mirrors. Prey animals mark loose bolts, dry seedcases, alarm fungus. A grazer brushing a certain chain can make the whole lane rattle; a rat biting a warning stem can release a smell that sends the kiosk mammals flat into their holes.

But the animals are only half the inhabitants.

The plants are citizens with plans.

Black leaves broad as trays grow where light cannot justify them. Their surfaces are matte, but beneath them run coppery nerves that twitch when old cables wake. They feed on warmth, mineral seep, trace electricity, the carbon-rich breath of crowds. Some unfold toward the engines rather than the cracks of gray light above. They are not mistaken. They know their sun.

There are vines that imitate utilities. They run in straight lines along ceilings, bundle themselves like cables, enter walls through old conduit holes. Some carry water from sweating girders to root-built nurseries. Some conduct small charges. Some are parasitic on the old electrical system and bloom only after a shock, flowers opening in quick blue spasms, pollen released like smoke from a blown fuse.

There are roots with civic habits. They make ramps. They brace brick. They narrow unsafe passages and widen profitable ones. They grow handrail-smooth where animals rub them, bench-flat where bodies rest, stair-stepped where generations have climbed. They are not benevolent; they are responsive. Traffic feeds them. Droppings, shed skin, blood, spilled fruit, crushed insects, all settle into their mats. A clear road is a mouth held open.

Fungi are the law here, or the memory of law.

They arbitrate decay. They decide what remains a wall and what becomes food. Leathery panels spread over brick and concrete, patterned in bruised purples, greens, creams, and dull golds. They digest paint by color. They soften paper into loam. They enter dead machines and return them as mineral gardens. Some glow when touched. Some emit warnings chemical and precise. Some form ledgers of the place in their own tissue: rings of drought, bands of electrical abundance, scars where herds changed route, dark seams recording the year an engine stopped for seven days and the narcotic flowers almost took the lower market.

The flowers are dangerous because they are persuasive.

They open to engine rhythm. They open to silence. They open to breath. Some mimic lamps: green, reassuring, arranged at eye level along a path that narrows gradually until only small animals can pass and larger ones must either retreat or be held. Some produce fruit exactly the weight and shape preferred by a particular rodent jaw. Some smell like warm milk to mammals, like exposed copper to insects, like rain to amphibians. They are not intelligent in the human way, but they have inherited consequences. They have kept whatever worked.

The machines live there also.

Not as rulers. Not as ghosts. As old, blunt, persistent animals of another phylum.

Belts slap in their loops. Pumps cough. Fans inhale whole neighborhoods and exhale spores across borders. Compressors sleep and wake with the temper of hibernating beasts. Mechanical arms in the factory hall descend with obsolete tenderness, pruning the wrong thing so consistently that the wrong thing has evolved to need pruning. Their grippers cradle moss trays, crush vine knots, lift egg clusters, irrigate seedlings, sort scrap from larvae and larvae from scrap with a precision no longer related to any original product.

Birds nest in their shoulders. Vines trip their sensors. Beetles lay eggs in their housings. Ant colonies polish contacts. Roots drink from leaks. The machines are not independent of the ecology; they are organs in it. They create rain. They create wind. They create rhythm. A flower that opens to a conveyor’s vibration is as much a factory product as anything ever stamped or assembled there.

There may be people, but if so they are not the measure of the place.

Perhaps a few live in the root-built rooms near the quieter arch: small communities wrapped in damp cloth and caution, keeping lamps hooded, learning which fruits are loans and which are traps. Perhaps they wear bark sandals to avoid waking the glossy rail, keep beetle-shell calendars of pump cycles, trade with rat colonies by leaving salt in cracked saucers. Perhaps they are descendants of maintenance crews, evacuees, tunnel markets, children of people who came down and stopped believing in return.

Or perhaps no humans remain except as habits in the architecture: benches at sitting height, signs at eye height, doors widened by remembered shoulders, machines still sorting according to hand-made categories. Even without them, the settlement is inhabited by the shape of them. The animals use their routes. The roots hold up their ceilings. The fungi eat their names slowly. The whole hollow lives inside an abandoned human sentence and has turned the sentence into soil.

The true resident is the combined organism: city-plant-engine-swarm.

It breathes through shafts. It sweats through pipes. It thinks, if thinking is permitted to mean distributed adjustment over time, through roots, scent trails, fungal threads, alarm calls, electrical pulses, seasonal jams of machinery. Its moods are weather. Its hunger is maintenance. Its memory is route and scar. It does not distinguish sharply between animal and tool, building and body, corpse and foundation. Everything that enters is tested for use. Everything that dies becomes addressable.

A fallen grazer becomes first a feast, then a bloom, then a cleared place, then a nursery, then a district whose soil remembers hooves. A broken fan becomes a dry cave for moths, then a trellis, then a mineral deposit, then perhaps, if beetles and roots collaborate long enough, a source of copper for leaves that drink current. A lost boot becomes an ant granary. A sign becomes bark. A station clock becomes a hive with twelve entrances.

Who lives there?

The ones who can read vibration as weather.

The ones who know water as condensation, not river.

The ones who accept machinery as climate and architecture as geology.

The ones whose bodies have made treaties with rust, heat, fungus, voltage, darkness, and each other.

They live there not secretly, not marginally, but completely. The hollow is their district, their grove, their station, their organ. They have made a civilization without needing to separate nest from street, street from vein, vein from cable, cable from root. And all around them the old city continues overhead, unaware that one of its forgotten intentions has succeeded: it built a place where life could transfer, circulate, wait, feed, and go on.
