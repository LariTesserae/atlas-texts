What lives there is not a single species of thing so much as a temperament shared by many bodies.

The inhabitants of the high city are people, animals, mosses, insects, birds, water, stone, and custom—each altered by the same law of restraint. Everything that lives there seems to have learned how to continue without spreading too far, how to occupy a place without overwhelming it, how to take part in a balance so old that obedience to it feels less like submission than instinct.

The people are small-voiced, deliberate, and observant. Not timid exactly; rather, economical. They spend their strength carefully, the way they spend iron nails and clear water. They are not a hurried people. Their gestures have the precision of crafts learned from scarcity: tying, pouring, cutting, lifting, sealing, mending. A child there learns early not simply where water is kept, but how water behaves in each district—where droplets gather, which wall sweats first at dawn, which gutter must never be touched, which moss border marks a prohibition older than any elder alive.

They likely have broad hands, strong calves, shoulders made by climbing terraces and carrying jars, faces weathered more by damp chill than by sun. Their clothes are layered and practical: wool, felt, hide, stitched cloth darkened at the hems by wet stone. They smell faintly of hearth smoke, lanolin, moss, leather, and mineral water. Even ornament follows utility: carved horn pins, dyed cords, shell beads from old trade routes, bronze rings inherited until their edges blur.

They do not speak loudly because the city does not require loudness. Sound travels cleanly between stone walls and open terraces; a word can cross a courtyard as distinctly as a thrown pebble. Their speech is probably full of distinctions outsiders miss: names for kinds of dampness, for rain that darkens but does not feed, for cistern-water that has rested, for condensation gathered from lintel versus roof-thatch, for moss that is healthy but must not thicken. They bargain quietly, argue quietly, marry quietly, grieve in processions that climb upward through the terraces.

Their society is governed less by kingship than by custodianship. The most respected people are not conquerors or builders of large things, but water-keepers, map-tenders, cistern-priests, roofwrights, moss gardeners, hoist makers, burial stewards, counters of grain, and old women who remember which repairs were done in which generation. Authority belongs to those who can distinguish maintenance from alteration.

A foolish person, in that city, is not someone who wastes money. A foolish person is someone who shifts a channel without asking. Someone who thinks an inconvenience is reason enough to change the angle of a gutter. Someone who says, “It is only a wall,” or “It is only a tree,” or “Surely a little more water here will do no harm.”

Their faith is practical and exacting. They do not worship water as abundance, because water there is not abundant. They do not worship stone as dead permanence, because the stone is young, sharp, and somehow held still. They worship agreement: the pact by which moss remains within its edge, basins remain at their level, snowlines do not wander, and rain stops where it should.

Their priests are probably not ecstatic figures. They are washers of hands, keepers of basins, readers of condensation, people who know when not to intervene. Their rituals may look almost like maintenance: uncovering jars, clearing pebble-lined depressions, touching cedar moisture maps in prescribed order, cleaning algae from one side of a channel but never the other, placing a cup of water into the Still Source though everyone knows the level will not rise.

They have taboos against excess. Excess building. Excess noise. Excess hunger for novelty. Perhaps even excess mourning. The dead go upward, into cliff chambers, joining the cool stillness rather than descending into the hidden valleys. The living do not speak as though death is disappearance; more likely, death is being placed where one will no longer disturb the balance by needing, eating, burning fuel, moving through doorways, asking questions.

And yet they are not joyless. Joy survives there by becoming precise.

Children race beetles across chalk lines. Young lovers meet behind drying screens, where wet cloth smells of reeds and mineral rain. Someone paints a door lintel with a blank-eyed hare. Someone sings a song old enough to have lost its original valley. Someone laughs too loudly and is hushed, then laughs again into their sleeve. Weddings beside cisterns are tender because everyone knows the poured water will not change the level, and yet the pouring matters. It is the gesture, not the quantity, that joins the households.

Their art must be full of boundaries: lines, channels, nested basins, terraces, repeating droplets, animals rendered accurately but with blank eyes. The blank eyes matter. Perhaps to paint the eyes of an animal is to make it too present, too seeking, too changeable. Perhaps animals, like water, must be represented without being summoned. Or perhaps the people believe sight belongs only to the living creature itself, never to its image.

The animals share the city’s restraint.

The goats know ledges and gates. They crop without stripping. Their bells sound once, as if embarrassed by echo. The dogs are shaggy, sleepy, and solemn, lying in thresholds where condensation gathers on their fur before dawn. They bark rarely; when they do, people listen. Pigs nose patiently in stone pens. Small cattle graze the outer meadows with the same measured abundance as the bees in their clay cylinders.

The bees are especially telling. They do not swarm. There are always enough of them, never too many. Their hives hum along warm walls with a contained industry, neither failing nor overflowing. They make honey the way the city makes life: sufficient, treasured, bounded.

The wild creatures are neighbors rather than invaders. Rock hares live in walls as if the masonry has always included them. Pale lizards claim dark stones. Blue beetles cross doorsteps slowly, and perhaps no one steps on them, not from sentimentality, but because each thing has its route. Thumb-sized frogs live in cistern gardens and speak only after rain, their tiny clicking calls like permission granted and then withdrawn. Roof birds sit in rows and turn their heads together. Their gray bodies hide yellow wings, so that color appears only during movement, briefly, like a secret allowed under certain conditions.

The white-breasted ravens on the upper towers are probably the city’s boldest nonhuman citizens. They see the most: vapor valleys, fixed snow seams, funerals climbing, children sneaking dried berries from market baskets. Their cries are rare and therefore important. A raven calling twice from the highest terrace may be taken as weather, omen, irritation, gossip, or judgment, depending on who hears it.

The plants are not a wilderness pressing inward. They are citizens too, but citizens with assigned districts.

Mosses occupy negotiated borders. Ferns fill cistern gardens with lush restraint. Liverworts spread as far as agreement allows. Vines hang like small hands from places where water has invited them. Tiny succulents hold beads in their centers like private reservoirs. Black-berried shrubs line outer walls, silver underneath, turning pale when wind lifts them. Algae furs the shaded plaster. Roots descend from leaking gutters like hair from an old creature asleep inside the wall.

There may be gardeners whose task is not to make things grow, but to recognize when growth has reached its correct shape. Their pruning is ceremonial as much as horticultural. They clip, and by morning the garden is “as it should be”—not magically restored in any dramatic sense, but corrected by the quiet law of the place. These gardeners must develop a subtle humility. They do not command plants. They remind them.

Even the water lives there like an animal with habits.

It arrives in beads, films, trickles, breath, and recurrence. It sleeps in jars, darkens plazas, gathers beneath shrines, slicks lintels, hangs from hooks, wets dogs’ hair, enters moss. It does not flood and does not vanish. It allows itself to be guided but not possessed. The people understand this, which is why their architecture feels less like capture than hospitality. Roofs, gutters, jars, basins, pebble depressions, cistern gardens—all are invitations.

The true resident, though, may be the balance itself.

It is not inert. It is watchful. It permits aging but not drift, use but not alteration, life but not expansion beyond appointed form. Children grow; elders die; tools thin; meals are eaten; smoke rises. But the western watchtower crack remains the same. The condensation pool fills to the same depth. The lichen border holds. The same drop appears on the same bronze hook each morning, as if the city is being rewritten overnight from a perfect memory.

So the beings who live there are shaped by a paradox: they inhabit young stone under an ancient law. Their mountains look recently made, their ravines shallow, their cliffs sharp, their geology exposed like fresh-cut bone. But their customs are older than ruin. The landscape has not had time to soften, yet the society has had time to become almost ritualized into stillness.

An outsider might first call them conservative. That would be too simple. They are not preserving a past against a changing world; they are cooperating with a world that refuses certain kinds of change. Their caution is not nostalgia. It is survival, theology, engineering, and etiquette combined.

They live as though every action asks the mountain a question, and only some questions are safe.

To widen a doorway might invite a wind that has waited generations for a new path. To move a stone might alter the condensation on a wall three terraces below. To overbuild might cast a shadow where a basin garden depends on morning light. To block a gutter might offend not a god of wrath, but a system of agreements so intricate that no single mind can hold it.

What lives there, then, are the agreed-upon.

People who have agreed to be careful. Animals that have agreed not to overrun. Plants that have agreed to keep their borders. Water that has agreed to return in small portions. Stone that has agreed not to fall. Weather that arrives already tired and does only what is necessary.

And beneath all of them, or among all of them, lives something stranger: a local treaty with time.

Not immortality. The people age. Dogs die. Beams are repaired. Knives wear thin. Songs are forgotten and relearned incorrectly. But place itself does not accumulate accident in the usual way. It does not slump, flood, encroach, erode, or sprawl. It holds its arrangement with impossible courtesy.

The inhabitants have become the kind of beings suited to that courtesy: quiet, exact, sturdy, superstitious in useful ways, tender toward small amounts, suspicious of abundance, attentive to edges. They are not frozen. They are not trapped. They are living inside a pause so complete that every ordinary act—stepping on a stair, pouring water, setting out an empty jar beneath an eave—feels consequential.

By morning they rise again into the clear light: water-keepers touching basin rims, children tracing moisture maps by hand, goats moving to ledges, ravens watching from pale towers, moss shining within its borders, dogs steaming faintly in the chill.

The city is inhabited by those who have learned not to disturb the miracle that permits them to remain.
