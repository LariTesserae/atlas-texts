What lives there is not one people, nor even one order of creature, but a whole height-bred economy of beings that have learned to treat cold, pressure, darkness, and the mountain’s hidden pulse as ordinary facts of life.

They are compact lives. Hoarding lives. Lives built around condensation, tremor, brief warmth, and silence.

The most visible inhabitants are not animals at all, but the low forests of alpine kelp-root that occupy the cracks and terraces where trees cannot live. They stand shoulder-high in crowded colonies, ribbed and flexible, their dark green bodies slick as sealskin and edged with translucent membrane. In strong wind they bend all together without sound, as if obeying a submerged current. At night, or during the plateau’s long near-night, they drink from the air. Moisture beads along their fronds, collects into threads, then runs downward into moss basins at their feet. These colonies are not passive plants so much as weather organs: they catch cloud, slow wind, comb minerals from vapor, and feed the sodden mats beneath them.

The mosses are another major life-form, perhaps the truest citizens of the place. They grow in deep, furred banks over stone, often thick enough to swallow the lower leg of anything careless. They are not soft in the way lowland moss is soft. Their cushions are muscular, water-storing, cold-resistant, and faintly responsive. During dry hours they shrink close to the rock, dark and tight. When the plateau sweats, they swell almost imperceptibly, lifting beetles, eggs, seeds, bones, and amphibian tunnels with them. Their roots and rhizoids enter hairline fractures in stone and follow warmth downward. Around vents they build deep green craters, layered over generations with dead matter, mineral crust, and living velvet.

Lichens plate everything that remains exposed. Some are black-green and scaled like old armor. Some burn sulfur-yellow in crevices. Some hold an eerie glacial blue, a color that seems luminous without casting light. They are slow, exacting feeders, eating stone by centuries. They armor cliffs, map vibration-lines, mark old avalanche tongues, and glow faintly when warmed by breath or pressed under a paw. Animals use them as food, signal, camouflage, medicine, and perhaps as a kind of calendar.

Beneath all this surface vegetation live the small white amphibians.

They are improbable creatures for such height: pale, soft-bodied, lidless, and damp-skinned, with narrow limbs and toes that spread delicately over wet stone. Their eyes are milk-clear and do not blink. They spend most of their lives under moss shelves, in condensation gutters, inside the warm margins of vents, or in the hidden wetness under gravel where snowmelt travels without showing itself. They emerge when the air thickens. Then hundreds may appear where there had seemed to be none: creeping over black rock faces, licking mineral films, taking silver larvae from water threads, pressing their bellies against faintly warm stone.

They do not call. Sound would waste water and expose them. Instead they find one another through tremor and chemical traces left in dampness. Their eggs are laid not in ponds but in wet moss chambers, translucent clusters tucked along the undersides of stones where vapor pulses regularly enough to keep them from freezing. Many never reach adulthood. They feed beetles, raptors, fox-things, and the blind mouths below the moss. But they are numerous, patient, and perfectly fitted to the brief windows when the plateau becomes wet enough for them to cross open ground.

In the grass basins live the hare-like browsers.

They are small, dark-backed mammals with pale undersides and elongated, articulated muzzles, more delicate than a hare’s and stranger in motion. Their noses divide subtly at the tip, able to work between black grass blades, peel lichen scales from stone, and sip condensed droplets without lowering the whole head. Their ears are short and furred close; large ears would freeze. Their eyes sit high and wide, polished black, reflecting almost no light.

They move in sudden collective decisions. One moment a basin is only black grass and frost-pale stone. Then ten bodies are present, angled into the slope, chewing quickly. A tremor passes through the rock, or mist bends wrong, and all ten launch sideways in perfect silence, each choosing a different shadow. Then they freeze. Their dark backs resemble stones damp with cloud; their pale bellies vanish against frost.

Their burrows are shallow but intricate, dug into moss banks and under rock ribs where warmth lingers. They line these shelters with clipped kelp-frond fiber, shed fur, dry grass, and the brittle skins of old fungi. In severe cold they gather in clusters, reducing breath and movement until they seem less asleep than stored.

The predators that follow them are long-bodied, highland fox-like animals, though they are not foxes in any simple sense.

They have wide, furred paws that spread over moss and scree. Their legs are low but powerful. Their tails are heavy and used as insulation when curled around the body. Their coats vary from ash-gray to dark umber, with paler masks around the eyes and muzzle, often crusted with frozen condensation. The most striking feature is their whiskers: long, stiff, and numerous, not only around the snout but along the brows, jaw, forelegs, and flanks. These whiskers read air pressure, vapor disturbance, and the tiny mechanical rumors that pass through the ground.

They hunt by waiting for the mountain to move.

When the buried pulse rises and the rock gives its near-silent shudder, prey lose a fraction of certainty. Footsteps are masked. Moss tightens. Mist shakes loose. In that brief confusion the fox-things slide forward, belly low, placing each paw where lichen and moss will swallow the sound. They do not chase far. Long pursuit at that altitude is costly. Instead they calculate, burst, seize, and stop. If they fail, they fold back into the terrain and wait for another pulse.

On the cliffs live the pale raptors.

They are broad-winged, ragged-edged birds with mottled white, gray, and dirty ocher plumage, like torn cloud over stone. Their heads are narrow. Their eyes are large but not especially bright; sight matters here, but it is unreliable. Mist edits the world. Darkness flattens distance. These birds hunt partly by seeing, partly by listening, and partly by reading disturbance in vapor. Their facial feathers form shallow disks that catch minute shifts of air. Their wing edges are frayed into fine, soundless filaments. When one passes overhead, the fog briefly warps around it, revealing the bird more clearly by its absence than its body.

They nest on ledges warmed faintly from within the mountain. Their nests are made of kelp-ribs, old bones, grass blades, shed horn, and mats of dry lichen. They rarely cry. Mates find each other by repeated flight routes, scent marks, and pressure changes caused by wingbeats close to the cliff face. Their chicks are dark at first, soot-colored and downy, lying flat when the vents breathe hard.

There are larger herbivores too, though they appear less often and usually at distance.

The shaggy ones move in single file over saddle-lines, black against vapor. Their bodies are heavily furred, almost rectangular, with sloping backs and horns swept backward like exposed roots. Ice gathers along their chin hair and shoulder coats. They crop the rubbery fronds of the alpine kelp and paw through moss to reach stored growth beneath. They migrate not by season in the ordinary sense, but by the pattern of vent activity. Old matriarchs know which basins warm after pressure falls, which avalanche chutes green earliest, which tarn rims hold edible mineral crust after condensation.

Their hooves are broad, split, and edged hard enough to grip stone polygons. They do not panic easily. When threatened, they stand facing different directions, steamless in the cold air, their breath condensing low and being caught almost immediately by nearby vegetation.

The narrow high-stepping ungulates are more nervous. They have long legs, small bodies, and heads held close to the ground as they browse lichen crusts from rock. Their teeth are specialized for scraping. Their tongues are dark, tough, and slightly abrasive. They climb fractured escarpments with impossible delicacy, placing their hooves into fissures and old frost cracks. Their young are hidden in moss hollows, nearly scentless, with coats patterned like wet shale.

Under the moss are the blind burrowers.

Few see them whole. Their presence is known by raised ridges, collapsed cushions, and the slow traveling hum of displaced roots. They are thick-bodied, pale or gray, with shovel skulls, sealed eyes, and forelimbs made for pushing through peat, moss, and frost-softened gravel. They follow warmth lines and the deep vibration of the buried mechanism. Around active vents their tunnel systems are complex, layered, and ancient. They eat roots, fungi, mineral-slick biofilms, dead animals, and occasionally the eggs of amphibians or beetles.

They are among the chief undertakers of the plateau. A carcass that falls into moss may lie visibly unchanged for weeks in the cold, fur rimed and eyes frozen. Then the ground beneath it loosens. Blind heads and rootlike feelers work upward. The body settles by degrees, not dragged away so much as accepted downward. Later, white bone appears in lichen, mineralized and half-grown over, part of the plateau’s insulation and memory.

The insects are numerous, but subdued by cold.

There are wingless black beetles with polished backs and hooked feet, slow until touched by vent warmth. They graze on films, fungi, spores, and dead tissue. They gather under stone lips before condensation, then emerge in orderly black seams. Their larvae are silver-white and live in water skins: the thin films between moss and rock, or under translucent fungal curls.

There are moths with furred bodies and narrow ash wings, incapable of long flight except when warm vapor rises. They vibrate without audible sound, heating themselves at the mouths of vents. Their caterpillars feed on the oily leaves of dwarf shrubs and incorporate mineral pigments from lichens, making them distasteful and faintly luminous when crushed.

There are midges that never fly, springtails pale as dust, frost mites red as sparks, and translucent spiders that string low webs between moss stalks where droplets gather. Some webs are not for catching prey alone but for harvesting condensation. The spiders drink from them first, hunt from them second.

The fungi around the vents may be animals in another grammar.

They grow as curled, glasslike shelves, transparent at the edges and smoky toward the root. During cold stillness they are rigid and brittle-looking. When vapor pulses, they soften, unfurl, and expose dark inner gills that collect minerals from the warm breath of the mountain. Small insects shelter among them. Amphibians lick their surfaces. The hare-things avoid certain species, while the fox-predators sometimes chew them before hunting, perhaps for stimulation, perhaps for the ability to smell pressure more keenly.

Some fungal stalks flower, or seem to. Dark rods rise from moss near active vents and remain closed for weeks. After a sequence of subterranean pulses, they open into black-petaled stars, glossy and heat-sensitive. These release spores only when mist is thick enough to carry them low along the ground. In the dim mineral glow, a vent-field in bloom looks like a congregation of small burned lamps.

The tarns hold quieter residents.

Under their black, oil-still surfaces live slow cold-water animals that seldom break through. Something rolls there with a broad back or finless flank, making rings that touch stone without splashing. It may be a fish descended into darkness, or an amphibian grown large and patient, or a soft-bodied grazer of mineral algae lining the submerged rock. Whatever it is, it keeps to deep pockets and undercut edges, surfacing when air pressure falls and vents tremble beneath the basin. It feeds on drowned insects, drifting larvae, microbial mats, and perhaps the pale young of other creatures washed in from moss channels.

There are also smaller tarn lives: glass shrimp, flat worms, black snails with mineral-heavy shells, threadlike plants that grow without much light, and mats of dark photosynthetic or chemosynthetic film that cloud and clear according to rhythms from below rather than the sky.

All of these beings live by the mountain’s hidden machinery.

The pulse beneath the plateau is part of their world as surely as tide is part of a shore. They do not wonder what it is. They respond. Burrows follow its lines. Moss thickens where it is regular. Kelp-fronds grow denser above faults that exhale. Raptors lift when pressure drops. Beetles climb before a vent breathes. Black grasses release pollen after the third deep tremor. Amphibians cross stone during the sweating hour. Predators strike when the ground’s hum swallows the faint noise of their movement.

If there is a dominant intelligence here, it is distributed.

It is in the spacing of animals on a slope, each far enough from the next to preserve the land’s thin resources. It is in the moss storing water for roots, insects, eggs, and decay. It is in raptors reading mist as text. It is in the old herbivores remembering warm basins invisible from afar. It is in fungi opening only to the correct breath of stone. It is in the whole plateau maintaining itself without central will, as if the buried turning beneath it has taught every living thing the virtue of timing.

Nothing here is lavish. Even abundance is rationed. Life appears empty from a distance because it has learned not to waste itself in display. Then the condensation comes, and the truth shows: every surface has inhabitants; every crack is a passage; every wet stone is being grazed, crossed, tasted, warmed, or remembered.

The creatures of this place are not merely adapted to cold. They are adapted to intervals: to the pause between pulses, the hour of sweating stone, the brief vent-breath, the moment when mist thickens enough to hide movement, the long darkness in which moisture is more trustworthy than light.

They live quietly because quiet is the law of the height.

They live sparsely because the land permits no crowd.

They live close to stone because stone is the only constant.

And beneath them all, under moss, root, burrow, tarn, bone, and lichen, the mountain turns; and each life, from the blind burrower to the pale raptor, from the glass fungus to the white amphibian, has made that deep, almost mechanical labor part of its own heartbeat.
