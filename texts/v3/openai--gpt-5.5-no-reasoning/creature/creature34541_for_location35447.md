The inhabitants are people who have become, over generations, almost indistinguishable from maintenance.

They are not numerous. The city could not feed many, and it would not know what to do with abundance if it arrived suddenly at the gate. A few hundred perhaps, or fewer: enough for kin groups, disputes, marriages, apprenticeships, old grudges, remembered favors, and the kind of public knowledge that makes privacy possible only in small, negotiated forms. Everyone knows who left a gutter uncleared, whose goat limped yesterday, whose daughter can splice cord finer than her mother, who takes too much salt, who dreams of the lower valleys, who coughs when the damp comes in.

They are a highland people: narrow, durable, economical in gesture. Their bodies are shaped by thin air, steep paths, cold mornings, and work done close to stone. Many are small-boned but wiry, with strong hands, sinewed calves, and backs slightly inclined from carrying loads in straps across the forehead or shoulders. They move carefully rather than slowly. Even children learn early not to waste motion: where to put a foot on frost-dark stone, how to pass someone in a lane without brushing a water jar, how to duck under roof gutters, how to shut a door without shaking its old pegs loose.

Their faces tend toward weathering before age. Cheeks redden and chap in the cold; lips crack; eyes narrow against pale glare. Skin takes on the colors of the place: ash-brown, olive, umber, clay-pale, wind-darkened at the nose and brow. Hair is usually worn contained — braided, wrapped, bound under caps or scarves — not from modesty alone but because loose hair clogs plaster, contaminates stored water, tangles in looms, catches sparks, and freezes damp at the neck. Beards, when worn, are trimmed short. Nothing that sheds, trails, or flaps is admired.

Their clothing is layered, repaired, and local. Wool is everywhere: felted vests, wrapped leggings, short cloaks, tunics patched at elbows and shoulder seams, sleeping wraps that become carrying cloths by day. The wool is coarse but warm, usually undyed or colored with subdued earth pigments: smoke grey, dull brown, lichen yellow, root red faded almost to rust. Outer garments are rubbed with fat or clay mixtures to shed mist. Leather is used sparingly and carefully: soles, belts, hinges for bags, coverings for jar mouths, water skins, knee patches. Bone toggles and horn pins fasten what metal would fasten elsewhere. A bright metal clasp, if someone owns one, is heirloom, dowry, tool, and status all at once.

They do not decorate lavishly, but they are not without ornament. Their beauty is hidden in edges and habits. A cloak may have three lines of black thread near the hem, showing the maker’s house. A water jar may bear thumb marks in a pattern used by one family for six generations. Children wear beads of polished bone or fired clay until they are old enough to be trusted with a first tool. Marriage gifts are practical things made beautifully: a knife with a handle fitted to the hand; a loom weight incised with rain marks; a pair of roof-scrapers; a covered basin; a spindle whorl smooth as an old tooth.

Their language is quiet and probably full of distinctions outsiders miss. They have many words for water in states of usefulness: roof-water, first-run water, stored water, settled water, bitter seep, stone-cold water, drinking measure, wash measure, goat measure, water not yet counted. They distinguish kinds of damp, kinds of frost, kinds of silence before weather changes. Their verbs may be precise about repair: to patch temporarily before a rain, to reset a stone in its old place, to fit a reused lintel into a new wall, to clean a gutter without widening it, to strengthen without burdening. They speak in low tones, not because they are timid, but because volume implies urgency, and urgency must be justified.

Their society is governed less by rulers than by custodianship.

Certain families may be associated with certain cisterns, roofs, terraces, or wall sections, not as private property exactly, but as inherited responsibility. A house is not simply owned; it is kept. A roof is not simply repaired; it is answered for. A cracked channel is a moral failure if ignored. Children learn their kin histories through infrastructure: “Your mother’s mother reset that drain after the black frost.” “Your father’s brother wasted lamp oil there.” “This step came from the old west room before it fell.” Memory is architectural, and architecture is evidence.

Leadership rests with those who have kept things alive the longest without drama. The most respected elders are not necessarily the oldest, but the ones who know where water goes after dark, how clay behaves after three dry seasons, which storage vaults sweat in early thaw, which beams are smoke-hardened and which are only darkened on the outside. A persuasive person here speaks not in promises but in remembered examples. “When the south gutter was widened, the lower basin silted.” “When goats drank at the open pool, two children took fever.” “When we burned green shrub, the smoke spoiled the grain.” Such statements carry the force of law.

They are suspicious of novelty, but not stupidly so. If something works, they adopt it slowly and bind it into rules. A new tool is tested by several hands, then discussed, then modified until no one can remember whose idea it was. A new crop from lower country might be grown first in a cracked pot, then a trough, then perhaps one court if three damp seasons agree. They have survived by refusing both panic and enthusiasm.

Children in the city are raised communally in the way scarce places require. They are everyone’s concern because every careless child can waste water, break a jar, spook a goat, or fall from a terrace. They are taught through tasks before explanations: wiping condensation into a bowl with a strip of wool; carrying pebbles to weight an awning; sorting shards by curve and thickness; listening at a cistern lid for the sound of dripping; rubbing ash into clay; keeping count of goats on the slope. Play exists, but it mimics labor: building tiny walls from chips, racing droplets down carved channels, making dolls from cord and bone, trading imaginary measures of salt and onion and wool.

Their games are probably quiet too. A child who shouts in play is hushed not harshly but immediately, as one might put a hand over a lamp flame in wind. They learn that sound travels, that startled animals waste strength, that night work depends on hearing water, coughs, hoofsteps, stone shifts. Listening is one of their first disciplines.

The old are not removed from usefulness. Even those who can no longer climb steep lanes sit under awnings sorting fiber, mending nets, teaching knots, watching weather, counting jars, telling which child carried a load unevenly. Blind elders may know the city by thresholds and echoes. A hand laid on stone tells them whether mist has come in from the east. Their authority is not sentimental. They are repositories of calibration.

The people eat plainly. Their food is dense, sour, dried, smoked, pounded, soaked, stretched. Barley or another hardy grain, perhaps, kept in stone-lined bins. Flat cakes cooked on heated slabs. Goat milk when available: fresh rarely, more often soured, thickened, dried into hard pieces, traded carefully. Onions, bitter greens, tough herbs, dried roots, medicinal leaves, small legumes if the season allows. Meat is not everyday food except in cured strips used for strength or ceremony. Broths are precious because they consume fuel and water, but also restorative enough that they mark care: illness, birth, severe cold, return from a journey.

Cooking fires are small and watched. Fuel is too valuable for indulgence. Dung, twigs, dried shrub, scraps from carpentry, old reed matting too worn for roofs: everything has its flame category. Smoke flavor is normal; soot is part of the taste of home. The smell of a richer stew would travel through the lanes and become public knowledge at once.

They keep goats not as picturesque companions but as walking conversions of slope into milk, hide, horn, dung, sinew, and occasional meat. The goats are lean, intelligent, exasperating, and known individually. A child may be trusted with a younger sibling later than with a difficult goat. The animals wear small bells only when needed, and even the bells are muffled in certain weather. A goat that learns to nose open a water cover is remembered with the same irritation as a thief.

They also live with small commensal creatures: wall mice, insects after rain, nesting birds, lice in bad years, beetles in stored grain, moths in wool. They do not romanticize these. Their relationship with animals is practical, attentive, and unsentimental. A bird nesting under a roof may be tolerated if it does not foul the channel. A mouse hole in a retaining wall is plugged with stone and clay. Insects after rain may be watched by children as marvels and then swept away from stored food.

Their religion, if called that, is probably inseparable from maintenance and measure.

They may not imagine gods in grand, human shapes. Their sacred powers are more likely obligations: the held water below, the mountain that does not hurry, the first mist, the old dark beams in the meeting house, the ancestors whose hands cut channels that still work. They may speak to cisterns before opening them at the beginning of a dry season. They may touch a lintel when leaving on a journey. They may place the first cup of roof-water from a new repair on a stone outside the wall, not wasted exactly, but returned to the slope as acknowledgment.

Their rituals are brief because nothing can be allowed to consume too much fuel, water, or daylight. A birth might be marked by washing the infant’s hands with a thimbleful of settled water and pressing damp fingers to the threshold. A marriage might involve the joining of two roof channels with a temporary clay bridge, showing that the households now share runoff in a specific, witnessed way. The dead are perhaps wrapped in old wool and taken beyond the wall to stone cairns, or placed in dry chambers, or given to the ravine in some controlled ancestral manner. Burial in good soil would be impossible or wasteful; cremation too fuel-hungry except for rare persons or epidemics. Bones might remain near, incorporated into memory, not hidden far away.

Their dead do not vanish. They persist in reused doors, inherited tools, named cisterns, cautions, and measurements. A grandmother may become “the one who sealed the east basin.” A failed ancestor may be remembered just as strongly: “the one who ignored the crack.” Shame and honor both endure because both are useful.

Outsiders come rarely but not never. Traders from lower valleys or neighboring saddles arrive with salt, metal bits, oil, news, perhaps dyes, seeds, stories of cities with rivers running openly through them. Such stories are received with fascination and distrust. A river in the street would sound to these people like wealth so extreme it borders on obscenity. Children might ask whether people there let animals drink whenever they want, whether roofs are built carelessly, whether rain is allowed to fall on bare ground.

The inhabitants are polite to strangers in a bounded way. Hospitality exists, but it is measured. A visitor may be given a place out of wind, a cup filled to an exact level, food appropriate to their status and the season. They will be told the water rules before they are told the names of their hosts. Any outsider who empties a basin thoughtlessly, washes dust from boots near a clean channel, or lets a pack animal nose toward a cistern will feel the entire city’s opinion harden around them like drying clay.

Yet they are not cold people. Affection here is simply expressed through preservation rather than display. To mend someone’s sandal before a journey is tenderness. To wake in the night and check another household’s roof channel during rain is devotion. To save a sweet root for a child, to warm a stone and put it under an elder’s blanket, to sharpen a neighbor’s knife while they sit grieving, to carry water uphill without mentioning it — these are the local forms of love.

Their humor is dry, exact, and probably merciless in small ways. They joke about people who boast, because the mountains make boasting absurd. They mock badly made repairs for decades. A lazy person may be compared to a flat gutter. A gossip may be called “a cracked jar with legs.” A child too restless might be told, “Go ask the ravine if it needs help falling.” They laugh quietly, but not rarely. In a place of constraint, laughter is another kind of pressure valve, used carefully but needed.

Conflict exists, sharpened by scarcity. Water theft, hidden storage, negligence, marriage disputes, inheritance of roof rights, goat damage, failed repairs, accusations over spoiled grain — these can divide households for years. But open violence would endanger too much. A broken arm in planting or repair season matters to everyone. A feud that damages a channel becomes a public crime. So anger is formalized. People bring disputes to the meeting house, speak in order, produce witnesses, point to stones, jars, measures, marks. Judgments likely involve labor more often than punishment: repair the public steps; clean silt from the settling pool; surrender a measure of stored grain; tend another family’s goats; give up claim to a cistern share for a set number of rains.

They think in cycles, but not in dreamy ones. Their calendar is made of tasks: frost-scraping time, roof-sealing time, first mist, jar inspection, goat breeding, lower-path opening, salt arrival, damp storage, smoke curing, cistern sounding, wool beating, clay tempering. The year is not a wheel of festivals so much as a sequence of vulnerabilities. Celebration occurs in the brief intervals when no immediate failure presses.

When they do celebrate, it is probably with restrained intensity. A low drum made from hide stretched over a jar. Bone flutes with thin, reedy tones. Footsteps in a court, patterned but not pounding. Songs sung under the breath in close harmony, designed for stone walls and small rooms rather than open fields. Their music may imitate dripping water, loom rhythm, goat bells, wind in roof reeds. Its beauty would be austere to outsiders, but to them rich with remembered sounds.

Craft defines much of identity. There are water-keepers, roof-menders, potters, wool-workers, hide-scrapers, stone-fitters, bone-carvers, herb-tenders, goatherds, path-watchers, traders, midwives, smoke-curers, lime-burners if fuel allows, people who know kilns, people who know knots, people who know how to judge stored grain by smell. No craft is wholly separate from the others because no material is single-use. The potter thinks of drainage shards before a jar is fired. The weaver thinks of roof patches as well as blankets. The butcher thinks of horn, sinew, bladder, bone tools. The builder thinks of where water will go fifty years later.

Their hands are astonishingly competent. They can make useful things with almost nothing: a scraper from a shard, a plug from wool and pitch, a hinge from rawhide, a lamp from broken pottery, a child’s toy from a goat knuckle, a measuring scoop from horn, a roof weight from a stone chosen not for size alone but for how it sits in wind.

They distrust waste more deeply than they distrust strangers. Waste is almost a spiritual offense: not simply losing a thing, but breaking faith with all the labor and weather that made it available. A cracked bowl is not dead until it has been mended, then used for dry goods, then as a scoop, then as a shard, then ground into temper. An old cloak becomes patches, then binding strips, then lamp wick, then ash for plaster. Human life itself is understood similarly: childhood absorbs instruction, adulthood bears loads, age keeps memory, death leaves tools and names.

Their dreams may be full of water. Not floods, perhaps — floods would be terrifying — but deep, quiet reservoirs; rain lasting just long enough; a spring no one else has found; a basin that never silts; a roof that catches every drop. Some may dream of lower lands with trees taller than meeting-house posts, streams you can hear before you see them, soil soft enough to push a finger into. A few young people leave for such places. Some return changed, louder or sadder. Others do not return, and their names become complicated: envied, pitied, disapproved of, missed.

Those who stay are not necessarily resigned. Staying here is an active practice. It requires intelligence, endurance, and an almost artistic loyalty to limits. They know the city is small. They know the mountains are not generous. They know their lives are narrower than lives in fertile valleys. But they also know every stone that keeps wind out, every channel that brings water underground, every silence in which danger can be heard. Their pride is not expansion but continuation.

They are people of thresholds and coverings: lids, doors, awnings, caps, wrappings, sealed jars, patched roofs, closed cisterns, folded cloths. What matters is protected. What is protected must be opened correctly. A careless opening is almost as bad as a theft.

And beneath all their restraint there is a deep intimacy with the place. They know how dawn beads along a parapet before a damp day. They know which court keeps cold longest after night. They know the smell of a cistern that needs cleaning, the note of a roof reed loosening, the weight of a jar filled to the proper measure in mist season. They can read a dark streak under a drain like a line of writing. They can hear, in the pause between loom clicks, whether the wind has shifted.

They do not live in the city as occupants live in a structure.

They are part of its working.

The city drinks through their roofs, remembers through their elders, repairs itself through their hands, counts itself in their rules. They are its warm, fallible, temporary organs: breathing thin air, saving water, wearing wool, speaking softly, carrying jars across courtyards while the mountains hold still around them.
