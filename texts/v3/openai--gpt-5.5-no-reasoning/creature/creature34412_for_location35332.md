What lives there is not an animal or a people, but an ecology of beginnings.

The dominant inhabitant is the surface itself: warm masonry, porous ceramic, lime-bound plates, salt seams, cistern walls, underfloor chambers breathing heat. It is not alive in the animal sense, but it behaves too responsively to feel inert. It expands, ticks, sweats, drinks, stores, darkens, cracks, and releases. It holds the first authority of the place. Everything else lives only by negotiating with it.

The true residents are small, patient, low forms of life adapted to construction rather than soil.

They live in margins.

They live where water hesitates.

They live where shade lasts an hour longer than elsewhere.

They live in the flaw between two fitted plates, in the mineral-darkened lip of a cistern, in the damp underside of a ledge, in the salt-stained trough where dew falls before sunrise.

There are gray-green plants, but they are almost more like decisions than vegetation: sparse, tough, and exact. Tufts of stiff grass flatten themselves against the baked ground, conserving their shape through heat, then lifting slightly when rain darkens the seams around them. Their blades are narrow, waxed, and pale, the color of oxidized sage or ash. From a distance they barely register. Only after rain do they reveal that they were waiting all along.

There are mat-forming growths that look like stains during dryness: olive, black, rust-green, nearly indistinguishable from mineral discoloration. When water arrives, they soften, swell, and become briefly vegetal. They creep along basin rims and channel floors in thin films, occupying the difference between wet and gone. Their life is timed not by seasons exactly, but by contact: dew contact, seep contact, rain contact, shadow contact.

There are solitary shrubs, each one a small weather system. They grow only where the architecture permits accumulation: at the foot of a wall, beside a hairline fracture, on the down-slope side of a seam where runoff leaves a little more salt, a little more dust, a little more organic residue. Their roots enter the made ground like questions. Their branches are pale, dry, angular, and jointed-looking, so similar to cracks or broken plaster ribs that they can vanish into the landscape until they cast a shadow.

Their leaves are small and thick, not numerous. Some are gray with wax. Some are blue-green and almost ceramic themselves. They do not flutter much. In heat they turn edge-on to the sun, becoming lines. In dampness they open slightly, darkening by one shade. Around their bases lie faint circular territories: mineral halos, trapped dust, tiny accumulations of dead matter not yet soil.

No creature eats them. Nothing nests in them. Nothing strips their seeds. Their reproduction must be mechanical, elemental: gravity, wind, flowing water, adhesion to mud films, perhaps the cracking and resealing of the ground itself. A seed falls into a seam and may remain there for years, inert as grit, until one private rain fills the channel and leaves it lodged in a pocket of silt.

The most numerous living things are likely nearly invisible.

Microbial skins bloom in the basins after rain. They arrive as a greening of still water, a slickness on warm shallows, a dark rim where evaporation concentrates minerals. In the early hours of a temporary pool they are suspended, golden-green or brown, clouding the water just enough to make sunlight visible inside it. As the pool shrinks, they gather at the edges. When the water is gone, they remain as varnish, flakes, crusts, stains—life flattened back into latency.

There are lichens, or things close to lichens, on shaded plaster and porous lime. They live slowly enough to seem like weathering. Smoky green colonies spread beneath lips where condensation forms each dawn. Black freckles gather near seep points. Orange powders mark older surfaces where minerals and moisture have made a tolerable chemistry. They are the first handwriting of biology on architecture.

In deep cisterns there may be bands of moss or moss-like growth, but only where light and dampness meet without either becoming too abundant. These bands would ring the walls at precise elevations, showing where water once lingered, where evaporation slows, where shade holds. Below them: slick bare mineral. Above them: dry, pale construction. The moss exists as a narrow treaty.

Nothing here is restless.

There are no insects worrying the pools, no birds tracking the channels, no lizards on the warm plates, no burrowers making use of the underfloor chambers. The absence of animals makes the plant life stranger and more solemn. Nothing is grazed. Nothing is pollinated by wing or mouth. Nothing is hidden from predators. There are no alarm responses, no camouflage in the ordinary sense, no chase-shaped evolution. Survival is not against hunger from outside; it is against dryness, heat, salt, exposure, and time.

The inhabitants do not form a food web so much as a moisture web.

Dew feeds the ledge crusts.

Rain feeds the basin films.

Seepage feeds the wall stains.

Runoff feeds the seam plants.

Shade feeds endurance.

Dust feeds the slow possibility of soil.

Dead matter does not cycle through bodies. It oxidizes, powders, dissolves, catches in cracks, and becomes chemistry. A fallen leaf may lie undisturbed until wind pushes it against a curb. There it softens in the next rain and stains the masonry beneath it. Over years, such stains become a pocket. Over longer years, the pocket becomes a place where something else may root.

The life of the place is therefore positional. To live there is to occupy the correct millimeter of the built world.

A wrong surface is death: too flat, too exposed, too sealed, too hot.

A right surface is barely better: a seam with shade at dawn, a shallow groove where water pauses, a porous wall that cools before the air does, a basin cell whose last pool remains for one extra day.

What lives there has no surplus. It is not lush, not exuberant, not crowded. It is exact.

The made desert’s living residents are also seasonal in an unusual way. They do not disappear in dry periods; they become almost indistinguishable from the materials around them. Grass becomes fiber and dust. Mats become dark stains. Lichens become powdery maps. Shrubs become pale geometries. Microbial blooms become rings and varnishes. Life withdraws into form.

Then rain arrives, and the place wakes without becoming noisy.

Green intensifies along joints.

Basin rims darken.

Dormant films loosen.

The smell changes from hot lime to wet mineral and faint algae.

Channels that seemed empty become corridors of possibility.

For a short time the artificial continent hosts a thin, distributed awakening—more chemical than dramatic, but unmistakably alive.

If there is a central organism there, it may be the whole constructed substrate and its dependent crusts together: an architectural desert learning to become a biome. The plates catch water; the seams shelter seeds; the walls create shade; the basins breed films; the cisterns preserve dampness; the plants and microbes stain, split, soften, and slowly alter the surfaces that permit them. Nothing rules it except process.

So what lives there?

Dew-fed lichens.

Salt-tolerant mats.

Heat-sleeping grasses.

Solitary runoff shrubs.

Microbial blooms in temporary water.

Moss bands in cistern shade.

Seeds held in cracks like small unfinished arguments.

And beneath all of them, the greatest resident: a vast artificial landform, not alive but becoming habitable, its masonry body slowly being interpreted by water and green.
