What lives there has learned not to be an animal first.

It is a resident of edges: not the broad flats, not the centers of basins, not the obvious shelters with their failed doorways and roofless walls, but the lips of things. It keeps to crack-rims, cairn-shadows, the seams where condensation appears bead by bead, the shallow gutters where old water once decided to be a river and then forgot. It is not rare in the way the white bird is rare, crossing the plateau as a ceremonial interruption. It is not rare in the way the horned thing is rare, standing until it is more stone than body. It is rarer than sight but less rare than moisture. It is present wherever the plateau has made a boundary and kept it.

At first it might be mistaken for one of the low shrubs.

A black, jointed stillness. A small figure crouched against stone, no higher than a person’s knee when folded, no higher than a standing person when unfolded completely, though it almost never does this. Its limbs have the segmented austerity of alpine twigs. Its outer covering is dark and matte, neither fur nor bark nor hide, but some cold-grown surface that holds frost along its seams. In dim light it seems merely black; under the old internal sheen of the rocks it shows buried colors: violet bruising, green mineral dullness, the faint brown of old bone steeped in iron water.

It has too many joints for a mammal and too much patience for an insect.

The body is narrow, made for sheltering inside cracks that should not admit it. The shoulders, if they are shoulders, are sloped and close. The spine rises in small knobs like a chain of pebbles laid under skin. The belly is pale-gray and faintly translucent, and when it presses itself against ice one can see within it a slow dim movement, not of organs exactly but of folded air, threads of mist traveling through chambers. Breath, in this creature, does not enter and leave so much as circulate.

It has a head, but the head resists interpretation. From one angle it is blunt, marmot-like, eyeless, with a soft triangular muzzle dusted white by frozen salts. From another it narrows into something beaked. From another there is no head at all, only the front end of a careful body investigating a crack with four thin feelers. Its face changes according to attention, not rapidly, not theatrically, but with the same calm inconsistency as distance on the plateau. Looking directly at it produces a smaller, harder animal. Seeing it from the corner of the eye reveals a larger and older one.

It has no visible eyes.

Instead, there are two shallow depressions above the mouth where frost gathers in perfect circles. These do not shine. They do not track movement. But when the plateau’s background roar thickens, the circles tremble, and the creature turns toward whatever has changed in the sound. It hears with its face, with its joints, with the damp membrane between its ribs. It hears through contact. A foot placed on schist a hundred paces away arrives in it as a pressure, an angle, a slight alteration in the long note of the world.

It is not deaf to quiet. Quiet is its brightest signal.

When the white bird passes and leaves a bird-shaped absence in the roaring air, these edge-dwellers emerge. One by one, from cracks no wider than a hand, they lean out and lift their faces into that temporary silence. They do not chase the bird, do not fear it, do not call. They drink the silence as other creatures might drink warmth. The frost-rings on their faces widen. Their pale bellies pulse once or twice. Then, as the roar seals itself again, they withdraw.

Their mouths are small and difficult to notice until they open. There are no teeth in the ordinary sense, only fine ridges of mineral-hard tissue, combs for scraping. They feed on what grows where the world fails: lichen at the rims of air-cracks, salt from stones that have doubled themselves, the cold oily sheen that appears on rocks at certain angles, the residue left beneath suspended words. They are meticulous grazers. They do not tear a lichen colony away. They shave one crescent from its edge and leave the geometry intact. They know, in whatever manner they know, that a hand-sized patch may be older than a forest. Their hunger has manners.

They lick condensation from stone with long colorless tongues, thinner than grass blades and divided near the tip. These tongues can enter seams where water is heard but never seen. Sometimes one will stand for hours with its mouth to a crack, drinking not liquid but the idea of liquid carried upward as mineral breath. Afterward its belly glows faintly blue, like ice over depth.

They do not drink from pools.

No animal native to the plateau trusts reflections that arrive late. These creatures approach tarns only from the side, never looking into them. They harvest the frost from the stones around the margin, nibble the salt-white crust, and withdraw before their own delayed forms can climb into view. If accidentally reflected, they become rigid until the image settles. If the reflection shows them doing something they have not yet done, they will not do it. In this way, whole lines of action are pruned from their lives.

Their tracks are the paired perfect marks sometimes attributed to blind hares or marmots, but those tracks are not footprints in the usual way. The creature’s feet rarely touch the ground all at once. Each limb ends in four narrow pads arranged around a central claw, and between the pads is a frost membrane that stiffens or softens according to the surface. On ordinary stone the feet grip. On uncertain stone they hover a hair’s breadth above it. In places where the ground is reluctant to accept prints, the creature does not press harder; it lets the idea of its step occur, and the frost records that instead.

That is why each mark is identical.

They reproduce slowly, if reproduction is the right word. In the black shrubs, the hard pale nodules that do not bloom sometimes begin to click in a different rhythm. The creatures gather around them, standing motionless in a rough ring. They do not mate there. They listen. Over many dim intervals, the clicking arranges itself into a pattern of tiny impacts, a map of a body not yet present. Frost forms around the nodule. Lichen darkens beneath it. The shrub’s branch bends under a weight no one can see.

Eventually a new creature is found nearby, small as a curled hand, soft and almost transparent, clinging to the underside of a ledge where condensation falls every forty-seven breaths of the plateau’s sound. It carries no wound of birth. The shrub’s nodule is gone, leaving only a pale socket. Whether the creature grew from the nodule, was called by it, or merely arrived through the crack the nodule had been keeping open is not a question the plateau encourages.

They age by becoming more definite.

The young are blurry at the edges. They cannot always maintain the same number of limbs. Their bodies tremble when distances contradict themselves. They may attempt to cross an open flat and arrive repeatedly at its beginning, growing weaker with each return. Older ones learn the cairns. They learn which cracks accept breath, which shelters make the roar domestic, which doorways render the sound almost articulate. The oldest carry patches of lichen on their backs, not as camouflage exactly, but as proof of tenure. Frost grows in permanent seams along their joints. Their black surfaces become pitted and granular. Some develop tiny stone inclusions at the elbows and crown.

A very old one may settle beside a cairn and remain there so long that passing travelers count it among the stones.

Yet they are not plants, not rocks, not ghosts. They move with unmistakable intention. Slow, yes, but not sluggish. Their stillness is chosen and exact. When startled, they can unfold with startling delicacy, lifting themselves on thin angled limbs and slipping sideways into the nearest discontinuity: a crack in rock, a crack in sound, a crack in the air where breath-threads vanish. They do not flee forward or back. They flee between.

Their social life is sparse, like everything there. They do not gather in herds. They do not nest in colonies. But they maintain agreements. One may find three or four living along the same ridge, each occupying a different seam, each emerging at fixed intervals to graze, never crossing another’s chosen condensation line. If one dies—or finishes becoming something else—the nearest will eventually visit the empty seam and scrape a small sign into the frost: a hooked mark, a ring, two parallel cuts joined by a third. These marks last until the air reconsiders them.

They sometimes add stones to cairns.

Not often. Perhaps once in a century for a given cairn. A creature will carry a flat stone under its body, gripped by all four inner claws, and place it with great care among the others. It does not choose the top. It chooses the exact place where the cairn’s agreement has thinned. The new stone may be low, almost buried, wedged between older stones fused with lichen. Afterward the cairn sounds slightly different. Not louder, not quieter. More continuous.

This suggests they did not build all the old structures, but they know how to maintain them.

They enter the roofless shelters during the scant rain. Not because they dislike wetness, but because raindrops on open stone are too loud. The amplified impacts confuse their inner organs. Under a wall, in the lee of basalt-like beams, they fold themselves tightly and press their frost-ringed faces to the packed earth. There the plateau’s sound becomes the low booming of a kettle in another room. The creatures seem calmed by it. Some hum in return, though the hum is not made in the throat. It vibrates from the pale belly and passes into the ground.

Their voices are not words. Words are dangerous here. In the amphitheater where old syllables hang as frost-knots, they come to feed but never vocalize. They nibble the lichen thickened beneath the suspended speech and keep their mouths closed. If a traveler speaks there and leaves a fresh knot of language in the air, the creatures may return later and arrange themselves beneath it, listening to the shape of the person’s need after meaning has frozen out.

They understand need better than speech.

Need is what built the shelters. Need is what placed cairns where paths could otherwise forget themselves. Need is what teaches a living thing to become small, still, and compatible with paradox. These creatures are made almost entirely of such lessons. Their bodies are not elegant, not monstrous, not beautiful in any easy way. They are necessary bodies. Bodies edited by height, cold, dimness, and contradiction until only the usable strangeness remains.

A traveler might never see one fully. More likely they would notice that a lichen circle around an air-crack has been shaved clean on one side. They would find a row of identical tracks beginning at no visible burrow and ending at a cairn. They would feel, while resting in a roofless hut, that something in the wall was also resting. They might wake with their exhaled breath still stretched as a pale thread before them, and see a black jointed figure standing beside it, holding the thread between two delicate forelimbs as if testing its strength.

The creature would not steal the breath.

It would wait until the thread drifted into the standing crack in the air and vanished. Then it would lower its face to the place where the thread had been, taste the remaining cold, and remember the traveler as a brief warmth moving through a very old agreement.

In the deepest hollows, where sound has weight and lies waist-deep like invisible water, the largest of them live. They wade through the roar with only their backs exposed, black ridges moving slowly beneath the pressure. Pebbles rest on the surface of the sound around them. Condensation beads on their shoulders. These deep individuals are broad, almost turtle-like, with shells formed not from bone but from compacted listening. Their frost-rings are huge and blind. They surface at long intervals to scrape the underside of floating stones, where lichen has grown in the gap above the frost pedestal.

They are the keepers of the plateau’s loudness, or perhaps merely its oldest livestock.

When one of them dies, the level of sound in that valley does not change. Nothing so dramatic occurs. But for a while the roar has a hollow in it shaped like the dead one, and smaller creatures come from the ridges to stand around that absence. They do not mourn by crying out. They let the absence pass through them. Later, the dead body becomes a low black hummock. Moss grows along the lines where its shell plates were. Its legs stiffen into roots. Its face sinks inward, leaving two frost-lined circles that fill with condensation at fixed intervals.

Eventually travelers call it a stone.

The horned things on the ridges do not hunt them. The white birds do not take them. The blind burrow-creatures share their cracks uneasily but without war. There is almost no predation on the plateau in the ordinary sense. Hunger is too slow, space too uncertain, bodies too costly. Death comes by misalignment: a young one stepping into a distance that refuses to end; an old one listening too long to the invisible stair and descending in parts; a careless feeder opening its mouth beneath a suspended word and having the word remain inside it.

Those so afflicted become articulate for a short time.

They make sounds like almost-speech from their bellies, syllables bent by stone. The others avoid them gently. Eventually the trapped word freezes in the throat and the creature curls around it. Lichen grows thick there afterward.

If they have a name for themselves, it is not pronounceable in air. It would be a pattern of pressure against rock, a tremor sent through frost, a silence held after a bird passes. To human description they might be called seam-keepers, frost-grazers, cairn-kin, the listeners at the cracks. None is exact. Names here remain too long and become hazards.

Better to say: the plateau’s inhabitants are small black jointed beings that live where reality leaks.

They feed on condensation, mineral residue, lichen, silence, and the nutritious errors of distance. They maintain old cairns without claiming them. They are blind because sight is unreliable and patient because time is not a trustworthy measure. They are half animal, half weathering process, and half agreement, which is too many halves but appropriate to the country. They do not master the plateau. Nothing masters it. They belong to it by yielding precisely where ordinary life would insist.

They are almost nothing.

And in that place, almost nothing is enough to live.
