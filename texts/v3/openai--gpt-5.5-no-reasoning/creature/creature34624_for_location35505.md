What lives there is not a population in the usual sense. It is a civic ecology: a people thinned almost to weather, a machine trained to behave like a monastery, and many small lives kept carefully in the seams so that the city remains answerable to the world.

The inhabitants are few, or else they have learned to be few at any given time.

They are not absent. Absence would be too simple for a place like this. They occur intermittently, in the way moisture occurs: appearing when conditions permit, condensing out of architecture, leaving evidence out of proportion to their bodies. A cup turned over on a table. A glove thawing beside a pipe. A line of small tools arranged in descending order of temperature. A bench recently warmed, though no figure is visible in either direction of the lane.

They are probably people, at least in origin. Human or near-human enough to need coats, lamps, thresholds, boot trays, handrails, privacy, ritual, and warm drinks. Their dwellings have the scale of bodies with shoulders and knees. Their stair treads remember feet. Their handles are placed for hands. Their low beds and narrow alcoves suggest sleepers who curl against cold. They have made rooms not merely for function but for pausing, and no wholly nonhuman intelligence would invent so many places to sit facing a wall of weather.

Yet living here has altered them.

They are slight, bundled, patient creatures, most often seen at angles or through wet glass. A passerby might glimpse one crossing a bridge above an alley: a narrow figure in layered black and grey garments, hood pulled low, face obscured by a mask of translucent horn or smoked mineral. Their clothes are practical but ceremonially maintained. Outer coats are stiff with frostproof lacquer. Sleeves are long and weighted. Belts carry vials, folded maps, measuring pins, heat capsules, little bone-white keys, and swatches of cloth used to test condensation. Their boots have segmented soles that flex over uneven paving and leave prints which sometimes appear before the foot touches down.

Their faces, when visible, are pale from indoor light and cold climate, but not sickly. The skin has the polished, slightly sealed look of those who live among mineral vapors. Their eyes tend to be dark-adapted and reflective, protected by inner membranes or lenses. Some have silvering at the temples regardless of age. Some bear small geometric scars on the throat or behind the ear, marks not of injury but of registration: seals against spatial drift, hydrological citizenship, permitted access to rooms that do not remain rooms for everyone.

They speak quietly. Not because they are afraid, but because the city answers.

A loud voice in certain corridors can wake dormant acoustics, summon stored echoes, or loosen old conversations from shutters in the archive quarter. So the inhabitants use murmurs, hand signs, taps on railings, beads slid along cords, breath against copper reeds. Their public language may be partly tactile. A greeting is two fingers pressed to a wall before touching one’s own chest, acknowledging both person and structure. A question may be indicated by opening the palm under a drip line and waiting to see which way the droplet crawls.

They do not hurry. Hurrying wastes discrepancy.

Children, if they exist, are educated early in the arithmetic of consequence. They learn that a shortcut must be paid for, that a room left empty becomes unruly, that northern water should not be poured into southern basins without witness. They learn the safe alleys by warmth, by smell, by the grain of frost under mittened fingertips. Their games are small and exacting: guiding beads of meltwater through carved channels; memorizing which door returns to the same afternoon; arranging stones in vacant rooms so that the shadows remain calm.

The handprints appearing each morning may be a child’s, but perhaps not a living child in the ordinary way. The city may be keeping a record of one. Or replaying a necessary occupant. Or allowing someone young, somewhere adjacent, to practice presence against the black wall until they are strong enough to arrive.

The adult citizens seem less like rulers of the city than its attendants.

They are cistern-keepers, frost-harvesters, corridor-gardeners, error-clerks, membrane doctors, archivists of weather, negotiators of distance. Their work is maintenance, but maintenance elevated to metaphysics. They prune impossibilities. They label anomalies before anomalies become appetites. They feed lichens to vacant chambers. They separate waters according to directional memory. They open hooded mirrors only under supervision. They listen to walls whose shadows have entered the wrong century and file the disagreement in cold ledgers.

Their tools are delicate and severe: needles that test the age of ice; spoons used to lift cubic droplets; threadlike probes lowered into cracks in gravity; thermal combs; clay tablets that darken in the presence of an unmapped passage; little bells rung not for sound but to establish sequence. Many tools are old and polished. Others are grown overnight from black basal matter, used once, then dissolved into gutter moss.

They may not all be biological.

Some citizens are distributed through the city’s functions. A certain door may be a person’s patient extension. A line of condensation along a vestibule wall may be someone remembering. The maintenance organisms in the drains may carry fragments of civic intention. The machines folded beneath bridges may dream in civic dialect. A citizen might consist of a body, three archived voices, a dwelling’s thermal habits, and a share in the central fountain’s upward-falling drops.

Death here is probably not a clean departure. The dead are not buried far outside the wall, because outside is too raw and forgetful. Nor are they simply cremated, because heat is costly and smoke too easily crosses boundaries. Instead, the dead may be redistributed: names stored in shutters, salts dissolved into directional water, bones mineralized into handrails, last breaths condensed onto glass and read during winter festivals. A person who has died might continue as a bench that warms for descendants, a stair that lengthens in warning, a small voice in a bowl-landscape, a preference in the routing of thaw.

There are ceremonies, but they are quiet enough to be mistaken for calibration.

At the cistern court, figures gather rarely, perhaps at the hour when the basin is almost full and the next upward drop is due. They stand apart, not in ranks but according to hydrological relation: those of northern condensation, those of buried clay, those of thaw channels, those licensed to handle remembered rain. They hold cups rim-down until a signal passes through the paving. When the drop rises into the suspended basin, no one cheers. A bell may be touched with a wet finger. Names may be breathed into cloth. One measure of water is released into the city’s capillaries, and one measure is withheld for futures not yet cold enough to receive it.

Their ethics are shaped by scarcity and paradox. Waste is not sin exactly, but vulgarity. Emptiness is dangerous. Abundance is suspicious. Clean lines are childish. A good citizen leaves behind stabilizing traces: a stone in a room, a note in a shutter, a lichen watered, a discrepancy paid. A bad one opens too many doors, mixes incompatible waters, maps continuously, shouts into archive glass, or tries to make the whole city make sense at once.

They may be descendants of builders, or refugees, or custodians stationed here by an empire long gone. But the original purpose has softened into culture. Whatever technical mission founded the settlement—to contain a break, study young geology, harvest impossible water, prototype a new physics—the inhabitants have turned it into daily life. They have recipes, grudges, lullabies, winter sayings, taboos about mirrors, jokes about southern roofs, superstitions concerning foxes at the gate. They argue over repair priorities. They court one another in condensation gardens. They leave gifts in empty rooms to keep the rooms polite.

And around them live the lesser citizens.

The lichens are not decoration. They are witnesses, sensors, treaties. Each crust and moss-thread has been placed or tolerated for a reason: to calm a surface, feed on mineral bloom, indicate the health of a wall, anchor emptiness. The miniature gardens are civic signatures. A room with one living thing in it is less likely to attract unwanted occupants from possibility. A stairwell with moss at the third landing remembers verticality more reliably. A pane of glass with silver lichen around its frame is less apt to show seas that never happened.

The maintenance organisms are the city’s quiet livestock, or perhaps its immune system. Dark bridge-clingers scrape vents clean. Translucent channel-worms filter water. Jointed pale insects patrol warm pipes, harvesting salts and microbial films. They are bred, persuaded, or printed from living tissue, and they obey environmental grammars more than commands. Children may be taught not to touch them. Elders may speak to them anyway.

There are also machines that are treated almost as animals. Folded devices sleeping under eaves. Cistern regulators that must be warmed with gloved hands before use. Gate counters that become irritable during dry months. Archive shutters that prefer certain voices. These are not worshipped, but neither are they reduced to instruments. In a place where no enclosed space may be empty, even a machine acquires manners.

The foxlike animals outside are not citizens. They are neighbors of the older world. Their refusal to enter matters. They remind the city that ordinary life still possesses veto power. Birds visit, midges hatch, reeds grip the clay, pinhead flowers open and vanish. The inhabitants likely watch these events with an attention bordering on reverence, because such creatures live without permits from broken physics. They are precious for being merely themselves.

What lives there, then, is a restrained community of caretakers and partial beings, adapted to cold, scarcity, and negotiated reality.

They do not dominate their city. They keep it company.

They move through narrow streets by touch and timing. They maintain water as others maintain law. They seed rooms against dangerous vacancy. They accept that a person may be partly in a chamber, partly in a sound, partly in a future warmth stored in a railing. They know the city cannot be fully mapped, fully emptied, fully explained, or fully trusted. Still, each morning someone clears frost from a threshold. Someone checks the basin. Someone tends the red jointed shrubs under mesh. Someone places three stones and a spoonful of substrate in an alcove and waters the one living thing there.

Their civilization is small because smallness protects it. It remains no larger than a cathedral close because beyond that scale its contradictions might become weather. It speaks softly because miracles are easier to manage when not startled. It saves every droplet because water is memory with a body.

And slowly, as the young basin learns erosion and the city learns itself, its inhabitants continue their careful tenancy: human enough to polish benches, strange enough to pay distance with frost, humble enough to let moss hold the walls of the world together.
