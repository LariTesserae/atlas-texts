What lives there is not one species, and not quite a community in the gentle sense. It is an arrangement of survivors, workers, opportunists, and things that do not know they are alive but persist with the patience of living bodies: pumps, fungal films, heat, water, routes, habits. The Warm Levels are inhabited by whatever can accept repetition, dimness, and damp warmth as a kind of weather.

The rats are the first citizens, though they would not claim the place because claiming is slower than using. They know it completely, not as a map but as a network of smells, drafts, temperatures, escapes, risks, and edible accidents. Their bodies are dense and glossy from the heat. Their whiskers are always wet at the tips. Some are old enough to have torn ears, pale scars across their backs, yellowed teeth broken and worn down by concrete edges and crate corners. They move in family streams along the gutters and pipe saddles, over cable trays, under warped doors. They do not wander. Every motion belongs to a route.

They are bold in the way animals become bold when people are intermittent. A rat may sit in the mouth of a drain and watch a worker pass, not frightened, only measuring whether the boot is relevant. They gather behind the fruit cages where peaches split and sugar leaks into cardboard. They raid the mechanic’s room for crumbs hardened in oil. They carry away the soft insulation stripped from old pipes and pack it into inaccessible hollows. Their nests are warm, fibrous, crowded, loud with small blind bodies and the scrape of claws. The Warm Levels provide for them better than the streets above: fewer dogs, steadier heat, reliable refuse, and walls within walls.

They are not vermin here in the moral sense. They are infrastructure of another kind. They test the district constantly. They find cracks first. They know when a grille has loosened, when a chamber has gone unused, when floodwater has changed the floor. Their paths become visible before human plans do: dusty bands across silver pipe jackets, greasy rub marks under conduit, tiny beaten tracks in silt. The city’s official routes are numbered and painted. The rats’ routes are simply true.

The pigeons live higher, in the uneasy border between underground and sky. They nest where ventilation shafts flare open and the air moves upward in slow warm drafts. Their ledges are white with droppings, gray with old feathers, littered with straw, plastic twine, cigarette filters, and the brittle remains of eggs. They come down in sudden committees, clattering and indignant, to drink from sump edges and puddles bright with oil. Their bodies are city-colored: soot blue, dull pearl, necks flashing green and violet only when a lamp catches them correctly. Underground, they seem heavier than they do in squares and station roofs. Their wings strike the air hard. When they startle, the whole chamber feels briefly occupied by falling cloth.

They bring the upper city with them. Bread crusts. Seeds. Roof dust. Mites. The knowledge of daylight. They stand on warning signs and transformer cages, blinking with orange eyes, as if the human symbols belong to cliffs. Sometimes one gets trapped deeper than it intended and becomes a frantic noise in a service corridor, beating itself against fluorescent light until it tires. Then a cat notices. Or a worker with gloves catches it and throws it upward into a shaft, where it vanishes into traffic-colored brightness.

The cats are not numerous, but each one alters a room by entering it. They are the aristocrats of the Warm Levels because they appear to have chosen the place, and because nothing there quite owns them. Some are lean ferals with torn noses and old bite abscesses hard under the fur. Some are half-domestic, named by pump crews and fed from lunch tins: Valve, Grease, Madam, Orange Boy, Little Inspector. They learn shifts. They wait at certain doors when the evening crew descends. They sleep on housings, switch cabinets, warm pipe bends, folded tarps, scooter seats, anything that holds heat without dripping.

Their paws stay astonishingly quiet on metal stairs. Their tails lift and curve when they cross narrow rails over black pits. Their eyes return the light more cleanly than any painted sign. They hunt rats, but not with the urgency people imagine. There are too many rats to make hunting a crusade. A cat kills when it wants to, when hunger sharpens, when a young rat misjudges distance, when movement passes too close to the body’s old mathematics. The cats also watch insects, drink from condensation trays, vanish for days into the bricked culverts, return smelling of rust and wet stone. They have learned which machines become hot enough to punish carelessness. They know which workers tolerate them and which throw bolts.

Beneath the market, the frogs keep their own damp republic. They are hard to see until they move. Their backs match the concrete, mottled gray, brown, green-black, stippled like mineral stains. They sit among orange rust flakes, hair-thin algae, onion skins, bottle caps, drowned beetles, and the fine grease that floats at the edge of settling pools. Their throats pulse softly. Their eyes are polished and old-looking, gold-rimmed, entirely without surprise.

No one knows their origin, so stories have grown around them. Eggs came in on plants from a restaurant. Tadpoles washed down from a fountain. A child released pets through a gutter. A flood lifted them from some forgotten courtyard pond and installed them below the city like a rumor. The frogs themselves do not care. They have found warmth, water, insects, and ledges. At night they call, and the pipes make them larger. Their voices travel upward through grates and drains until pedestrians pause, hearing from beneath the pavement a hollow pulse like a marsh dreaming under stone.

Their population changes with the pumps. A maintenance adjustment can dry a shelf where eggs were laid. A blocked drain can create a nursery overnight. Tadpoles flicker in shallow brown water no wider than a boot print. Young frogs, tiny and perfect, scatter under corrugated hoses. They are eaten by rats, cats, spiders, perhaps by one another, and still each year there are more in the neighboring pool, one expansion farther into the engineered dark.

The insects are the real multitude. Cockroaches own the electrical rooms with an authority older than keys. They live behind panels, under rubber mats, inside wall cracks warmed by current, in the backs of cabinets where paper labels curl and adhesive softens. They are bronze, black, amber, some tiny as seeds, others large enough to make a worker curse when they rush across a hand. They feed on paste, glue, crumbs, mildew, dead skin, each other, whatever the city renders soft. When a light snaps on, the floor seems briefly to break apart and reassemble elsewhere.

Silverfish inhabit paper stores and old file boxes, trembling like quick ash. They eat labels, manuals, maintenance logs, cardboard dividers, the forgotten instructions for machines replaced long ago. Their damage is delicate: scalloped edges, powder, absence in the shape of reading. Moths sleep in vertical ranks on tile and concrete, wings folded like drab petitions. Their larvae find wool in lost gloves, fibers in nests, dust in corners. Mosquitoes rise from pans and sumps in thin whining columns, local weather made of hunger. Spiders take advantage of all of it.

The spiders are prosperous beyond modesty. They string webs between conduit and valve wheels, across unused lockers, in the corners of control booths, under railings where hands no longer pass. Some build neat geometric traps near lamps where midges gather. Others live as dark knots in pipe brackets and rush out when vibration touches silk. Their webs catch steam droplets and become jeweled for a moment before sagging with grime. In the Warm Levels, silk is both net and archive. It holds wings, dust, mineral powder, flakes of paint, hair, tiny bones, the shed skins of insects that have outlived their own shapes.

There are other small beings too, less visible but everywhere. Bacteria slick the drain lips. Algae darkens the standing water and gives it a skin. Mold blooms behind insulation, under wood, in the seams of rubber gaskets, on the undersides of benches, soft and gray-green and patient. Fungi thread through damp cardboard and fruit crates, lifting pale fuzz over peaches too long below. Lichens attempt the older brick near light shafts and fail slowly. Moss makes dull pads where moisture and shade remain steady, then browns when heat surges or pumps dry the wall. The few ferns beneath ventilation shafts seem almost ceremonial, their fronds dusted and fragile, proof that the world above has sent a green signature into the place and the place has not entirely rejected it.

The human inhabitants are more intermittent, but no less shaped by the district. The workers who descend regularly acquire Warm Level habits. They listen before they speak. They put one boot down and test. They touch railings with the backs of their fingers before gripping. They smell for insulation, sewage, hot oil, ozone, gas. Their faces shine with sweat within minutes. Damp maps fold in their pockets; tablets fog; gloves never dry properly. They carry keys on retractable chains, lamps with scratched lenses, radios that crackle and fail in certain bends.

They are not romantic about the place. They know it as labor: gauges, schedules, blockages, corrosion, reports, bad bolts, impossible angles, alarms at inconvenient hours. But familiarity makes a second knowledge under the official one. They know which pump has a cough before failure. Which stairwell grows slick first after rain. Which door sticks in summer. Which cat steals sandwiches. Which stretch of wall has sounded hollow for three years and may one day matter. They remember men who slipped, floods that rose, repairs done quickly and repairs done properly. They leave marks not meant as art: chalk arrows, initials, dates, a zip tie looped where a latch should be, a cable hung from a nail because that was where a cable had to go.

The small unofficial trades are another species of occupancy. The mechanic in the side room belongs there differently from the pump crew. He or she works under a lamp that turns every wet surface black beyond its circle. Scooters come in broken, delivery carts with bent axles, municipal machines with unofficial problems. Parts are sorted into old biscuit tins and plastic trays. Oil stains make a dark continent under the bench. A radio plays if the fans permit it. Rats know this room. Cats know it better. The mechanic has learned to hang food from wire, to keep rags in sealed tubs, to kick a pipe before reaching behind it.

The vendors who store fruit below have made a bargain with warmth. Bananas yellow quickly. Tomatoes soften. Stone fruit comes to fragrance, then rot, in a span of hours if forgotten. Their crates bring sweetness into the Warm Levels, and sweetness summons everything: flies, roaches, rats, mold, cats, workers, complaint. For a little while, the room smells almost generous, like a market at closing under rain. Then the district takes its share.

The rented cages hold the dormant life of the city above. Chairs stacked upside down like sleeping insects. Festival lanterns wrapped in plastic. Bags of salt hardening at the corners. Spare tiles, mannequins, signs from shops that have changed names, artificial wreaths, broken shelving, umbrellas by the dozen, a crate of chipped dishes, a neon letter no longer connected to its word. These things do not live exactly, but underground they become part of the ecology. They shelter spiders. They wick moisture. They grow mold. They are gnawed, stained, forgotten, reclaimed in fragments.

And then there are the inhabitants that are not creatures but behave like them because repetition has given them appetite.

Heat lives there. It presses its blunt body into every chamber, rises from pipes, collects beneath ceilings, softens adhesive, fatigues paint, ripens fruit, fattens rats, wakes insects, opens smells. It is not dramatic enough to be feared moment by moment, but it rules everything. It determines where cats sleep, where workers pause, where mold thrives, where metal expands with a knocking sound like someone hidden in the wall.

Water lives there. Not as a river, usually, but as presence: seep, bead, drip, film, sump, breath. It knows the slopes better than surveyors. It finds seams in poured concrete, carries rust from one chamber to another, lays mineral lines on walls, fills footprints, erases chalk, turns dust to paste, brings down the city’s leavings in diluted form. In rain it grows muscular. The drains inhale. The pumps answer. Low walkways become temporary borders. The district’s sound changes, and everyone who belongs there hears it.

Routes live there too. Rat routes. Worker routes. Cat routes. Water routes. Cable routes. Emergency routes that no one has tested in years. They cross without acknowledging one another, yet each alters the rest. A blocked rat run may send animals through a breaker room. A new conduit may give spiders a bridge. A temporary pallet may become a dry step, then a habit, then something everyone uses without remembering who placed it. The Warm Levels are made of such adoptions.

What lives there, finally, is the underside of the city learning to be a habitat. It has no wilderness innocence. Everything in it is touched by planning, repair, leakage, neglect, salvage, and use. Its animals are ordinary animals, but abundance has made them strange. Its people are ordinary workers and marginal users, but the place has trained them into attentiveness. Its plants are accidental, but each fern seems like an event. Its machines are not alive, yet their rhythms set the terms under which life gathers.

Nothing down there is entirely hidden from the city above, and nothing above is entirely absent below. Bread becomes rat muscle. Rain becomes pump labor. Heat becomes insect season. Lost toys become shrine objects. Fruit becomes flies. Traffic becomes ceiling vibration. A tree root finds a crack and hangs in the dark like a question.

The Warm Levels are inhabited by the things that can make a home from surplus: surplus heat, surplus water, surplus space, surplus refuse, surplus time. They do not transform the district into something pretty or mythic. They make it used. They make it watched from pipe mouths and ledges. They make it rustle, blink, breed, call, molt, sweat, and continue.
