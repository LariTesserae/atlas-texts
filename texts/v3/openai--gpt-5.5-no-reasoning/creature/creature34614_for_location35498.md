What lives there is not one creature, though sometimes it can seem to gather itself into one.

It lives in layers.

Nearest the water skin are the small quick lives: transparent things with dark internal sparks, larvae with paddle tails, worms flat as torn petals, bead-eyed crustaceans, mites that walk upside down under the surface tension as if the pool has a ceiling. They live by grazing films, by eating eggs, by being eaten almost immediately after becoming beautiful. Their bodies are made for brief certainties. Hatch. Rise. Sense warmth. Mate. Vanish into a beak, a jaw, a ripple. They do not know the age of the place, but their timing does. Rain pressure enters them. Light enters them. A mineral change in the mud sends a whole generation upward at once.

Below them, in the mud, live the patient engineers of rot.

They are pale, soft-bodied, mostly blind. Some are animals, some fungi, some bacterial veils stretched through soil like thought without a head. They digest what falls: leaves, wings, scales, dead machines, the outer skins of roots, the shed gill-feathers of amphibians. They loosen the land without disturbing its shape. They make the dark soil breathe. Some of them thread themselves through ancient alloys and learn, very slowly, which substances answer to hunger and which answer to signal. Their colonies form dim maps around buried circuitry. If one could see the marsh from beneath, these lives would appear as a low constellation, a warm net of appetite and maintenance.

In the reeds live the listeners.

They are cord-thin snakes, reed-boring beetles, glass-winged flies, small frogs with skin like polished moss, and narrow-faced mammals no larger than a hand. Most are silent. Their bodies are arranged around attention: wide tympanic membranes, whiskers that tremble at the passage of insects, heat pits, pressure bladders, conductive hairs that rise when one of the buried devices wakes. They know the marsh by intervals. The pause before rain. The changed pitch before a predator passes. The brief deadness around the central ring at noon. They are not intelligent in any grand declarative way, but their caution has grammar.

The amphibians are among the most visible citizens.

They come in many designs, as if the place has been experimenting with the idea of wet skin for a million years and has never grown tired of it. Some are little lacquer-backed things that cling to leaves and drink condensation through their bellies. Some are root-climbers with feathered red or blue gills, their toes spread into soft pads that grip bark, ceramic, and slick black stone equally well. Some never leave the pools and look less like frogs than like expressions of water given eyes. A few grow large enough to leave palm-wide tracks in the mud. They move at evening with grave, inflated patience, swallowing beetles, silver midges, and occasionally the stray machine-seed if it has been too long motionless and smells enough like mineral prey.

Many of them sing, but quietly. Their calls are not chorus but notation: a rubber click, a hollow gulp, a two-part whistle from under a leaf. They call to mates, to rivals, to rain, and perhaps to the old systems under the marsh, which sometimes answer with a pure ascending tone.

The birds live as if they have made treaties with invisible geometry.

The long-legged ones are the most striking. They stand in shallow pools on black, jointed legs, white or smoke-gray or green-bronze depending on the angle of light. Their necks fold with mechanical elegance. Their beaks are narrow and sudden. When they hunt, they do not simply watch prey; they calculate permission. One foot lifts, hovers, lowers exactly between submerged lines no eye should see. They step around dry tiles hidden under moss. They avoid the central ring before its noon thickening. They nest on low branches that overlook both water and ruin, weaving reeds, fibers, shed wire, and conductive leaves into platforms that hum faintly during storms.

Their young are born with pale markings along the skull and wings, delicate branching lines like circuit paths. These fade with age, but never fully. In strong light the old birds show them again, ghost diagrams under feather and skin.

There are smaller birds too: parasite-pickers that ride the backs of the glossy herbivores; seed thieves with blue throats; low, darting insect hunters whose wings beat once and then vanish into glare. Some sleep inside perforated towers, safe in holes too smooth for snakes. Some line their nests with fragments of dry moss taken from tiles that never become wet. Whether by instinct, accident, or instruction, they use the ruins correctly.

The herbivores are slow islands.

They emerge when heat has made the marsh sleepy. Their backs are low, their limbs thick and quiet, their hides dark with moisture and patterned in mineral dapples. Sensory fronds crown their heads and run in shorter rows down their necks, opening and closing in response to humidity, electric charge, pollen, and the approach of unseen bodies. They crop plants with mobile lips, favoring the soft newest growth near seepage lines. Where they feed, the marsh changes course by inches. A reed bed opens. Sun reaches mud. Eggs hatch there the next week. Fungi come after.

They are not numerous. Perhaps three or four use the whole basin, each with its customary routes, each attended by birds and by small crabs that climb their legs to feed on softened parasites. Their dung is rich with seeds, spores, and microscopic machines that pass through them unharmed. In this way the herbivores are gardeners without intention, distributing instructions older than their species.

The predators are mostly withheld.

There are needle-mouthed aerial hunters, of course, and fishlike things in the pools that wait beneath mats of algae with only a seam of mouth showing. There are snakes in reeds and pale crabs in instruments. But the true larger predator is known more by subtraction than appearance.

When it passes, songs shorten. The long-legged birds become statues. Amphibians deflate their throats and sink. Insects drop lower, as if pressed by a hand. The water in separated pools trembles at once, not from footsteps exactly, but from some shared recognition traveling through mud and root.

It may be a single species. It may be several. It may have a long body carried close to the ground, skin mottled like wet stone, sensory pits along the jaw, feet broad enough to leave almost no print. Perhaps it hunts by pressure. Perhaps by heat. Perhaps by reading the same buried fields the birds step over. It rarely needs to rush. The marsh is small, and all paths eventually return to water.

In the old structures live the adapters.

Pale crabs occupy hollow instruments and defend them with formal little gestures. Their shells are matte white, stained green at the joints, and their claws have become asymmetrical: one for crushing shells, one for scraping mineral films from impossible materials. Beetles live in the seams of ceramic panels, their larvae tunneling through accumulated moss rather than through the panels themselves. Moths sleep on stone with delta-patterned wings open flat, vanishing against maps no living river follows. Tiny mammals den beneath curved ribs, warm and dry where nothing ought to remain dry.

Some animals have changed to suit the machinery. Some machines have changed to suit the animals. It is no longer useful to ask who began the arrangement.

The machines live there too, if living may include persistence without desire.

They are small now, mostly. Silver seeds in the water. Reedlike antennae bending after light. Dry tiles under moss. Black stones holding alien skies in their polished faces. Devices that move not by propulsion but by making the next place slightly more probable. They sample, mend, divert, discourage, encourage. They tend boundaries whose purpose has outlasted the makers. They do not rule the marsh. They participate in it.

Roots grow around them and learn their warmth. Fungi retreat from some and embrace others. Tadpoles mouth them and spit them out. Birds incorporate their fragments into nests. Crabs polish their surfaces with generations of feet. The machines continue to enact protocols, but softened by mud, misread by moss, translated into ecology.

And there is something broader than any of these.

Not a ghost, exactly. Not a mind seated in the central ring. More like a distributed habit. The whole place remembers itself through repeated corrections. A root grows too close and is discouraged. A pool becomes too acidic and water is slowly redirected. A species declines and some flowering reed blooms heavily for three seasons. A silver seed is removed and begins correcting for absence. The system is not benevolent. It is conservative in the old sense: it keeps.

It keeps moisture near the surface.  
It keeps light diffuse.  
It keeps the ring clear at noon.  
It keeps the routes of predators open.  
It keeps certain nests possible.  
It keeps old instructions moving through bodies that have no names for them.

So what lives there is an ecology that has inherited a machine, and a machine that has decayed into ecology.

Its inhabitants are amphibians slick with rain-bright skin, birds that step through invisible mathematics, herbivores with fronded crowns, predators made of pressure and silence, crabs in abandoned instruments, insects brief as sparks, fungi with mineral memories, roots that know where not to grow, and patient devices carrying out ancient work in the manner of weather.

Above them all, or beneath them all, lives the marsh itself: low, warm, saturated, exact. Not young. Not ruined. Finished with catastrophe and therefore full of delicate continuations.

It lives by seepage, by condensation, by quiet correction.

It lives softly because everything sharp has already passed.
