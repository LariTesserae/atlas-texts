What lives there is not one people or one species so much as a whole discipline of life.

The inhabitants of the hollow summit are small in the way old, high places make things small: wiry, economical, close-jointed, with bodies adapted less to strength than to endurance and carefulness. They move through their town sideways, stooping under trellises, ducking past cages, stepping without looking where a stranger would crush seedlings, spill ash, kick loose a drying rack, or knock a clay bottle from a ledge. Their shoulders are narrow. Their hands are capable, dry, and lined early. Their lips crack in childhood and heal into habitual restraint. They speak softly in enclosed places, not out of meekness but because stone carries sound too well and nothing here benefits from waste, not even breath.

They are a thirsty people, but not a barren one.

Their faces tend to be shaded by woven visors, headcloths, or plant-fiber hoods stiffened with resin. Many wear garments made in layers: loose cloth against the skin, outer aprons patched with leather, pockets for bone tools, ceramic tokens, seed packets, and stoppered vials. Children wear charms not of gold or bright stones but of drilled shell, goat teeth, old beads, bird claws, fragments of ancestral pottery. Adults carry measuring cords. Elders carry keys to cistern stones, or the right to say when one may be lifted.

Their beauty, if seen on their own terms, lies in composure. They have an elegance of minimization. They pour without spilling. They cut without hesitation. They can strip a thorn branch bare and leave each leaf sorted by use. They can read the sag of a dew net from across a courtyard. They can tell by smell whether a fermenting mash will feed bees, goats, children, or the soil. They rarely gesture broadly. A broad gesture might strike a hanging pot.

The oldest among them are not merely respected. They are infrastructural.

An elder may remember which wall contains an old flue, which family once grafted the black-fruiting vine near the western shade, which cistern lining was repaired with poor clay, which ancestor’s niche must not be warmed by kiln smoke. Memory is as practical as rope. A foolish young person can mend a fence. Only an old person remembers why it must be mended before the red moths hatch.

They do not divide the living world sharply into wild, domestic, sacred, and useful. Such categories would be too crude. A lizard in the wall is a tenant. A bee tube is a household obligation. A goat is appetite under treaty. A vine is a relative one feeds and wounds in order to keep alive. Moss is crop, poultice, water sponge, and green witness. Even rot is not simply decay but transition into account.

Their children are taught gentleness before obedience. Not sentimental gentleness, but precision. They learn to cup a moth without damaging its wing dust; to move eggs one at a time with warm fingers; to scrape dew from nets without tearing fibers; to step over a root without looking at it; to listen before opening a storage chamber. They play quietly, though not joylessly. Their games involve knots, seeds, shadows, memory chants, mimicry of bird calls, and tiny races held with beetle shells or carved clay counters. A child who can identify every household’s roof garden by scent alone is praised. A child who wastes water is not beaten immediately, perhaps, but everyone becomes still, and the stillness teaches more than shouting.

They are not innocent. No people living so closely could be.

They quarrel over inheritance, over shade rights, over pollinator access, over whether a trellis has crept too far into a shared alley, over the ownership of a vine that rooted through a wall and fruited on another family’s side. They gossip fiercely. They remember debts for generations. Marriages are partly matters of affection, partly of cistern shares, grafting lineages, goat rights, and the balancing of mouths against roofs. A family with many daughters skilled in dew gathering may become wealthy without seeming to possess anything a lowland trader would recognize as wealth. A household with a deep cistern but poor shade may be pitied and courted at once.

Their laws are intimate. They concern thresholds, lids, smoke, droppings, roots, the angle of a wall, the timing of a harvest, the sound of water where water should not be heard. Punishments are rarely grand. A person who spills through arrogance may be denied first scraping of the nets. A thief of moisture may be made to tend the dead niches through a hot season, climbing daily to wipe dust from ancestral screens while the whole town watches. Exile is nearly death, so it is invoked rarely and feared always. More common is enforced usefulness: repairing cistern clay, turning dung, cleaning bird cages, hauling ash, grinding bone into soil until the offender’s arms tremble.

They worship, if that is the word, through maintenance.

Their sacred acts look like chores performed without haste: sealing jars, tying shade knots, feeding root beds with the powdered remains of meals and bodies, smoothing clay over cracks in cistern mouths, placing feathers before the ancestors, opening certain flowers by hand before dawn so the tube-bees can reach them. They do not beg the mountain for abundance. They thank it for not refusing them. Their prayers are full of verbs: hold, thicken, shade, condense, bind, remember, return.

The ancestors above them are not distant spirits. They are upper neighbors. The dead remain in the architecture because departure is wasteful and because continuity is literal. Hair becomes cord. Bone becomes meal for the soil or tool for the living. Names become roof rights. Stories become warnings. A grandmother may stand at dusk beneath the niche of her grandmother and complain aloud about the stubbornness of a grandson, not because she expects an answer in words but because the town itself is listening through its accumulated dead.

They have music, but it is made for stone.

Reed flutes that imitate vent wind. Ceramic whistles shaped like birds, insects, cistern mouths. Frame drums muted with skin patches so the beat travels through walls rather than across the open air. Songs are often rhythmic lists: plant lineages, water debts, the sequence of dawn tasks, the names of those who repaired a terrace after a collapse, the birds that returned in a dry year. Lullabies teach counting. Mourning songs teach geography. Harvest songs teach restraint.

Their food is dense, sour, bitter, smoky, and precious. They eat paste from ground seeds, cakes of cactus meal, roasted roots, lichen porridge, fermented pods, goat milk when there is any, tiny eggs, dried insects, broth strengthened with bone, herbs so strong a few leaves flavor a pot. Meat is not festive because it is abundant; it is festive because it signifies that something has been fully accounted for. A goat slaughter is an audit as much as a feast. Hide, sinew, horn, bladder, hoof, gut, bone, dung, blood—each has a destination before the knife is lifted.

Their animals are not background. They are citizens of another grammar.

The goats are clever, resentful, and watched like criminals with legal standing. They know which knots loosen if rubbed, which children are inattentive, which thorn cuttings are being withheld. Their bells are deliberately irregular so each animal can be identified by sound. A missing bellbeat at dusk causes alarm.

The dogs are lean, dusty, and formal. They belong to doorways, alleys, night patrols, and children. They sleep lightly. They do not bark at every bird or stranger-shadow; they conserve alarm for cliff foxes, loose goats, cistern disturbances, and unfamiliar footsteps after lamps are covered. Old dogs are given warm wall places and broth. Dead dogs are mourned with practical gratitude.

The lizards are almost sacred by tolerance. No one owns them. They appear in proverbs as models of attention and opportunism. A house with many lizards is considered fortunate, unless the lizards begin descending toward the cisterns, which means insects or damp where damp should not be.

The birds form a second, restless town woven through the first. Caged layers mutter and blink in shade. Cliff swifts scissor the upper air. Doves nest where they are half-welcome and half-resented. Black seed-thieves are cursed, trapped, sometimes admired. Red-throated singers are fed crumbs during certain festivals because their return marks some subtle turning of insects, flowers, or wind. Children learn bird etiquette as seriously as human etiquette: which nests may be approached, which droppings collected fresh, which feathers belong to the dead, which calls mean hawk, fox, leak, dawn.

The bees—or bee-like things—are perhaps the most tenderly managed creatures in the town. They live in clay tubes stacked in walls and warm niches. Each tube is marked by household signs. Their morning labor ties the settlement together more reliably than law. Without them the pale flowers fail, the grafted vines weaken, the seed stores shrink. At dawn, while people scrape dew, the bees begin their own gathering from blossoms that will close before full sun. The sound of them is the sound of permitted sweetness.

And beneath this visible life is another population: beetles in bran, grubs in brood jars, moths in roof thatch, fermentation cultures in sealed crocks, lichens brushed onto stone screens, fungi hidden in dung cakes, bacteria souring mash, yeasts passed down like heirlooms. The people may not name them as a scholar would, but they cultivate them with exact inherited skill. Much of the town is alive at a scale below reverence but above neglect.

What lives there, then, is an agreement.

Human beings, animals, plants, insects, stone, ash, bone, and vapor have entered into a crowded pact. None can afford purity. The people are not masters of the crater; they are its current method of remaining green. The gardens are not decoration; they are stomach, lung, archive, dowry, cemetery, and scripture. The houses are not separate from the bodies inside them; they are extended skins, shade organs, water traps, memory chambers. The dead are not gone; they are elevated into walls. The animals are not possessions only; they are transformations waiting their turn.

The inhabitants are therefore intensely local in body and thought. A stranger from below would seem loose, loud, dangerously spacious. Someone accustomed to rivers would be morally suspect without having done anything wrong. To pour water visibly, to wash with abundance, to leave peelings to rot in an unused heap—such acts would appear obscene. The summit people might trade with outsiders, might even depend on them for shell, rare metal, pigment, stories, and mates from time to time, but they would never fully trust anyone who has heard running water all their life.

They have myths of water, certainly. Not simple fantasies of endless lakes, but troubling stories. Water in excess is often monstrous in their tales: a thing that erases paths, rots roots, loosens walls, drowns names, carries soil away from the dead. Paradise is not a sea. Paradise is a shaded cistern that never cracks, a fog that comes three mornings in a row, a vine that fruits without exhausting its root, a child who grows to old age without leaving the rim.

They live with constant scarcity, but they are not miserable by definition. Joy exists there, though it is compact. A flower opening at the correct hour. The first hum from a newly occupied bee tube. A child’s first successful dew scraping. The return of birds at dusk in such numbers that the air seems stitched. A new graft taking. A repaired wall surviving its first hot season. The taste of a sweet root split among five people. The smell of dust struck by a fog heavy enough to darken the nets.

Their laughter is probably sudden and dry. Their grief is probably prolonged and practical. Their love is expressed in maintained shade, in sharpened tools, in a sealed jar left near a sleeping mat, in the quiet moving of a pot so an old person need not step high.

They are a people made by limits, but not reduced by them. Limits have given them form. They live as if every object has several futures, every creature several duties, every death several uses, every drop several claimants, and every morning is a negotiation with disappearance.

In the dead summit’s hollow, life has not conquered the mountain. It has learned to fit inside the mountain’s last open hand.
