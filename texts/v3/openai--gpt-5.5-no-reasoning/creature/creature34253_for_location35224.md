What lives there is not only a people, though a people live there.

They are small only in the way marsh things become small: compressed, weather-leaned, close to their own warmth. Most stand a little shorter than a reed bundle set upright, though some are long-limbed and narrow from years of crouching along lanes. Their backs learn the height of doorways early. Their feet are broad, or made broad by wrappings: layers of softened hide, bark fiber, woven sedge, and flat pads of corky root strapped under the sole to spread weight over the floating ground. Barefoot children have toes that splay instinctively on wet planks. Old people’s toes bend around wood like roots taking hold.

Their skin tends toward the colors of the city: peat-brown, gray-olive, cold umber, pale fungus-cream, sometimes almost blue in the dimness. Not because they are not human, exactly, but because nothing here stays uncolored by the place. Smoke stains them. Tannin stains them. Iron water stains their palms orange at the creases. Moss dust greens their cuffs and hairlines. Babies are born with ordinary softness, but within weeks they smell faintly of milk, peat smoke, and leaf mold, and the marsh has accepted them enough to begin marking them.

Their hair is rarely loose. Loose hair drinks mist, tangles in budded doorways, collects seeds, brushes against things better left unstirred. So it is braided, felted, netted, or tied into long damp ropes threaded with bone beads, snail shells, carved seeds, bits of coppery bog iron, bird claws, or knots denoting kin. Children’s hair is often cropped unevenly with shell blades until they learn not to lean too close to lamps. Elders wear hair bound flat against the skull under caps of lichen felt, each cap patched and repatched until it resembles an old wall.

Their eyes are large-seeming, not from deformity but from habit. The face grows attentive in the dark. They look without staring directly, using the edge of vision to catch motion: a rail slipping between huts, the shiver of a pool skin, a stranger’s hand moving toward a knife. Many have pale marks painted or tattooed around the eyes with ash, lime, or crushed shell—not decoration alone, but glare-cut, family sign, mourning mark, and ward. Teeth are often darkened by bitter roots chewed against cold and hunger. Hands are strong, narrow, scarred by cord-burns and fish spine pricks, with nails kept short except among certain specialists who use one hardened thumbnail for splitting reed, scraping fungus, or teasing larvae from bark.

They dress as if clothing were another kind of dwelling.

The first layer is close and oily: fish-skin, softened hide, felted hair, or woven nettle rubbed with rendered fat. Over that come wraps of reed mat, bark-cloth, hare fur, bird down, and stitched leaf membrane. Their garments overlap like scales or thatch, shedding drip downward. Nothing swings freely if it can be tied down. Sleeves narrow at the wrist. Hoods close tight around the jaw. Belts are not belts but tool gardens: little hooks, loops, pouches, knives, awls, shell cups, wax packets, dried fungus, spare cord, a whistle never used except underwater, a child’s tooth charm, a twist of salt moss.

They do not wear bright color. Bright things call attention across the marsh. The only vividness appears close up: the red interior of a hood, a blue-black beetle wing sewn into a cuff, a hidden yellow thread marking marriage, a white feather tied beneath a collar for the dead. Their finery is not shine but complexity. A festival cloak may look at first like a gray heap of moss, but its surface is a map of grafted houses, ancestor willows, safe channels, forbidden pools, and the lineages of those who have slept under one roof.

They move softly, but not timidly. Their walk is a knowledge. They place weight through the outer foot first, testing. They pause with one knee bent when boards breathe. They shift baskets to counterbalance the slow roll of rafts. A child who can cross the old alder causeway at frost-melt without touching a hand to the rail is considered more accomplished than one who can recite a hundred names. No one runs unless something is already lost. Speed belongs to water, foxes, flame, and panic.

Their language is low and close-grained. It has many words for wetness, many for rot, many for varieties of silence. There is a verb tense used for things that are becoming dangerous but are not dangerous yet. There are separate kinship terms for those born into a house, those grafted into it by marriage, those raised there after flood, and those whose dead have fed its beams. Names are often temporary. A person may be Reed-Under-Frost as a child, Three-Knots after apprenticeship, Eel-Mender in middle life, and finally Old Doorway if they become a patient guardian of thresholds. The city keeps the names that are useful. The spring may keep the rest.

They speak softly not because they lack passion, but because loud feeling is considered badly made. Anger is shown by unwinding cord too fast, by setting a knife down edge-out, by leaving a door-knot untended. Joy is a hand pressed briefly to the crown of another’s hood. Grief is practical and almost unbearable to watch: mats washed, tools sorted, hair cut, body wrapped, root cradle dug, willow chosen, knots tied by those whose hands do not stop shaking. They do not wail in the city. They go out past the listening cattails if they must make such a sound.

They are governed, if that is the word, by pruners, water-listeners, midwives, knot-keepers, and those old enough to remember floods no one else survived. No single person commands the city for long. Authority passes according to the problem. A channel belongs to the dredgers while it is silting. A sick house belongs to the rot-doctors. A dispute over marriage belongs to the knot-keepers. A winter flowering belongs to no one until the dreamers have slept beside it and woken, or not.

The pruners are among the most respected. They carry crescent blades of shell or iron and know where a wall may be cut without killing its room, where a roof vine must be pinched to keep insects away, how to persuade two neighboring huts not to strangle one another. Their arms are crosshatched with scars from thorns and splinters. They are listened to like physicians, because in the city architecture can fall ill.

The water-listeners sleep badly. They are trained from childhood to read pressure through planks and skin: tremor, pulse, draw, seep, lift. Some press their ears to bowls of spring water. Some lie belly-down on causeways with their fingers spread. They can tell when a submerged channel has changed course, when eels are massing, when thaw is coming under ice, when something large is turning below. People fear them a little because they are often right before there is evidence.

The knot-keepers maintain memory. They tie records into cords: births, debts, flood heights, marriages, seed stores, crimes, repairs, bad seasons, good mushrooms, years when the starlings abandoned the canopy. Their archives hang in dryish lofts over smoke, great curtains of knotted fiber that can be read by touch in darkness. A knot-keeper’s hands are never idle. Even while listening, they twist, measure, loop, and bind. To lie before a knot-keeper is thought foolish; even if they cannot prove the truth now, they will tie the uncertainty somewhere, and it will wait.

The midwives are also deathwives, as is sensible in such a place. They know how to warm a premature child inside a wall-nest lined with hare belly fur. They know which fungus slows bleeding, which snail slime seals cracks in skin, which reed tea brings sleep without stopping breath. They receive babies over steam and send the dead into roots. Their huts smell of iron, milk, bitter herbs, and old moss. People bring them broken tools to bless because anything that has opened a body may also open a path.

Children in the city are not sheltered from the marsh; they are introduced to it formally. They learn the safe planks by rhyme and bruise. They learn to distinguish edible beetle larvae from sacred ones, not because the sacred are prettier but because they live near old root cradles. They are taught to cup frogs gently, to never mock a rail for vanishing, to apologize to a house if they pull a live twig from its wall. Their games are quiet and exacting: balancing on sinking pads, threading reed rings in the dark, imitating bird clicks, guessing which covered bowl contains warm water by the steam on the wrist.

Adolescents are restless in a place that punishes restlessness. They sneak to the outer reeds to see wider sky. They dye hidden patches of clothing with risky colors. They dare each other to touch the frost rim of the central spring, to sleep one night in an abandoned doorway, to cross the hungry planks slowly. Some return with marsh lovers from other hidden pockets. Some leave and become almost mythical, reported years later by traders, thin and sun-blinded, speaking too loudly in dry villages. Some come back unable to sleep unless water is moving beneath them.

They do trade, though outsiders rarely see the city itself. At certain reed screens beyond the hollow they exchange smoked eel, medicinal moss, black peat dye, woven water-proof mats, rare fungi, snail purple, bog iron slivers, and trained willow cuttings. In return they take salt, flint, amber, antler, stories, sometimes grain that mostly ferments before it can be eaten. They distrust hard bread. They distrust wheels. They distrust animals taller than a deer. But they are curious, intensely so, and will spend nights taking apart a stranger’s bronze buckle to understand how it holds.

They have domesticated little in the dryland sense. Rather, they maintain agreements.

There are marsh hares that know which doorways leave peelings. Otters that may steal from anyone except the smoke-house, because long ago one was drowned for that and the lesson, people say, entered otter law. Certain frogs are encouraged in roof gutters because they eat midges. Beetle colonies are moved from wall to wall like livestock. Blind fish are penned in root pools and fed on scraps. Starlings are tolerated, honored, cursed, and watched, because their collective silence predicts things no person can. The tiny rails are never trapped. No one agrees on why. That is reason enough.

Dogs do not thrive there. Their paws rot, their barking brings trouble, and they dream too loudly. Instead, some families keep reed-cats: low, gray, half-wild creatures with webbed toes, long whiskers, and a habit of sleeping in warm sleeves. Whether they are truly cats or something the marsh made from cats over generations is unclear. They hunt water shrews and moths. They stare into empty corners. They refuse to cross the central pool bridges when offerings have been recently lowered.

The people’s bodies are healthy in some ways and burdened in others. They are hardy against cold damp that would sicken outsiders within days. Their lungs know smoke. Their stomachs accept bitterness, slime, fermentation, and hunger. But joints ache early. Coughs linger. Skin splits in winter despite grease. Some carry pale fungal scars where infections were stopped just short of taking hold. A beautiful person, by their standards, is warm-handed, steady-footed, quiet-eyed, and free of rot smell.

They fear dryness almost as much as flood. Too much dry weather shrinks the mat, cracks cisterns, opens old gas pockets, and turns friendly plants brittle. Fire can run invisibly through peat, eating the ground beneath the city until a floor collapses into heat. In drought, everyone becomes watchful and irritable. Lamps are hooded tighter. Cooking nearly stops. Children are forbidden to scrape sparks from stone. The old stories of red years are told in whispers, though they are already whispers in ordinary times.

They do not imagine themselves as masters of the hollow. Their oldest saying might be translated: We are permitted here because we are useful. Useful to whom is left deliberately broad. To the willow, because they prune and spread it. To the reeds, because they dredge channels. To birds, because they leave scraps. To the spring, because they remember it. To the dead, because they give them upward paths through roots. To one another, because no one survives alone on ground that thinks about swallowing.

Their religion, if separated from daily work, would lose its shape. They pray by repairing. They confess by cleaning filters. They bless a child by tying it to a living wall during its first storm, loosely enough that the wall may breathe. They mark adulthood by sending the youth alone to cut a willow wand from the root-cradle grove and return without letting either end touch black water. Marriage is a grafting: two door knots trained together over a season. Divorce is pruning, painful but sometimes necessary; if done badly, both houses may sicken.

The dead remain citizens. Not symbolically—materially. A grandmother may become the beam over the sleeping alcove. A drowned uncle may live on as bowl-wood if his willow grew straight. A stillborn child might be tied into a cradle hoop used for later infants, not morbidly but tenderly, so they continue to rock with the family. Outsiders sometimes find this horrifying. The marsh people find burial in sealed earth equally horrifying: to be locked away from use, from weather, from kin.

They are not gloomy, though the city is dark. Their humor is quiet and often cruel in the way of people who know exactly how fragile everyone is. They make jokes by misnaming planks. They imitate pompous traders by shouting in tiny voices, then shushing themselves theatrically. They carve obscene little figures from tubers and hide them in condensation bowls. Children put frogspawn in the boots of apprentices. Old women tell stories in which heroes die because they stand upright at the wrong moment.

Their art favors touch over sight. Carvings are made to be read by fingertips: ridged fish, spiral seeds, maps of current under smooth wood. Songs, when sung at all, are hummed into bowls or through reed tubes so the sound enters water rather than air. They make shadow plays behind leaf membrane screens, not with bright flame but with fungus glow and moving fingers. Their finest works are living ones: a doorway coaxed into the shape of a sleeping heron, a roof that sheds rain into seven bowls at once, a root bridge trained over twelve years to flex but not break under flood.

They count time by cold, hatch, bloom, molt, and water level. A year is not a circle but a breathing: swell, loosen, rot, freeze, crack, seep, green, swarm, darken. The central spring has its own calendar. Some nights it steams straight upward. Some nights the steam crawls low. Sometimes no offering is made because everyone wakes knowing it would be wrong. No one admits the spring speaks. No one denies it either.

And beneath all the human life, around it, through it, making use of it, lives the greater citizenry of the hollow.

The beetles in the walls are not pests exactly; they are masons, composters, thieves, omens. Pale worms turn the sleeping floors from below. Fungi stitch roof beams together and sometimes unmake them. Frogs inherit gutters from grandparents. Owls dream in chimneys and cough up bones that children collect for needles. Blind fish circle in black pools, remembering light only as a pressure on the skin. Bog-deer browse door moss with the delicate arrogance of nobility. White foxes pass between huts like thoughts no one invited. Otters know every unsecured knot.

There are also older, slower inhabitants.

The rooted houses live there. Not metaphorically. They respond to handling. A well-loved house thickens its walls on the windward side, sprouts hooks where baskets are often hung, closes cracks with pale callus. A neglected one grows inward, drops damp into bedding, flowers strangely, welcomes insects. Doorways that have watched generations pass under them acquire expressions: suspicious, patient, hungry, amused. Children greet certain lintels by name.

The pools live there, each with temperament. One takes dropped things and returns them days later coated in white film. One shows reflections only of birds. One freezes before all others and thaws last. One is shallow unless crossed by a liar. Whether these qualities are chemistry, rumor, spirit, or accumulated coincidence is not a distinction the inhabitants find useful.

The central spring lives most of all.

It is not worshiped as a god exactly, nor treated as a mere water source. It is a presence with which the city has an unresolved agreement. It gives cold, concealment, channels, fish, frost, and dreams. It takes offerings, warmth, names, tools, and occasionally people. Those who fall in do not thrash long. Sometimes they come back to the edge without breathing, eyes open and calm, their hair full of tiny bubbles that do not burst. Sometimes they do not return at all. Afterward, some house elsewhere in the city may bud out of season.

What lives in the root-pelt beneath the marsh, then, is a braided society: human, animal, plant, fungus, water, stone, dead, and almost-dead. The people are only the quickest-speaking portion of it. They are negotiators in skin, carrying knives and condensation cups, pruning their shelters while being pruned in return. They live by not insisting too hard on separation: between home and organism, ancestor and timber, law and taboo, food and neighbor, path and creature, city and marsh.

If you saw one of them at the edge of the hollow, you might first mistake them for a crouched stump under a lichen cloak. Then the stump would turn its head. Two dark, steady eyes would measure the placement of your feet, the loudness of your breath, the smell of dryland on your clothes. A hand would emerge, cord looped around the wrist, knife hidden but ready.

They would not ask who you are.

Not first.

First they would whisper, “Are you sinking?”
