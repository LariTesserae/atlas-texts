The ones who live there are not a single people so much as a set of habits that learned to take human shape.

They are light-boned, narrow, weather-darkened where skin is exposed and pale where it is always wrapped. Their faces tend to look older than their years, not because they age quickly, but because they spend their lives listening. Wind carves attention into them. Their eyes are creased from watching dim distances, from reading movement in fog, from judging whether a bridge is merely swaying or beginning to fail. They do not stand loosely near edges. Even children know how to place their weight in three points, hand-foot-knee, shoulder-hip-rope, never trusting only the surface beneath them.

They are climbers before they are walkers.

Their toes are strong and separated by habit, their hands thick-palmed, scarred with resin burns and thorn cuts. Many wear fingerless mitts made from moth-fur and inner bark, with rough pads sewn across the palms for gripping wet wood. Their calves are corded from ascending root slopes; their shoulders slope slightly forward from years beneath harness and load. They move with an odd mixture of hesitation and certainty: pausing often, touching the bark, testing with a heel, then crossing some terrifying span as if the abyss were only another room.

They speak quietly indoors and loudly outdoors, but rarely waste a shout. The wind steals speech, bends it, carries it where it should not go. Their everyday language is full of clicks, hums, whistles, and chest-tones meant to travel through wood or under weather. Families have tapping names as well as spoken ones. A person may be called by a sequence knocked against a hollow root, recognizable at a distance when no face can be seen. Lovers tap along sleeping platforms. Children learn rhythm before letters, if letters are known at all.

Their settlements are temporary in principle, even when they have lasted three generations.

A village might occupy the lee of a colossal root-buttress where the wind breaks into tolerable currents. From far away it would barely be visible: a cluster of low, dark shapes tucked into the folds of bark, smoke hidden, walls curved to avoid catching gusts. The houses are woven rather than built, their frames bent from reedlike shoots, sealed with resin and layered with bark plates. Nothing is nailed unless it must be; lashings can be cut, beams can be carried, roofs can be unthreaded from their anchors when the place begins to change.

Every dwelling has more ropes than furniture. Ropes under sleeping mats. Ropes over doorways. Ropes along the floor. Ropes tied to the old, to children, to storage jars, to tools, to roof beams, to anyone feverish or drunk enough to forget the edge. They braid these from vine-fiber soaked, stretched, smoked, and rubbed with oil until it becomes tough as gut and supple in frost. A household’s ropes are intimate things. They carry hand-grease, old knots, repairs made during storms. To cut another family’s climbing line without cause is close to murder.

They dress in layered vegetable cloth, treated hide, moth-fur, and lacquered fiber. Their outer garments are dark green, brown-black, gray, colors that vanish against bark and hanging moss. Around the throat and wrists they wear tighter wrappings to keep out cold rain and crawling insects. Many carry hooded capes made from waxed leaf-skin, folded and refolded until soft, which can shed a brief storm or serve as a sling, shelter, or corpse-wrap. Bright colors are rare and meaningful: a red cord for someone who survived a bridge-collapse, a white feather for a child born during rime, amber beads for those who know the old routes through living wood.

They eat what the height permits.

Their staples are not grain fields, but gathered and tended foods: frost-fruits that must be opened with warm knives, black nutlets shaken from wind-pods, fungal loaves cultivated in shaded boxes of compost, sour lichens scraped and dried into sheets, root-pith steamed until its bitterness leaves it. They keep small animals in hanging pens—soft-hoofed nibblers, blind burrowers, cold-feathered fowl—that feed on peelings and fungal mash. They hunt vine-runners with nets, gliders with barbed darts, moths with lamps and sticky poles. Meat is smoked in low heat from fungus-cakes and resin scraps, becoming dense, bitter, and precious.

Water shapes their manners more than hunger does.

No guest is given food before water. A host who opens a filled gourd is making a promise. Children are taught to drink in small mouthfuls, never gulping unless frostbite or fever demands it. Rain-catch frames are tended like altars. When condensation falls at dawn, everyone rises. Even the sick lift cups if they can. For those few minutes the settlement becomes all motion: bowls set under dripping eaves, membranes tightened, cistern leaves emptied before eels or larvae find them, hair and cloth wrung into jars. Wastefulness is a moral failing. Spilling clean water indoors can silence a room.

They do not think of the plant-continent as scenery. Nor exactly as a god. It is older than their agreements and younger in places than their children. It feeds them, closes around them, betrays them, warns them, ignores them. To live there is to accept that intention may be everywhere or nowhere, and that survival does not require settling the question.

So they practice courtesy toward surfaces.

Before cutting, they touch the blade flat to the bark and wait. Before crossing a major root-bridge, they press ash-oil into listening knots, tap the family rhythm, and pause for the answer—not a voice, usually, but a shift, a tremor, a change in scent, a silence where insects had been. Skeptics exist, but even skeptics wait. Disbelief is no excuse for clumsiness.

Their religion, if called that, is mostly caution remembered beautifully.

They honor the Fallen: people, animals, limbs, villages, bridges, entire routes that vanished into the dark. The abyss beneath them is not simply empty space. It is the receiver of all that loosens. They do not throw refuse over edges casually. Scraps are composted, burned, fed to burrowers, or placed in appointed drop-clefts with words spoken after them. To drop something useful without attempting recovery is shameful. To fall beyond recovery is not shameful, but it changes a family. The dead who vanish downward are given wind-masks instead of graves: small carved faces tied in sheltered hollows, oriented toward the gap where they were lost.

Their elders are mapkeepers, but their maps are arguments, not records.

A map is made on cured bark, scratched with routes, knots, breathing cracks, cisterns, hostile vines, owl hollows, listening groves, and known echoes. Every wet season makes liars of these markings. So old maps are layered with corrections: burned dots, stitched lines, pieces cut away, new strips tied on. A good mapkeeper is not someone who remembers where the path was, but someone who knows how a path likes to change. They can look at a taut young ridge, at the angle of frost on a fin-trunk, at the direction of new tendrils after rain, and say: not there now, but there in six days; not that bridge in thaw-wind; this hollow will seal by next moon.

Their children are cherished and trained early. Toddlers are tethered by soft waist-lines to wall roots or older siblings. Games involve balance, knotting, identifying safe and unsafe fruit, naming winds by taste. A common nursery rhyme is a list of deaths made gentle by rhythm:

Do not sleep where green bark cracks.  
Do not drink where black water lacks.  
Do not answer under-cry.  
Tie your heart before you fly.

They laugh often, but their humor is dry and situational. They mock overconfidence more than fear. Fear is sensible. A person who admits fear can be taught where to put it. A person without fear is given short tasks far from the edge until humility returns.

Their specialists are many.

There are bridge-readers, who crawl out along living spans with ear pressed to wood, listening for hollows, sap pressure, beetle galleries, and the deep note of strain. There are cistern-watchers, usually patient old women or boys with quick eyes, who know the difference between harmless surface tremor and the slow rise of a transparent eel. There are sporewives and sporemen who cultivate warming fungi, medicinal molds, and edible rot without letting them devour the house. There are resin-handlers who collect amber tears from fresh wounds, risking fumes and sealing flows. There are echo-hunters who shout into certain high groves and wait hours for the world to return their voices full of news.

Hunters are respected but watched. To hunt in this place is to bargain with appetite. They wear little chimes of seed-shell or bone, not to scare prey, but to tell companions where they are when fog thickens. Their bows are short, made for crouched shooting among trunks. Their arrows carry heads of bone, black glass, thorn, or rare metal. Some use glider-cloaks—membranes stretched over light frames—to cross narrow gaps in emergencies, though only the reckless call this flying. True flight belongs to animals and fools.

A good hunter knows when not to pursue. Warm smells downward are left alone. A wounded animal that enters a newly opened plant-chasm is mourned as lost. If vine-runners fall silent all at once, hunters freeze. If moths abandon the undersides of leaves during still weather, hunters return home without complaint.

They have medicine born from discomfort. Salves against frost-crack. Poultices to draw thorn poison. Bitter teas that slow fever. Fungal pads packed into wounds. Resin splints that harden around broken fingers. For deep cold, they place a sufferer over fermenting leafbeds and cover them with moth-fur, warming them from below like seed. For grief, they prescribe labor that requires both hands: rope-braiding, scraping lichen, tending water frames. Not because work cures grief, but because idle grief near an edge is dangerous.

Their music belongs to wind and hollow wood.

They make flutes from reed-stems, drums from stretched membrane-fronds, and great low horns from naturally curved root tubes. Much of their song is responsive: one singer begins with a phrase, the wind takes part of it, another answers from another ledge. In storm shelters they sing into the walls, letting the wood deepen their voices until the house itself seems to hum. Some songs are meant less for ears than for structures. Bridge-songs are sung at pitches that reveal cracks by sympathetic buzz. Lullabies use chest-tones that soothe infants and, people say, calm the listening knots.

They count time by wet seasons, frost-whitenings, migrations of bridges, ripenings of dark fruit, and the births of cracks. “Before the Red Split opened.” “Three rains after the East Tendril touched.” “In the year the old ladder disappeared.” Age is relational. Someone may be “old enough to remember open sky beyond the black ridge,” which means more than a number.

Their trade is infrequent but vital.

From lower altitudes, if such places can be reached, come metal scraps, salt, hard stone, pigments, bone from large beasts, stories of broad water and fixed ground. In return they send resin, medicines, moth silk, rare cold fruits preserved in wax, echo-maps, and lightweight cord prized by climbers elsewhere. Traders are strange figures: part diplomat, part acrobat, part weather prophet. They travel with redundant ropes, sealed water, gifts for isolated shelters, and apologies prepared for plants they may offend.

Hospitality is cautious but real. A stranger is first anchored, then questioned. Their gear is inspected for pests, spores, and careless blades. If they carry an axe uncovered, someone will cover it for them without asking. If they step too heavily on a breathing surface, a child may hiss a correction. But once accepted into a shelter, they are fed, warmed, dried, and warned in great detail about everything nearby that can kill them.

Their architecture has no straight lines unless imported. Straightness catches wind, cracks under flex, and insults the shape of the world. Doorways are low and curved. Floors are slightly concave so spilled water gathers at the center. Sleeping niches are set into walls like nests. Storage hangs from ceilings in sealed baskets safe from burrowers. Every house has a storm posture: shutters tied, inner ropes strung, roof vents sealed or opened depending on pressure, lamps hooded, knives sheathed, children clipped to wall-lines.

Fire is distrusted. Flame eats air, calls wind, angers resin, and spreads too quickly through dry dead curtains. They prefer smoldering fungus-cakes in clay bowls, red and oily and controlled. True open flame is ceremonial or desperate. A funeral for someone whose body is recovered may involve a thumb-sized flame held in a seed cup, passed from relative to relative, then pinched out before the wind can take it.

Their ceremonies are practical acts given memory.

At first harness, a child climbs three body-lengths above the home platform while relatives hold the safety line below. At first solo water-gathering, they are given a gourd carved with their hand-tap. At first blood from a useful task, not an accident, they receive a work knife with a rounded back so it can be pressed respectfully to bark before cutting. Pair-bonding involves braiding two ropes into one length and then deliberately unbraiding part of it again, acknowledging that no life can be only together; people must move, scout, gather, return.

Pregnancy is handled with elaborate care. The pregnant do not cross uncertain bridges if any other path exists. Birthing rooms are warmed by fermentation boxes and lined with clean moth-fur. Newborns are shown water before sky. A drop is placed on the tongue, then on the brow, then on the sole of each foot: drink, think, cling.

They fear many things, but most deeply they fear sudden newness. Fresh cracks. Unweathered bark. Warm vapor from an opening that was not there yesterday. A familiar route altered in silence. They know old dangers can be negotiated; new growth has not yet learned them, and they have not yet learned it. Young ridges are treated like unpredictable animals. No one camps on them. No one trusts their smoothness.

They also fear calls from below.

Everyone has heard one, or knows someone who has: a voice rising from under a bridge, sounding like a lost child, an old friend, a goat, a song half-remembered. Explanations vary. Wind in root hollows. Predators. Echoes returning wrong. The hungry dead. Certain flowers exhaling through cavities in the plant-mass. The rule remains the same: do not answer. To answer gives location, or attention, or permission. None of these should be given downward.

Their relationship with animals is close but unsentimental. Vine-runners raid their stores; they are also omens. Moths provide fur and silk; their sudden flight can warn of pressure change. Transparent eels are hated, respected, occasionally eaten after dangerous capture. Ribbon-cats are admired for silence and cursed for taking fowl. Hook-clawed owls are left offerings of gut and bone near nesting hollows, not from worship exactly, but because an owl that hunts away from the village is preferable to one that learns the taste of tethered children.

In the deepest plant-chasms, where larger things move, the people place no names unless necessary. Naming invites story, and story invites young hunters to prove themselves. Instead they speak of “that which brushes,” “the low caller,” “the branch-breaker,” “the warm below.” Their caution has preserved them better than heroism would.

They are not primitive in mind, only constrained in material. Their engineering is exquisite because mistakes fall for miles. Their knots are mathematical. Their rain-catch angles are adjusted by wind season and leaf-curl. Their shelters flex rather than resist. Their medicines distinguish between thirty kinds of rot. Their navigators can cross a dark, moving, vertical wilderness by texture, smell, echo, and the direction condensation runs along a stem.

Their stories say they came from below, though “below” may mean ground, another green mass, a mythic root, or a time when the continent was smaller. In some tales the first people climbed to escape floods. In others they were born from seedpods split by lightning. In others they were abandoned by traders who found they could not descend. The stories disagree without conflict. Descent is difficult enough that origin has become less important than continuation.

Some among them dream of lower worlds: of soil that does not flex, of rivers too wide to cup, of fires that can burn in open air, of animals too heavy to climb. A few leave, roping themselves to caravans and vanishing down through cloud. Fewer return. Those who do speak of terrible abundance and oppressive stillness. They say the ground below never sways, and this unsettles the listeners more than danger would.

Others dream outward instead. Across the blue-black gaps, other plant-masses loom, ragged and half-hidden. The people watch them as one might watch neighboring islands or stars. Sometimes lights appear there: orange points, fungal blooms, lightning scars, maybe other villages. Echoes arrive from impossible distances carrying chopped fragments of calls. There are legends of root-bridges that once connected the great masses, and of travelers who crossed when the wind froze and the living spans stiffened. There are also legends of whole clans caught midway when a bridge decided to abandon one side.

The boldest expeditions are not downward but across.

They carry folded glider frames, spare rope, resin pitons, bone pegs, water bladders, sleeping cocoons, and seed-offerings. They move during certain cold intervals when winds are predictable. They mark their passage with hanging knots and scent smears, hoping the route will remain long enough for return. If they succeed, they bring back new fruits, new diseases, new songs, new fears, spouses with unfamiliar tapping names. If they fail, wind-masks appear facing the gap.

What lives there, then, is a people who have made a culture out of not falling.

But not only fear. Also grace.

They know the joy of crossing a bridge at dawn when frost sparks along every vein and the air below is full of cloud. They know the first warm mouthful of fungus broth after a storm. They know how children laugh when condensation begins its morning cascade and everyone is suddenly wet together. They know the hush inside a living chamber lit by green-gold saplight, columns rising like the ribs of a hidden beast. They know the satisfaction of a knot that holds, a cistern cleared of eels, a route found open after weeks of dread.

They love their perilous world because love, there, is inseparable from attention.

To love a child is to tie the harness correctly. To love a spouse is to check the bridge before they cross. To love the dead is to remember where the edge broke. To love the plant-continent is to take only what can be taken without waking too much hunger. To love oneself is to admit that the abyss is patient and does not need help.

In the dim green morning, they emerge from their low shelters one by one, hooded, roped, carrying gourds and hooked poles. Their lamps are shuttered. Their breath smokes. Around them the vast suspended botany creaks and drinks and shifts. Droplets gather on every hair and thorn. Far below, darkness moves like weather.

A child taps a question into the house-root.

An elder places her palm against the bark, waits, and taps back.

Then the condensation falls, and the people lift their cups.
