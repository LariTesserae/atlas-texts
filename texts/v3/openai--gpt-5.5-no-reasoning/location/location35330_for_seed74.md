The place is a small industrial town almost entirely built out of living plants.

Not overgrown in the abandoned sense. Not ruins swallowed by green. The green came first, and the town is a practiced arrangement inside it: pruned, persuaded, lashed, grafted, coppiced, tapped. Everything has been here long enough to stop looking improvised. The trunks lean in ways that have become architectural. Root-buttresses have been worn smooth by hands and boots. Vine-bridges sag at exactly the right angle because generations of gardeners, mechanics, and municipal crews have known where to cut, where to bind, where to let weight teach the fiber.

It lies low, in a shallow basin or old river-bend, though no river moves openly through it now. The ground is cool and dark, padded with layered vegetable matter: corky mats, fibrous loam, compacted leaf-skins, old pollen dust made into paste by mist. Here and there are pockets of water—cistern hollows in root bowls, black rain caught in the cupped bases of giant leaves, narrow rills running beneath grates of woven stem. Moisture appears intermittently rather than generously. A wet sheen gathers on surfaces in the morning, then retreats. Rain comes in brief, fine episodes, almost industrial in its timing: twenty minutes of silver ticking on foliage, then a long pause during which the town exhales condensation from every wall.

The air is cool. Not winter-cold, but cellar-cool, greenhouse-at-dawn cool, with a dampness that touches the skin and never quite becomes rain. Breath shows faintly in the early hours beneath the leaf-roofs. Metal sweats. Fungal beads form under railings. The town smells of chlorophyll, iron filings, wet rope, sour sap, peat, and the faint mineral tang of old machinery.

Vegetation is not scenery here. It is ground, building material, civic system, weather-maker, occupation. The streets are corridors cut through living mass, floored by tough rhizome plates and old root pavements. Some avenues are broad enough for carts and narrow engines; others are just cool green seams between hedge-walls, shoulder-wide, where leaves brush your sleeves and drip on your neck. The houses are grown frames: willow ribs, bark panels, lichen-sealed seams, roofs of overlapping leaves trained to shed rain into gutter-vines. Windows are not holes cut into walls but places where the plant has been taught to thin itself—translucent membranes, amber-green, veined and slightly elastic, glowing when daylight passes through.

Because the town is small, it can be understood by walking it in an afternoon. Its edges are clear. Beyond the last tended ring of trunks the vegetation becomes lower, drier, less inhabited: scrub, reed, old orchards gone tough and sparse, the pale grasses of the basin. Within the boundary, however, plant-life rises into a compact civic volume, like a grove organized into streets and worksheds. A person standing at the central clearing can hear almost all of it: the distant turn of belts, water dripping into collection tanks, the clack of seed-sorters, the sigh of vents opening in the leaf canopy.

There is space between things. That is one of the surprises. For all the dominance of living matter, the town is not jungled or choking. It is sparse in the way an old workshop is sparse: each object has a purpose; each trunk has been given room to thicken; each machine stands apart on its platform with walking space around it. Courtyards open unexpectedly, floored with moss or pale compacted fiber. A single dark tree may occupy the center of a square, its roots descending into maintenance pits, its crown clipped flat beneath a lattice of cables. Benches grow out of wall-stems. Drying racks stand empty for hours. Long green arcades contain little besides echo, condensation, and shafts of ordinary daylight.

The industrial parts are not hidden. They sit inside the plant town like organs in a body.

There are engine houses grown around iron cores, their walls made of living bark swollen around riveted plates. Flywheels turn behind screens of split bamboo and braided wire. Steam or vapor escapes through hollow trunks that function as chimneys, emerging above the canopy as pale breath rather than smoke. In the cool air it hangs low before dissolving among leaves. Workshops hum with belt-driven cutters, presses, pulpers, looms. The belts themselves are not leather but cured vine-fiber, kept flexible with oils expressed from seed. Conveyor roots—trained, muscular runners—shift slowly along channels, carrying bundles of reed, bricklike cakes of compressed leaf, crates of harvested pods.

The machines are old, but not derelict. Weathered, patched, balanced. Their housings have been softened by moss and hand-polish. Their bolts are dark with age. Their rhythms are steady and unhurried: a piston sighing every twelve seconds, a millstone turning under a crust of green dust, a sap-pump knocking faintly underground. Many devices seem less manufactured than cultivated into collaboration with metal. A turbine sits in a shaft where intermittent water gathers after rain; when the cistern fills, the turbine wakes for an hour, lighting bulbs along the lower streets with a weak honey glow. Then it falls silent, and the bulbs fade, and the town returns to grey-green daylight.

Water is treated as a visitor with rights and obligations. Rain is caught everywhere. Roof-leaves funnel it into stem-gutters. Gutter-vines lead it into settling pods, cistern bladders, clay-lined root cellars. In the central square, a shallow basin fills only after weather, reflecting the canopy and the factory stacks for part of a day before draining away through municipal roots. Children, when there are children, know the schedule of puddles the way others know bells. A certain alley holds beads of condensation longest because its eastern wall is thick with cold sap. A certain stair becomes slick three hours before a storm. Some doorways grow little glassy films of water at dusk, membranes that tremble when touched.

Erosion has had its say. Nothing here is sharp unless recently cut. The town is settled into itself, every path worn to its correct hollow, every threshold compressed by use. Steps formed from root-knuckles are polished in their centers. Stone, where it appears, is ancient and low: foundation slabs, old culvert mouths, curb blocks swallowed halfway into bark. Corners have rounded. Channels have deepened. The living structures have thickened around past damage until scars have become ornament: spiral callus ridges around old pipe mouths, bark lips grown over iron brackets, whole walls warped around forgotten machinery and still standing because the tree accepted the burden.

There is an unusual rule to the place, though at first it only feels like local custom: nothing dead may remain unclaimed.

Not a leaf, not a splinter, not a bent nail if it has touched sap. Organic waste goes into digesters: warm, dark pits breathing faintly beneath grates. Broken beams are soaked and seeded until they root again, or else pulped for panels. Dead insects, rare as they are, vanish overnight into the small economy of mold and mite. Even smoke is discouraged because it is waste escaping too quickly. The town metabolizes. It does not throw away; it recirculates, composts, ferments, steeps, extrudes.

Perhaps because of this, animals are scarce. One expects birds in so much green, but there are few. Sometimes a small grey thing slips along a cable and is gone. Sometimes a pale moth opens and closes against a warm engine casing. In the cisterns live translucent larvae that appear only when the water is high. There may be a fox-sized creature in the outer scrub, known by prints in damp loam and the occasional careful absence of stored seed. But the town belongs primarily to plants, people, and machines. Pollination is handled by bellows, brushes, vibration rigs. Pest control is done with scent, pruning, and patient inspection. The lack of animal noise makes every mechanical sound more present.

Mostly, though, it is quiet.

Not silent. There is too much happening for silence. Leaves creak as they slowly turn toward light. Drips fall at long intervals into tanks. Fibers tighten with tiny ticks. Somewhere a pulley complains softly. In the workshops there are periods of concentrated activity—saws whining, presses thumping, carts rattling over root-plates—but these sounds do not dominate the whole town. They are absorbed by vegetation. Bark and moss drink echoes. A shout travels poorly. Bells are muffled unless hung in open clearings. Even rain arrives quietly, because so much of it lands on leaf, sponge, loam.

The light is ordinary but filtered into many ordinary variants. Morning comes milky and green through the canopy, not dim but diffused. Noon reaches the open courts in clean grey-white shafts, showing the fine mist that rises from damp walls. Late afternoon warms the bark facades and makes the sap-windows glow like old bottle glass. At night, if the cistern turbines have power, low lamps burn at intersections: amber bulbs nested in calyx-shaped housings, enough to mark routes but not enough to banish shadow. Some leaves fold at night, opening gaps where stars can be seen in small, precise pieces.

The town’s activity is cyclical and layered. It is never rushing, but always doing something.

Before dawn, condensation crews move through the lanes with hooked poles, tipping leaf-cups into collectors. They wear oilcloth aprons and felted gloves, and their footsteps are dull on the root pavement. Then the vent-keepers open slatted blossom-vents in the factory walls to equalize humidity. The first engine starts when enough water pressure has gathered in the high cistern. Its slow cough wakes a line of belt-driven looms where reed-fiber is woven into mats.

By midmorning, pruning crews are at work on the roofs, cutting away excess growth before it blocks light from the lower dwellings. Sap is drawn from marked trunks into glass gauges, then diverted to vats where it is thickened into resin, adhesive, lacquer, fuel. In the outer yards, long racks of plant-fiber dry during the rare bright hours. If clouds gather, the racks fold themselves under awnings by counterweight. If rain actually comes, everything changes: gutters begin speaking, cistern valves knock, turbines spin, children run out to watch the central basin fill, and the whole town becomes briefly electrical.

After rain, the machines become livelier. Workshops schedule their heaviest tasks for the wet hours and the hours just after. A stamping mill may run then, pounding seed husks into board. A cold press may squeeze oil from blue-black kernels. Lamps may glow in offices otherwise left dim. The town seems to seize moisture as permission to intensify. Then, as the caught water lowers in the tanks, work slows again. Belts stop. Valves close. The soundscape thins to dripping and leaf movement.

Because the place is old, its strangeness has become municipal. There are ordinances for shade. Licenses for grafting. Public notices etched into bark plaques, renewed each spring as the surface grows outward. Streets are renamed when their supporting trees flower. Property lines are not straight but seasonal, negotiated according to root spread, canopy right, drainage obligation. A dwelling may be required to open one wall for airflow during the damp months. A workshop may owe heat to the nursery groves. No one may cut a load-bearing vine without three witnesses, one of whom must be a gardener-engineer.

The settlement has infrastructure in the way a body has circulation. Under the paths run bundled roots, pipes, mycelial cables, drainage cords, insulated electrical lines patched with resin. Some carry water; some carry signal; some carry nutrients to civic trees that no longer feed themselves fully from the soil. Maintenance hatches are oval bark-doors set low in walls. Opening one releases a smell of damp earth and metal. Inside are valves crusted with mineral bloom, pale roots moving slowly over ceramic conduits, beetle-shell gauges, copper tags green with age.

The weirdness is not spectacular. It gathers gradually.

A wall may pulse once as you lean against it, not like a heart exactly, more like a pressure adjustment. A street may be narrower in the evening than it was at noon because the flanking leaves have relaxed. In certain workshops, tools must be hung in prescribed positions or nearby tendrils will incorporate them by morning. The town’s oldest tree, in the square behind the municipal mill, grows around a clock face whose hands are turned by seasonal sap pressure rather than gears. It keeps poor hours but accurate months. People trust it for planting, curing, fermenting, grief.

Some doors open only after rain, when their hinges swell. Some stairways are safe only in dry weather. Some rooms are grown for a purpose and then sealed when the purpose is complete, left as green knots in the building mass. Occasionally one is reopened decades later and found lined with pale root-hair, containing perfectly preserved tools, letters, or the outline of furniture absorbed and remembered by bark.

The town has boundaries but not walls. Its perimeter is marked by low living fences, drainage trenches, and warning posts grown from thorn. Beyond them the land is open and quieter in a different way. The outer basin is sparsely vegetated compared with the interior: tussocks, old reedbeds, damp hollows, patches of exposed clay, and the occasional black stump of a former support tree. From outside, the settlement looks like a compact rise of green infrastructure in a mostly muted lowland, with vapor lifting from it in thin breaths. Not a forest exactly, not a factory district exactly, but a dense planted mechanism keeping itself alive.

The low elevation means the sky is broad wherever the canopy opens. Weather approaches visibly. Clouds come in flattened bands. Mist pools before sunrise and withdraws reluctantly from the alleys. Frost is possible but uncommon; when it threatens, the town closes itself. Leaves overlap, vents shut, warm digesters are opened beneath vulnerable roots, and the industrial heat of the workshops is routed through wall channels. In those nights the whole place becomes a sheltered lung, breathing slowly, smelling of fermentation and hot iron.

Materials are ranked by how well they cooperate with damp and growth. Iron is used, but it is painted, oiled, wrapped, or allowed to rust only where rust is harmless. Copper appears in tags, wires, gutters, green-stained handles. Clay lines cisterns and drainage troughs. Stone is too inert for many tastes but respected for foundations. The true civic materials are willow, reed, bark, root, resin, fungal felt, compressed leaf-board, and vine cable cured to a tensile hardness that can hold bridges, lifts, and roof trusses.

There are plazas where nothing much happens, and their emptiness feels intentional. A circular moss court beside the old press. A long rectangular drying yard with racks folded like sleeping insects. A ferry dock at a channel that carries water only after rain, its mooring posts alive and leafy. These spaces give the town breath. They keep the plant-mass from becoming oppressive. They also allow light to reach the lower growth, and allow people to gather when machines are stopped.

The people, if present, would move with the attentiveness of those living among slow powerful neighbors. They do not slam doors. They listen before cutting. They carry knives, gauges, twine, oil cans. They know which walls are asleep and which are under tension. They can distinguish the drip of clean condensation from the thicker fall of sap. They speak quietly because loudness is unnecessary and because the town itself is always listening in some botanical, mechanical way: not conscious perhaps, but responsive.

In late afternoon, the place becomes especially itself. The day’s ordinary light slants through the trained canopy. Steam from the last active engine gathers under the leaves. A pump knocks three times and stops. Water threads down a gutter-vine into a dark cistern. The green walls cool. Somewhere a rare bird, or something birdlike, makes a single questioning note and is not answered.

Then the leaves shift, almost together, adjusting to evening. The streets darken by degrees. Lamps come on only where the stored rain allows. The old industrial town of plants settles into its night processes: fermentation, root pressure, slow repair, valves closing, membranes thickening, sap descending through civic trunks. It is quiet, but not asleep. It is established, balanced, weathered into function. A small low town made of living architecture and patient machinery, damp only in intervals, green beyond reason, cool to the touch, always working.
