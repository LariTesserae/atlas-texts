At this height the world has forgotten weather in the ordinary sense.

There are no rivers, no dew, no buried aquifers, no fog lifting in the morning from hollows. The air is too thin and too cool to carry any generosity. It moves instead as a dry pressure, a mineral wind sliding over miles of elevated plateau and through the empty ribs of an ancient city. Nothing here gleams wetly. Nothing softens at the edge. Stone powders to dust, dust compacts to crust, crust is taken into root and rind and old vegetable bone.

And yet the place is almost entirely plant.

Not green, or not mostly green. From a distance the plateau appears covered in a low, dark, fibrous hide: black-violet mats, ash-colored lichen towers, coppery ribs rising in disciplined curves, pale photosynthetic membranes stretched between ruined pylons like old sails. The plants do not grow from soil so much as constitute the soil. They are the ground, the walls, the roofs, the roads, the drains that never drained water, the retaining terraces, the public stairs. Their tissues are dry and tough, layered like horn, cork, paper, woven carbon. They creak when the wind moves through them. They click as they expand and contract under the dim sky.

The city is built, but not built out of inert matter. Or perhaps it was once built out of inert matter and has since been patiently translated. Blocks of basalt, ceramic, metal, and pale engineered stone remain, but only as inclusions, fossils within the greater growth. Root-bridges grip avenues from both sides, meeting overhead in vaulted arcades. Apartment stacks are honeycombed with woody chambers. Towers lean under mantles of dry vine and articulated bark, their outer skins replaced by plates of living tissue that open and close in the weak light. At plaza centers stand enormous trunks without leaves, fluted and hollow, rising several hundred meters into the cold air. These are not trees exactly. They are atmospheric engines.

They drink no water. Nothing here drinks water.

The life of the plateau runs on other exchanges: salt gradients, wind charge, mineral volatiles, the faint rain of cosmic particles, the temperature difference between exposed surface and shaded interior, the slow exhalations of deep rock. The great trunks are rooted through the city and into fractures in the uplifted crust. Their lower filaments enter seams of metallic ore and ancient buried machines; their upper crowns comb the sky for static and dust. At night—if the dim lowering of light can be called night—their branches lift hair-fine vanes into the wind and crackle with blue-white discharges. By day, under a small remote sun or perhaps beneath a permanent veil of high particulate haze, they unfurl matte membranes the color of bruised slate and old olives, catching what little light reaches the plateau.

The result is abundance without lushness.

Every surface is colonized, but not crowded. Life is everywhere, yet spaced, economical, austere. The avenue floors are broad and open, paved in hardened root-skin that has grown over whatever original material lies below. Between the avenues rise neighborhoods of plant-architecture: clusters of pod-houses, terraced racks, hollow stems, ribbed chambers stacked like seed capsules. But wide spaces remain between them, salt-pale courts where nothing grows except a few dark knots of dormant tissue. These gaps are important. They are not failures of fertility. They are reservoirs of emptiness, exposure fields where wind can accelerate, where charge can gather, where migrating animals can run without being harvested by the city’s slower mouths.

The settlement extends for many kilometers along the flattened spine of the highland. It is neither ruin nor living metropolis in any simple way. It has settled into a final-form urbanism, a civic ecology so old that intention and succession can no longer be separated. Streets still follow a plan. Districts still have borders. Some towers align to horizon markers. Processional ways lead to platforms at the cliffs, where one can see the rest of the plateau dropping in vast eroded steps into dimness. But the planners are gone, or incorporated, or no longer recognizable as separate from the vegetal infrastructure.

There are signs of technology everywhere, though almost none of it presents itself as machinery.

A wall may flex away from a passerby before contact, its bark tessellating into hexagonal scales. A seed-pod the size of a tram car may hover for several minutes above a plaza without visible lift, humming as if thinking through stone. Thin root-threads crossing the ground rearrange themselves overnight into new glyph-like circuits. In certain districts, towers are connected by taut lines of grown crystal and lignin, and along these lines travel pulses of darkness rather than light: small absences that move against the eye, data encoded in occlusion, in interruptions of the dim sky behind them. The city computes in shade, tension, ionic imbalance, scentless chemistry, and the memory of growth.

Some streets are impassable not because they are blocked, but because they are being solved.

You come upon them and find the paving raised in slow waves, root-ribs lifting, splitting, joining, sinking again. A building leans open, displaying layers of rooms as dry and intricate as a wasp nest. Filaments extend across the street, touch opposing walls, withdraw, reattach elsewhere. It is construction, repair, thought, digestion, politics—perhaps all at once. No workers appear. No tools strike. Yet the noise is immense.

Sound dominates the plateau. The lack of water does not make it silent. It makes every dry sound carry.

The wind roars through the high hollow trunks, each one tuned differently by age, diameter, and internal architecture. Some moan in low organ notes that seem to come from underfoot. Others shriek at frequencies felt in the teeth. Bark plates clatter. Seed-cases rattle in suspended racks. Ceramic fragments embedded in old walls tap together. Across the open courts, herds of small six-limbed grazers—or pollinators, or thieves—move in sudden waves, their chitinous feet producing a sound like thrown beads. Overhead, gliding fauna snap and boom as they alter the stiffness of their wing-sails.

There are calls everywhere: metallic chirps from narrow-bodied animals nested in the ribbing of towers; hoarse barking from larger creatures that prowl the outer terraces; ultrasonic swarms whose presence is betrayed by the trembling of dust; deep percussive knocks made by something inside the great trunk columns. At intervals, the whole city answers itself. A charge front passes through the aerial vanes, or a pressure wave crosses the plateau, and thousands of structures resonate at once. For several minutes the air becomes architecture audible as thunder: pipes, drums, rattles, bowed strings, dry leaves, bone flutes, engines without combustion.

Then it subsides into clicking.

The animals are numerous, though never packed thickly. They appear and vanish in the wide intervals between plant structures. Most have adapted to dryness by becoming lacquered, sealed, clever with waste. Their bodies are armored in translucent mineral plates or dense felted scales. Many carry gardens in grooves along their backs—not moist gardens, but cultures of powdery symbionts that process salts and exhaled gases. Some feed by rasping the city’s outer skins; others sip electrical discharge through whiskers or antennae. There are tall browsing things, jointed like walking tripods, that stand beside the living walls and scrape off the day’s photosynthetic film with black tongues. There are flocks of paper-bodied gliders that roost beneath the wind-vanes and unfold only when the updrafts strengthen. There are predators with silent padded feet and huge heat-sensing pits, moving at dusk through the alleys of root and carbon.

Nothing smells of rot. Decay here is too dry for rot. Dead matter delaminates, splinters, powders, is sorted by static, collected by crawling detritivores, passed into root mouths, and compacted into new structural tissue. Bones do not bleach so much as become polished, incorporated into pavements or hung in fibrous netting as resonators. Excrement is rare and valuable. Breath is valuable. Shade is valuable. Motion is valuable because it disturbs charge.

The climate is cold enough that exposed surfaces hold the memory of night long into the day. In shadow the plant-walls are almost blue. Touching them would feel like touching old leather stretched over stone. Sun-facing membranes warm slightly and open their pores—not to transpire, but to admit dust, ions, trace gases, and microscopic migrating things. The plants have solved the problem of thirst by abandoning tenderness. Their leaves are vanes, blades, scales, mirrors, antennae, mouths for light and electricity. Their roots are cables, miners, anchors, nerves.

In the oldest districts, erosion has nearly won and yet has not changed anything quickly for a very long time.

The plateau edge is rounded and lowered, its cliffs cut back into amphitheaters of wind-scoured rock. Ancient stairways descend into air, ending abruptly where the land has retreated. Whole neighborhoods have been planed down to knee-high foundations, then recolonized by low mat-growth whose patterns preserve the vanished street grid. Here the city looks like a fossil of itself, but the mats are active, listening. They lift tiny cilia when shadows cross them. They harden beneath footsteps. They remember pressure.

Farther inward stand the civic spires: older than the surrounding architecture, perhaps older than the plateau’s current height. They are vast, tapering, grown from some material between wood, ceramic, and bone. Their surfaces are eroded smooth on the windward side and extravagantly folded on the lee, where centuries of new growth have accumulated in pleats and buttresses. Around each spire is a cleared ring where no large plant rises. Animals gather there despite the exposure. Some come to mate, some to shed skins, some to be hunted, some to listen. The spires emit a constant low vibration, and at certain times the ground around them arranges dust into delicate radial figures.

It is difficult to tell whether these spires are monuments, organs, transmitters, or elders.

Perhaps the distinction is meaningless here.

The rules of the world reveal themselves gradually:

Water is poison, myth, or extinct reagent. Any free droplet would be seized, isolated, dismantled chemically before it could disrupt the dry metabolisms.

Growth is architecture. A plant does not merely occupy space; it zones it, loads it, bridges it, insulates it, remembers it.

Light is scarce and therefore supplemented. The ecosystem feeds on gradients: thermal, electrical, gravitational, mineral, informational.

Death is a construction phase.

Emptiness is cultivated. Open ground is not barren but functional, part of the breathing and sounding of the city.

Noise is communication, weather, mapping, mating, warning, and computation.

Nothing moves fast for long unless it is animal, windborne, or dangerous.

The more one looks, the more the city seems arranged not for inhabitants but for processes. A boulevard channels afternoon wind toward a grove of resonant towers. A plaza accumulates dust in spiral banks that are harvested by creeping mats at night. Elevated rootways connect districts not by shortest distance but by charge potential. Some buildings turn their sealed facades toward the sky for years, then slowly invert their inner chambers outward when a long cycle completes. There are gates that open only during electrical storms, though there is no rain; markets whose stalls are seed-cups exchanging spores, salts, heat, and memory; amphitheaters where animals gather to have their shells etched by plant filaments in return for carrying pollen-like data to distant colonies.

Under the dimness, colors remain subdued until the city charges.

Then, in gusts, hidden pigments ignite: dull maroon becomes ember-red, ash-gray flashes silver, black bark reveals violet circuitry, pale membranes pulse with green-gold veins. Gliders wheel in the lit air like scraps of stained paper. The great trunks throw crawling coronas from their crowns. Every animal freezes or accelerates according to its kind. Predators use the glare. Browsers kneel. The mat-ground tightens. For a moment the whole dry city appears drenched—not in water, but in signal.

Then the light falls back.

Dust resumes its slow migration. The wind keeps sawing at the high edges of the world. Somewhere beyond the nearest district, a tower cracks, not catastrophically but as part of a controlled molt, shedding an outer century of growth. The falling plates strike bridges, roofs, courts, and resonator bones, and the noise rolls outward through the plant-city like applause from something enormous and unseen.

This is a place at the end of erosion and not yet dead; a cool, high, waterless plateau where vegetation has become the remaining form of civilization. It is sparse because it is complete. It is ancient because it is still active. It is urban because every root knows the plan of the streets.
