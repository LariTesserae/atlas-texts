Below the surface, where one expects stone, there is a city of plants.

It lies in a vast excavated hollow—too broad to read as a cave, too enclosed to be a valley. The ceiling is somewhere far overhead, a pale continuous vault of mineral and root, pierced at intervals by engineered light-wells. These apertures do not reveal the sky directly. Instead they admit a level, cool illumination, filtered through lenses, mirrors, mats of translucent fiber, and the ribbed membranes of living leaves. The light has the steady quality of a winter morning or a greenhouse in overcast weather: visible, useful, almost natural, but without sunbeam or glare.

There is no water.

Not merely no rivers, no pools, no dripping walls—the place is dry in a deeper sense. The air carries almost no humidity. Surfaces do not glisten. Pipes do not sweat. The soil, where there is soil, is not soil in the ordinary way but a powdery mineral bed threaded with roots and filaments, gray-white, ocher, and dull green, like ground bone mixed with ash and pollen. The plants here do not drink as surface plants drink. They draw on salts, vapors, carbon-bearing gases, and slow chemical gradients. Some feed from buried industrial lines that exhale measured quantities of nutrient mist too fine to feel as moisture. Others grow directly into exposed seams of soft mineral, dissolving what they need with dry acids and crystalline enzymes.

The whole city is botanical, but not lush in the wet sense. It is not a jungle. It is an arid forest of architecture.

Plant matter forms the streets, the walls, the roofs, the bridges, the railings. It is fibrous, lacquered, corky, leathery, mineralized. Towers grow as bundled trunks braided around iron frames; avenues pass through vaults of living bark; balconies bud out as shelf-fungi the size of rooms, hardened and polished until they can bear carts, workshops, crowds. The dominant vegetation is structural: column-plants, arch-vines, scaffold reeds, tessellated bark panels, tendonous root-cables, and pale underleaf membranes stretched like awnings between buildings.

Because the place is dry, the plants conserve themselves fiercely. Their leaves are small or folded inward, often silvered, black-green, blue-gray, or the color of old copper. Many photosynthetic surfaces are not leaves at all but broad vertical skins facing the artificial daylight. These skins hang from buildings like banners, rippleless in the still air. Some are translucent enough to show the veins within: geometric vascular maps pulsing very slowly with amber or greenish sap.

The city is open in extent but sparse in occupation. Districts are separated by wide mineral flats, unfinished plazas, raw trenches, and bare corridors of pale dust where nothing has yet taken root. One can stand at the edge of a grown neighborhood and see, across a half-mile of salt-colored emptiness, another cluster of living towers rising around a processing plant. Between them run rails, conduits, and root-lines on low trestles. The gaps matter. They give the city a sense of being newly seeded, not fully filled in. It is less a city completed than a city germinating according to a civic plan.

Many areas are under construction, though the construction is partly mechanical and partly horticultural. Gantries move quietly along tracks, suspending frames into which vines are trained. Industrial arms scrape mineral beds into terraces so root-systems can colonize them. Crews—or perhaps caretaker organisms shaped by crews—bind young trunks into lattices with clamps of ceramic and bone-colored composite. In some places, entire streets are still soft, their surfaces made of fresh, pale cambium covered with woven mats to prevent bruising. Elsewhere, foundations are being coaxed upward from seed cores planted in hexagonal pits.

The erosion here is embryonic. Nothing has yet been worn into gentleness. Edges remain raw: cut stone, exposed strata, sawed root, newly extruded resin. Instead of weathering, there is becoming. Walls are swelling into shape. Pillars thicken by the week. Doorways are pruned open, sealed, reopened elsewhere. The city does not crumble; it differentiates.

Cool air moves through it in regulated breaths.

Not wind exactly. Ventilation. The underground atmosphere is kept in motion by great slow fans and pressure lungs housed in botanical towers. These towers resemble seed pods merged with factories: ribbed, oval, studded with vents, their bases tangled in rootworks that penetrate deep into dry mineral. Inside them, turbines turn with a subdued rotational hush. They pull stale air through filters of mossless, dry frond-mats and push it out through avenues lined with sensory hairs. When the air changes direction, the hairs lean together, and the whole street seems to listen.

Despite the industry, the city is mostly quiet.

There are sounds, but they are kept low by plant mass and distance. A soft clacking from conveyors. The creak of trunks adjusting to load. The faint zippering noise of seed-valves opening. Electric hum from substations grown into bulbous lignin shells. Far-off impacts from excavation galleries, flattened by the fibrous architecture until they resemble someone knocking on a door in another building. Occasionally a freight cart passes on a suspended rail, its wheels murmuring over a track padded with cork. After it goes, the silence closes again.

The factories are not separate from the vegetation. They are grown into it.

One industrial quarter is arranged around tall compression mills where harvested structural reeds are stripped, braided, resin-hardened, and extruded as beams. The machines are enclosed in living housings whose inner bark absorbs vibration. Another district processes mineral salts: pale dust is lifted from subterranean beds, sorted electrostatically, and fed into root-reactors where fungal analogues and engineered plants convert it into pigments, catalysts, and slow fuel. There are kilns too, though flameless—induction furnaces set within heat-resistant husks, their glow pulsing behind veined black walls like coals seen through skin.

Power moves through the city as both electricity and metabolism. Thick cable-vines hang under bridges in bundled loops, carrying current along conductive fibers plated with metals drawn from the surrounding strata. Older lines are dead and woody, left in place as reinforcement. Newer ones flex slightly when load increases. At junctions, transformer trees stand in fenced gardens of porcelain insulators, their crowns pruned into antler-shapes. They tick softly, discharging static into arrays of copper thorns.

The streets are wide, but rarely crowded. Urban, yes—planned, zoned, inhabited, serviced—but with room, sometimes unsettling room, between things. A plaza may contain only three towers, a loading crane, a row of dormant market stalls grown shut for the cool cycle, and a herd of small browsing animals nosing at the shed scales of a maintenance vine. A boulevard may extend straight into the gray-green distance, bordered by living colonnades that are still no taller than a person. Empty foundations wait as circular beds, each with a seed-engine at its center.

Fauna is abundant if one learns to notice it.

Most animals are small or narrow-bodied, adapted to dry air and plant architecture. Pale lizards cling to bark walls, their skins patterned like flaking resin. Beetles the size of fists move in maintenance swarms, polishing leaf-skins with brushy forelegs and collecting mineral dust in baskets along their abdomens. Long-legged rodents nest in the cavities of old root conduits and emerge at dusk-shift to chew nutrient cork. Moths with paper-dry wings sleep in the undersides of bridges, waking when certain factories vent warm gas. Blind birds, or birdlike descendants of something once avian, travel through the open districts by sound and pressure; they perch on railings and click into the silence.

There are larger creatures too. Slow grazing forms with segmented lips move through permitted feeding corridors, trimming excess shoots from municipal vines. Their hides are layered like bark, and their breath smells faintly of alkali. Predators follow them: lean, cool-blooded animals that use the shadows between structural plants, stepping soundlessly across mineral flats. They are rarely seen in full. More often one notices a line of small animals suddenly freezing, the tilt of sensory grasses, the absence of beetle-noise from a wall.

Predation is tolerated. Perhaps necessary. The city’s rules include ecological ones as much as civic laws. Nothing here is simply wild, and nothing is wholly domesticated. Certain alleys are left unlit to preserve hunting routes. Certain towers host nesting cavities by regulation. Some root-lines are armored because burrowers will otherwise enter them; others are intentionally exposed to feed pollinator colonies. Waste is not dumped but allocated: bark shavings to grazers, insect chaff to fungal vats, mineral fines to seedbeds, dead animals to dry decomposition chambers where carrion plants unfold pale tongues in the dark.

The strangeness of the city does not announce itself all at once. At first it is only an underground settlement made of improbable plants. Then the rules accumulate.

Doors open by wilting. Staircases grow steeper during low-use cycles as their treads retract to conserve surface area. Street signs are pigment blooms that change according to nutrient traffic. Public clocks are flowers whose petals furl in response to pressure changes from the ventilation lungs. Some neighborhoods are legally immature and cannot be entered until their architecture finishes hardening. Others are senescent by design, scheduled to be harvested and regrown elsewhere when their load-bearing trunks reach a particular brittleness.

There are districts where buildings remember touch. Handrails thicken where many hands pass. Thresholds broaden for commonly carried loads. A frequently used shortcut may, over months, become an official passage as surrounding tissue withdraws, hardens, and forms lintels. Conversely, disused streets slowly close—not by collapse but by healing. The city edits itself according to pressure, chemistry, and habit.

At the lowest points, where one might expect groundwater, there are dry basins full of pale crystalline growth. These are not lakes but salt-gardens. The floor is cracked into polygonal plates, and from the cracks rise forests of glassy mineral needles protected by cages of rootwork. Plants here grow slowly, almost invisibly, their tissues incorporating salt into translucent ribs. Workers harvest the ribs with ceramic saws. The cut ends seal immediately, leaving rings like agate.

Above the basins, walkways curve between hanging gardens of xeric canopy. These are not decorative. They are air organs, carbon sinks, food farms, and insulation. Their leaves click faintly as they turn toward the light-wells. At intervals, pollination engines start: not roaring machines, just low bellows and charged grids that send clouds of dry pollen drifting through selected corridors. For a few minutes the air becomes golden and particulate. Animals retreat into shutters and hollows. Human or humanoid residents—if they are present—wear veils, masks, or smooth breathing shells. Then the fans reverse, pollen is drawn into capture nets, and the streets return to clarity.

The temperature remains cool throughout, never icy but persistently below comfort for bare skin. The plant surfaces hold the cold differently: cork and bark are temperate to the touch, mineral flats chill the feet, metal fittings sting. In shaded construction trenches the air feels older and denser. In factory zones, warmth gathers around machines, attracting insects and small animals. These warm pockets are socially and ecologically busy: repair crews, feeding swarms, heat-seeking reptiles, fungal processors, and vagrant seedlings all clustered around the same industrial exhalation.

Light varies by district but seldom disappears. The city has no true day and night, only operational phases. During growth phase, illumination is broad and white-green, encouraging architectural tissues to thicken. During maintenance phase, light narrows into amber bands along transit routes, leaving unused areas dim but not dark. During pollination phase, ultraviolet lamps wake patterns on leaves and animal skins: maps, warnings, invitations. The mineral flats glow faintly where dust catches the light, making distance difficult to judge.

Because it is underground, the horizon is architectural rather than celestial. Far views end in walls of root, columns, suspended ductwork, or the curve of the cavern vault. Yet the scale remains open. There are long sightlines down transit corridors, across basins, through scaffold forests. One can see whole neighborhoods at once: a young district of pale sapling towers; a mature industrial band with blackened reactor pods; an administrative grove of enormous columnar trunks whose crowns fuse into a vaulted civic hall; beyond that, raw excavation faces where the next quarter of the city is being prepared.

The excavation faces are perhaps the most honest part of the place. There the underlying world shows through: layered mineral, dry clay, compressed ancient sediment, veins of metal ore, pockets of crystalline gas hydrate sealed under transparent caps. The city has not yet grown over these wounds. Survey marks shine on the rock. Seed pits are drilled in regular grids. Robotic crawlers test chemical gradients. Bundles of dormant root-cable wait on reels like sleeping serpents. Nothing has softened. Nothing has aged. It is all intention, exposure, and the first gestures of life.

And still, even in these raw zones, plants have begun.

A hairline crack turns green at its edge. A sensor post sprouts an unauthorized tendril. Fine dust gathers around a seed no larger than a tooth. The first living film spreads over cut stone, matte and gray, almost indistinguishable from mineral until it divides into veins. The city is not expanding by conquest but by adhesion, by insinuation, by the patient conversion of emptiness into tissue.

The absence of water gives everything a severity. No rot, no mud, no dripping echo. Death dries quickly. Leaves shed as scales. Bodies desiccate and are carried whole to processing chambers. Smells are sharp and separate: resin, dust, ozone, alkali, cold bark, machine oil, bitter pollen. Fire is rare and feared, but combustion has difficulty taking hold in the regulated low-oxygen corridors; where heat is used, it is sealed inside ceramic throats and watched by sensor vines that blacken at the first trace of smoke.

The laws of the world seem built around restraint. Moisture is mythic or forbidden. Growth is abundant but rationed by chemistry. Space is plentiful but assigned. Light is artificial yet treated as weather. Buildings are alive but inspected like bridges. Animals are free until they interfere with circulation or load-bearing organs. Machines serve the plants, and plants house the machines, neither subordinate exactly. The city survives by keeping every process partial: enough industry to feed growth, enough growth to absorb industry, enough emptiness to prevent suffocation.

From a high walkway near the cavern wall, the place resolves into patterns.

The living districts appear as islands of muted green, black, ivory, and copper set in a pale underground plain. Transit lines connect them like veins between organs. Light wells descend as columns of cool brightness. Fans breathe. Factories pulse. Herds move slowly through pruning corridors. Predators travel the margins. Dust lifts and settles. New buildings swell by fractions. Old ones harden toward harvest. Far off, at the edge of visibility, a construction grove raises its first arches, thin and tentative, as though the city is sketching itself in air before committing to wood.

It is not a ruin, though it has the quiet of one. It is not a garden, though nearly everything is plant. It is not a cave, though no sky touches it. It is a dry, subterranean, industrial ecology in the act of becoming urban—a cool underworld city grown from disciplined vegetation, mineral hunger, and machines that have learned to whisper.
