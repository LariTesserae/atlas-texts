At this height the city is more like a weathering than a construction.

It spreads across a high plateau whose edges have long ago been softened by age. There are no sharp mountains here, though the place stands far above the lowlands; the summit country has been worn down over inconceivable time into broad backs, shallow saddles, rimless basins, and terraces of stone hidden under living matter. The horizon is open and pale. Distances seem easy to cross until one begins walking and discovers that everything is larger, slower, and older than it appears. Streets run for miles along the contours of the plateau, vanishing into curtains of leaf and pale industrial vapor. Districts sit separated by wide intervals of green emptiness, as if the city has thinned itself out to survive the altitude.

Vegetation is not decoration here. It is the ground, the wall, the roof, the insulation, the repair crew, the archive. The plateau’s actual rock is rarely seen except where erosion has polished it into exposed gray knuckles or where old machinery has cut through the root-mass. Most surfaces are plant: tough matting mosses layered thick as roadbed; lichenous plates that interlock like paving stones; woody vines braided into retaining walls; trunks hollowed and inhabited; fern-ribs grown into awnings; reedlike towers clustered around vents and chimneys. Buildings do exist, but they are difficult to separate from the forested tissue around them. Warehouses have barked over. Rail stations bloom. Water towers wear crowns of small, wind-flattened trees. Pipes emerge from leaves and vanish back into them.

The city seems planned by engineers and then slowly accepted by plants until both plans became indistinguishable.

The streets are broad, but sparse. Not empty in the clean sense—nothing here is clean—but open, with long distances between standing structures. A tramline may run alone across a mile of mossed causeway, flanked by low shrubs like dark wool and occasional towers of rusted iron latticed with vines. One district might consist of six huge factory-halls and a loading yard, half swallowed by green, with no other buildings visible except faint rooftops rising in the distance through the cool brightness. Then the way opens into a public square the size of a dry lakebed, its flagstones replaced by living pads of springy, silver-green tissue that close slowly after being stepped on.

The air is cool and wet only in episodes. Moisture appears in pockets, moods, and cycles rather than as abundance. Morning condensation beads on every surface, thick enough to fall as a local rain beneath the canopies. Gutters drip for hours under a sky that is already dry. Fog forms in the low places of the city—station cuttings, vine tunnels, the sunken courtyards of administrative blocks—and then tears apart in the plateau wind. Actual rain comes in brief diagonal visits, sweeping over one borough while another remains crisp and bright. Water gathers in cistern-flowers, in the cups of roof-bromeliads, in rubbery roadside hollows, in the interiors of giant curled leaves trained to serve as reservoirs. There are few rivers, but many suggestions of rivers: chains of pools, damp corridors, canals filled with root-filtered seepage, troughs where water once ran constantly and now returns only when the city exhales.

The light is surprisingly ordinary, and that makes the place stranger. Daylight falls plainly across impossible arrangements. The sun catches on windows embedded in bark, on conveyor belts disappearing into hedges, on electrical lines supported by living poles that lean and correct themselves over decades. Shadows are sharp enough to read by. The sky is not alien: high, cool, moving with white cloud. Yet perspective misbehaves under it. Roads sometimes appear to bend uphill and downhill at once. A block seen from afar has twelve smokestacks; approached, it has three; seen in a puddle, it has hundreds, all smoking backward into the earth. Alleys open into courtyards larger than the districts that contain them. Some stairways climb only seven steps but deliver a walker to a roofline from which the whole plateau city is visible, spread like an old green circuit across the height.

The erosion here has gone beyond ruin into composure. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even collapses have been digested into the civic order. Fallen walls are root terraces. Cracked chimneys are fungal apartments. The oldest roads are no longer roads but shallow hardened channels, polished by vanished traffic and occupied now by moss so dense it has a nap, like animal fur. Stone inscriptions have worn down to grammarless grooves, and the plants have learned them anyway: vines thicken around official names; flowers appear annually where boundary markers used to stand. The plateau has been settled so long that habitation has become geological. The factories, the public works, the municipal gardens, the erosion surfaces—all seem to belong to one final stable layer.

And yet the city is loud.

Sound travels strangely in the high, open air. From one side comes the steady hammering of a mill whose hammers are not seen, beating metal or seedpods or time into sheets. From another, the groan of turbines turned by intermittent water and assisted by great plant-muscles that contract when wet. Tram bells ring from miles away with disturbing clarity. Wind drags through vast leaf arrays mounted on towers, producing a continuous chord like an organ built for weather. Steam coughs from vents. Pumps thud below the moss. Conveyor chains clank inside green walls. Leaves rattle, scrape, whisper, and slap in gusts with the volume of crowds.

There are few animals, so few that each one becomes an event. A pale moth the size of a hand may cross a boulevard and vanish into a factory window. Small black birds nest only in abandoned insulators and sing at the exact pitch of failing brakes. Occasionally something mammalian moves under the plant mat, raising a traveling ripple, but it is almost never seen. The city’s ecology is overwhelmingly botanical, microbial, fungal, and mechanical. Pollination is often performed by vibration. Certain flowers open only when trains pass. Others require the sub-bass hum of power stations. Seed dispersal is handled by fans, exhaust, pneumatic tubes, workers’ coats, and storms.

Many plants here have adapted to industry not as a threat but as climate. They grow around heat differentials, feed on mineral dust, time their blooming to shift changes, and send roots into brickwork not to destroy it but to brace it. Long-rooted figs anchor old viaducts. Mosses insulate steam lines. Glasshouse algae are piped between neighborhoods to clean water and produce oils. The municipal lamps, still lit at dusk, are wrapped in translucent leaves that photosynthesize by day and glow faintly after sunset, extending the civic grid in green-gold points across the plateau.

Some districts are plainly urban. There are boulevards, plazas, foundries, depots, worker dormitories, archives, schools, water exchanges, power houses. But their organization keeps slipping between human logic and vegetal habit. Streets branch like stems. Neighborhoods reproduce by sending out runners: a line of sheds, a conduit, a band of moss-paving, then a cluster of new structures five miles away around a moisture source. Abandoned districts do not die; they go dormant. Their doors seal with bark. Their windows cloud with spores. Their machines slow until they are only ticking occasionally under leaves, waiting for rain or instructions or the return of a workforce that may no longer exist.

The factories do not all manufacture objects. Some manufacture conditions.

One plant works day and night to condense fog from passing air, its roof covered in metal ribs and feathery growths. In dry weather it shrieks softly as wind runs through it; in wet weather it roars with collected water, sending pulses through transparent pipes into cistern groves. Another factory presses leaf-mass into structural panels, feeding on cultivated mats that regrow behind it in rectangular fields. There are seed foundries where capsules are sorted by weight and memory. There are engine houses whose pistons are partly wooden, grown to tolerance and then iron-banded. In the higher boroughs, arrays of cable and vine move freight between towers, swaying above empty green avenues with a constant squeal and creak.

The people, if they are present, are not immediately visible. The city has the feeling of being maintained just before or just after a shift. Tools hang from hooks under leaf-eaves. Lamps burn in control rooms. Fresh cuts shine pale on trained branches. A kettle steams in the window of an overgrown signal house. Elevator platforms rise and descend inside vine shafts with no passenger apparent. The loudness suggests labor, but the sparseness withholds the laborers. Perhaps they live mostly inside the plant-architecture, in dry chambers grown within trunks and under root vaults. Perhaps the city itself performs the work now, following civic routines older than its inhabitants.

Rules operate here, though not all of them are physical.

Moisture grants permission. When the air is dry, certain roads are closed by tightening tendrils; when fog arrives, they loosen. Bridges grown from living wood will bear weight only after condensation has darkened their grain. Some doors open to heat, others to song, others only to the pressure of many hands at once. Names matter. A plaza called by its old name may remain flat and passable; called by the wrong one, it may fill with waist-high grass within minutes. Maps are accurate only while folded. Unfolded, they begin to root in the user’s hands, updating themselves according to soil chemistry and municipal memory.

Distance is unreliable but not chaotic. It has habits. Industrial routes are usually shorter when traveled in the direction of production and longer when walked against the flow. Streets leading to water lengthen during drought, as if protecting the reservoirs. Stairways remember the ages of those who climb them and adjust their number of steps accordingly. Old erosion surfaces—those smooth gray places where the plateau shows through—are the most stable zones. Stand on exposed stone and the world holds still. Stand too long on living pavement and the city slowly includes you in its circulation.

The breakdown of reality is not spectacular most of the time. It is administrative, ecological, cumulative. A wall grows around an absence and labels it a room. A chimney smokes before the fire is lit. Rain falls upward for twelve seconds under the fog condensers, then resumes its normal duties. Certain avenues have a second noon in the late afternoon, a local brightening caused by no sun anyone can find. Reflections in water are often of earlier centuries: sharper roofs, fewer vines, more pedestrians, animals that no longer live here. Workers use such pools for planning, because the past shows where foundations remain strong.

The loudest place is the Root Exchange, a vast open concourse near the center of the plateau. It is both station and market, both botanical organ and civic machine. Rail spurs enter through leaf tunnels. Pneumatic tubes thump overhead. Beneath the floor, huge roots converge from across the city, their movements visible as slow pressure waves under translucent paving. Moisture, minerals, heat, waste, seeds, electrical charge, labor orders, and perhaps memories are traded there. Every few minutes the whole exchange emits a deep resonant boom as valves open underground. The sound rolls across the plateau and returns from distant districts altered, softened by moss, multiplied by impossible geometry.

After each boom, plants across the city respond. Windows flower. Street mats lift to drink. Factory vines tighten around rotating shafts. Condensation trembles from cables in glittering showers. For a moment the city seems less like a place inhabited by life than a single immense plant that has learned to wear industry as a skeleton.

Beyond the built districts, the plateau continues in long green barrens. Vegetation still dominates, but it thins into low mats, wind-pruned shrubs, crustose lichens, and isolated groves clustered around old cisterns or collapsed vents. The spaces between are wide and quiet compared with the city, though never silent: the wind carries engines, bells, hammering, the drone of leaf-organs. Here one can see how ancient the land is. The surface lies smooth and patient under its covering, all former violence erased. Drainage lines are shallow. Ridges are low. The world has been worn to a table and then planted with a city that is itself being worn into landscape.

At dusk the temperature drops quickly. Moisture returns from hiding. Leaves fold and unfold according to rules of species and street code. The lamps come on through green membranes. Steam from the factories hangs low, caught in cool air, and the high city becomes a constellation of muffled machines and glowing plant tissue. Sounds grow larger in the dark: pumps, rails, wind, roots shifting under avenues, distant bells marking hours that may not be consecutive.

There is no sense that the place is young, or even middle-aged. It feels after history, after expansion, after catastrophe too old to name. But it is not dead. It is active in the way old things are active: conserving, cycling, repeating, correcting, remembering. It grows slowly over its own ruins. It manufactures weather in small amounts. It listens for rain. It adjusts its streets. It blooms from smokestacks. It lets animals pass rarely, like rumors. It holds its altitude under a normal sky while space and time fray in the leaves.

And everywhere, between the widely spaced towers and factories, between the old stone bones and the living pavements, there is room: cool open air, long green distances, pale light, the plateau wind moving across a city that has become almost entirely plant without ever ceasing to be urban.
