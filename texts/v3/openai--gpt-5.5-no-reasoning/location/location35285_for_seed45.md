At first there is only height and darkness.

The land stands far above any ordinary plain, though there is no clear sense of what it rises from. It may be the shoulder of a planet, or the exposed crown of a buried moon, or a plateau lifted so abruptly that the lower world has fallen away into unlit distance. The horizon is wide but unreliable. It comes and goes behind moving walls of dust, behind curtains of suspended black grit, behind ridges that seem newly thrust upward and not yet accustomed to existing.

There is no water here. Not frozen in shaded hollows, not hidden under stone, not gathered as mist. The absence is total enough to become a material in itself. Every surface has the unsoftened look of things never washed, never rounded, never darkened by rain. Dust lies where it falls and stays there until wind tears it up. Salt or something salt-like crusts the flats in thin pale membranes, but it is not the residue of vanished seas. It seems instead to have sweated out of the rock during cooling, a dry efflorescence from mineral pressure: bitter white seams, yellow scales, dull lavender blooms of crystal powder.

The terrain is young, almost embarrassingly young. Nothing has been worn smooth. Nothing has had time to settle into the persuasive shapes of age. Ridges are sharp as broken ceramic. Slabs lean at impossible angles, lifted in the last convulsion and not yet collapsed. Cones of ash stand with their sides too steep, fresh and friable, their summits venting heat without flame. Long cracks run across the plateau in unfinished lines, opening not with the patience of fault systems but with the suddenness of a thing still deciding its pattern.

Some of the cracks glow faintly. Not with fire exactly, and not enough to illuminate the sky, but with a low internal redness, like metal seen through a slit in a furnace door. Warmth rises everywhere. The air is dry and dense with mineral breath. It smells of hot stone, alkali, old lightning, and something like burnt glass. In places the ground is too warm to touch; in others it gives off a steady animal heat despite there being no animals and no memory of animals.

The darkness is not night in the usual sense. It is broader and more permanent. Above, the sky is packed with soot-colored vapors, a high ceiling of ash and mineral cloud through which little light passes. Sometimes a dim copper smear appears behind it, suggesting a sun too distant, too weak, or too deeply veiled to govern the land with confidence. Most shapes are read by contrast: black ridges against less-black air, pale crusts faintly visible on the ground, red vents pulsing through fissures, occasional sparks inside dust storms where grains strike one another.

The wind is nearly constant, but not always loud. Much of the time it moves as a pressure rather than a voice, pushing across the highland in broad invisible sheets. It combs the dust into long lines between stones. It empties shallow basins and fills them again. It worries at the edges of new formations, but erosion has barely begun its long work. The wind has not yet sculpted; it is only testing.

Then, abruptly, the place becomes violent.

A ridge that had seemed solid begins to shiver along its length. There is a dry ticking sound as mineral scales detach and slide. A section of slope slumps downward in slow silence, then breaks apart into a run of angular blocks that clatter once, twice, and stop. Farther off, a black pillar rises from the plain — dust, ash, powdered crystal, all twisted together — and walks laterally across the plateau. It does not resemble weather formed by moisture. It is a storm of dry collisions, of electrostatic hunger, of particles furious at being separated and drawn back together.

Lightning occurs without rain. It crawls horizontally through the dust, brief violet threads between suspended mineral grains. Sometimes it dives into the ground and leaves behind branching scars of fused glass. These scars are among the few markings that endure. They vein the dark flats with glossy black roots, though there are no roots here and no plants to imitate them.

Vegetation is almost absent. Almost. In the lee of certain stones, where heat rises in steady drafts and dust gathers in sheltered crescents, there are dark stains on the mineral crust. They are not moss. They do not grow upward. They have no leaves, no stems, no color that belongs to life as most worlds know it. They are thin, matte colonies, perhaps fungal, perhaps chemical, perhaps only self-organizing films of carbon and metallic salts. When the ground trembles, they fracture like dried paint. Later, under the warmth leaking from below, they knit back into contiguous patches, not alive enough to be called resilient, not dead enough to be dismissed.

Their presence changes the mood of the emptiness. Without them, the place would be purely geological. With them, it becomes preliminary. The plateau seems not barren after life, but before some difficult invention of it. A rehearsal space. A draft.

Scattered across the heights are structures, though they do not feel built by hands. There are low rings of stone on the flats, too regular to be accidental and too crude to be architecture. There are short walls half-submerged in ash, running for ten or twenty paces before ending without purpose. There are stacks of basalt plates balanced in columns, some upright, some toppled, some fused at their bases as if the ground grew around them while they stood.

No tools remain near them. No roads lead between them. No inscriptions mark them. If someone made them, they came before technology, before the idea of shelter had separated from the idea of ritual, or else they were made by the land itself in a phase when it briefly experimented with order.

In one valley — though valley is too generous a word for a depression between newborn scarps — several small dark huts sit far apart. They are made from slabs of the same stone as the surrounding ridges, tilted inward and chinked with pale mineral crust. Their roofs are low and heavy. Their entrances face away from the prevailing wind. Inside, there would be nothing: dust, warmth, the smell of stone, perhaps a single upright block placed where a hearth should be, though no wood has ever grown here and no fire of fuel has ever burned.

The huts seem less inhabited than anticipated. They look as if the world prepared spaces for occupants that did not arrive.

Beyond them, the plateau opens.

The view extends across miles of broken highland: ash plains, razorback ridges, salt-white pans, conical vents, and dark fields of cooling glass. Because there is little density to the landscape, every object stands with a strange emphasis. A single boulder can dominate an acre. A fissure can divide the world. A cluster of pale crystals glimmering in the dark can seem ceremonial. Distances are large but not empty in a peaceful way; they are empty like a stage before the dangerous part of the performance.

The ground is still making itself.

Here, a blister swells beneath a crust of black glass. It rises slowly over hours, a dome the size of a house, its skin shining where the dust slides off. Hairline cracks appear, then widen. Heat breathes through them. The dome caves inward with a sigh, revealing a hollow lined with red mineral threads. By the next cycle of dimness and darker dimness, the hollow has partly filled with windblown grit and collapsed flakes. It looks ancient until it shifts again.

Elsewhere, towers form out of vapor and falling dust. Warm gas escapes through narrow vents, lifting particles in a column. Static binds the finest grains into fragile lattices. Layer by layer, a chimney accretes: porous, brittle, dark outside, pale inside. It may stand for days. Then a change in wind or pressure makes it sing, a thin flute-note over the plain, before it breaks and falls into its own shadow.

Sound is rare enough that each sound feels intentional. The usual state is a thick quiet, not silence but restraint. There is the hiss of dust moving low over crust. The distant granular roar of a storm crossing another basin. The occasional pop of stone cooling too quickly. A faint subsonic groan from beneath the plateau, felt in the ribs before heard by the ear. When rockfalls occur, their noise travels far, striking the empty ridges and returning as dry echoes.

There are no birds. No insects. No burrowing things underfoot. No tracks except those made by sliding stone, wandering dust, and the occasional rolling mineral nodule pushed by wind. These nodules gather in shallow trenches and knock softly against one another, hollow clicks like teeth in a bowl. In darkness, some of them hold a weak ember-glow, not bioluminescent but triboluminescent, bruised into light by pressure and impact.

The ecology, if it can be called that, belongs to heat, dust, crystal, charge, and collapse. Energy moves through the place without bodies to carry it. Fissures feed warmth into crusts. Crusts crack and expose reactive mineral surfaces. Vapors condense into salts, then sublime again. Static lifts particles into suspended veils. Dry films spread where chemistry permits, vanish where the ground grows too hot, reappear in the lee of new stones. Nothing eats, but many things transform. Nothing hunts, but instability pursues everything.

The rules of the world are raw rules.

Edges persist until they suddenly fail.  
Structures rise by accretion, not design.  
Nothing decays; it fractures, fuses, or is buried.  
Absence of water means absence of mercy: no smoothing, no softening, no rot, no mud, no bloom.  
Warmth comes from below more faithfully than light comes from above.  
Time is not measured by seasons, but by collapses.

The turbulence is not constant motion everywhere. Rather, the landscape is full of waiting instabilities. A plain may lie still for a long while, every stone fixed, every dust-line motionless. Then some hidden threshold is crossed. A vent opens. A salt pan buckles. A static storm ignites. A wall splits and reveals a younger wall behind it. The whole highland seems governed by delayed decisions.

In the farthest visible region, dark mountains are being born. Their silhouettes change between one dim interval and the next. Not by the slow erosion of peaks, but by uplift, extrusion, and fracture. One summit grows a jagged horn; another loses its flank in a muted avalanche that sends dust outward like breath from a sleeping giant. The mountains are extreme but unfinished, tall without dignity, immense without age. They are not yet part of the background. They are events.

Occasionally, the cloud ceiling opens just enough to admit a narrow column of weak amber light. Wherever it touches, the minerals answer. Pale salts flare ghost-white. Glass fields shine like oil. The dry black films in the wind-shadows become visible as irregular constellations on the ground. For a few minutes the landscape appears almost mapped, almost legible. Then the dust closes over and the world returns to heated obscurity.

There is beauty here, but it is not welcoming beauty. It is the beauty of beginnings before any creature has been invited to survive them. The place has not learned gentleness. It has not learned repetition. It is a high, warm, dark, waterless province of first attempts: first ridge, first basin, first shelter, first chemistry, first ruin.

And all through it are the scattered marks of almost-habitation — the stone rings, the slab huts, the balanced columns — quiet witnesses without witness-makers. They do not civilize the plateau. They make it stranger. They suggest that form itself is trying to occur here, that the world is practicing the idea of dwelling before it has produced anything that can dwell.

By the time one has crossed a mile of it, the path behind is partly gone. Not erased by rain, not overgrown, not consumed by life, but rearranged by heat and dust and the small continuous failures of newborn stone. A crack has widened. A crust has curled. A dust-line has migrated. One of the huts has lost a roof slab without sound. A chimney has risen where there was only flatness.

The land is not empty because nothing happens.

It is empty because what happens does not need anyone.
