You are below the surface of the world, though not in darkness.

The place is an ocean, or something occupying the role of an ocean: continuous water in every direction, cool and heavy, illuminated by a broad, level, workable light. It does not feel magical. It feels engineered beyond comprehension.

There is no sky, only a ceiling so far above that it behaves like one. It is a manufactured firmament of glass, ceramic composite, ribbed metal, and translucent panels, stretching across distances too large to believe at first. The panels glow with a pale white daylight, not warm, not sunlike exactly, but steady enough to let the eye range for kilometers through the water. In some places the ceiling is lost in suspended mist and planktonic haze. In others it appears with startling clarity: long seams, maintenance gantries, spillways, drainage lips, cold vapor dripping from pressure joints. Rain falls underwater there, if that is the right word for it — descending curtains of colder, denser liquid leaking from overhead reservoirs and collapsing through the upper strata in silvery veils.

The seafloor is also made.

Far below, visible only when the water clears, lie plains of fitted plates and newly poured basalt-colored polymer, gridded and beveled, their edges still sharp. There are no ancient sediments. No soft accumulation of ages. No forgotten geology. The terrain is young because it has not yet had time to become anything else. Ridges are crisp. Anchor blocks stand square. Trenches have straight sides, cut to receive conduits and lock-rails. The slopes descend in terraces, not by erosion but by plan: one shelf to another, one pressure district to the next, a continental machine stepped down into blue depth.

It is vast in a way that defeats landmarks. A tower that seemed nearby is twenty kilometers away. A wall becomes a horizon. A pipe the thickness of a town runs out of visibility without bending. Entire weather systems move through the water: slow rivers within the ocean, cold fronts released from refrigeration plants, warm exhaust plumes rising from industrial hearts, milky fogbanks of microbubbles travelling like clouds along the underside of the ceiling. There are currents with names, schedules, accidents. There are seasons of flow caused by pumping cycles and maintenance tides. When the main sluices open, regions of the sea tilt without showing it, and everything alive turns its body to face the change.

For all its size, the place is strangely empty.

There is room here for nations of fish, for forests, for wrecks, for mountains, but much of the water holds almost nothing. Blue distance, faint particulate glint, a structural line now and then. The emptiness is not peaceful. It is functional, like the interior volume of a tank, a cooling reservoir, a pressure buffer built too large for any one purpose. Presences appear isolated and monumental: a single service column rising from floor to ceiling; a school of silver animals flashing through a kilometer-wide gap; a suspended cluster of maintenance buoys blinking amber; a vertical factory stack venting strings of bubbles into open water.

Sound travels everywhere.

The whole constructed ocean hums. It is not one sound but strata of sound layered through the liquid: the bass throb of pumps, the long iron groan of thermal expansion, turbine chatter, chain rattle, distant impacts, valve-claps like submerged thunder. Some sounds arrive minutes after their source has vanished. Some seem to have no direction, only pressure. The water carries them through bone and suit and hull. Near the great intake mouths, the noise becomes physical — a steady shove against the chest, a vibration in teeth, a roar without air.

Every surface is hard.

The walls, when you reach them, are not cliff or reef but armor: ceramic plating, carbon-black rubber seals, bolted frames, old white enamel already scratched by larvae and tools. Freshness is everywhere. Weld lines are bright. Warning paint is still visible under thin films of algae. Exposed concrete has not yet softened. Even where life has begun to take hold, it does so tentatively, in narrow bands and stains: green-brown fuzz along handrails, translucent mats on the downstream sides of pylons, pale weeds trembling from cracks where mineral-rich seepage escapes. Vegetation is sparse, because there is little soil, little purchase, and no true sun. What grows here is opportunistic and low: cable-moss, filament algae, mats of engineered seagrass in trial plots, occasional ribboning plants anchored in slotted trays along the terraces.

The animals have adapted faster than the plants.

Fish are everywhere in motion, though not evenly distributed. In the open blue, long-bodied schooling fish travel like quick thoughts, appearing suddenly as metallic sheets and vanishing just as fast. Around warm outlets cluster clouds of small translucent feeders, each one almost invisible until it turns and catches the light. Larger hunters patrol the edges of structures: blunt-headed, patient, industrially colored in gray, white, and dull yellow, their skins scarred by mesh and metal. Eels have claimed the drainage cavities. Crabs move along the maintenance ladders as if they were reefs. Shrimp swarm in the halos of inspection lamps. Soft animals pulse near the cooling vents, thriving in chemical gradients too subtle for human senses.

Nothing about the life here feels alien. That may be the strangest thing about it. The world is artificial, but the appetites inside it are ordinary. Things feed, hide, breed, flee, foul surfaces, exploit shelter, follow heat, avoid noise, learn the rhythms of machinery. A predator waits beside a floodgate because every sixth hour the current pins smaller fish against the grille. A nesting colony occupies the lee side of a sensor mast because the vibration keeps certain parasites away. Larvae drift in vast empty volumes until they strike a cable, a seam, a roughened bracket, and then the first soft beginning of ecology appears.

The water is cool, sometimes cold. Not polar, not lethal at once, but draining. It has the chill of depth and steel. Touching a surface with an ungloved hand would feel like touching a railway line in winter. Warmth exists locally and with obvious cause: a vent, a motor housing, a pipe carrying waste heat from some lower district. Around these warm places the water shimmers faintly. Fish crowd them. Algae brighten. Mechanical smell, if you could smell underwater, would be strongest there: oil, ions, hot metal, disinfectant, wet concrete.

Because there is normal light, there are shadows.

The great structures cast them. A bridge of trusswork spans two towers and throws a lattice downward through blue water. Hanging service tracks darken the plains below. The underside of a floating refinery becomes an artificial night, full of echo and slow animals. When maintenance lamps sweep on, the shadows move, and the fish move with them. Some predators use the beams. Some prey have learned to freeze at the first white scan.

There are no beaches. No surface in the human sense. To go upward is to approach infrastructure: the glowing roof, the rain manifolds, the airless ducts, the anti-condensation vanes, the undersides of platforms where machines crawl along rails. To go downward is to approach more infrastructure: pressure roads, transit tunnels, production beds, pump housings the size of cathedrals. The ocean is held between made things. Even its depth is an architectural decision.

Yet it is not clean or controlled in the way its builders must have imagined.

The place is active. Constantly active. Currents shift. Gates open. Filters clog and are backflushed in explosive blooms. Sediment — not natural sediment, but fine particulate from machining, corrosion, shell fragments, dead plankton, shed polymer dust — gathers in drifts against curbs and bulkheads, then lifts again when the flow changes. Maintenance drones travel in slow convoys along painted guide lines, brushing panels, cutting fouled nets, replacing sensors. Their propellers whine in high frequencies, and animals follow them for the edible debris they disturb. In the far distance, harvest machines crawl across the seafloor terraces like patient insects, tending sparse beds of kelp analogue and filtration mats.

Storms occur here, though there is no wind.

A storm begins as a schedule error, or perhaps as part of the schedule too large to understand locally. The ceiling darkens section by section as light panels dim for thermal management. Cold water descends from overhead reservoirs in white vertical bands. Pumps reverse in several districts. A pressure wave comes first, felt as a tightening in the ears, then the sound: a rising concussive roar passing through hundreds of kilometers of chambered sea. Particles lift from every ledge. The open water turns opaque. Fish school hard against the current, then give up and stream with it. Crabs lock themselves into grates. Eels vanish into pipe mouths. The sparse vegetation lies flat and shines.

Then, hours later, clarity returns. The light steadies. The new currents have rearranged the world in small ways. A mat of algae has been torn free and now drifts like a flag. A field of larvae has settled along a fresh weld seam. A dead fish spins slowly against an intake screen while scavengers gather. The sharp young structures remain sharp, but the first signs of wear have advanced.

The rules of this world are simple and strict.

Do not trust still water; it means a gate is about to open somewhere else. Do not shelter in a pipe unless you know what breathes through it. Follow warmth if you are small, avoid it if you are hunted. Sound travels farther than sight. Light means work, but darkness means larger work. All open space belongs to current. All edges belong to life.

From a distance the built landscape can appear serene: pale water, enormous forms, sparse green, the clean geometry of a civilization that has sunk its architecture beneath a managed sea. But inside it there is no serenity. There is only circulation. Exchange. Intake and output. Feeding and flushing. Construction becoming habitat before the bolts have dulled. A new ocean learning, very quickly, how to be old.
