At this height the city seems less built than deposited.

It lies across a plateau of old mineral, a tableland lifted far above any living basin and then forgotten by weather. There is almost no erosion in the familiar sense now, because there is nothing left vigorous enough to erode it. No rain has fallen here in epochs. No rivers ever remember passing through. The air, if it can be called air, is warm, thin, and dry enough to rasp metal clean. Dust does not drift often. When disturbed, it rises in slow brown veils and hangs, undecided, before settling back exactly where gravity instructs it.

The ground is not sand, though from a distance it has the pallor of a salt desert. It is a compacted crust of pale evaporites, vitrified ash, powdered ceramic, and older substances whose names have been lost or were never human. Underfoot it rings faintly. In places the crust has crazed into polygonal plates, each one several meters wide, edges darkened by age. Between them are seams of black grit, iron filings, carbon dust, and granular slag. Nothing grows in those seams. Nothing has ever rooted there. The surface is sterile without feeling clean.

Beyond the plateau’s rim there is only depth and darkness. The city occupies the high ground like a diagram drawn on the last solid page of the world. Its avenues run straight for kilometers, but many are not streets in the ordinary sense. They are service trenches, rail beds, conveyor corridors, empty cable-cuts, heat channels. They cross and recross in a severe geometry, vanishing into industrial districts where low structures crouch apart from one another with ceremonial spacing. There is room everywhere. Blocks are separated by expanses of unused mineral flatness. Towers stand alone. Gantries lead nowhere for hundreds of meters before meeting a processing hall, an intake stack, a sealed depot, another field of silence.

The darkness is almost complete.

There may be stars above, but they are hidden behind a ceiling of particulate haze or by some ancient artificial occlusion still suspended in the upper atmosphere. The sky has no blue in it, no twilight gradient, no moonlit tenderness. It is a blind, warm vault. The city’s own lights are weak and reluctant: red pinpricks on mastheads, amber strips along maintenance bridges, the low violet glow of buried transformers breathing through grates. Vast forms reveal themselves only edge by edge. A furnace tower becomes visible when its heat shimmer bends the blackness around it. A row of cylindrical tanks appears as a deeper darkness cutting into dark. Rail lines show as paired threads of dull reflection, then vanish.

The place is urban, but not inhabited in a lively way. It has the scale of a city and the discipline of a factory. Everything was arranged for handling mass: ore, salts, gases, heat, powders, pressure, charge. Broad loading aprons surround squat refineries with walls of cast stone and oxidized alloy. There are stacks so old their tops have been worn blunt not by wind, but by centuries of thermal stress. There are hopper towers with ribbed sides like fossilized organs. There are crane skeletons frozen over quays that never bordered water, quays built instead above dry basins where loose material once pooled and was drawn down through grates into systems below.

Some buildings are sealed absolutely. Their doors have no handles, only circular plates and lines of bolts. Others gape open into interiors of layered catwalks and motionless machinery. Belts hang slack but intact. Gear wheels taller than houses rest with their teeth sunk into shadows. Pipes run overhead in bundles, crossing from district to district like root systems of a plantless ecology. Many are labeled with symbols that have outlasted speech: triangles, divided circles, black chevrons, white glyphs on ceramic plaques. The signs are not warnings anymore. They have become ornament by persistence.

The city is ancient, but not ruined in a dramatic way. It has settled into a final condition. Walls have not collapsed because there is little weather to bring them down. Roads have not been overrun because nothing grows. Machines have not rusted deeply because there is no moisture to feed corrosion. Instead everything has aged by fatigue, by heat, by microscopic abrasion, by the long slow migration of atoms. Corners are softened. Glass is devitrified into cloudy stone. Steel has taken on a dark, matte skin. Rubber has become brittle mineral. Paint survives as ghost color under dust: ocher, white, furnace red, a chemical blue seen only when a lamp passes near.

The warmth is constant. Not hot enough to burn, not cool enough to comfort. It rises from below, from buried reactors or from the plateau itself, which may be the roof of an older engine. At night—if night is meaningful here—the warmth remains. At day—if day occurs—the darkness remains. No cycle announces itself. No season enters. Thermometers bolted to walls hold steady. Pressure gauges tremble around fixed values. Reservoir indicators point to levels that have not changed in centuries.

The world here has rules, and the first is equilibrium.

Nothing spills unless pushed. Nothing decays quickly. Nothing blooms, ferments, floods, or freezes. Chemical reactions are rationed and slow. Heat travels through conduits and stone with the patience of geology. A dropped bolt may lie where it falls until the city itself is ground flat under some remote astronomical event. A footprint pressed into dust will remain sharp until another foot, animal or mechanical, overwrites it.

The second rule is dryness.

Water is not merely absent; it is excluded. Door seals are designed against vapor. Pipes are jacketed to prevent condensation that will never come. Old warning panels show a droplet crossed by a bar, repeated on gates and storage drums, as if water were poison, contaminant, explosive, sacrament. In laboratories near the central works, there are desiccation chambers within desiccation chambers, glass vessels filled with powders that must never clump, pale crystals stored under inert gas, powders that crawl from one side of a tray to another when charged by static. If any liquid exists here, it is not water. It is dark, viscous, metallic, sealed behind quartz sight windows, circulating so slowly it seems solid.

The third rule is that the city continues, but only barely.

Now and then, deep underground, an engine turns once.

It is not a roar. It is a suggestion of motion transmitted through kilometers of structure: a low adjustment, a pressure equalizing, a shaft aligning with another shaft. The sound arrives through the soles before the ears. Somewhere a relay clicks. Somewhere a compressor exhales one measured breath into a pipe. Then quiet returns, not as an absence but as a substance laid over everything.

Most of the time the soundscape is sparse. The high plateau holds silence well. A loose panel taps perhaps once every few hours, though there is no wind strong enough to explain it. Ceramic insulators hum faintly along the elevated lines. Grains of dust tick down the sides of slag heaps after some imperceptible shift in temperature. In the factory courts, old chains hang without swaying. If one listens long enough, one hears the electrical field: a pressure in the teeth, a dry sizzle too fine to locate.

There are rare fauna, or things filling that role.

They are not common enough to make the place feel alive. Occasionally, under the red service lamps, a flat many-legged creature crosses an avenue with the delicacy of a piece of torn film. Its body is the color of graphite and mica; it leaves no trail. It may feed on electrostatic buildup, on flakes of insulation, on colonies of mineral bacteria in the warm seams below the city. In the pipe galleries are pale jointed animals like folded wire, blind and slow, able to wait years between meals. Their shells are ceramic. Their mouths are polishing surfaces. They graze on oxide blooms and the faint organic residues left by machines that once required lubricants derived from life.

Higher up, around the antenna fields, something winged sometimes hangs in the dark without flapping. It is difficult to tell whether it is animal, kite, or maintenance device. It moves only when one stops watching directly. Its underside catches infrared as a dull membrane stretched between black struts. The city tolerates it. The towers do not signal alarm.

There is no vegetation, yet there are arrangements that resemble ecology because everything persists in relation. Dust collects on the leeward sides of structures. Static fields sort the dust into bands. Certain salts migrate toward warm foundations and form pale beards along vents. Magnetized filings gather in crescents around transformer housings. In abandoned assembly halls, fungal-looking mineral extrusions grow from cracked batteries, though they are not alive in any botanical sense. They accrete atom by atom, blue-black and branching, mapping old currents through the floor.

Some streets descend into districts more strange than industrial. There the plan of the city changes from rectangular to radial. Buildings are lower, heavier, older. Their walls are made from a black glassy stone that reflects almost nothing. Channels are carved into the ground in nested rings, all dry, all leading toward a central pit covered by a lattice of immense bars. The bars are warm. Below them is darkness with depth to it, and a smell like hot mineral, old ozone, and sealed time.

No flame burns here, yet the pit gives a sense of combustion restrained indefinitely. Around it stand pylons engraved with calibrations rather than prayers. Perhaps the city was built to extract something rising from below: heat, gas, charge, a non-aqueous vapor, a memory of volcanic pressure. Perhaps the plateau is not natural but the hardened scab of an ancient industrial wound. Perhaps the city was founded because this place was already dry beyond contamination, high above weather and ocean, perfect for processes that could not tolerate life.

In the outer quarters, residential forms appear, though they are austere. Stacked cells, narrow courtyards, stair towers, transit platforms. Windows are small or absent. Rooms are arranged around shafts carrying warm air from below. The inhabitants, if they were human or close to human, lived as components of a sealed metabolism. Their public squares are broad mineral plazas with no fountains, no trees, no statues softened by pigeons or moss. Instead there are calibration stones, schedule walls, arrays of metal discs that may once have indicated shifts, pressure states, civic obligations, atmospheric purity.

One plaza contains thousands of upright rods set into the ground, each a different height, each tipped with a dull bead. They might be memorial markers. They might be sensors. In darkness, when a charged cloud passes overhead unseen, faint blue sparks crawl from bead to bead, and for a moment the whole square becomes a map of silent lightning. Then the charge dissipates and the rods return to being black lines in black air.

The age of the place is difficult to measure because it has not passed through recognizable stages of decay. It has only become more itself. The city was dry; it is drier. It was ordered; its order has hardened. It was sparse; emptiness has claimed the spaces between functions and made them formal. Its machinery was built to endure; endurance has become its principal activity.

From a high maintenance bridge, one can see the districts spread out across the plateau: refinery blocks, storage fields, rail corridors, sealed domes, antenna farms, slag terraces, worker cells, the old central pit, the outlying mineral flats where half-buried pipes enter the ground and do not return. Everything is separated by shadow. Nothing hurries between one part and another. The city’s scale becomes apparent not through crowds or movement, but through repetition: stack after stack, tank after tank, avenue after avenue, each receding until the darkness edits them away.

There is a sense that if dawn came, it would be an intrusion. Light would simplify the place too much, reduce it to architecture and ruin. In darkness it remains operational in imagination. A row of unlit windows may be watching. A cable may still carry a command. A factory may resume after ten thousand years because some threshold has finally been crossed by one tenth of a degree.

But thresholds are rare here.

The dynamic of the place is almost null. This is not a world of events. It is a world after events, or before the next permissible one. It has achieved a kind of mineral bureaucracy, where every force has been filed, damped, insulated, routed, and brought into compliance. Even strangeness has become stable. The impossible creatures keep to their circuits. The buried engines make their scheduled fractional turns. The warm plateau radiates into the dark at a constant rate. The dust settles. The sealed chambers remain sealed.

And yet the city is not dead in the simple sense. Death implies an organism, a decline, an end that happened. This place feels instead like a system designed to outlast the need for its designers. It is a dry, high, warm, black metropolis of industry without production, infrastructure without traffic, habitation without daily life. It holds itself at the edge of function forever.

If one speaks, the voice does not carry far. The air takes it quickly, flattening it against walls and mineral flats. Footsteps sound too intimate. Breath seems wet and temporary, almost indecent. The city listens with its grates, its empty conduits, its ceramic ears, but it does not answer except in heat, faint current, and the occasional distant click of a mechanism deciding, after centuries of calculation, to remain exactly as it is.
