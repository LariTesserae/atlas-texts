It is a low city in a warm, dim basin, built where the ground has only just become ground.

The land around it is not old enough to have learned erosion. Nothing has yet been worn smooth by ages of weather. There are no rounded hills, no softened gullies, no patient river-cut banks. Instead the terrain looks assembled recently from wet mineral matter: shelves of pale clay, black ribs of stone, raw terraces of packed silt, mounds with sheer sides where the material was lifted and left. Edges remain crisp despite the damp. Rainwater clings to every face but has not yet made channels. Puddles sit in hollows like placed objects. The whole basin has the unfinished quality of a kiln-yard after a flood: earth and construction indistinguishable, both warm, both sweating.

Water is everywhere, but never in a grand river or lake. It seeps. It beads. It runs silently behind walls and loudly through gutters. It rises through the floors of low rooms in dark rings and gathers under raised sleeping platforms. Stone steps are greenless but slick. Courtyards are paved with fired tiles whose seams glimmer with shallow water. The air is so humid that surfaces condense even when it has not rained; every lintel weeps, every roof-edge drips. At dawn — if the dimming of the sky can be called dawn — the whole town exhales a mineral mist from its warm foundations.

There is almost no vegetation. No grass softens the lanes. No moss takes the stone. No reeds stand at the waterline. The wet places are bare and exposed, their mud shining without root or blade. Where a world like this might be expected to swarm with green, there is instead a dense vocabulary of mineral surfaces: ocher clay, blue-gray marl, shell-white lime, black slag, pink fired brick, brown rope, bone, wet hide, unpolished wood brought from somewhere far away or long ago and reused until it has become more patch than plank. The absence of plants gives the city a nakedness. Nothing screens anything. Every wall, animal, tool, and human figure is seen plainly in the damp half-light.

The settlement spreads broadly across the basin floor, neither a single village nor a great empire of stone, but an open, low urban field. It extends farther than one can take in from street level: roof after roof, kiln after kiln, cistern after cistern, animal yard after animal yard, all pressed close yet rarely rising more than two or three stories. The city is dense in the way a tidepool is dense, though without algae: many bodies, many chambers, many functions occupying the same warm volume. Alleys pass beneath drying racks and over drainage trenches. Homes share walls with workshops; workshops share hearths with cookhouses; cookhouses back onto pens where animals nose at troughs of mash and mineral salt. There is little empty space. Even the open squares are crowded with posts, jars, tether-rings, troughs, basins, racks, piles of unfired brick, stacks of shells, heaps of broken tile sorted for reuse.

Everything seems made by hand and by habit. The city has infrastructure, but it is primitive, not crude so much as intimate. Drains are stone-lined channels cleaned with hooked poles. Water is lifted with leather buckets, counterweighted beams, shoulder-yokes, and teams of patient animals turning creaking wheels. Roofs are sealed with clay and bitumen. Walls are thick, sun-baked or kiln-fired, patched so often that each façade is a record of repairs. The streets are paved where traffic is heaviest and simply tamped elsewhere, but the tamped lanes remain firm, as though the basin has agreed to hold their shape.

There is no sense of recent decay or growth. That is the strange calm beneath the noise: the place feels fixed. Not abandoned, not lifeless, but held in a permanent state of operation. Bricks dry on racks, yet never seem to progress toward being stacked. Kilns glow, but their chimneys have the same stains they have always had. Droplets hang from roof-edges and fall at intervals so regular they might as well be part of the masonry. The water level in every trench is stable. The animals shift and call, but the herds are always the same size. New construction and old construction are difficult to tell apart, because the city itself appears to be perpetually under construction without ever becoming more complete.

The dim light gives everything a bronze-gray cast. The sky is low and brightless, a warm overcast ceiling of vapor. Shadows are soft but persistent. Firelight matters here. Hearths and kiln-mouths glow in doorways; oil lamps hang under arcades; coals redden the undersides of smoke-blackened pots. Reflections tremble in wet paving and in the eyes of animals. In the deeper lanes the light is less like daylight than the illumination inside a shell — diffuse, close, faintly pearled.

The sound is immense.

It is not a single roar but an urban, animal, watery thunder, continuous enough to become weather. Water slaps through spillways and gutters. Bucket chains clatter. Mill wheels groan. Troughs overflow. Clay is beaten under mallets with wet, flat impacts. Potters’ wheels hum and squeal. Kiln doors scrape. Stone pestles pound grain or mineral feed in deep mortars. Hooves strike tile and wood. Dogs bark from roofs. Pigs scream in pens. Goats bleat from crowded platforms. Fowl shriek under baskets. Larger beasts — cattle, donkeys, perhaps squat water-loving draft animals with wide hooves and patient eyes — bellow from the turning yards. Human voices must be raised at all times; conversation is a form of calling across noise.

In some districts the din becomes nearly physical. The drainage quarter is worst, where every lane slopes toward a series of stepped settling basins. Water pours there from the entire city, but because the land barely falls, it must be coaxed and lifted and redirected by hand. Men and women stand knee-deep on stone ledges, levering gates, clearing silt screens, shouting timing to the wheel crews. Animals turn capstans in circles worn not by erosion but by repetition: perfect rings stamped into packed clay, maintained daily, never deepening. The splash and creak and chant are so loud that newcomers feel their ribs vibrate.

Yet outside the city, a short walk beyond the last pens, the basin does not become countryside. There are no fields. No orchards. No reedbeds. Instead, low raw flats spread outward, wet and bare under the dim sky. Raised causeways connect clay pits, lime mounds, shell heaps, brine pans, and shallow basins where mineral-rich water stands opaque and warm. The city feeds not on agriculture in the visible sense but on animals, stored meal, fish or shell creatures from unseen waters, traded goods, and the labor of transforming mud and mineral into shelter, vessel, salt, plaster, pigment, and tile. Perhaps plant matter arrives bundled and dried from distant uplands, precious and quickly consumed. Perhaps the people cultivate something not plantlike in vats — pale yeast-cakes, rindless mats, fermenting pastes kept in covered jars — but if so it remains indoors, part of the craft life rather than the landscape.

Fauna is abundant, almost too abundant for a place without vegetation. Animals occupy every tier of the city. Birds nest in roof vents and under eaves, not among leaves but in baskets, niches, and rafters. Their droppings mark the walls below favorite perches. Rats, lizards, beetles, and pale water-insects thrive in the warm wet seams of construction. Crabs or crablike things live in the drainage trenches, clicking under grates. Eels or mudfish coil in cistern shadows. Dogs patrol the refuse lanes. Cats haunt the kiln warmth. Domestic herds crowd the yards, fed from troughs, driven along set routes, washed at public basins, slaughtered in tiled rooms where the blood is immediately sluiced away.

The animals have made the city their ecology. Since there are no thickets, no meadows, no forest margins, habitat is architectural: holes in brick, shade beneath platforms, warmth beside ovens, spilled grain at mill doors, black water under bridges, rope tangles, roof gutters, dung heaps, wall cracks, leather awnings, abandoned jars. Life does not green the world; it inhabits its joinery.

The materials of the city are simple but numerous. Clay is everywhere, in all states: raw, kneaded, stamped, dried, fired, cracked, powdered, rehydrated, painted, sealed. Stone appears as threshold slabs, grinding beds, weights, anchors, curb blocks, and sacred markers. Bone and horn become handles, needles, toggles, pipe flutes, hinges. Leather is used for buckets, straps, door flaps, bellows, roof seams. Rope sags between posts in dark wet loops. Reed would be expected, but there is none; where light structures are needed, people use split wood, woven hide, corded hair, or thin fired latticework. The lack of plant fiber has shaped every craft. Baskets are rare and repaired obsessively. Mats are made of leather strips or animal sinew. Fuel is dung, oil, imported charcoal, dried cakes of unknown ferment, and the constant low-burning mineral pitch dug from black seams beyond the city.

The rules of the world are ordinary, almost stubbornly so. Water runs downhill, though the hills are hardly there. Clay dries when heated and softens when soaked. Animals hunger, breed, complain, sicken, recover. Walls crack if neglected. Knots fail when wet. Smoke rises until the damp air flattens it. Nothing floats upward, nothing sings without lungs, no impossible colors gleam from the mud. And yet the normality makes the place more peculiar, because all of it persists in a balance too steady to feel historical.

The city seems to exist in an equilibrium of maintenance. If a wall weeps, someone wipes and replasters it. If a gutter clogs, someone clears it before overflow changes the lane. If a roof sags, props appear. If a pen grows foul, sluices open. There is no visible expansion into the basin, no abandonment of old quarters, no invasion of green, no deepening of channels, no slow collapse. Labor prevents time from taking hold. Or perhaps time has taken hold in the form of labor itself: the same gestures repeated so continuously that change is canceled.

There are civic places, though none are grand in the monumental sense. A low hall with many pillars, its floor always damp. A counting court where clay tokens dry in shallow trays. A market of awnings and stone counters, loud with bargaining and animal cries, smelling of salt, hides, warm milk, fish, plaster dust, oil, dung smoke, and wet clay. A public kiln complex whose fireholes glow like a row of red eyes in the murk. A water court at the city’s center, not decorative but sacred by use: a square basin, waist-deep, fed by slow seepage from below and drained at exactly the same rate through a hidden channel. Its level never varies. People come there to wash tools, swear agreements, cool fevered animals, mix ceremonial clay, and listen to the thunder of the city soften briefly into lapping.

Because the elevation is low, the horizon is never dramatic. From the highest roof one sees the city spread outward in shallow layers: rooflines, smoke, pale vapor, animal backs moving in lanes, the glimmer of countless puddles, the black mouths of kilns, the straight lines of causeways crossing the raw flats. Farther off, low mineral mounds rise like unfinished thoughts. The sky presses down without storm. Rain comes often, but not as an event; it thickens the air, darkens the walls, adds another voice to the already deafening world. When it stops, nothing seems to have changed except that every surface has become more itself.

At night the city does not quiet. It lowers by a degree, perhaps, but the water continues, and the animals continue, and the night crews work the kilns because warm damp air makes the firing delicate. Lamps tremble behind hide screens. Reflections slide along the alleys. Hooves shift in dark pens. Somewhere a wheel turns, turns, turns, lifting water that will fall into a channel and be lifted again elsewhere. The sound fills the basin so completely that silence becomes unimaginable.

To walk through the place is to feel surrounded by beginnings that never proceed and endings that never arrive. The geology is young, the city ancient in habit, the crafts primitive yet systematized, the ecology rich yet almost leafless, the climate wet and warm without lushness. Everything is close, dense, handmade, damp. Everything touches everything else through water, sound, labor, and animal breath.

It is a city at the marsh-edge of a world without marsh plants, a settlement built on raw clay before rivers have learned to carve it, held in place by maintenance and noise. A warm mineral hive under a dim sky, crowded with beasts, ringing with tools, slick with condensation, forever unfinished and forever exactly as it is.
