At that height the world should be bare, and mostly is. The plateau lies like an old bone lifted into cold air: not jagged, not dramatic in the young way of mountains, but worn down to a long, patient flatness. Its edges fall away in pale steps, and beyond them the lower weather moves in distant basins, cloud-shadowed and inaccessible. Here above, everything has been abraded for so long that even ruin feels too recent a word. The stone has passed beyond brokenness into composure.

The place is small enough to cross in an hour, if one knows the paths, though it feels larger because of the emptiness between things. It is a town, or the shape left after a town and a reef and a forest agreed to become one another. There are streets, but they are not paved so much as grown bare by centuries of wind. There are walls, but no mortar, no tool marks, no deliberate corners one can trust. The walls rise from the plateau in low, rounded courses of dark schist and white mineral crust, as though the rock has remembered an urban plan rather than been arranged into one. Doorways occur. Courtyards occur. Narrow lanes pass between clustered structures and end at terraces facing open sky.

Nothing here is young.

Every surface is softened: lintels slumped into arches, steps hollowed in the middle, rooflines reduced to humps. The whole settlement has the look of architecture after geology has accepted it. Some houses are no higher than the waist; others stand like small chapels or granaries, roofless, open to the cold. Their interiors are floored with wind-packed grit and patches of low green life. In sheltered corners, water collects in black bowls worn into the stone. The bowls are shallow and perfectly smooth. Some are no wider than a hand, some broad enough to reflect a whole doorway and a strip of sky.

The cold is constant but not clean. It carries dampness in threads. Mist condenses on stone before dawn and again after sunset, silvering the ruins. Rain comes irregularly in brief, hard visits: slanting needles that strike the plateau, run along ancient grooves, vanish into cracks, and leave everything darker for a while. There is no river in the ordinary sense, but the town is riverine in its habits. Water knows routes here. It appears, disappears, reappears. It slides under walls. It gathers in gutters that may be natural channels or streets eroded into use. During rain the lanes fill with motion, dozens of little currents speaking at once; afterward they empty, leaving only beads on lichen and a glassy skin inside the stone basins.

Vegetation is lush only where the place permits lushness. It does not cover the plateau evenly. It erupts in pockets, dense and low, like secret gardens pressed into crevices. Moss grows thick as fleece along the shaded sides of walls, saturated green and almost black at the root. Lichens spread in maps of sulfur yellow, ash blue, iron orange. Tiny cold ferns crowd the mouths of drains. Cushion plants swell between stones in tight domes, resilient and bright, their leaves small as scales. In certain courtyards the growth is astonishing: waist-high stands of silver grass, mats of red-stemmed sedge, dwarf willows no taller than a sleeping animal, their branches braided by wind.

But step a few paces away and there is almost nothing. Open stone. Pale gravel. A single tuft ticking in the air. The density of the place is strange: abundance gathered into knots, then long emptiness. One can see across a square and count every living thing.

The settlement’s plan is simple at first glance and impossible at second. A central hollow lies near the middle, something between a plaza and an amphitheater, though it is only a shallow depression worn into the plateau. Around it stand the oldest structures, their walls low and thick, their thresholds facing inward. From this hollow, five lanes radiate outward toward the plateau rim. None run straight. Each bends around boulders so ancient they seem to have grown tired of being boulders and entered civic life: cornerstones, benches, shrines, roadblocks.

At the hollow’s center is a pool that should not remain. The air is too thin and the winds too persistent, yet water sits there most of the year, dark and cold, fed by condensation, seepage, and perhaps something deeper. Its surface is usually trembling. Not from visible life, but from a low vibration that travels through the rock.

The landscape is loud.

The loudness is not the clamor of crowds, for there are no crowds, and perhaps never were. It is the sound of weather and stone and small waters amplified by the architecture. Wind enters the lanes and changes pitch from one passage to another. It booms in roofless chambers. It whistles under fallen lintels. It hums across cracked thresholds like breath through bone flutes. When rain comes, the whole place begins to play: drips from moss-heavy walls, tapping from ledges, gutter-rush in hidden channels, hollow knocks from water striking submerged stone. The central pool receives each drop with a deep, resonant plunk, far larger than the impact should be.

Even in dry weather there is noise. Frost works in the cracks with faint reports. Gravel skitters across exposed squares. Somewhere under the town, water moves intermittently through old cavities, groaning and gulping, then falling silent for hours. The plateau edge answers the wind with a continuous low roar, as if a sea lay just below sight, though there is no sea—only valleys filled with cloud and distance.

Fauna is rare and wary. Now and then a white-backed corvid crosses the sky, small against the ordinary daylight, its wings flexing hard in the cold air. It does not land often. Thin insects hatch in brief pulses near the pools when the sun reaches them, hovering above the water like living static. Once in a long while, a hare-colored creature appears among the sedges, pauses with one foot raised, and vanishes between stones. Its tracks fill with condensation before they dry.

There are also snails in the sheltered courtyards, though they look less like animals than mineral thoughts: pale shells flattened against moss, bodies translucent, moving only during rain or fog. They graze the lichen letters that cover the walls.

The letters may not be letters.

This is part of the strangeness of the place: everywhere, on wall faces and lintels and interior thresholds, there are markings in lichen, frost stain, mineral bloom. They form rows, loops, clusters, repeating bars. From a distance they resemble inscriptions, but no hand made them, no tool cut them. They appear where water runs, where wind stalls, where moss edges advance and retreat. In some seasons the markings change. A wall that seemed blank in autumn may speak all winter in white crystals. A doorway covered in green script may lose half its grammar after three days of sun.

The town reads itself and forgets.

There is no technology here. No metal, no glass, no wheel rut, no carved mechanism, no hinge. If this is urban, it is urban by accumulation rather than invention: paths used by water, chambers made by shelter, walls encouraged by freeze and thaw, squares opened where wind needed room. The city is not built with tools but by persistence. Stone leaned against stone until there were rooms. Roots entered seams until there were doorways. Frost lifted slabs until there were steps. Water found desire-lines and made streets.

Yet the organization is too strong to dismiss. The five lanes. The inward-facing thresholds. The drainage channels that meet at the central pool. The recurring alcoves at knee height, each holding a cushion of moss or a shell or a dry twist of grass. There is intention here, but not human intention. Or not only human. The world’s processes have settled into civic form.

The rules of the place are old and local.

Nothing tall lasts. Anything that rises too sharply is planed down by wind, split by frost, or eaten into roundness by time. Nothing dry remains purely dry, because the air lays moisture on it when the temperature drops. Nothing wet remains stable, because the cold takes it, skins it, shatters it, gives it back as seepage. The town is always being emptied and refilled in small amounts: water, fog, sound, spores, insects, shadow.

Movement prefers edges. Water runs along wall bases. Plants cling to cracks. Animals use the seams between open ground and shelter. Wind occupies the centers, rushing through plazas and roofless halls with public force. To cross the central hollow is to enter the loudest air. To stand against a wall is to hear tiny things: thawing moss, insect wings, the minute collapse of grit.

The daylight is plain and revealing. Not mystical gloom, not radiant dream-light. A cold, serviceable light moves across the plateau and shows everything: the pitted stone, the black wet channels, the lichen crusts, the old walls casting short blue shadows. Clouds pass quickly. Sun arrives without warmth, brightening the green pockets until they seem impossibly alive against the gray. Then the sun is gone and the place returns to iron.

At noon the settlement looks abandoned.

By evening it seems inhabited by processes.

Condensation begins first inside the western rooms, where the shaded stones cool early. Droplets bead along ceilings that no longer exist except as ledge-lines. The moss darkens. Tiny ferns uncurl. Snails detach from their pale stillness and begin to move. From the central pool rises a faint vapor, not warm, merely less cold than the air. The wind shifts downward from the rim and pours into the lanes.

Then the city sounds awake.

A fluting in the northern passage. A bass thrum under the plaza. Water ticking in thirteen different rooms. Grass hissing in the courtyards. Pebbles rolling one by one down a stair that leads nowhere. From far below the plateau edge comes the dull boom of cloud against cliff, or thunder caught in the valleys, or some ordinary meteorology made strange by distance.

The active life of the place is not fast, but it is continual. Erosion here is ancient, almost finished, and yet never complete. The end state still moves. Each day subtracts a grain, opens a pore, feeds a root, loosens a wall by less than sight can measure. The town has been nearly flattened for ages and will spend ages more becoming flatter. Its drama is the drama of persistence after climax: a world past youth, past ruin, still busy with minute transformations.

There are boundaries. One knows when one has entered. Outside the town, the plateau is bare and wind-rubbed, with only scattered stones and a few dry mats of lichen. Inside, the air changes. Sound gathers. Moisture holds. The ground remembers passage. Walls, even low ones, divide the cold into rooms. The vegetation thickens in the protected hollows, and the strange lichen markings begin. At the outermost ring, five threshold-stones lie half sunk in gravel. No gate stands there, but crossing between them feels like stepping indoors.

The place may once have been a village. It may never have been.

Perhaps, long ago, people came to this high worn tableland and built with the only material that would remain. Perhaps their roofs were skins or reeds or snow blocks, gone now. Perhaps they cut no inscriptions because the weather wrote better ones. Perhaps they left because the cold deepened, or because water became scarce, or because the town no longer needed them.

Or perhaps the plateau itself tends toward settlement, the way some minerals tend toward crystals. Given enough time, stone here organizes into courtyards. Moisture into streets. Moss into gardens. Wind into speech.

The most sheltered place is a narrow room near the eastern rim, where two leaning walls almost touch. One must turn sideways to enter. Inside, the floor is a deep mat of moss, wet but springy, and from a crack in the rear wall a thread of water appears and disappears according to no obvious schedule. The room is small, hardly more than a body-length, yet the acoustics make it feel tall. Every outside sound arrives softened and vertical, as if falling from above: the wind, the pool, the distant roar. A few pale insects cling to the underside of a stone lip. There is no smell of decay, only wet mineral, cold leaves, and the faint peppery scent of crushed lichen.

From this room, looking outward through the slit, one sees the whole town reduced to layers: near moss, mid-distance walls, central hollow, far rim, sky. Empty, but not dead. Built, but not made. Lush, but only in the cracks. Cold, but constantly sweating. Ancient, but active.

If one stays through the night, frost rims the pools. The lichen letters pale. The wind becomes less directional and more like pressure. Stars appear with hard clarity whenever the clouds tear open. The central pool blackens until it seems not reflective but deep, as though the plateau has a single open eye.

Before dawn, the loudness drops.

For a short hour there is only the almost-sound of freezing: minute expansion, minute strain, a whisper of crystals forming on stone. The city holds itself still. Then first light reaches the western walls, dull and gray-blue, and the thaw begins in the thinnest skins of ice. Drips resume. A bird calls once from somewhere beyond the rim. Water begins again to choose its streets.

And the old town, which has almost finished becoming landscape, continues.
