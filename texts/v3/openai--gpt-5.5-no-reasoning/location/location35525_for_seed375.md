At first it seems like a town abandoned before anyone ever lived in it.

It lies in a low basin, not a deep valley but a shallow settling of ground, a palm-shaped depression cupped by ridges of raw mineral crust. The horizon is close. You can see the whole place from any of the surrounding lips: an urban arrangement no larger than a hamlet or cathedral precinct, streets and courtyards and clustered verticals gathered inside a boundary wall that is not quite a wall. Beyond it, the land becomes a pale, dry emptiness of powder and splintered stone.

There is no water here. Not a hidden spring, not dew, not ice under shade. The air has learned dryness so completely that it feels less like air than a gap between materials. It does not carry scent far. It does not soften edges. Every exposed surface keeps its own sharpness. The ground is young and unweathered, as though raised from below only seasons ago: angular plates of gray salt-mineral, glassy seams, crisp fractures, knobs and fins that still remember the shape of whatever force pushed them upward.

The city is made of the same stuff. Or perhaps the same stuff has made the city.

There are streets, unmistakably: narrow corridors between walls, squares with open centers, arched recesses, towers as thin as fingers, rows of alcoves resembling doorways. But nothing shows the mark of tools. No chisel scars, no joints, no stacked blocks, no hammered metal, no timber, no hinges. The built forms have grown by rules more mineral than human. Walls rise as continuous extrusions from the basin floor, folded and ribbed like cooled wax or bone. Facades repeat in softened geometries, window-like voids arranged in patterns too orderly to dismiss as accident, yet too bodily to call architecture.

It is urban without technology: a city as lichen might dream one, a settlement crystallized by habit rather than design.

The outer boundary is a ring of low, fused parapets and leaning spires, broken in several places by passages just wide enough for a person or animal to enter. Inside, the town opens into districts. One quarter is all galleries: parallel roofless lanes covered by overhanging mineral ribs, making stripes of dim light and darkness on the floor. Another is a court of short towers, none taller than a modest bell tower, each hollowed by vertical slits that lead nowhere. At the center lies a flat plaza of matte white crust, nearly circular, empty except for a single black extrusion like the stump of a column or a cooled flame.

The scale is intimate. You could cross the place in minutes if the streets did not constantly turn you aside. It has boundaries, rooms, thresholds, sightlines. There are corners that feel private. There are plazas that feel ceremonial. Yet the density is strangely low. For all its urban patterning, the place is mostly emptiness: air in corridors, voids in walls, unoccupied chambers, blind wells, vacant nests of shadow. The structures are thin shells around silence.

The light is dim but not dark. A cool, diffuse glow filters down from a sky the color of tarnished pewter. There is no direct sun, or if there is, it remains hidden behind a permanent high veil of mineral haze. The light seems tired before it reaches the ground. It catches on crystalline edges, making them glimmer briefly, then vanish into dull gray. In recesses the shadows have a bluish cast. In narrow lanes they deepen to violet-black.

The temperature stays cool enough that stone holds a faint chill. Not freezing, not hostile in the way of ice, but a dry coolness like underground chambers or dawn on bare rock. Touching the walls would leave no moisture on your skin, only a powdering of fine mineral dust. Breath would sound too loud, then be swallowed. Footsteps would not echo properly; the city’s hollows seem to take sound in and refuse to return it.

The silence is almost complete.

Not peaceful silence. Not dead silence either. It is the silence of processes too small, too slow, or too well-contained to make ordinary noise. The place is active, but furtively. Surfaces tighten and release without cracking. Dust migrates along grooves in hair-thin streams. Thin membranes hidden in wall fissures open and close in response to the dimming sky. Salt plates lift at their edges, curl a fraction, settle again. In the plaza, the black column warms and cools on a cycle not tied to daylight, expanding just enough to shed translucent flakes from its sides.

If you stay long enough, you notice motion everywhere.

Powder gathers in the streets though there is no wind strong enough to be called wind. It moves as if the ground underneath breathes through pores. A pale drift will build along the base of a wall, then draw back into a seam. Fine grains creep uphill in branching lines, attracted to certain facades. At intervals, the entire basin seems to exhale: not audibly, but visibly, as a faint lifting of dust from every surface at once. The dust rises to ankle height, hangs in the dimness like smoke, then sorts itself by color and weight, falling into bands.

This is the weather here.

Without water, weather is mineral and electrical, pressure and powder, temperature and charge. The city collects the dry atmosphere, strains it through its corridors, and precipitates structures rather than rain. During the long cool phases, pale efflorescences bloom on walls: fans of brittle needles, crusts like frost but utterly dry, small branching forms that resemble coral or leaf skeletons. When the basin warms slightly, these growths collapse into dust and feed the streets. The dust drifts into cracks, where new walls begin.

The settlement is still forming. That is why its edges are so sharp. It has not been rounded by time. New alleys extrude from the floor, lifting in ribs overnight. Door-shaped openings seal halfway and become niches. Niches stretch, join, and become arcades. A plaza may wrinkle into a field of low fins, or a blind wall may thin until light shows through it like skin.

There is sparse vegetation, if vegetation is the right word.

In protected corners, where walls meet at angles and dust accumulates in stable pockets, dark tufted growths cling to the mineral. They are not green. Most are charcoal, rust-red, or a dull bruised purple. They grow as mats of wiry threads, low cushions, or upright bristles no taller than a hand. Some resemble dried grasses made of filament and ash. Others form flat, circular colonies like stains or scorch marks. They do not drink; there is nothing to drink. Instead they feed on trace gases, mineral charge, and the slow shedding of the city’s crust.

Their growth is patient but not inert. In the dimmest lanes, black thread-plants extend across the floor during the coldest hours, making loose nets from wall to wall. When the basin exhales dust, the threads twitch and catch particles along their length. Later they retract, pulling nourishment into knotted bases tucked safely in cracks. Where they are older, they have altered the architecture: stone around them becomes softer-looking, more porous, darkened by chemical digestion. Tiny alcoves become groves. A window slit becomes a root-mouth. A stairless tower becomes a chimney for spores.

In one district, the vegetation has taken possession of an entire colonnade. Each pillar is wrapped in sparse, upright filaments, all leaning inward toward the lane as if listening. They release no fragrance. They make no rustling sound. But their tips glow faintly when the sky dims, a low ember color, not enough to illuminate the ground but enough to reveal their positions. Walking there would feel like passing through a crowd that does not move aside.

Fauna is rare. You might see none for days.

Then, suddenly, one appears where there had been only dust: a small pale creature unfolded from a seam in the pavement. It has too many joints and moves with extreme deliberation, as if unwilling to waste motion in this dry world. Its body is flat, translucent at the edges, armored with mineral flakes. It crosses a lane without sound, pauses beneath a thread-plant, and scrapes particles from its filaments with delicate mouthparts. It is not thirsty. Nothing here is thirsty. Hunger follows different chemistries.

There are larger animals, but they are more like rumors than inhabitants. In the outer ring, shallow tracks sometimes appear: paired punctures and a long drag-line through the dust. On the parapets at dim phases, a wingless glider may cling upside down beneath an arch, its body shaped like folded paper, absorbing charge from the wall. Predators exist mostly as absence. A feeding mat will one day be neatly cut away. A dust-creature’s track will stop in the middle of a courtyard. One of the brittle plant-groves will fold all its filaments at once, though nothing visible has passed.

The rules of the world are not hidden, but they are not human rules.

Water is not the solvent of life here. Time is measured in deposition and collapse. Buildings are not separate from ecology. A wall is food, shelter, lung, skeleton, and future ground. A street is a channel for powder migration. A tower is a condenser of charge. A plaza is a basin where fallen crusts are sorted into layers fine enough for living mats to mine.

There is no craft, yet there is organization. No inhabitants laying stones, yet there are neighborhoods. No machines, yet cycles run continuously. The city is a living diagram of non-aqueous weather. Its architecture is the visible residue of invisible gradients: heat, pressure, static, chemical hunger, shadow.

Sometimes the entire place changes orientation in your mind. What seemed like a town becomes a reef stranded in air. What seemed like buildings become the exposed upper chambers of an organism buried beneath the basin. What seemed like streets become cracks between slow-growing organs. The central plaza, with its black column, begins to feel less ceremonial and more anatomical: a vent, a heart, a cold furnace.

At the lowest point behind the plaza is a chamber open to the sky through a ragged oval. The floor slopes inward, not much, just enough to gather the finest dust. The walls are smooth compared to the rest of the city, pale beige and gray, lined with thousands of shallow pits. In each pit sits a bead-sized nodule, dry and hard, waiting. During the dimmest part of the cycle, the pits open. The nodules extrude threads so fine they are almost invisible, spanning the chamber in a fragile mesh.

No sound accompanies this. The mesh forms in silence.

Then the black column in the plaza releases a pulse of warmth. Dust lifts throughout the city. In the chamber, the floating grains catch on the threads until the air itself seems woven. By the next cool phase, the threads have thickened, hardened, and withdrawn, leaving new mineral lace along the walls. Some of it will become structure. Some will become food. Some will break and seed other districts.

This may be how the city grows.

It is young enough that many forms remain unresolved. Half-born arches rise from streets and end abruptly, their tips sharp as broken antler. Courtyards contain grids of low ridges that may become foundations or feeding racks or something with no human equivalent. Along the outer rim, new spires push through the crust at awkward angles, pale and tender-looking despite being stone. The city has not yet learned symmetry perfectly. It repeats patterns with variation, testing which shapes hold dust, which hold charge, which invite the dark plants, which repel the unseen grazers.

The surrounding landscape is simpler and harsher. Low flats of compacted mineral extend outward, almost featureless except for fissures and small upheavals. The basin’s rim shelters the city from whatever broader emptiness lies beyond. There are no dunes in the familiar sense, because there is almost no wind; instead there are settled sheets of powder, smooth and undisturbed, broken by fresh-looking cracks. The world outside appears younger still, unfinished, a raw substrate waiting for rules to take.

Standing at the edge and looking in, the place feels both abandoned and busy. Nothing hurries, but everything is underway. Walls are being eaten. Streets are being written. Plants are listening. Rare animals pass through like punctuation. The architecture is not ruin, not construction, but metabolism.

And always, the silence.

A silence so complete that motion becomes visible before it becomes audible. A silence in which falling dust seems dramatic. A silence that makes the dim light feel solid, makes the cool air seem sealed under glass. If a stone flake detaches from a tower and lands in the street, the sound is a tiny click, startling as thunder. Then the city absorbs it. Dust creeps around the flake. A filament touches it. A seam opens nearby.

The place continues, quietly, to build itself from dryness.
