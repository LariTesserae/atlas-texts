The city stands high enough that the idea of ground has become ceremonial.

It is built across a young range of black mountains, though “range” may be too confident a word. The land has not finished deciding how to be mountainous. Slabs of basalt lean at improbable angles, not yet softened by weather. Ridges break off into raw planes with sharp, glassy seams. Whole cliffs seem recently lifted, their undersides still pale and damp, as if they have been turned up from beneath the skin of the world and have not yet learned the color of air.

There are summits, but they are blunt and unfinished, some capped by scaffolds, some split by vents breathing thin white vapor. There are valleys, but shallow ones, still sketchlike, their streams uncertain and wandering. Many gullies do not go anywhere. They descend for a few hundred meters, collect a line of condensation, then simply stop at a shelf of new stone, their future course uncarved.

The city occupies this incomplete geology the way lichen occupies a statue: in scattered colonies, dense where conditions allow, absent where the surface refuses. Districts cling to terraces and saddles between peaks. Bridges cross gulfs that may become canyons in ten thousand years. Inclined railways run up slopes too steep for roads. Elevator towers descend into mist, then reappear impossibly far below on another shoulder of rock, connected by cables, catwalks, and pressure pipes.

From a distance the city looks like a constellation of industry pinned to the sides of a dark mineral animal.

The light is poor. Not night, not quite storm, but a persistent dimness, the kind that removes color from distance and makes every lamp seem necessary. A low ceiling of cloud drags over the high ridges. It moves slowly, scraping itself on antennae, chimneys, wind vanes, and crane booms. In some quarters the clouds pass through streets directly, turning alleys into cold white tunnels. Windows glow amber, blue, greenish-white. Signal lamps blink along cable routes. Foundries pulse with dull red light from behind ribbed shutters.

Rain comes and goes without drama. It does not fall in hard sheets; it appears as a fine slanting presence, a drifting wetness, as though the air has remembered water briefly and imperfectly. It beads on pipes, gathers on roof edges, hangs in the moss between masonry blocks. It slicks the stone stairs and darkens the planked walkways. Then it stops, leaving condensation to do the rest.

Water is scarce in the way it is scarce on high, cold ground: not absent, but hidden, intermittent, distributed in pockets and rituals. Cisterns crouch under plazas. Narrow canals carry meltwater through covered troughs. Tanks are painted with district marks and guarded by municipal seals. Some neighborhoods hear water only as a hollow ticking inside walls, collected from roofs and fog nets stretched between towers. Elsewhere, on the windward faces, every surface weeps. The undersides of bridges drip steadily into ravines where pale ferns grow from cracks.

Vegetation is not continuous, but where it takes hold it is vivid and almost excessive. It comes in bands and clumps, lush islands in the spare city. Moss grows thick over old retaining walls, furred and deep enough to swallow dropped tools. Ferns crowd around steam vents, their fronds blue-green and silver-backed. Cold vines climb chimney stacks and water towers, fastening themselves with red-black tendrils to brick and iron. On high roofs, communal gardens sit inside windbreaks of salvaged glass: cabbages with dark waxy leaves, medicinal herbs, dwarf trees trained low against the weather.

In the older districts, courtyards have become miniature forests. Rain collects there, trapped between buildings; soil has been imported over generations in baskets and rail carts; birds nest in the hanging roots of fog-fed trees. The leaves are broad despite the cold, adapted to drink from air rather than earth. Many are translucent at the edges, and when factory light passes through them they glow like preserved skin.

Beyond these green pockets, emptiness returns quickly. A district may end at a railing. Past it: open air, a slope of raw stone, a few cable pylons, and the slow turning of cloud below. The city is urban, but not packed in the usual way. It is organized around gaps. The gaps are essential: ravines, immature valleys, avalanche corridors, sacred unbuilt ridges, future roads awaiting land that has not settled. Sound travels across them strangely. A hammer blow from one district may arrive minutes later as a soft metallic answer from another.

The industrial body of the city is old in habit but young in setting. It is a place of engines, ropeways, foundries, pump houses, stamping mills, condensers, and manufactories built not on a plain but along gradients of pressure, heat, and height. Factories are terraced into cliffs. Their walls are heavy stone below, riveted iron above, glass where glass can survive the wind. Tall chimneys rise only a little higher than the surrounding towers before vanishing into cloud. Many do not smoke so much as exhale: vapor, mineral steam, cold gases drawn from vents in the newborn mountain.

There are cranes everywhere, but they move slowly. Their arms turn with glacial patience. Loads of quarried stone, green timber, coal, cages of birds, crates of machine parts, and ceramic water filters sway across drops so deep the bottom is often weather rather than visible land. Winches mutter. Pistons sigh. Flywheels rotate behind grilled windows, not fast, but with the authority of things that have rotated for decades and intend to continue.

The loudness of the place is not constant, but it is layered. Near the mills it becomes a physical medium: belts slapping, gears clacking, ore hoppers releasing their stony coughs, steam valves shrieking and then subsiding. Higher up, in residential terraces, the sound spreads and thins. One hears bells, animal calls, the creak of signboards, rain in gutters, the distant groan of loaded cables. At night—or what passes for night under the permanent dimness—the whole city hums at a low pitch, as though the mountain itself has been fitted with a machine too large to turn off.

Fauna is abundant, startlingly so for a place of height, cold, iron, and stone.

Birds dominate the air. Not just pigeons and crows, though there are plenty of both, their feathers dark with soot and condensation. There are cliff swifts nesting in drainpipes, white-throated ravens that mimic factory whistles, heavy-bellied gulls from some unseen inland sea, and translucent-winged mothbirds that gather around sodium lamps in murmuring spirals. In the fog one hears wings before one sees bodies. Flocks travel between districts like loose weather.

The ledges support goats, but not ordinary goats. Their horns grow asymmetrically, often branching like frost. They walk along pipes, roofs, crane beams, and raw cliff with the same indifferent balance. Children feed them peelings from municipal gardens; workers curse them for chewing insulation. Larger animals move in the outskirts: shaggy deer with heat-loving moss along their backs, blind stone-marmots that tunnel into warm vents, narrow foxes that hunt by listening to the pulse of electrical lines.

In the wet courtyards are amphibians, impossible at this elevation and climate yet undeniably present. Small black frogs breed in cistern overflows. Newts with copper flecks in their skin live in condensation trays under cold pipes. Something like a salamander, long as a human arm and pale as uncooked dough, inhabits the drainage galleries beneath the oldest foundries. The workers leave bowls of saltless broth for them. No one remembers why, but production suffers if they do not.

Insects thrive wherever warmth leaks. Beetles gather under boiler houses. Silver flies hatch in the mist around steam vents. Pollinators resembling furred wasps move between rooftop gardens, drunken and slow in the cold air. At certain hours, especially before rain, the city becomes filled with small movements: legs in moss, wings in eaves, claws in gutters, tails vanishing under doors.

But the animals are not the strangest inhabitants.

The rules of the place are damaged, or perhaps not yet fully assembled. Reality here behaves like the geology: raw, uplifted, only partly eroded into stable form.

Perspective fails across the open gaps. A bridge may look two hundred steps long from one side and half a day’s journey from the other. Towers seen through fog are sometimes younger than themselves: scaffolds appear around buildings completed a century ago; windows show rooms before they were furnished; a chimney might exhale smoke from a fire that will be lit tomorrow morning. Most citizens have learned not to stare at distant architecture after dusk.

Gravity has local opinions. In certain stairwells, descent takes more effort than climbing. Loose pebbles roll uphill along one famous lane in the brassworkers’ quarter, collecting in a shrine-like mound beneath a broken clock. Rain sometimes falls from below in narrow shafts, rising from ravines to bead against the undersides of balconies. Laundry hung in the wrong alleys twists toward invisible centers of pull.

The city maintains departments for such things. There are municipal surveyors of gradient and anomaly, wearing gray coats marked with plumb-line symbols. They walk with brass levels, cages of canaries, and sealed jars of water to test whether down remains down. Streets are posted with warnings:

DO NOT ENTER WHEN THE THIRD LAMP IS LIT.

THIS STAIRCASE HAS BEEN RETIRED.

PERSPECTIVE TAX APPLIES BEYOND THIS POINT.

WATCH FOR RETURNING ECHOES.

The echo warning matters. Sound does not always fade correctly. In some courtyards, a word spoken aloud may return months later, aged and hoarse, carrying soot and weather from wherever it has been. The foundry horns are scheduled carefully to avoid collisions with previous alarms. There is an archive of dangerous echoes stored in padded vaults beneath the civic hall, though on wet days they can still be heard whispering through the drains.

Time is slow here, but not still. Change happens with the patience of condensation forming on metal. The mountain rises by increments too subtle to witness directly; doorframes go out of square over generations; rails must be regraded each winter. New cracks open in plazas, not violently, but with a sound like a book spine flexing. Moss crosses stone in decades. Buildings settle into new angles and are buttressed, then buttressed again, until some streets resemble forests of beams.

The people have adapted to gradual instability. Houses are built with adjustable foundations and sacrificial walls. Bridges have slack engineered into them. Streets are named not by fixed positions but by their relation to functions: Lower Condenser Row, West of the Old Lift, Above the Third Cistern, Under Where the Observatory Was. Maps are printed on flexible cloth and corrected by hand. The most accurate maps include blank areas labeled “pending.”

There is an urban order, but it is provisional. The city has districts: the Boiler Terraces, the Moss Courts, the Cablefields, the High Abattoirs, the East Glasshouses, the Foundling Quarries, the Aviary Markets, the Unmeasured Ward. Trams run where rails remain trustworthy. Elsewhere, funiculars haul passengers in enclosed cabins that smell of wet wool, oil, and animal feed. Freight moves by cable, by mule, by goat-cart, by conveyor belts passing through tunnels warm enough to grow mushrooms.

Markets occupy covered arcades, their roofs glazed with thick green glass. There are stalls for machine parts, lamp oil, root vegetables, feathers, fungal bread, salvaged bolts, charms against spatial drift, dried berries from roof gardens, and bottled fog of various grades. Fish appear rarely, packed in ice from lower rivers, and draw crowds. More common are cliff eggs, smoked goat, beetle flour, and pale tubers grown in condensate beds.

The atmosphere is cold but inhabited, dim but not dead. Breath shows in the air inside unheated corridors. Railings are always wet to the touch. Metal smells strongly: iron, copper, old oil, rainwater, hot bearings. Stone smells new, almost sharp, especially after mist. In gardens the scent changes abruptly to leaf mold, crushed fern, wet bark, animal musk, and the mineral sweetness of collected water.

The sky is close. It presses against the city, crowding the towers. Yet the scale of the place is open, regional, larger than any one view. From a high platform on a rare clearing, one can see other neighborhoods miles away across intervening voids: lamps strung along ridgelines, smoke lying flat beneath cloud, cranes like patient insects, green patches glowing wherever warmth and water have gathered. Below them, unfinished valleys descend into blue darkness. Above them, further peaks rise, some with lights on their flanks, some entirely raw and unclaimed.

The edge of the city is difficult to define. It frays into worksites, then into survey camps, then into markers drilled into bare stone. But sometimes beyond the last buildings one finds infrastructure already waiting: a lamppost lit beside no road, a flight of steps carved into a cliff that has not yet been connected to anything, a water pipe emerging from rock and disappearing into sky. These may be future constructions arriving early, or abandoned plans from a version of the city that failed to happen. No one removes them. In a place like this, an unused structure may become necessary centuries later, or yesterday.

At the highest elevation is the Crown Works, though the name applies less to a single factory than to a belt of installations circling the upper peaks. There the air is thin, cold, and always wet. Massive condensers spread like metal forests, combing moisture from cloud. Their fins sing when the wind passes through them. Water collected there descends through pipes to the districts below, measured and taxed and blessed.

Around the Crown Works, vegetation grows in eerie abundance. Not trees, mostly, but mats of moss, cold orchids, fern towers, and hanging curtains of algae stretched between condenser ribs. Birds nest among the machinery in such numbers that the workers carry umbrellas even when indoors. Feathers clog gutters. Eggs appear in toolboxes. The line between machine and habitat has blurred beyond repair.

Sometimes, in the dimmest weather, the entire upper city seems underwater. Workers move through vapor with lamps on their belts. Fish-shaped shadows pass overhead where no fish could be. Voices arrive muffled, rounded, delayed. The towers sway slightly, or seem to. The cranes turn slowly, moving loads through cloud like offerings through milk.

Down in the Unmeasured Ward, the breakdown is more intimate. Streets repeat themselves. A doorway in a bakery opens onto the same bakery from the opposite wall, but only before dawn. A public fountain contains water on Wednesdays and stars on Fridays. A row of tenements has thirteen third floors and no second. Residents navigate by smell, habit, and neighborly warning. Children raised there are excellent at arithmetic but distrust straight lines.

Yet life continues with remarkable practicality. Laundry is hung. Boilers are fed. Gardens are weeded. Animals are counted. Taxes are disputed. The strange is catalogued when possible and accommodated when not. Reality may be unreliable, but hunger is not, nor winter, nor the need for dry socks.

The slow pace of the place gives even its impossibilities a sedimentary feel. Miracles become infrastructure if they persist long enough. A levitating boulder has been chained in place and used as a tram counterweight. A warm crack in the stone has become a public bathhouse. A hallway that lengthens during arguments now hosts municipal negotiations, ensuring that tempers cool before anyone reaches the door.

Erosion has barely begun, and so the city does some of erosion’s work artificially. It cuts channels, grades roads, drills drainage galleries, blasts ledges, pours retaining walls, directs meltwater, teaches the young rock how to shed rain. But the mountain resists being finished. Every construction feels like a proposal submitted to forces older or stranger than law.

There are no broad rivers yet, only intentions of rivers: trickles collecting in engineered grooves, runoff guided through ceramic lips, rivulets vanishing into fractures. In the ravines below, these small waters gather into temporary silver threads. They sound louder than they should in the cold air. They carry soot, pollen, metal filings, frog eggs, and the dust of new stone.

At certain seasons, when the cloud thins and the temperature drops, frost forms over everything wet. The city becomes edged in white. Pipes glitter. Moss stiffens. Ferns bow under crystal. The animals grow quiet except for the ravens, who become louder, laughing from the cranes. Factory heat rises and freezes on upper beams as feathery accumulations. Workers knock ice from cables with long poles, and the falling shards vanish into fog before they strike anything audible.

Then the thaw returns, mild and damp. Water ticks in thousands of places. Green resumes its patient work. The geology shifts by a hair’s breadth. A surveyor marks a new crack in red paint. A goat gives birth in the lee of a condenser. A bridge echo returns from last year, warning of a storm already survived. Somewhere beneath the foundations, the mountain continues being born.

The whole landscape feels suspended between construction and emergence: not ruin, not completion, but a city collaborating uneasily with a world still drafting itself. It is industrial and feral, urban and alpine, damp in pockets and dry on exposed stone, loud in its engines yet slow in its transformations. Its lights burn dimly against cloud and raw rock. Its animals multiply in the seams. Its streets hold their breath over open air.

And every morning, if morning is the correct word for the slight paling of the mist, the city wakes to find that something has changed just enough to require attention, and not enough to be called disaster.
