It is a small place, but it does not feel enclosed in the ordinary way. Its boundaries are obvious only when you try to leave.

From the middle it resembles a grove built inside a roofless room: warm, low, densely crowded, no farther across than a hamlet square or the nave of a modest cathedral. There is level ground underfoot, though “ground” is not quite the word. Nothing here seems to have come from a planet. The floor is a fitted surface of pale ceramic plates, horn, compressed fiber, fired clay, bone-white slag, woven reedlike strips that cannot have been reeds because there is no water here and never has been. The seams between the pieces are dark and full of dust. In some places the plates have lifted along their edges, still sharp from recent making, not yet rounded by wear. In others the floor has sagged by a finger’s width, as if the whole grove is settling into a body beneath it.

There is no soil. No mud, no mossy dampness, no dew. Yet the place is lush.

The vegetation grows from slots, hooks, shelves, hanging pockets, knotted frames, and lattices fixed into the built ground. Each plant is installed. Each root has been trained into a vessel, clamped into a groove, braided through clay rings, or wrapped around little idols of pumice and chalk. The grove has the density of a greenhouse but the dryness of a kiln. Leaves overlap in curtains: waxy dark paddles, translucent amber fans, coils of grey-green ribbon, feathery crimson organs like gills that never drank anything. Some hang downward from racks in slow spirals; some stand stiffly upright like green knives; some spread fine glass-thread fronds that quiver when approached, though no wind can be felt.

They are lush because they do not live by water. Their sap is resin, oil, volatile mineral milk, powder in suspension, memory of light. When their stems are cut, if they are cut, they do not bleed but smoke faintly, releasing a smell of warm varnish, crushed pepper, ash, and old fruit. Their roots enter dry cartridges filled with salts, powdered bone, charcoal, mica dust, and ground seeds from previous generations. Growth here is a craft. Leaves are replenished with powders blown through reed tubes. Branches are combed. Sheaths are peeled away by hand. Dead matter is not composted but scraped, saved, burned without flame, reduced to shining flakes, then fed back into the slots.

The air is warm, nearly still, and filled with suspended scents rather than humidity. It has the closeness of an occupied room. Not stifling, but intimate. Breath feels slightly too visible in the lungs, as if it is moving through a medium thicker than air. Every smell stays where it was made: resin near the red coil-vines, alkali near the white troughs, bitter pollen near the high nets, animal musk under the hanging cages. If you walk through the grove, you pass through distinct invisible chambers of odor.

The light is ordinary only at first glance. It falls from above in a soft, warm wash, like late morning through a clean skylight. There is no visible sun. Shadows behave properly until one studies them; then they can be seen to belong to different hours. Under the broad black leaves the shadows are evening shadows. The clay uprights cast noon shadows. A row of twig-lattices near the eastern wall casts shadows that lean toward each other and braid like fingers. In certain corners light seems to have been folded and stored, brightening slowly when no one looks at it directly.

The grove’s walls are close but not oppressive. They are made from stacked, hand-shaped modules: brick, tile, bone, paper-clay, shell-like ceramic, varnished fiber, and slabs of something like sun-dried skin stretched over frames. Their construction is total, deliberate, and primitive. There are no machines visible, no smooth industrial surfaces, no wires or polished panels. Everything has been shaped by hand, tamped, knotted, fired, carved, tied, pressed with thumbs. The walls bear tool marks everywhere. Finger ridges. Gouges from stone blades. Rope scars. Burnish from palms. The built world has no natural substrate beneath it, but it has the intimacy of craft. It is artificial all the way down, yet handmade.

Many things live here.

Small animals move among the dense growth with dry, soft noises. Their abundance is mostly suggested rather than seen: a tremor in the thread-fronds, a quick shifting weight inside a clay pipe, three beadlike eyes withdrawing behind a leaf, a line of tiny feet crossing the underside of a shelf. There are pale lizards with parchment skin and no mouths, nourished by basking against warm tiles. Beetles with ceramic shells click once every few minutes and then freeze for an hour. Thin fox-sized animals sleep curled in resin nests, their tails wrapped around porous mineral loaves. Moths drift without wingbeats, held aloft by rules that do not always apply to the rest of the place. Little jointed things like walking seeds carry dust from plant to plant in baskets grown from their backs.

The fauna is abundant, but the grove is mostly quiet. It has the quiet of creatures adapted not to waste sound. No stream, no wind in trees, no birdsong. A faint granular settling can be heard if one stops walking: dust moving through tubes; husks opening by fractions; a claw adjusting on fired clay; far inside the walls, a slow wooden knock like someone counting years. Occasionally a leaf snaps taut with a dry papery crack, and every animal nearby pauses. Then the quiet repairs itself.

Because the place is young, many edges are still too clean. The ceramic ribs are sharp at their corners. The floor seams have not filled. The painted signs on the walls have not faded, though some already contradict the geometry around them. Fresh plaster appears beside older-looking fossils that must have been embedded only yesterday. The grove feels recently emerged, perhaps recently assembled, perhaps recently remembered into existence. It has not yet learned which of its details are permanent.

The terrain, if it can be called terrain, is low and gently uneven. It rises nowhere higher than a terrace or platform, sinks nowhere deeper than a crawlspace. Narrow paths thread through the planted density, tiled in ocher and white. There are shallow basins full not of water but of pale granular powders: salt, ash, powdered feldspar, scented lime. These basins are raked into patterns by animals or by unseen keepers. Some patterns resemble irrigation channels, though nothing flows in them. Others resemble maps of places too large to fit here. At the center is a circular depression lined with black ceramic petals, warm to the touch. It might be a hearth, a root-bed, an altar, or a mouth that has chosen not to open.

The world’s rules are not absent. They are local.

Weight changes by material. Clay is heavy. Bone is heavier than it should be. Leaves weigh almost nothing unless named aloud, at which point they sag perceptibly. Sound travels poorly through air but very well through lattices; a tap on one trellis may be answered by a faint tremor in another across the grove. Distance is faithful along the paths and treacherous between them. Step off the walkway into dense growth and a shrub only an arm’s length deep can contain a passage, a chamber, a corridor of leaves longer than the whole enclosure. Step backward and the path is short again.

The walls do not move, but they revise themselves. A niche that held three white stones may later hold two stones and a small sleeping animal that everyone remembers having always been there. Doorways appear as painted outlines before becoming actual gaps. Some painted outlines remain flat but allow smells to pass through. There are ladders leading to no upper level, but climbing them lowers the ceiling of the sky.

Nothing flows quickly. Change is slow, patient, almost bureaucratic. Flowers open over the course of weeks with the brittle incremental sound of cooling pottery. Fruits form as hard dry blisters, ripen into sealed resin eggs, and are harvested by small animals who roll them into shadow. A cracked tile takes days to finish cracking. A creature molting may remain half-out of its old skin for a season, attended by mites that polish the exposed parts. Dust falls upward on certain afternoons, but only a little at a time, gathering on the undersides of leaves in soft mineral beards.

There are signs of inhabitants, though perhaps not current ones. Low stools sit beside the planting frames. Hooks hold tools made of wood, bone, shell, and chipped glassy stone. Brushes with bristles of stiff plant fiber hang from pegs. A row of masks rests along one wall, each shaped for a face with too many or too few features. Clay jars contain powders labeled with scratch marks, knots, or embedded teeth. Nothing here suggests advanced machinery, but the craft is exact and ritualized. Whoever maintains the grove understands its impossible botany through touch, timing, taste, and taboo.

There are rules scratched into the walls in pictographs:

Do not pour.  
Do not whistle into the white roots.  
Do not count the animals twice.  
If a path repeats, leave a seed of ash.  
If a leaf casts your shadow, stand still until it forgets.  
Never bring water.

The last rule is carved most deeply. The groove has been filled with red pigment and then sealed under translucent resin. Water would be a catastrophe here, not because the plants would thirst for it, but because the world might remember a different chemistry. Moisture would soften agreements. It would wake old erosion. It would make the powders clump, the salts run, the ceramic roots swell, the painted doors open all at once. The grove is lush by refusal. It has made abundance from dryness, density from restraint, ecology from sealed bargains between mineral, animal, craft, and light.

At the outer edge, where one might expect a gate, there is instead a narrow threshold of woven clay strips. Beyond it is not desert exactly, nor void exactly, but an unfinished margin: a pale blankness of warm air and suspended grit. The grove ends with shocking precision. One step before the threshold, there are leaves brushing both shoulders, animals watching from holes, trellises layered so thickly they seem to hold up the sky. One step beyond, there is almost nothing: a salt-colored openness without horizon, a dry field waiting to be made. The boundary hums faintly, or perhaps the silence outside is so complete that the body invents a hum to protect itself.

Seen from that edge, the place appears even smaller and stranger: a dense handmade oasis with no water, a fabricated thicket rooted in craft rather than earth, a warm pocket of negotiated life inside a world that has not yet decided how real it will be.

And if one waits long enough, very quietly, something changes.

A white bud near the central black basin opens by a single scale. The shadows adjust. A ceramic beetle turns its head toward a wall where no door was a moment ago. From behind the wall comes a soft dry brushing sound: not wind, not rain, but the careful sweeping of hands preparing another room.
