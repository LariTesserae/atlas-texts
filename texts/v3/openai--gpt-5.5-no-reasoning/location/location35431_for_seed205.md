At this height the heat feels misplaced.

The plateau stands far above the lowlands, high enough that the horizon is a pale blue curve and clouds often pass below the ledges, yet the air is hot and close, thick with the breath of plants. It is not a mountain summit in the usual sense. It is an immense tableland, a continent lifted into the sky and held there for ages, its edges bitten into cliffs, its interior worn smooth by weather, hooves, roots, rain, and time. From below it might seem like a green world floating over a drier earth. From within, it has no visible end.

Vegetation occupies almost everything, but not as a dense forest exactly. It is more fundamental than that. The ground itself is plant.

Underfoot is not soil so much as layered living matter: braided root felt, springing mats of waxy moss, old compressed stems, woody plates, fibrous humus, and pale fungal threads running like buried nerves. The surface gives slightly with each step. In places it is dry and crisp, crackling like woven reed; elsewhere it sweats in the heat and darkens to a spongy green-black. If cut, the ground beads sap. If burned, it smolders rather than flames, releasing a bitter steam that brings insects from kilometers away.

The landscape is vast and strangely open. Plants fill the world, but they do not always crowd it. There are broad intervals of low, shining leaf-mats stretching for leagues, interrupted by solitary pillar-trees or clumps of jointed reed-towers. The spacing gives the place a ceremonial emptiness, like an orchard planted by something too large to imagine. Between these widely set growths lie corridors of sun and moving sound. Nothing is truly barren, yet there is room everywhere: room for heat shimmer, room for herds, room for the long shadows of insects passing overhead.

The great plants have architecture without builders. Some rise as columns twenty, fifty, a hundred meters tall, not branching until their crowns, where they unfold into circular canopies like enormous green parasols. Others are ribbed buttresses that grow in arcs, forming open halls beneath them. There are vine bridges strung naturally between old trunks, their fibers tensioned by growth and weather until they hum in the wind. There are walls of thorned succulent, separated by clean lanes worn by animals. There are root-caves, leaf-shelters, and vaulted hollows where old stems have died inside living skins, leaving long, cool chambers smelling of resin and damp ash.

Water is present, but never everywhere at once.

Rain comes in narrow events. Heat stacks clouds above the plateau during the afternoon, but they often fail to break; when they do, the rain walks visibly across the distance in gray curtains, soaking one valley of leaves while the next remains dusty and glaring. Showers last minutes. The plants are built for this. Every surface catches, cups, channels. Leaves fold into gutters. Bark wrinkles into runnels. The roots beneath the ground-mat open pores and drink quickly. After rain the whole plateau ticks, drips, exhales. Steam rises from acres of green. Condensation forms on the underside of broad leaves before dawn and falls in a second, private rainfall when the morning wind touches them.

There are rivers, but they are intermittent and half-hidden. They do not carve stone channels so much as follow seams in the living ground. In the wet months they swell into brown-green torrents beneath tunnels of roots, audible before visible, carrying seeds, shells, eggs, drowned insects, strips of bark, and the bodies of small animals. In dry intervals they retreat into chains of pools. These pools are black, warm, and crowded, each one an entire frantic world: tadpole-things writhing in clouds, armored beetles skating the surface, pale roots descending from above like drinking fingers, fish with lungs waiting under mats of floating leaves.

The plateau is old enough to have settled into itself. Its forms are weathered, not young. The cliffs around its perimeter are fluted and undercut; great terraces descend in steps where older plant-mats have died and become dark bands in the exposed strata. Some exposed stone remains, especially along wind-scoured ridges, but even there lichens, creepers, and heat-loving grasses occupy every crack. Erosion has not stripped the place bare. Instead, erosion and growth have come to an agreement. Rain cuts channels; roots lace them closed. Animals trample roads; vines roof them. A tower-tree falls and opens a scar of sun; within a season the wound is a nursery of ferocious green. The land is constantly being damaged and constantly being re-grown into its preferred shape.

The heat has weight. It lies under the plant canopies like an animal sleeping. At midday the open mats shine with oils and transpired moisture; the air wavers. Resin smells sharpen. Fermentation rises from the ground in sweet, alcoholic gusts. In the shade, fungi pulse and split. Flowers open with small wet sounds. Some are bright enough to seem artificial—orange cups, violet funnels, white starbursts as large as shields—but many are dull, brown, fleshy, carrion-colored, evolved not for beauty but for attraction.

The fauna is everywhere, even when the vegetation looks sparse.

Movement begins at the smallest scale: gnats in columns, mites on bark, transparent flies rising in sheets when a leaf is disturbed. Ant-like creatures travel in arterial trails along root ridges, their bodies lacquer-black and red, carrying cut fragments of leaf above their heads like sails. Termite towers grow from the living mat, but many are colonized by plants, their clay walls pierced by roots and flowering stems. Beetles as large as fists chew at fallen pods. Soft-bodied grazers cling to the undersides of leaves and exude protective foam. Every cup of water has larvae in it; every hanging fruit has something inside it, eating outward.

Then there are the larger animals, and their scale changes the meaning of the open spaces.

Herds move across the plateau in long, broken lines: heat-adapted browsers with tall shoulders, narrow heads, and flexible lips for stripping the wax leaves from thorned shrubs. Their hides are patterned like shadow and sun. They travel from condensation fields to fruiting groves, guided by scent, memory, and the seasonal thunder of insects. Smaller animals follow them to feed on disturbed insects. Predators follow the smaller animals. Above, gliders pass between the parasol crowns, membrane-winged and loud, croaking as they bank on thermal currents.

Some of the dominant beasts are not easy to classify. There are creatures like walking bundles of reeds, tall and thin, with many jointed limbs and flat tooth-plates for rasping fungus from trunks. There are heavy, low grazers whose backs host gardens of symbiotic moss, so that from a distance they resemble slow-moving islands. There are bright arboreal hunters with four eyes, prehensile tails, and throats that inflate before they scream. There are things in the pools with patient nostrils, waiting for anything warm to lean too close.

The sound is immense.

It is not one sound but a total pressure of sound, as if the plateau has a voice too large to separate into parts. Insects make most of it: continuous saw-drone, glassy ringing, metallic ticking, rasping pulses, shrill mating waves that sweep across the vegetation like wind. Amphibians call from hidden water in rubbery booming notes. Birds—or birdlike animals—shout from the high crowns, some with falling whistles, some with harsh laughter, some mimicking the bark-crack of splitting seed pods. Herds bellow and cough. Branches creak. Leaves clap together in dry gusts. Seed capsules detonate in the heat with sharp reports. Distant rain hisses. Predators scream, and for a few seconds entire regions of the soundscape change shape around them.

Silence is not known here. At night the sound grows denser rather than softer. The day insects recede and another population begins: grinding, pulsing, knocking, clicking things hidden in bark and root. Some trees themselves make noise. Their internal water columns shift after sunset, and they release heat with a slow popping from trunk to crown. Flowering stalks vibrate to attract pollinators, producing bass notes too low to hear clearly but strong enough to feel in the chest. In the root-caves, the living floor hums faintly with the passage of fluids.

There are people here, but they do not dominate the plateau.

Their structures are scattered and small, almost apologetic beside the vegetable architecture. On dry ridges stand huts woven from rib-leaves and cane, raised on short stilts to keep them above rot, insects, and the nightly movements of ground animals. Some are seasonal shelters, abandoned when the pools vanish or when a herd migration makes the region dangerous. Others are little more than platforms lashed between root buttresses, with roofs of overlapping bark scales. Smoke-blackened hearths sit in circles of baked clay. Stones are arranged to keep creeping roots out for a few months at a time.

Paths exist, but they must be remade constantly. A path here is a negotiation, not an incision. People travel along animal trails, dry root ridges, and the shaded undersides of arching vines. They mark routes with knots of pale fiber, carved seed-shells, or strips of reflective beetle wing. Tools are simple: stone blades, bone awls, fire-hardened digging sticks, woven traps, resin glue, cords braided from inner bark. Some groups use hammered copper or meteoric iron for prized edges, but metal is rare and quickly stained green by the living air.

Their craft is mostly botanical. They know which leaves hold water, which vines bleed poison, which flowers summon biting swarms, which seeds can be ground safely only after three boilings. They build with growth as much as with dead material. A family may bend young shoots around a frame and wait seasons for the walls of a shelter to thicken. A bridge may be trained from paired vines rooted on opposite sides of a gully. A shrine may be nothing but a living trunk scarred repeatedly over generations until it grows into the shape of a face, a gate, an animal, a warning.

Remains of older habitation appear sometimes, half-swallowed. A line of stone steps disappearing beneath root-felt. A circle of postholes filled with flowering weeds. Clay jars fused into a termite mound. Carved slabs tilted among fern fans, their markings softened by lichen. No ruin stays bare. The plateau consumes memory gently but relentlessly, covering it with green, then using it as structure.

The rules of the world are rules of heat, growth, thirst, and attention.

Nothing left still remains merely itself. A dropped tool becomes a mineral lick for insects, then a root-anchor, then a node in a vine mat. A carcass becomes a festival, stripped by flies, opened by scavengers, furred with fungus, sprouting seedlings from its ribcage. A clearing is temporary unless defended daily. Shade moves like territory. Water appears and disappears according to patterns too local for maps. Fruitings can redirect whole migrations. A storm ten kilometers away can bring predators here by scent before any rain arrives.

The plants are not passive scenery. Some close around heat. Some react to footfall. Some shed irritating hairs when grazed too heavily. Some produce sweet pods only when particular animals are nearby, timing their ripening to migrations. Certain groves maintain open space around themselves by poisoning the ground beyond their roots, creating circular courts of low vegetation where mating displays, hunts, and human gatherings occur. Other plants depend on trampling and grow best along roads, so that the more a path is used, the more it becomes bordered by thorn, fruit, and noise.

Strangeness accumulates in the details.

There are fields of blade-leaves that all tilt in the same direction before storms, giving the plain a visible shiver hours before the sky changes. There are heat mirages among the green flats where distant parasol trees seem to walk. There are seed pods that float upward when dry, drifting into the lower clouds and raining down elsewhere after absorbing moisture. There are pools whose surfaces are covered by tiny plants that open only at night, revealing blue bioluminescent mouths beneath. There are trees that grow around the skulls of large animals, incorporating them into their trunks as resonating chambers; when wind passes through these groves, they moan in many species’ voices.

In some regions the living mat is thin over caverns. Walking there, one hears hollowness beneath the root-floor and feels warm drafts rising through fissures. Roots descend into those depths in curtains. Animals vanish into them during the hottest part of the day. People say entire under-forests hang below the plateau, inverted and pale, feeding on mineral steam and the corpses that fall through. Whether this is true everywhere or only in certain rifts, the ground often behaves like a membrane rather than earth.

Weather belongs to the plateau itself. By late afternoon, heat rising from the vegetation forms local towers of cloud. Thunderheads gather over fruiting basins where transpiration is strongest. Lightning strikes the high reed-towers, which often survive by conducting the charge through wet inner tissue into the root-web below. After lightning, new growth appears in strange abundance. Burn scars become flower fields. Animals dig in them for roasted tubers and stunned insects. People collect glassy fused beads from the struck soil and wear them as charms against dry seasons.

At dawn the plateau is briefly gentle. Condensation pearls on every surface. The air cools enough that scents separate: minty crushed leaves, wet bark, musk, clay, pollen, distant rot. Mist lies in the low root-corridors, and the tall plants emerge from it like islands. Herds move quietly then. Pollinators hang sluggishly on flowers. Human figures may be seen walking the ridgelines with baskets and long hooks, harvesting dew from folded leaves before the sun takes it back.

By midday gentleness ends. The light becomes ordinary in brightness but merciless in exposure. Shadows shrink. The plant oils gleam. Sound thickens into a wall. Predators sleep with open eyes. Reptile-bodied things bask on dead trunks. The air above the living ground ripples with vapor, pheromones, spores. To breathe is to take the place into the lungs.

Evening brings motion again. Flowers unfold. Calls begin from the pools. Large grazers rise from shade and begin to feed. Hunting parties—animal and human—move along the cooler lanes. Vines relax in the dropping heat, and bridges that were taut all day sag slightly under their own weight. The sky over the plateau turns copper, then violet. Far off, beneath the tableland’s rim, lower clouds flash with storms that never climb high enough to arrive.

The place is not lush in the comforting sense. It is too hungry for that. It is a high hot continent of living material, spacious yet overrun, old yet constantly renewing itself. Its abundance is not fullness but activity: mouths, pores, roots, wings, calls, traps, migrations, evaporations, returns. Water passes through it in brief gifts. Heat drives it. Animals shake it. Plants hold it together.

To cross it is to move across the back of something awake.
