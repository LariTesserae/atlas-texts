At this altitude the world has not yet decided whether it is mountain, machine, jungle, or weather.

It rises in immense tiers—continental shelves lifted into the warm upper air, a highland so broad that its horizons curve under their own climate. Valleys do not lie below so much as between upward thrusts: fresh black ridges, pale glassy scarps, red mineral seams still sharp from whatever convulsion made them. The stone looks young. Its fractures have no patience, no rounded wisdom. Cliffs are split clean as bone breaks. Basalt fins stand in rows like cooling blades. Whole ranges still steam along their joints.

The air is warm, dim, and wet only by interruption. Moisture does not belong everywhere here. It gathers in pockets, in hollows, in the undersides of leaves, in fogs that appear suddenly and vanish before they can become rain. A ravine may be dripping and fern-choked while the plateau above it is dry, hot, and dusted with volcanic grit. In the evening, condensation beads along every vein of grass and every exposed cable-root of the forest, then lifts at dawn in a low silver exhalation. Rain comes as localized events: a curtain falling over one slope and nowhere else, a single dark cloud emptying itself into one amphitheater of stone, droplets forming midair around invisible axes and descending in slow spirals.

There are rivers, but they are unreliable. Some run clear for a day through channels of new rock, then reverse, braid, evaporate into vapor, or disappear through newly opened seams. Others are not water at all except at the edges: warm suspensions of clay, pollen, dissolved salts, and living threads that creep downhill with the deliberation of something thinking. Pools collect in the young terrain like unfinished eyes. They are often green-black, often perfectly still, often ringed by luxuriant growth too vivid for the surrounding dryness.

Vegetation has taken the high country quickly and with an almost indecent confidence. It crowds wherever moisture pauses. There are forests in the clefts, stacked vertically up walls, hanging from ledges in curtains of fibrous leaves. Vines bridge cracks before soil has formed. Mosses lacquer the undersides of overhangs, glowing dull emerald in the dimness. Fern-trees with coppery fronds cluster around steam vents. Thick-leaved shrubs grow out of bare mineral faces, their roots not so much penetrating rock as persuading it to soften.

On the wide plateaus, vegetation comes in dense islands: groves like dark knots, each with its own damp interior. Their canopies are low and interwoven, glossy, warm to the touch, breathing faint vapor at night. Between them stretch fields of tough, blade-like grasses and pale lichen crusts that crackle underfoot. The lushness is not continuous, but wherever it appears it appears in force—layered, crowded, near-touching, full of hidden motion. A dry slope may suddenly contain a sink of green abundance, a bowl dense with leaves, stems, spores, and small animal heat.

Nothing has been built here. There are no roads, no walls, no huts, no ruins, no tools left behind.

And yet the land is industrial.

Not by civilization. By process.

The mountains run engines under their skins. Deep inside them, unseen pistons of pressure drive geysers in measured strokes. Slopes tremble with a subaudible cycling, a heat-pulse that moves through the ground like the turning of enormous buried wheels. In certain valleys, vents cough rhythmically, exhaling black vapor in timed bursts. Mineral belts extrude from cracks in long, continuous ribbons, cooling into ridged tracks that resemble conveyor lines but are wholly geological. At night, faint electric glows crawl along fault seams. Whole escarpments hum with the induction of storms that have not yet formed.

There are factory sounds without factories: the slow clank of cooling stone contracting in sequence; the sigh and intake of vents; the grinding of newly raised strata; the tick of crystals growing in mineral fog. Far off, a slope may collapse with the muffled authority of machinery being switched. Then quiet returns, enormous and uneasy.

The quiet is one of the strangest things about the place. It is not stillness. Everything changes, but much of it changes without noise. Weather forms and dissolves soundlessly in nearby air. Plants turn their leaves in coordinated waves. Small animals move through the foliage in numbers, yet with padded limbs, membranous wings, or bodies too light to disturb the leaves. The whole highland seethes, rearranges, breeds, erodes, erupts, condenses, and consumes itself under a dim blanket of near-silence.

Light arrives weakened. The sky is usually low and dark, not with ordinary clouds but with layered vapor, ash-fine pollen, and broad suspended shadows that may or may not be weather. The sun, when visible, is a hot coin behind gauze. Most colors are seen as if underwater: deep greens, iron reds, bruised violets, sulfur yellows along vents, the wet black of new stone. There are hours when the landscape seems lit from within rather than above—by fungal undersides, mineral seams, ionized mist, the slow pulse of animal markings in the canopy.

Because the highland is vast, it has its own weather systems, and because the world is unstable, those systems are not fully meteorological. Storms hatch from ravines. Fog falls upward out of sinkholes. Warm winds move in circular patterns across basins, herding spores and insects into living spirals. A cloud may drag its shadow across a slope and leave the vegetation on the other side rearranged into unfamiliar species. Lightning sometimes travels through roots rather than air. Rain can strike the ground and bounce back as mist, or stop a few meters above the soil and hang there in vibrating sheets through which birds swim rather than fly.

The fauna is abundant, though much of it resists classification. The obvious animals are everywhere once the eye adjusts: gliders nesting among cliff-ferns; long-legged browsers moving along vertical walls; translucent amphibians in condensation basins; beetle-like grazers that rasp lichen from young basalt; soft-bodied burrowers leaving warm trails through ash. Great herds cross the upland grassfields at dawn, their bodies low and plated, steam rising from their backs. They travel without calls, guided by tremors in the rock.

In the dense ravines, life becomes layered and intimate. Small primates or not-primates hang from vines by all six limbs. Blind fish climb wet stone in films of water. Moths with leaves for wings sleep in stacks along tree trunks. Predators bloom from bark-colored stillness, unfolding too many joints. Some creatures appear to be half plant, rooted for part of their lives, then walking when rain wakes them. Others are like mobile weather: swarms of luminous pinpricks that condense around heat, feed, scatter, recombine.

The animals know the rules better than any visitor could. They pause before stones that have not yet finished becoming stones. They avoid shadows that do not match the objects casting them. They drink only from pools with insects on the surface; a pool too still is not trusted. When the ground hum deepens, burrowers climb trees, winged things crawl under rocks, and the browsing herds turn their blunt heads all at once toward some pressure front beneath the earth.

Reality here is not absent, but under revision.

Distances flex. A ravine seen across a narrow gap may take three days to reach, while a far blue escarpment can arrive suddenly behind a grove. Perspective folds in the dim air. Cliffs repeat themselves with small differences, as though the landscape is testing variations. Certain slopes have more surface than their shape permits. You can walk around a boulder and find its far side hosting an entire moist hollow of ferns, frogs, dripping vines, and a weather system of gnats.

Time has local textures. In some basins, leaves unfurl visibly in minutes and rot before nightfall, feeding soil that was bare stone at dawn. Nearby, a falling drop of water may hang from a root for hours, lengthening but never falling, while animals come to drink from its suspended surface. Fresh lava can cool into forest floor under one storm. A carcass can become a thicket by morning. A thicket can become mineral by noon.

The terrain participates in its own invention. New ridges shoulder up through the vegetation, splitting groves and carrying their roots skyward. Pools deepen without excavation. Hills slump, then recover different outlines. Strata slide over one another like muscles beneath skin. In places, the ground extrudes repeating forms—arches, columns, toothed fins—that look almost architectural, but never settle into intention. They collapse, melt, sprout moss, or turn into animals.

There are zones where the industrial nature of the place becomes unmistakable. Broad plains of dark stone throb with geothermal rhythm. Lines of vents ignite in sequence across kilometers, each releasing steam, metallic vapor, spores, and fine ash in ordered pulses. Around them grow dense belts of heat-loving vegetation, glossy and black-green, fed by mineral fallout. Creatures gather there in tremendous numbers, not chaotically but as if attending a shift change: grazers licking salts, winged swarms rising at each exhalation, burrowers harvesting cooled crust, predators standing knee-deep in vapor.

No human factory could be so quiet. No machine could be so alive.

The smell changes constantly: wet leaves, hot stone, mineral acid, pollen, animal musk, electrical rain, bruised stems, fresh fracture, fungal sweetness, metallic fog. In dry stretches, the dominant scent is dust and resin. In damp pockets, it is loam so new it still remembers being rock. Near vents, it becomes sulfurous and oily. After one of the strange localized rains, the entire highland seems to release a warm green breath, dense and almost edible.

The dimness gives everything a secretive intensity. Leaves shine without brightness. Animals watch from just inside the gloom. The edges of things are sharp when close and uncertain when far away. A cliff may show every fresh fracture line, every mineral glitter, every root hair clinging to it, while the valley beyond is only a moving darkness filled with occasional wingbeats and steam.

The world has no paths, but it has currents. Animals follow them. Seeds follow them. Fog follows them. Loose stones arrange themselves along them. These currents cross the highlands in invisible bands—thermal, magnetic, gravitational, biological, perhaps narrative. Step into one and the landscape seems to accelerate around you: vines lengthening, insects hatching, clouds forming overhead from breath and leaf vapor. Step out, and the same valley becomes almost still.

There are dangers that behave like weather. A silence too complete can precede a reality shear, when a portion of slope briefly forgets its angle and everything on it falls sideways into the sky. A warm mist may cause forms to exchange properties: stone softening, bark ringing like metal, animal shadows rooting into the ground. Some storms do not bring rain but revisions. After they pass, a species may have always had horns; a river may have always flowed uphill; a cliff face may contain fossils of creatures not yet born.

Still, the place is not hostile in any simple way. It is generative. Excessively generative. It makes and unmakes with the unrest of youth. It has the impatience of fresh geology and the fecundity of a greenhouse, lifted into thin warm air beneath a dim, unstable sky. Its violence is not malice but overflow. There is too much pressure, too much fertility, too much unfinished law.

At night—if night is the correct word—the highland deepens into layered black-green shadow. Condensation forms everywhere, bright on leaves and stone. Pools wake with small rings. The buried engines slow but do not stop. Along distant ridges, vents pulse red through fog like banked furnaces. Vast herds move silently over the plateaus, their passage visible only as shifting darkness against darker grass. In ravines, luminous larvae write and erase constellations on wet bark.

Above, the sky lowers until it seems caught on the tallest stone spires. Sometimes there are stars. Sometimes there are multiple moons, or the memory of moons, or holes where moons have recently been removed. Sometimes the upper atmosphere opens and reveals another landscape inverted overhead: cliffs, forests, rivers, all descending toward this one without ever meeting it. Animals glance up but do not panic. They have seen such things.

By morning, some portion of the world will be different. A valley will have sealed. A new one will have opened, wet and full of ferns though it was not there yesterday. A cliff will have grown a mineral face like hammered iron. A grove will have migrated downslope, leaving root-shaped tunnels in the rock. The air will be warm, dim, lightly damp. The silence will hold thousands of bodies and innumerable processes.

And the highland will continue, vast beyond weather, young beyond reason, alive with its own unbuilt industry—an engine of stone and leaf, making itself in the dark.
