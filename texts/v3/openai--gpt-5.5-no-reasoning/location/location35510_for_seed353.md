At this height the world should be thin and bare, all sky and stone, but there is no sky in the ordinary sense, and no stone left uncovered.

The plateau is a country of plant matter built into machinery.

It extends for miles in the dark: a raised tableland or ancient roofscape, lifted above lower regions no one sees anymore. Its edges are not cliffs exactly, but downward walls of fused root, carbonized trellis, old scaffolding, and black-green vascular cable dropping into an airless haze. The surface is nearly flat from great age, planed and settled by eras of heat and abrasion, yet it is not empty. Nothing here is empty. Every space is occupied by growth, by construction, by growth pretending to be construction, by construction that has learned to grow.

There is almost no water.

Not in pools, not in mist, not in dew on leaves. The heat has long since driven liquid away, or locked it so deep into tissues that it no longer behaves like water. The ecology survives on dryness. It drinks salts, vapor metals, furnace exhaust, static, old sunlight stored in mineral films. The plants here are not green in any gentle sense. They are black, rust-red, sulfur-yellow, bruised violet, mirror-silver, and the matte dark of charred bone. Their leaves are plates, vanes, bellows, brittle ribbons, heat sinks. Their trunks are columns of layered lignin and ceramic ash. Their roots do not seek damp soil, because there is no soil; they clamp onto the made substrate and digest it slowly: brick, slag, glass, resin, woven carbon, petrified compost, machine casings, their own dead ancestors.

The ground underfoot is alive but engineered. It has seams. It has rivets grown over and reabsorbed. It flexes faintly, not like earth but like the skin of an old pressure vessel. In places the plateau is surfaced with overlapping scales of dry leaf-metal, each the size of a door, all tilted to vent heat from below. Elsewhere there are mats of hair-fine roots packed so tightly they resemble velvet, though touching them would cut the hand like fiberglass. Some districts are paved with hexagonal seed-pods hardened into tiles. Others are made of compressed stems set upright in bundles, like a butcher-block floor, polished by centuries of hot wind and internal vibration.

The darkness is heavy and almost complete. Light exists only as leakage.

A seam glows orange beneath a root-factory. A fruiting tower pulses once every few minutes with a low red internal flare. Far off, a line of induction coils flickers blue-white and goes dark again. Most things are sensed by heat, by outline, by the interruption of blackness. The plants do not need daylight as plants elsewhere might. Long ago, perhaps, they did. Now their broad receiving surfaces drink infrared from furnaces embedded below the plateau. They feed on chemical gradients and electrical storms trapped within the structure. They turn darkness into tissue by processes that are more industrial than pastoral.

Above, instead of stars, there is a low lid of soot and mineral vapor. It presses close. Sometimes it glows dull maroon from heat lightning contained inside it. Sometimes black clouds of spore-dust climb from vents and flatten against it, spreading like ink under glass. The air is hot enough to feel granular. Breathing it would be like drawing from a kiln lined with bitter herbs, warm resin, alkali, scorched cellulose, metal filings. It is dry beyond thirst. Anything wet brought here would become an event: a violence, a brief blossoming catastrophe, a point around which miles of roots might reorient.

There are no animals.

No insects chew the leaves. No birds nest among the towers. No worms aerate the underlayers. Pollination is mechanical, pneumatic, explosive, contractual. The plants have built their own substitutes: rattling fans that open and shut in the dark; piston-stems that hammer pollen into pressurized ducts; conveyor-vines that carry sealed seed capsules along rails of braided root. Some flowers are sockets. Some are vents. Some are mouths designed only for dust, opening toward one another across narrow alleys when the internal clocks align.

The entire region is a factory-garden, though neither word is sufficient.

There are neighborhoods of vertical growth: dense groves of chimney-cacti, each ribbed column pierced by tiny exhaust pores. They stand shoulder to shoulder, impossible to pass between, their tops lost in the smoky dark. They exhale hot gases in disciplined breaths. Around their bases, fan-ferns made of coppery membrane fold and unfold, not to catch sun but to regulate pressure in the rootworks below. When the chimneys inhale, the ferns flatten. When they exhale, the ferns rise all together, and the grove becomes briefly a field of black hands.

There are avenues, but they are not for walking. They are channels through which heat moves.

Some avenues are roofed by interlocking branch-trusses, grown into arches and reinforced with bands of dark metal. Along their walls hang thousands of dry seed-lanterns, most unlit, a few glowing weakly with amber fermentation. Pipe-vines run overhead in bundles as thick as tree trunks, sweating not water but oils, acids, conductive gels, powdered salts. Where they cross, they are clamped by old collars stamped with symbols eroded beyond language. The collars have been swallowed halfway into bark. Whether people once installed them or the plants grew them in imitation of vanished engineers is no longer clear.

Everything is old.

Not ruined, exactly. Ruin implies abandonment, looseness, collapse. This place has passed through collapse and emerged into a final maintenance of itself. It is ancient the way a reef is ancient, the way a bureaucracy is ancient, the way a scar is ancient: layer over layer, function revised until origin no longer matters. The plateau was once perhaps a city, or a refinery, or a botanical engine raised to harvest heat from a dying world. Now the distinction is gone. Walls photosynthesize chemically. Furnaces flower. Dead branches become load-bearing beams. Control rooms have filled with woody organs that still preserve the shape of consoles and chairs, not as memories but as useful cavities for cooling.

The erosion here is not rain-cut or river-smoothed. It is abrasion by dust, heat fatigue, root pressure, slow chemical gnawing. Corners have rounded. Towers have slumped and been regrown in place. Whole factory blocks have spread sideways as their supports softened, creating low bulging structures like melted cathedrals. Ancient walkways sag between plant-silos, woven through with creepers that tightened over centuries until the bridges became vascular ligaments. Old smokestacks, no longer straight, lean into one another and graft at the top, forming crude black crowns. The landscape has been trying to flatten itself for ages, and yet growth continually thrusts upward again: not young growth, but stubborn pressure, a hot dark metabolism refusing to finish.

There is turbulence without much noise.

The quiet is deceptive. At a distance, the plateau seems hushed: no calls, no wingbeats, no leaves stirred by cool wind. But within the silence are small compulsions. A dry clicking passes under the floor as valves change state. Root-cables tighten with a faint creak. Somewhere a bellows-organ exhales, and dust slides from an arch. A bank of thermal petals snaps shut all at once, sharp as a thousand books closing. Far away, a furnace-throat coughs, then settles. The loudest sounds are rare and local: the crack of a heat-stressed trunk splitting open to reveal a new ceramic pith; the sudden collapse of a scaffold-grove into itself; the rush of spores through a pressure duct like sand poured down a metal chute.

The place is always changing, but not with the liveliness of weather or animals. It changes like a system under strain.

Sections of the plateau surge. A district of root-factories overheats and vents through emergency blossoms the size of houses, their petals unfolding from black slits to expose white-hot interiors. Nearby structures respond by sealing themselves, plates overlapping, membranes thickening. Electrical charge crawls across the undersides of leaves, branching violet in the dark. Spore clouds are launched, caught, redirected through ducts. Some towers are consumed deliberately by neighboring towers, dismantled into fibers and salts. Others rise from below over a period of hours, forcing aside old paving, extruding ribbed stems already wrapped in metal bands as if pre-aged, pre-employed.

Creation and demolition are not separate operations here. They are one motion.

A building may flower into a machine. A machine may dry into a grove. A grove may compact itself into pavement and wait for centuries as a dormant floor. The plateau has rules, but they are not human rules. No unoccupied surface may remain bare for long. Heat must be caught, slowed, eaten, exchanged, vented. Dryness must be preserved; liquid is contamination unless locked immediately into tissue. Dead matter must not decay into softness but be carbonized, mineralized, laminated, made useful. Open space exists only as a conduit for pressure, pollen, dust, and thermal gradients. Shade is not absence but a resource. Darkness is the normal medium of growth.

In the deepest zones the plant architecture becomes almost urban. There are plazas roofed with ribbed membranes, packed with low squat organs like market stalls, each one processing a different mineral feed. There are alleys between wall-trunks so narrow that only dust can pass, their sides polished by the movement of hot air. There are spiral ramps of woody laminate leading to platforms where enormous disk-leaves rotate slowly on creaking stems, tracking not sun but subterranean furnace pulses. There are towers filled with chambers, each chamber a dry womb of seeds sorted by density, charge, hardness, memory.

Memory here is physical. Rings in the trunks record heat regimes from ages before the present darkness. Fossilized vines preserve the layout of obsolete roads. Root knots grow around buried instruments and continue to feed them, though the readings are no longer read. Some walls bear repeated patterns like written script: rows of thorn-stubs, blister marks, pigment scars, conductive veins. Perhaps these are instructions exchanged among plant-machines. Perhaps they are merely stress patterns. In such a place there is little difference between language and growth.

The heat is constant, but not uniform. It comes upward in moods.

There are warm plains where the surface breathes steadily, each tile expanding and contracting in time with buried engines. There are furnace fields where even the plants are armored against their own nourishment, their outer bark white and crazed like fired clay. There are cooler hollows beneath overgrown trusses, where dry spores settle in deep drifts and the darkness feels soft. Cool, here, means only that the air does not immediately shimmer. Nothing is comfortable. Comfort would be a sign of waste.

Because the plateau is high, the lower atmosphere thins around it, yet the density of life and construction makes it feel enclosed. The horizon is seldom visible. Growth blocks it. Chimneys crowd it. Braided pipes cross it. Where a long view opens, it reveals not emptiness but more of the same elaborate saturation: black groves, rust-red roofskins, dim furnace cracks, ranks of heat-vanes tilting and retilting, all disappearing into the soot-dark distance. The place is open in extent, but claustrophobic in texture. One can imagine walking for days without leaving the built vegetal mass, never finding soil, never finding water, never finding a truly natural object.

If there was an original landscape beneath, it has been eaten.

Maybe the plateau was a mountain cut down and capped. Maybe it was a city raised on pillars. Maybe it was the exposed back of some planetary machine. Whatever lay below has become feedstock and foundation. The entire visible world is an artifact, yet it has grown beyond design. Its industrial parts are not additions to nature; they are the bones nature chose here. Pumps are woody. Bark is conductive. Flowers regulate combustion. Seeds are cast in molds. Roots sort ore. Leaves serve as radiators and solar memories and pressure valves. The distinction between botany and manufacturing has eroded as completely as the old edges of the plateau.

In certain districts, tall frames stand over the growth: gantries made from braided trunks and blackened steel, moving slowly along rails of root. They do not seem autonomous, nor controlled. They are part of the ecology, ambulatory trellises that relocate shade and tension. Their joints are packed with dry mosslike tissue that secretes lubricant. Hanging from them are hooks, pods, cable-vines, desiccated sacs of pollen. When a gantry passes, the surrounding plants change posture, leaning away or offering branches to be clipped, grafted, tensioned. The movement is nearly silent, just a low granular grind and the occasional ping of cooling metal.

Storms occur without rain.

A storm begins as a pressure wrongness beneath the floor. The plants know first. Heat-vanes fold. Seed vaults seal. Long whip-vines draw themselves taut between towers, making the whole district into a tense instrument. Then the dark overhead flickers with buried lightning, and a hot wind rises from vents in the plateau. It carries mineral dust, carbon powder, fragments of leaf, charged pollen, metallic spores. The air becomes thick, abrasive, alive with non-animal intention. Structures blur. Edges vanish. The plateau rearranges itself under cover of storm: roots drill, panels molt, towers shed outer skins, furnaces disgorge glowing slag into waiting cups of bark. No rain falls. Nothing is washed clean. Instead the storm deposits another layer of usable dryness over everything.

Afterward the silence returns, but details have changed. A wall has become a hedge of black needles. A hedge has stiffened into a wall. An avenue is closed by a new knot of pipe-root. A tower has split into three. A field of thermal petals has been burned down to stumps, and already the stumps are sprouting metal-edged fans. The plateau never rests in the sense of stopping. It rests by cycling through states too old to be called new.

There is a smell like heated dust inside a sealed machine, mixed with resin, spice, and something medicinally bitter. There are no rot smells, no loam, no damp leaf mold. Death does not soften here. It hardens. Fallen material becomes charcoal lace, then reinforcement. Failed organs become insulation. Old flowers close around their own ash and form nodules that will one day be bearings, seeds, teeth.

At the margins of some plazas stand what appear to be habitations. Doorways, galleries, stacked chambers, balconies overgrown with dense botanical apparatus. But no animal body could comfortably inhabit them now. The doors are too hot, too narrow, or filled with breathing fibers. The galleries carry sap-cables instead of people. Window openings serve as exhaust slots or spore apertures. If inhabitants made this place, they are gone, translated into maintenance protocols, into nutrient layers, into the inherited habits of vines trained long after the trainers vanished.

Yet the plateau does not feel dead.

It feels overalive in a direction life elsewhere rarely takes. Life without softness. Vegetation without green shade. Fertility without water. Industry without workers. Architecture without vacancy. It is a kingdom of dry abundance, a packed and restless biome of constructed plants, high in the dark heat of an old world.

To stand within it is to feel surrounded not by wilderness and not by city, but by an answer to a question no one remembers asking: how much of a world can become plant, if plant is allowed to become factory, wall, law, atmosphere, and ground? Here the answer extends beyond sight, clicking and venting softly in the darkness, remaking itself from heat and dust, ancient enough to have forgotten all beginnings and turbulent enough never to end.
