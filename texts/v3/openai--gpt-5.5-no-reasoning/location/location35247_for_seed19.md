At that height the world has almost no weather left.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense, though mountains may have been its ancestors. It is a country of summits worn so old they have forgotten uplift: a high, broad tableland lifted above the lower air, planed by ages into terraces, rims, shallow basins, and long mineral flats. The horizons are far away and slightly wrong. They do not curve or vanish so much as repeat, each distance laying itself over the next like thin sheets of translucent stone.

There is no water.

Not no rain, not merely no rivers: no water in the idea of the place. The rocks are not damp under their crusts. The dust does not clump. The air holds no mist, no frost, no promise of weather. Hollows that would be lakes elsewhere are paved with salt-glass and gray alkali plates, but the salts are not the residue of vanished seas; they seem to have formed directly from dryness, precipitated out of absence. Every basin is a white or bone-yellow lid. Every crack is powdery to the bottom. If you break a stone, the interior is as dry as the face it showed to the sky.

The temperature is cool, steadily so. Not cold enough to bite. Not warm enough to soften anything. A standing, mineral coolness inhabits the plateau like cellar air without a cellar. Sunlight falls normally, even generously, but it does not accumulate. Shadows are clear and blue-edged, yet unthreatening. The day has the measured illumination of late morning prolonged indefinitely.

Nothing here seems to be happening.

Dust hangs, but does not drift. Loose pebbles rest on slopes steeper than they should tolerate. Tall flakes of shale lean from eroded banks, thin as door leaves, and have leaned that way for so long that small animals have built whole ancestral routes beneath their overhangs. In places, the ground is scored by gullies, fans, braided channels, ripple marks, and plunge pools — all the grammar of vanished flow — but none of it is recent. These are not features made by a former climate so much as carvings inherited from an earlier version of physical law. Erosion has gone as far as it can go without erasing itself. The land is ancient enough that decay has become architecture.

The dominant material is pale stone: ash-colored limestone, chalky volcanic tuff, greenish clay-slate, glassy crusts of evaporite, iron-black nodules, wind-polished hornfels protruding through sheets of dust. Because there is no wind, the polish is an old polish. Because there is no rain, the dust is an archive rather than a weathering product. It lies in shallow drifts, in seams, in hoof-shaped depressions, in the leeward side of rocks though there has been no leeward side for uncountable time.

The plateau is open, but not empty. It extends in many directions as a system of broad flats and compressed labyrinths. From a height one can see pale shelves stepping away, each shelf crowded with countless low objects: stones, bones, burrows, huts, pillars, eggs, carcass-shells, mineral shrubs, animal congregations, broken walls. The density is strange because the scale is large. There is room for a kingdom here, yet nothing is truly solitary. Everything stands near something else. Even the emptiness has fixtures.

Vegetation exists, but only as a marginal practice.

There are thorn mats in the cracks, black-green and close to the ground, more like woven wire than plants. Their leaves are small, folded, and dust-furred. They do not rustle. Some grow in circles around old animal droppings, not feeding on moisture but on salts and the faint heat stored by decay. There are pale stalks too, jointed and brittle, rising knee-high in protected basins. They have no blossoms, only little ceramic-looking cups that open toward the sun and close at the exact same angle every day. A few cliff faces carry hanging veils of dry lichen, silver on one side and soot-purple beneath. These appear dead until touched; then they tighten, slowly, over the course of hours.

The plants are sparse, but the fauna is overwhelming.

At first the animals are difficult to perceive because none of them hurry. The silence teaches the eye to mistake them for stones. Then the whole landscape begins to populate itself.

On the salt flats stand herds of tall, narrow ungulates with split hooves wide enough to distribute weight over brittle crust. Their bodies are ribbed like carved wood, their hides the color of old parchment. They do not graze in the ordinary way. They press their muzzles to mineral plates and rasp at them with wide lower teeth, harvesting films of lichen, salt, and something invisible. They stand so still that dust gathers along their spines. Calves hide inside the shadows of their mothers’ legs.

Among them move smaller creatures, thousands of them: bead-eyed runners with long tails held perfectly horizontal; flat-backed beetle-mammals armored in overlapping plates; pale lizards with translucent throats; blind, velvet-skinned diggers that surface only at the edges of shade. There are colonies of paper-thin insects packed into the cracks of boulders. They unfold at certain angles of light, not flying, but spreading like flakes of mica, then folding back into invisibility.

Birds are everywhere, though the air is quiet. Some perch in ranks along the eroded ledges, their heads all turned the same direction. Others hang almost motionless above the cliffs with wings locked open, riding updrafts so weak they may not exist. Their calls are rare and tiny: a single click, a dry scrape, a descending note like a nail drawn lightly across bone. The absence of louder sound makes every small noise seem deliberate.

There are predators, but they do not disturb the equilibrium. Long-bodied cats, if they are cats, sleep in the doorways of abandoned shelters. Their fur is ash-gray and patterned with the cracks of dried clay. They hunt by waiting beside paths that prey animals have used for generations. Sometimes they do not pounce until days after taking position. There are also tall mantid things, jointed and patient, whose forelimbs are edged with stone-like growth. They stand among the thorn mats, indistinguishable from dead stalks except for the occasional slow adjustment of a triangular head.

The animal abundance should make the place noisy, restless, fouled, alive in the usual unstable way. It does not. Life here has settled into mineral time. Herds shift by imperceptible increments. Burrow mouths remain open like punctuation. Wings open, close, open. Dung dries before it has an odor. Bodies die standing up and are gradually occupied by other bodies: insects in the ribs, lichen on the hide, nesting birds in the skull. A carcass becomes a cairn without passing through rot.

The silence is not absolute, but it is near enough that one begins to hear structure. A claw ticks on salt crust. A pebble settles after perhaps a century of indecision. Far away, one of the tall animals exhales through a bony nose, and the sound is like dust drawn through a reed. Wings make paper sounds. Teeth scrape minerals. A hut rope creaks once, though there is no wind.

The scattered built things are old but not grand. They are made by hands, not machines: rings of stacked stone, low walls, roofless cells, cairns with holes deliberately left in them, walkways paved with flat shale, ladders of bone pegged into cliff steps, marker poles wrapped in faded fiber. The technology implied is simple and intimate. Stone blades. Scrapers. Awls. Fired clay bowls. Rope made from thorn fiber and sinew. Hide screens stretched across frames. Nothing suggests engines, wires, wheels beyond rough sledges, or any mastery over the land beyond patient accommodation.

Many structures seem less like dwellings than agreements with animals. A hut may have one entrance for people and five for burrowers. A wall may guide herds around a fragile salt basin. Stone towers, no taller than a person, are perforated with niches for nesting birds. In certain saddles between ridges, fences of rib bones and shale slabs narrow into passages polished by countless flanks. The builders — whoever they are or were — did not separate themselves from the fauna so much as negotiate lanes through its overwhelming presence.

Some shelters are still used, though by whom is unclear. Their interiors contain mats, ashless hearths, storage jars sealed with clay, racks of worked bone, stone palettes stained with ocher, black salt, and powdered green mineral. There are handprints on the walls, but they are not decorative. They mark height, reach, permission, memory. Some handprints have too many fingers. Some are printed from the other side of the wall, visible as if the stone had accepted pressure from within itself.

This is where the place begins to break.

Reality here is not chaotic. It is too stable for chaos. Its wrongness lies in fixity that includes contradictions without resolving them.

A trail may be walked for an hour and lead to the ridge from which it was first seen, not by turning in a circle but by persuading distance to repeat. A basin viewed from above is empty, but when entered, it contains a herd, seven cairns, and the shadow of a tower that has no tower to cast it. Bones embedded in stone still articulate faintly when animals pass near them. Some cliffs show fossils of creatures currently walking below, but in postures they have not yet taken. If one chips a fossil free, the living animal it resembles pauses, shudders, and continues, now missing nothing visible.

There are places where depth is unreliable. Cracks in the ground as narrow as a handspan contain visible ledges, nests, paths, and moving herds far below, lit by the same sun at the same angle. Drop a pebble and it comes to rest on a ledge two inches down, though the eye continues falling into miles. Elsewhere, shallow depressions must be crossed with ropes because they are deeper than falling permits. The locals, if locals remain, mark these with forked stakes and strings of vertebrae.

The sky is normal in color, a dry blue fading paler at the horizon, but it too participates in the plateau’s instability. Birds sometimes perch on it. Not metaphorically: small black birds stand upside down against the blue as if on glass, pecking at specks too high to see. Shadows occasionally precede their objects by several minutes. At noon, certain stones cast shadows of creatures extinct or imaginary: antlered serpents, many-legged people, huge grazing shells. These shadows do not move until the light changes, and perhaps not then.

Despite all this, nothing develops. The breakdown is permanent and fully integrated. No event begins from it. No catastrophe approaches. The world has already suffered whatever transformation would have made it strange, and then endured long enough to become calm again.

The rules are not absent; they are local, old, and exact.

Do not step on the pale hexagonal crusts unless an animal has already crossed them today.

Do not answer a sound made twice in a row.

If a cairn has an opening through it, look through before passing on either side; if you see yourself looking back, wait until the image leaves.

Never move a bone that casts a shadow longer than itself.

Do not sleep in a structure whose doorway faces the same direction as all nearby birds.

Follow animal paths across the open flats, but human paths along cliff edges, because animals remember safe surfaces and humans remember safe distances.

If a plant opens while in shade, leave the basin.

These rules are not superstitions. They are fieldcraft, accumulated by beings who survived by noticing which impossibilities were harmless and which were hungry.

There are villages, perhaps, but they are sparse and half-merged with the terrain. A cluster of fifteen low stone rooms may occupy a ledge overlooking three dry basins. Their roofs are flat slabs sealed with clay. Their chimneys are not for smoke but for light; shafts angled to admit sun at particular hours into storage chambers where pale fungi, if fungi they are, grow on mineral cakes. The inhabitants would live quietly, speak little, and move with the same economy as the animals. Their tools would be worn smooth from generations of repair. They would count time not by seasons, which do not exist here, but by molt cycles, shadow alignments, the slow cracking of salt plates, the return of particular birds to particular holes.

Yet many settlements appear abandoned. Doorways are blocked from the inside with stones. Courtyards are full of animal tracks undisturbed by human feet. Clay vessels sit in rows beneath ledges, all empty, all facing outward. In one place a whole line of huts has been neatly bisected by a cliff face that seems always to have been there. The front halves open to the plateau; the back halves continue as painted interiors visible inside the rock, complete with tools, sleeping mats, and silhouettes of seated figures rendered not in pigment but in absence.

The animals make use of all of it. Lizards sun themselves on altars. Ungulates scratch their necks against doorposts. Birds nest in eye sockets of carved masks. Burrowers undermine thresholds and create cool chambers beneath floors. Nothing is sacred in the sense of being untouched. Everything sacred is touched continuously, incorporated into habit.

The air smells faintly of chalk, hide, old feathers, salt, and sun-warmed stone, though the sun gives little warmth. There is a dry metallic tang near the black nodules. Around the thorn mats there is a bitter resin odor, very slight. In deep cracks, a cold smell rises that is not damp but resembles the memory of dampness translated into mineral terms.

Textures dominate. Powder over plate. Smooth bone against granular stone. Fibrous plant spines that catch skin without bending. Salt crusts brittle as fired clay. Animal hides dusty, warm only at the surface. Feathers that feel like shaved mica. Walls built from flat stones without mortar, each stone placed by hand and held by the obedience of weight.

The whole region gives the impression of a final arrangement. Not dead, because it is crowded with life. Not thriving, because thriving implies increase. It is complete. Every niche is occupied; every path is known; every impossible behavior has been folded into routine. Predators wait. Grazers rasp. Birds balance on the sky. Plants tighten around their hidden salts. Villages persist or do not. Fossils remember forward. Shadows practice independence. The cool light remains.

If you stand on one of the higher rims and look outward, you see the plateau descending in vast shallow steps toward an indistinct perimeter. There are thousands of animals visible, perhaps tens of thousands, though none seem to move unless watched for a long time. Stone shelters prick the ridges like broken teeth. White basins shine without water. Dry gullies braid through the flats, leading nowhere, perfectly preserved. Above, the blue sky bears a few perched birds and the pale, motionless scars of clouds that never form.

The silence is so deep it feels inhabited.

And the land, for all its age and wrongness, does not threaten to change. It has arrived at the condition after ending: not ruin, not renewal, but equilibrium. An enormous dry high country where life has learned to be as patient as geology, and geology has learned to tolerate life crawling over it forever.
