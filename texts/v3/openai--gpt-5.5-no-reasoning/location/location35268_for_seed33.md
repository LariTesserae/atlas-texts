At the top of the world, where the air has become a thin mineral and sound has almost forgotten how to travel, there is a city with no builders.

It stands across a high plateau, though *plateau* is too simple a word for it. The land is lifted almost beyond land: an immense upthrust table of pale stone, salt-black glass, pumice-gray ridges, and dry basins filled not with water but with the memory-shape of water. Everything here has the posture of something exposed for ages. Cliffs have been combed by centuries of wind until their faces are fluted like bone. Towers of rock lean in the same direction, not because they are falling, but because the whole country has learned the pressure of one persistent, invisible hand.

The horizon is vast and close at once. Vast because the country opens for leagues — basins, ridgelines, sheer drops, dead avenues, broken courts, terraces stepped down into blue shadow. Close because the sky hangs low and dim, a pressed lid of violet ash and silver dust. There is no sun visible. Light seeps from everywhere weakly, as if the day is not shining upon the place but leaking through flaws in it.

No rivers run here. No snowmelt trickles. No moss darkens the stone. Even frost is uncommon, appearing only as faint metallic whiskers inside cracks before sublimating into nothing. The dryness is so complete that old surfaces do not rot, dissolve, or soften. They persist. A fallen wall remains a fallen wall for millennia. A track remains a track even if nothing has passed along it since before the current shape of the sky.

The city occupies the plateau’s middle distances.

At first it resembles ruins: roofless districts, colonnades, plazas, narrow high streets cut between windowless facades. But if one watches long enough, the idea of ruins becomes uncertain. The walls are not made of blocks, not exactly. They are strata of native stone raised into architectural intentions — basalt ribs, saltstone planes, glassy seams, carbonate arches grown or folded upward out of the plateau itself. The structures look weathered, inhabited by time, but not broken. They have settled into their proper age.

Some streets run level for impossible lengths, crossing ravines on natural causeways so thin they should have snapped. Others climb stairways too large for any human foot and too shallow for any animal gait. There are plazas the size of dry lakebeds, their floors scored with circular inscriptions that are not carved but revealed — darker mineral veins exposed by erosion in rings, spirals, intersecting arcs. From above, perhaps, these plazas would resemble astronomical diagrams. From within them, they feel more like mouths that chose not to speak.

There are no tools here. No machines, no lamps, no cables, no windows glazed by hand, no hinges, wheels, pipes, levers, furnaces, wells, drains, locks, signs, or doors in the ordinary sense. Nothing suggests manufacture. And yet the place is undeniably urban. It has districts, thresholds, processional slopes, boundaries, dead-end alleys, central spaces, outer walls, high overlooks, enclosed hollows, and broad avenues arranged with a civic sternness.

It is a city as geology might dream it: a settlement without technology, a plan without planners.

The materials are few and severe. Wind-polished basalt, chalky salt-crust, dull obsidian, porous gray stone, ocher dust, occasional plates of mica that catch the dimness and return it as a sickly internal glow. In places the ground is covered by hexagonal crusts, each plate curled slightly at the edges like old parchment. They do not crunch underfoot; they give off a faint powdery sigh. In other places the stone is smooth and black, flowing in frozen folds from ancient eruptions, though no volcano burns here now. The heat has left. The place is cool, and the cold gathers especially in the shade of the city’s own forms.

The air tastes alkaline, dry enough to sting. Breath seems indecently wet.

There is almost no vegetation. On the lee sides of certain walls, in hairline seams where dust gathers, there are colonies of something not quite lichen and not quite mineral. They appear as flat tarnishes: blue-gray, rust-red, sulfur-yellow, spreading in maplike stains no thicker than breath on glass. They do not seem alive until twilight, when their edges rearrange by fractions, opening minute pores to the dim sky. They feed, perhaps, on light. Or charge. Or difference. They are the closest thing to fields in this city: silent, planar ecologies clinging to stone.

In the deepest courtyards grow pale filaments like brittle wool, but they are not roots, and they hold no moisture. They tremble when shadows pass over them. Some shed a powder that rises instead of falling. Some retract into the cracks with the patience of geological thought.

Fauna is rare enough to feel ceremonial.

Now and then a high, jointed shape crosses a distant bridge: thin legs, angular body, the color of shadowed gypsum. It moves slowly, pausing for long intervals as if listening to the stone. Its head, if it has one, is a faceted nub tucked beneath a carapace. It leaves no print. These creatures may be animals, or mobile fragments of the city’s own mineral intention. They graze on the tarnished colonies along the walls, rasping without sound. Their mouths produce small fans of dust.

There are also things in the air, though never many. Dry gliders, translucent as shed skin, ride the updrafts from the cliffs. They have no wings so much as membranes held between branching mineral spars. They drift over avenues and vanish into bright cracks in the sky. When they pass close overhead, one hears the faintest tensioning, like silk pulled across a frame.

Predators, if that word applies, are mostly absences. An animal pauses. A patch of shadow lengthens incorrectly. The animal is no longer there. Nothing rushes. Nothing cries out. The world consumes quietly.

The silence is immense, but not empty. At this elevation, in this thin air, even collapse becomes modest. Pebbles tick down a slope with small, distant clicks. Dust moves across plazas in low sheets, whispering against edges of stone. Somewhere far below the plateau, unseen wind travels through cliff hollows and produces a tone so low it is felt in the teeth more than heard. Sometimes a whole avenue hums for several minutes, not loudly, but with a dry resonance, as if the city has been struck very gently by a finger the size of a mountain.

Weather comes without water.

There are dust tides, arriving with no visible stormfront. The air thickens. The weak light dims further. Fine pale material rises from basin floors and streams uphill against gravity, following the avenues, filling stairways from the bottom upward, pouring over ledges like reversed sandfalls. It collects in plazas, arranges itself into ridges and fans, then drains away through cracks that were not there before and are not afterward.

There are pressure nights, when the sky lowers until the tallest spires disappear into it. During these nights, distances change. A street walked straight may return to its own beginning. Towers seen from a plaza stand first at the horizon, then just behind the shoulder, then beneath the feet as reflections in stone that has never been polished. The city does not move exactly. Rather, the rules by which “near” and “far” are decided become negotiable.

Reality is weakest in the intersections.

Where four avenues meet, there are often vertical sheets of darkness thinner than cloth, hanging in open air. They do not cast shadows. They show, from certain angles, other parts of the plateau: a staircase plunging into stars, a district lit by a red sun that does not exist here, the same plaza buried under a thousand feet of salt, the observer approaching from the opposite direction a moment too late. Dust passes through these sheets and emerges altered — coarser, blacker, sometimes falling upward in slow beads.

Some buildings contain rooms larger than their exteriors. Others contain wind though their entrances are sealed by continuous stone. Some facades have windows opening not inward, but onto the underside of the plateau, where cliffs hang above an empty lavender abyss. There are archways that refuse certain bodies. A glider may pass through one and continue. A dust tide will divide around it. A rare grazing creature may enter and become, for several days, a pattern of stains on the wall before stepping out again, thinner and older.

The city’s geometry is not chaotic. It has laws, but they are local laws, territorial and moody. In one district, vertical surfaces are easier to walk along than horizontal ones; dust rests on walls in deep drifts while floors remain bare. In another, echoes arrive before sounds. In another, all shadows point toward a single sunken courtyard regardless of the light. There is a quarter where nothing can fall completely: broken flakes of stone descend halfway, slow, stop, and remain suspended for years in loose vertical fields, forming hazy curtains of debris. Creatures thread through them carefully, grazing on the mineral film that grows on the underside of each floating shard.

This instability has not destroyed the place. It is the condition to which the place has adapted.

Erosion here is old, established, almost administrative. The wind has learned the street plan. It does not scour randomly. It works routes, polishes corners, hollows lintels, keeps certain passages clear while allowing others to fill. Dust gathers in civic patterns. Salt migrates up walls in branching diagrams that resemble frozen lightning or root systems from a world where roots never evolved. Expansion cracks open each cold night and close each dim day, slowly breathing the city wider and narrower. Over ages, this breathing has rounded thresholds, thinned towers, deepened basins. The city is being worn away, but with such consistency that the wearing has become a form of maintenance.

At the edges of the plateau the land breaks into terraces and voids. Great cliffs drop away into blue-gray depth where nothing can be heard. Across the chasms stand isolated buttes, each crowned with outlying structures: solitary gates, amphitheaters with no seats, walls encircling empty summits. Natural bridges connect some of them, high and narrow, their surfaces grooved by wind and by the passage of rare, patient creatures. Others are unreachable except during certain atmospheric alignments, when distance folds and a far terrace arrives alongside a near one with a soft adjustment of perspective.

Below, there may be a desert. Or a cloud-sea. Or nothing. The plateau is high enough that the lower world has lost authority.

The atmosphere changes over long cycles. In the dim morning — if morning exists here — the stone is lavender and ash-blue. The city seems shallow then, almost comprehensible, its avenues cleanly laid out, its towers sober, its plazas empty. By middle light the minerals begin to answer one another. Mica seams glint. Salt plates bloom white. Black glass warms to a muted sheen. The air grows restless, though not warmer by much, and dust begins its slow migrations. By dusk the architecture deepens. Alleys become longer than geography. Courtyards appear where walls had been continuous. The tarnish-colonies open their pores and drink the fading light. Gliders descend. The grazing mineral beasts emerge from under colonnades and move through the silent streets like careful thoughts.

Night is not dark. It is dimmer than day, but also less stable. The sky fills with faint, motionless stars arranged in grids, spirals, and broken rings that echo the plaza-veins below. Some stars are visible through stone. Some shine upward out of cracks. At times the entire city seems suspended between two firmaments, one above and one beneath, both equally remote and equally dry.

There are no flames. No water. No green. No voices.

Yet the place is active everywhere.

It grinds, breathes, stains, folds, reveals, conceals. Dust travels like a population. Shadows hunt. Thin organisms open and close along mineral seams. Rare animals cross impossible distances in slow migrations from one wall-colony to another. Cliffs shed flakes. Salt grows. Stone hums. Streets exchange destinations. Old plazas rotate their meanings under the feet. The city continually revises itself without hurry, as if time here is not a river but a pressure field, moving through matter from all sides.

To enter such a place is to feel one’s own body become unlikely: too wet, too loud, too warm, too dependent on ordinary directions. A cough would seem violent. A footprint, if it remained, would be an act of vandalism. Even thought feels heavy here, clumsy with habit. The landscape does not oppose life, exactly. It simply belongs to older, drier principles.

It is a city at the summit of a nearly empty world, worn into permanence by wind and contradiction.

A settlement of stone without citizens.

A desert with streets.

A ruin of something that may never have been built, still functioning according to laws that are not dead, only inhuman.
