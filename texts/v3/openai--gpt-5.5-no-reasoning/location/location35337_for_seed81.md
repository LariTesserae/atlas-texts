The city is high enough that the air should be thin and cold, but it is not. It sits on a black plateau above lower cloud, in a belt of continual heat rising from the stone itself. The heat does not come only from sun, because the sun is seldom visible. It comes through the pavement, through the walls, through the ribs of ironwork laid under streets, through the warm wet breath of fissures that open between districts. The whole place feels built on the back of something feverish.

From a distance it is less like a city than a dark shelving reef, tiered across the shoulders of a mountain. Long, low industrial blocks step down from upper terraces to broad basin-streets. Chimneys stand everywhere, but most do not smoke. Some exhale only a dim vapor, yellow at the base and gray as it rises. Others weep hot condensation down their outer skins, so that mineral water runs in pale stripes over brick, slag-stone, and ceramic tile. There are towers too, but few tall ones; the altitude and the old winds have made the builders cautious. Structures squat and brace themselves, tied together by galleries, pipes, overhead rails, pressure-ducts, and cable bridges that sag between roofs like slack tendons.

It is a wet place without softness.

Water lies near the surface of everything. It beads on cast metal, trembles in the seams between paving slabs, collects in wheel-ruts and inspection channels. The streets are not flooded, exactly, but they are never dry. A person walking there would hear the faint tack and release of soles from warm mineral film, the occasional drip from cornices, the low gurgle of buried drains breathing steam. Rain must come often, though perhaps not from clouds in the normal way. Moisture condenses out of the dark air whenever the temperature shifts. Walls sweat before storms that have not yet formed. Grates exhale vapor that becomes droplets on nearby rails and then falls back again, as if the city is distilling itself endlessly.

There are no trees. No weeds split the paving. No moss softens the stone, though many surfaces are green, blue, rust-red, or white from mineral bloom. The few things that might first be mistaken for vegetation are not plants. Along some gutters grow brittle shelves of precipitate, branching like coral or antler, formed where hot alkaline water meets the cooler run-off from cisterns. They are sharp enough to cut cloth. In shaded underpasses, pale films skin the brick in soft-looking patches, fungal or bacterial perhaps, but even these seem uncertainly alive, more stain than organism. They pulse faintly when touched by warm vapor, tightening and loosening with changes in humidity.

The city has the exhausted permanence of an old industrial place that has not been abandoned, only emptied.

Everything is arranged for use. Rail spurs curve between factories. Loading courts open behind shuttered warehouses. Aqueducts and vapor-lines cross the avenues. Reservoir tanks sit on high platforms, riveted and banded, their sides dented by old pressure changes. Channels run along almost every road, carrying hot water, cooling water, waste water, rainwater, and stranger fluids kept apart by tile dividers and iron sluice plates. The infrastructure is dense, deliberate, and long maintained. Yet there are almost no inhabitants visible. Blocks pass without a figure in a window. Whole districts have the silence of a place waiting for a shift change that will not come.

The emptiness is not ruin. Doors are intact. Warning lamps still glow dimly above sealed portals. Gauges tick in wall-niches. Somewhere behind thick masonry, engines continue their slow turns. This may be why the city persists: not because people bustle through it, but because systems do. Pumps lift water from deep sumps. Condensers harvest the wet air. Foundry-hearts cycle at low heat. Pressure bells flex. Counterweights descend by imperceptible degrees. The city is active in the way a sleeping animal is active: circulating, regulating, twitching in response to internal weather.

The light is poor. Day comes as a diffuse gray-brown brightness, filtered through steam, cloud, and suspended mineral dust. Shadows are wide and weak. At noon the plateau seems lit from underneath more than above, the wet ground reflecting a dull copper glow from thermal vents and furnace trenches. At night, if there is a night, the city becomes a map of small industrial stars: green pilot flames behind grates, red inspection bulbs in tunnels, sodium glows inside fog, the occasional white flash from an arc-station where automatic contacts meet.

The architecture has weathered into its own geology. Brick has slumped, not from collapse, but from long heat; its edges are rounded like river stones. Concrete is lacquered with successive skins of mineral deposit. Metal plates are furred with oxide, scraped clean only where machinery still moves against them. Roofs bow inward to guide condensation to cistern mouths. Stairs are grooved by generations of wet boots or metal treads. The city is old enough that erosion has stopped looking like damage and started looking like design. Every drip has found a path. Every stain has a source. Every leak has either been repaired or accepted into the larger system.

The streets are broad, because they were made for hauling, but the air above them is nearly vacant. No market noise, no calling, no carts except for the occasional automated carrier moving slowly along a recessed rail. These carriers are low and blunt, built of black iron and ceramic shielding. They emerge from one gate, cross an open yard with solemn purpose, and vanish into another. Their wheels make a soft wet grinding sound. Sometimes they carry ore or sealed cylinders. Sometimes they carry nothing, as if fulfilling a schedule older than necessity.

Sound travels strangely here. The city is mostly quiet, but never silent. Distance is muffled by vapor. Near noises come close and intimate: the drop of water from a pipe flange, the click of cooling metal, the whisper of steam passing through a cracked valve. Far below, beneath the paving, engines thud at long intervals, not rhythmic enough to become comforting. In some streets there is a faint tonal hum from electrical lines, more felt in teeth and fingertips than heard. Occasionally a pressure release opens somewhere unseen, and a white roar fills several blocks, then closes off so abruptly that the remaining quiet feels physical.

The terrain beneath the city remains visible wherever construction thins. The plateau is made of dark volcanic stone, dense and glassy in places, porous in others. It breaks into terraces naturally, with escarpments that the builders incorporated rather than removed. Roads switchback down between retaining walls. Rail-lines enter cliffs through oval mouths. At the edge of the city, the ground falls away into ravines full of vapor, where hot springs strike cold high air and make permanent cloud. There are marshes too, though not green ones: shallow basins of warm water spread across flats of ash and polished basalt. Their surfaces are filmed with iridescent minerals. Bubbles rise through them slowly, each one swelling, trembling, and bursting with a soft plop that releases the smell of sulfur, salt, and hot metal.

The surrounding region extends in dim bands: upper city, refinery terraces, wet slag-fields, vapor marsh, cliff-edge, lower cloud. Everything is open but sparse. Buildings stand far apart in some districts, separated by yards of paved emptiness or by basins of steaming water. A warehouse may sit alone on a platform large enough for crowds, though no crowd gathers. A crane may stretch its arm over nothing. An avenue may continue for half a mile with only three lamps and one pressure station along it. The emptiness makes each object emphatic. A valve tower becomes a monument. A single ladder on a blank wall becomes an event.

There is fauna, but little of it, and it avoids easy classification. Now and then something moves in the condensation channels: a flat translucent body slipping under warm water, visible only by the displacement of mineral scum. At dusk, small heat-loving creatures cling to the undersides of pipes, jointed and soft-shelled, feeding on the pale films that grow where vapor accumulates. They scatter from vibration rather than light. In the marsh basins beyond the refineries, tall, thin waders are sometimes seen—if they are animals at all. They stand motionless in steaming shallows on articulated legs, their bodies wrapped in dull gray membrane, dipping narrow heads into the water with mechanical patience. They may be birds, machines, or something that learned from both.

Nothing sings. No insects make a field-noise. No leaves move. The ecology is based on seepage, heat, mineral, residue, and patience. Life, where it occurs, occupies margins: pipe-sweat, sump edges, warm vents, condensation gutters, the interiors of abandoned tanks. It does not cover the world; it annotates it.

The rules of the place are hydraulic and thermal. Pressure matters. Elevation matters. One district exhales because another condenses. One street is passable because sluices upstream are open. A quiet wall may be dangerous because it holds back boiling water under calm stone. Metal handrails burn in some alleys and are slick with chill condensation in others only a few steps away. Doors bear not names but flow symbols, heat marks, and pressure warnings: spiral for vapor, bar for weight, divided circle for chemical water, black triangle for ground too thin above a chamber.

The city seems to have been built not against nature, but as an agreement with an inhospitable process. It channels the mountain’s fever. It gathers atmospheric wetness and returns it below. It refines something out of the hot water, or once did. Perhaps salts. Perhaps metals. Perhaps energy. Perhaps memory, if the stranger machinery is to be believed: the sealed towers with listening dishes aimed down into the stone, the long ceramic halls where condensation runs upward along ribbed ceilings, the circular plazas paved with metal plates that tremble when no engine is near.

In the oldest quarter, the streets narrow and the buildings become almost mineral. Their foundations sink directly into black rock. Windows are small, round, and thick with amber glass. Many are lit from within by no visible lamp, only by a steady dull radiance like banked coals behind fog. Water runs down the center of the lanes in shallow carved channels. It is clear there, though too hot to touch, and it carries tiny metallic flecks that gather at bends in glittering drifts. Bridges cross these channels every few dozen paces, each bridge worn smooth by use. On their railings are thousands of notches, some deliberate, some made by tools, some by weather, until record and erosion have become the same language.

The air smells of rain on hot stone, copper, wet ash, closed rooms, and distant brine. Breathing it would feel heavy. It would dampen the inside of the nose and throat. Clothes would cling. Paper would curl. Leather would swell. Instruments would need constant calibration. Every surface would be either too hot, too slick, or subtly contaminated with mineral dust.

And yet the place is not hostile in a dramatic way. It does not rage. It endures. Its strangeness lies partly in its restraint. The city is dim, wet, hot, elevated, empty, and working. It has all the apparatus of occupation without the crowd, all the complexity of industry without much visible labor. It feels less abandoned than delegated.

At intervals, the weather changes without warning. A low cloud climbs the plateau and enters the streets like a tide. Lamps blur. Buildings across an avenue vanish. Condensation forms instantly on overhead pipes and falls in a sudden warm rain, loud for several minutes on sheet metal roofs. Drain-channels fill; sluice gates pivot open by themselves; pumps answer from below with deeper percussion. Then the cloud thins, the dripping slows, and the city reappears in sections: a stack, a bridge, a blank facade, a tower with its red light blinking patiently in the mist.

Beyond the last factories, at the high edge, the plateau becomes open stone. Here the city’s drains empty into a chain of stepped basins cut directly into the rock. Hot water moves through them in descending sheets, each pool depositing a different color: white carbonate, orange iron, blue-green copper, black manganese. There is no grass around them, no reeds, no soft mud. Just mineral lips, hard and scalloped, built up over centuries by flowing water. From this edge one can see lower clouds spread like a dim sea far below, and the city behind rising in terraces, mostly unlit, its pipes and roofs wet and faintly steaming.

Sometimes, from somewhere under the upper works, a horn sounds once. Not loud. Not urgent. A flat industrial note that rolls through the vapor and is swallowed by the plateau. After it, certain lamps change color. A few gates open. Water reroutes. Far off, one of the low carriers begins moving along its track.

Then the quiet returns—not empty of action, but full of processes too slow, too distributed, or too deep to be called noise. The city keeps condensing, pumping, cooling, heating, staining, eroding, sealing itself. It continues to be inhabited by its own maintenance.
