The land stands very high, so high that the idea of a horizon has partly failed.

Not because there are walls nearby. There are not. The spaces are broad, immense in a regional way: basins and shelves and miles of tilted alpine ground, a whole cold province lifted above the ordinary weather of the world. But darkness presses down from above, and cloud presses in from the sides, and the far distances appear only in fragments — a slope picked out in dim pewter, the black comb of a ridge, a pale vapor sheet sliding across a saddle — before vanishing again.

It is a lush place, but not in any warm or generous sense. The growth here is severe, compact, and excessive all at once. Mosses pile over stone in thick, sodden furs. Lichens plate the cliffs in overlapping scales of green-black, rust, sulfur, and a faint glacial blue that seems to hold its own light without illuminating anything else. Dwarf shrubs creep low to the ground, their branches pressed flat by wind and cold, their leaves small, oily, almost metallic. Ferns grow in ravines where vapor collects. Black grasses fill the shallow bowls between rock ribs, each blade beaded with condensation that freezes, thaws, and freezes again without ever fully becoming frost.

There are no trees in the ordinary sense. At this elevation the air is too thin and the cold too exacting. But there are things that have taken the place of trees: vertical colonies of alpine kelp-like vegetation rooted in cracks, rising only shoulder-high yet dense as a forest when gathered together. They are dark green and rubbery, ribbed like industrial hose, with translucent edges that shiver in the wind. Their fronds do not rustle. They flex. At night — and it is usually night, or something close to it — they drink from the air. Moisture condenses along their surfaces and travels downward in threads, feeding mats of moss below.

Water is present but never abundant enough to soften the whole land. It appears in pockets, in seams, in brief passages. There are tarns hidden under overhangs, their surfaces black and still as cooled oil. There are trickles running beneath the vegetation, audible only when one kneels close. Snowmelt travels through gravel for miles without showing itself, then emerges suddenly in a clear rill, crosses twenty feet of stone, and vanishes into a fissure with a small, final sound.

Rain comes intermittently, not as weather exactly but as an event in the breathing of the heights. Condensation thickens on the lee sides of boulders. Mist gathers out of nothing. For an hour the whole plateau sweats. Then the moisture withdraws, leaving the vegetation shining and the exposed rock matte and cold.

The terrain has been here a long time. It is not raw, though it is harsh. The mountains are weathered into their own logic: long-backed ridges, concave basins, fractured escarpments with edges softened by centuries of ice and wind. There are no fresh wounds except where the land is still moving in its slow, active ways. Scree fields lie arranged by size, as though sorted by an intelligence of gravity. Stone pavements are fitted by freeze-thaw into polygons. Old avalanche chutes descend like dark tongues through the vegetation, not bare, but colonized by younger, brighter growth. The place has settled into itself. Even its instability has patterns.

And yet there is an industrial character here with no industry.

No structures stand. There are no towers, no roads, no pipes, no mines, no visible machines made by hands. Nothing built interrupts the slopes. But the mountain behaves like a vast, buried works.

Deep under the plateau, something turns.

It is not loud. In fact the most unsettling thing about the region is its quiet. Sound does not carry properly here. The air is thin, the moss absorbs footfalls, and the vapor layers muffle distance. Most of the time there is only the small ticking of ice forming on leaves, the faint scrape of grit shifted by wind, the almost inaudible drip of condensation into stone basins.

But beneath that silence there are periodic tremors of mechanism: a low pressure-change felt in the teeth, a pulse through the rock like a flywheel completing one enormous rotation, the far-off sigh of vents opening somewhere beyond sight. Not natural in the usual way, not artificial in any visible way. The land seems electrified without wires, mechanized without parts. The ecology has grown around these rhythms as it would around seasons or tides.

At intervals, vents in the high ground exhale warm mineral vapor. They are difficult to see except when the dark shifts around them. Around each vent, the vegetation grows thickest: cushions of moss a meter deep, translucent fungi like curled glass, dark flowering stalks that open only when the vapor pulses. The heat is weak but precious. Small animals gather there. Insects, too, though they move slowly in the cold — wingless black beetles, silver larvae in condensation films, moths with furred bodies and narrow, ash-colored wings that vibrate without sound.

The fauna is abundant, though sparsely distributed across the open land. One can walk a long way through apparent emptiness and then discover that every surface is occupied.

Under the moss live small white amphibians with lidless eyes, surviving in hidden dampness far above where such creatures should be. They emerge during the condensation hours and creep across stone, feeding on mineral films and tiny invertebrates. In the black grasses, hare-like mammals browse with delicate, jointed muzzles, their coats dark on top and pale underneath, so that when they crouch among rocks they resemble patches of shadow over frost. They move in brief, synchronized bursts and then become completely still.

Predators follow them almost silently. Long-bodied alpine foxes, or something like foxes, with wide paws and whiskers stiff enough to sense air pressure. Pale raptors nest on cliff ledges, though they rarely cry. Their wings are broad and ragged, made for lifting through the slow vertical breaths of the escarpments. They hunt by listening not to sound, but to disturbances in vapor. When one passes overhead, the mist warps around it.

There are larger creatures too, seen mostly at a distance: shaggy herbivores crossing saddle-lines in single file, their horns swept backward like black roots; narrow, high-stepping ungulates that browse on lichen crusts; blind burrowing things that push ridges under the moss. In the tarns, something occasionally rolls just beneath the surface, making a ring that spreads outward and strikes the stone banks without a splash.

Nothing here is crowded. The density of the place is low, almost hollow. Between every clump of life lies a great interval of rock, air, darkness, and cold. A single animal on a slope can seem like the only animal in existence until the eye adjusts and finds ten more, spaced widely, each following its own small route through the immense quiet.

The light is scarce. There may be a sun somewhere above, but it arrives attenuated, defeated by cloud, altitude, and some atmospheric peculiarity that keeps the sky close to black-violet even at midday. Snow patches glow faintly. Wet stone holds dim reflections. Certain lichens emit a cold, greenish radiance when compressed or warmed by breath. The vents sometimes pulse with a dull red internal gleam, not flame, more like heated iron seen through ash.

In the deepest hours the land is not blind but legible in other ways. Moisture shines. Ice edges whiten. Animal eyes appear and vanish. The high kelp-fronds show pale veins. The world becomes a map of small signals suspended in darkness.

Weather moves continuously, though quietly. Cloud banks climb the slopes from below and spill over the plateau rims like slow smoke. Katabatic winds pour down from icefields in sudden sheets, flattening the vegetation and driving fine crystals through the air. Then calm returns so completely that droplets can hang from frond tips for minutes without falling. Sometimes the air pressure drops all at once, and the whole region responds: vents breathe harder, tarn surfaces tremble, burrows open, insects climb to the tops of moss cushions, and the raptors leave their ledges.

The rules of the world are not hostile, exactly, but exact.

Warmth is local and temporary. Moisture is caught, stored, hoarded, passed downward through living surfaces. Movement must be efficient. Noise is wasteful. Height changes everything: blood, water, growth, decay. What dies here does not rot quickly. It becomes part of the insulation of the land — fur in moss, bone in lichen, feathers frozen into peat, then mineralized by vapor. Nothing disappears fast, but everything is eventually taken in.

The industrial pulse beneath the plateau may be geothermal, tectonic, biological, or something for which those categories are only metaphors. The animals know it better than any explanation would. Burrows are dug along vibration lines. Moss colonies grow in rings around buried throbs. Predators time their hunts to the moments when the ground hums faintly enough to mask their steps. The black grasses release pollen only after the third subterranean pulse following a pressure fall. Tarns clear, cloud, and clear again according to cycles no sky could dictate.

There is no sense that anyone has ever discovered this place. There are no cairns, no trails, no cut marks, no discarded tools. If intelligence exists here, it belongs to the landscape itself: distributed through roots, frost, pressure, animal habit, and the deep repeating labor of whatever turns below.

The openness of the region makes its strangeness feel larger. One basin leads to another, then to a shelf of broken stone, then to a miles-wide meadow of black grass under vapor, then to a cliff where the world drops away into unseen lower cloud. Across the gap another ridge floats in darkness, faintly furred with green. Far below, perhaps, there are warmer valleys, forests, rivers, settlements, noise. Up here they are irrelevant. The plateau has its own economy of cold water and hidden heat.

At times the whole place seems dormant. Empty. Almost dead.

Then condensation begins.

The fronds darken and bow. Moss swells audibly, though the sound is barely more than a soft tightening. Beetles emerge from under stone lips. Amphibians appear by the hundreds on wet rock faces. The hare-things come out to feed. A raptor drops from a ledge and vanishes into mist. A vent exhales, and vapor slides low across the ground, lit from within by a dull mineral glow. Somewhere beneath the black tarn, a body turns. The mountain gives one deep, silent mechanical shudder.

Then all is quiet again, but not still.

The landscape continues working in darkness: growing, freezing, breathing, hunting, condensing, eroding by fractions, maintaining its strange equilibrium at the roof of the world.
