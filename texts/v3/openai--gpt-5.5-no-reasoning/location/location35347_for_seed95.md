At the top of the world, but not sharply so, there is a high plain so old it has forgotten how to be mountainous.

It lies under warm air, lifted far above the lower countries, spread wide across a broken plateau of stone and tawny soil. The horizon is open and reachable in every direction, yet the ground is crowded with detail: plates of weathered rock, low ribs of basalt or some older mineral, shallow basins crusted with pale salts, mats of lichen like old stains, burrows, shells, feathers, droppings, rootlets, bones. Nothing here is lush. The vegetation comes thinly, in resistant tufts and leathery pads, in wiry grasses folded into the cracks, in dwarf shrubs that seem less to grow than to persist. But between and beneath these sparse plants the place is dense with life.

The plateau has been eroded past drama. Whatever peaks once stood here have been sanded down by epochs of wind, rain, frost that no longer visits often, and the slow patience of gravity. There are no proud cliffs except where the land has finally split at the edges of the upland, showing long horizontal pages of strata. Most of the terrain is a subdued undulation: shallow swales, worn knobs, soft-backed ridges, stone pavements polished by blown grit, hollows holding darkness and a little water. The earth looks final. Not dead, but finished. As if all roughness has already been negotiated.

Warmth rises from the stones during the day. It is not desert heat exactly; the air has moisture in it, held close to the ground in pockets, hidden in shade, condensed on undersides, gathered in the mouths of caves and in the throats of grass. Rain comes rarely but decisively. It arrives as low cloud that drags itself over the plateau and opens without thunder, darkening the rock, filling the shallow basins, waking scents so old they seem mineral rather than botanical. For a few hours the whole plain shines. Water beads in the hair of animals, runs in narrow brown threads between stone plates, trembles in prints, gathers in the natural cups of roots and skulls. Then the warmth returns and the surface dries again, leaving dampness tucked away in crevices, under mats, inside burrows, beneath flat stones where blind things wait.

There are animals everywhere.

At first the plain seems quiet, almost empty. No trees move because there are no trees tall enough to move. No stream speaks continuously. The wind is soft and irregular, slipping low among rocks. But the longer one stands still, the more presences reveal themselves. Tiny black insects polish the stems of the dry shrubs. Long-legged beetles move in deliberate lines over the stone like beads pulled on invisible threads. Small lizards press themselves flat against the warm rock until they are indistinguishable from flakes of mica, then vanish with the speed of sparks. Pale moths sleep in the shade of thornless bushes. Ants occupy entire ridges. Termite-like towers, no higher than a hand in some places and taller than a kneeling body in others, cluster along old cracks in the bedrock, their surfaces hardened into fluted clay. They make miniature cities in a country without cities.

Birds are frequent but seldom loud. They pass low and watchfully, with soft wingbeats, landing on stones rather than branches. Some have long curved beaks for prying under lichens. Others step carefully through the sparse grass, picking parasites from the backs of larger creatures. Their calls are brief, dry, interrogative. A two-note whistle. A click. A descending murmur that stops as soon as it begins. The quiet of the plateau is not emptiness but restraint. The animals here do not advertise unless necessary. Sound travels too far over the open ground.

There are larger beasts too, though they blend into the age of the place. Herds of compact, high-shouldered grazers move through the swales, cropping the wiry growth nearly to the ground. Their hides are the colors of dust, old ochre, faded smoke, and rain-dark stone. They walk slowly, as if conserving not energy but history. Their hooves fit into trails used by generations before them, braided paths worn shallow into the plateau surface. Near them travel smaller animals that feed on disturbed insects. Above them circle patient scavengers, more numerous than one expects, riding warm air that rises from the broad rock.

In hollows where dampness lasts, life thickens almost violently. These wet pockets are no larger than rooms or courtyards, sometimes only the size of a bowl, but each holds a compressed ecology. Gelatinous green films coat the stones. Fernlike plants, stunted and dark, uncurl during the wet season and shrivel back to knots during the dry. Transparent worms thread through mud. Frogs no bigger than fingernails emerge from cracks after rain, sounding once or twice with soft wooden knocks before disappearing again. Dragonflies patrol temporary pools with a seriousness disproportionate to the water’s size. The pools themselves often seem older than the rain that fills them: circular depressions ground by vanished streams, lined with smooth pebbles, stained black around the rim by centuries of wetting and drying.

Some of the pools contain animals that should not have survived in so little water. Flat, armored things stir under the silt, waking only when the basin fills. Their eggs sleep through heat, drought, trampling, and years. When the water returns, they unfold from the mud like memories. For a week the pool is crowded with feeding, mating, molting, and predation; then it shrinks to a skin, then a stain, then a hardness indistinguishable from stone. The life inside it returns to waiting.

The strangeness of the place is not theatrical. It is woven into pattern and proportion. Lichen forms rings too perfect to be accidental, some large enough to encircle a hut, though there are no true huts. Certain stones are hollow and hum faintly when the afternoon warms them, exhaling air through pores too small to see. In some districts the termite towers all lean the same direction though there is no steady wind. At dusk, pale animals rise out of cracks in the rock — not insects, not quite mammals, membrane-winged and silent — and drift low over the ground, harvesting gnats from the air above the damp hollows. Their bodies seem unfinished, half fur and half leaf, with faces like folded seeds. They are common enough that nothing flees them.

There are scattered built things, if they can be called built. No machinery, no metal, no sign of recent hands. On one rise stand several low rings of stone, collapsed inward, each ring almost swallowed by sediment and lichen. They may have been shelters, traps, nests, ritual enclosures, or the exposed roots of some vanished mineral formation. A line of upright slabs crosses a saddle between two ridges, but half have fallen and the rest are rounded by age. They cast short shadows in the normal light of afternoon, ordinary shadows, yet animals avoid stepping between them. Bones collect there, not arranged exactly, but not random either.

Elsewhere shallow grooves run across the stone in long parallel sets. They resemble tracks left by sledges or dragging bodies, but they are too old, too worn, and sometimes pass under outcrops that must have formed later. Rainwater gathers in the grooves. Small plants grow there. Beetles use them as roads. The plateau accepts every mark and slowly makes it natural.

Light here is neither harsh nor dim. It falls plainly over everything, revealing without explaining. Mornings are pale gold, the stones cool, condensation silvering the undersides of shrubs and the backs of insects. By midday the air shimmers lightly, not enough to distort the horizon, only enough to make distance feel alive. Shadows shorten and retreat beneath stones. Animals become low and still. The warm silence deepens. Even the flies seem to move carefully.

Afternoons draw scents from the ground: dry clay, sunlit hide, crushed lichen, old dung, bitter resin from the shrubs, the faint copper smell of damp stone where moisture remains. When cloud passes, the temperature barely drops, but every animal seems to know. Heads lift. Beetles pause. Birds turn their bodies toward the coming shade. If rain falls, it does so with a soft ticking on stone and hide, a delicate noise spread over miles.

At the plateau’s edges, the land opens suddenly into worn escarpments and descending valleys blurred by distance. From above, the lower world looks younger: sharper drainage lines, greener bands, moving rivers. But up here the high country has entered another kind of time. Change is slow, often too slow to witness. A stone flakes once in a century. A shrub thickens by the width of a claw. A path deepens because hooves choose it for five hundred seasons. A basin fills and dries, fills and dries, until its rim becomes polished as bone.

The rules of the world are simple and severe.

Nothing wastes movement. Nothing grows tall unless it is stone. Water is brief, therefore every surface is a cup, every crack a refuge, every skin a storage vessel. Openness does not mean emptiness; it means exposure. Density hides low, underfoot, within pores, inside nests, in the stomachs of grazers, in eggs buried below dust. The living do not conquer the ancient ground. They inhabit its remaining permissions.

Night comes warmly. The rocks release the day by degrees. Stars appear with exceptional clarity in the high air, but the plateau does not become loud. Instead, it changes occupants. Small paws move over grit. Scales whisper through grass. Winged shapes pass without calling. From the damp hollows comes an occasional pop, chirr, or single throat-note, quickly reabsorbed by the dark. The stone rings hold starlight. The leaning towers of clay stand in their fixed attitudes. Far off, a herd shifts like a low cloud over the land.

By morning, dew has gathered again in the secret places. The sparse plants drink through their skins. Beetles emerge jeweled with condensation. Birds arrive to inspect the pools, the dung, the exposed larvae. The plateau resumes its almost motionless abundance, warm and high and immeasurably old: a crowded country of small lives laid over the final face of stone.
