The place is a hollow highland basin, not large enough to be a country and too enclosed to be called a desert, though it has the dryness and severity of one. It sits high above the lower world, cupped between black ridges of old volcanic stone. From the rim it might look like a dark bowl packed with rubble, a cramped mineral amphitheater, a place where nothing could live. There is no visible water anywhere. No stream has cut the basin, no moss darkens the cracks, no tree or grass interrupts the stone. Yet the hollow is not empty.

It is overcrowded with animals.

They are everywhere, though at first the darkness hides them. The floor of the basin is a rough pavement of salt plates, baked clay, and clinker, broken into scales by ages of heat. Mineral crusts overlap in thick, blistered layers: white salt gone gray with dust, red iron skins, sulfur-yellow seams, black glassy slag. The ground sounds brittle under any pressure, but so much has passed over it for so long that many surfaces are polished smooth. Paths exist without being built. They spiral between stone bulges and low shelters of fallen rock, rubbed into the ground by claws, pads, bellies, shells, and dragging limbs.

The basin is hot in a way that feels enclosed rather than sunlit. The sky above is nearly black, not with storm but with altitude, dust, and the long shade of surrounding cliffs. Light comes only as a faint copper seep along the upper rim, or as a dull furnace-glow from cracks in the stone. Heat leaks upward from beneath the basin floor. The air trembles in the dark. At times it smells of salt, ash, hot metal, old horn, and something like singed leather. There is no moisture in the breath. The throat tightens immediately. Every surface seems to be waiting to burn skin.

The ridges around the hollow are steep and close, making the whole place feel room-sized despite its true breadth. It has the scale of a cathedral nave filled to the roof with bodies, columns, relics, and shadow. One can see from one enclosing wall to the other when the dimness permits, but never clearly. Too many things occupy the space between. Boulders crowd the floor. Ribs of basalt thrust up like ruined pews. Salt chimneys rise in tight clusters, each one pitted with holes. Low mounds of powdered mineral surround vents where hot dry gas exhales without steam. The basin has boundaries: a ring of fractured escarpment, a few narrow notches leading in or out, and above them the heavy dark.

Scattered among the natural forms are built things, or things close enough to building to make the distinction uneasy. Small stone stacks stand against the cliffs, no taller than a kneeling person, made from flat black plates arranged without mortar. Some have triangular openings facing inward toward the basin. Others have collapsed into cairns, their purpose lost. Lines of pale chips cross the ground in short segments, as if someone once tried to mark paths and stopped, or as if animals pushed aside the crust along preferred routes until the old subsurface showed through. There are a few arch-like frames made from fused mineral and bone, half natural, half assembled. No tools remain. No metal has been worked. Whatever made these things either used only weight and patience, or never understood construction as separate from habitation.

Nothing here is green.

There are no plants, no leaves, no dry stems, no seeds waiting for rain. The life of the basin belongs to other arrangements. It clings to heat, minerals, breath, and decay. Thin black films line the undersides of shaded stones, matte and slightly ridged, not moss, not fungus exactly, but a crust that thickens around warm cracks and flakes away when touched. Pale knots grow in sheltered cavities, rubbery and mineral-fed, exuding no wetness but dusting themselves with spores or salts. These simpler lives do not soften the landscape. They make it older-looking, more sealed, more self-sufficient.

The fauna moves through this plantless world with the density of insects under a log, but larger, stranger, more various. There are low many-legged grazers that scrape mineral films from stone with plates in their mouths, leaving shining arcs behind them. There are narrow-bodied runners that pass in files along the hot cracks, each stepping exactly where the one before stepped, their backs armored in dark overlapping scales that catch the faint red glow. There are pale blind things like jointed roots, though there are no roots here, which emerge from holes in the salt chimneys and taste the air with fringed heads. There are heavy squat animals that crouch in the hotter places with their bellies spread against the ground, absorbing heat, exhaling slowly through vents along their sides.

Most of them are quiet.

The silence is not the silence of absence, but of restraint. The basin is full of small sounds kept low: scraping, clicking, the powdery slide of scales over salt, the soft tick of claws on cooled glass, the occasional dry cough from a vent. Bodies brush stone. Shells tap shell. Something inside a crack grinds its jaws in a slow rhythm. Every now and then a distant section of crust pops from thermal stress, and all nearby movement pauses, then resumes. There are almost no calls. Sound seems expensive here. Perhaps the air is too thin and dry to carry it well. Perhaps everything that calls is quickly found.

Predation happens with the same muted economy. A flat animal the color of basalt lies indistinguishable from a slab until one of the mineral-scrapers crosses it; then the slab folds around the prey without a cry. Along the basin wall, hook-footed climbers hang upside down in the darkness, plucking smaller creatures from the vertical paths. In the salt chimneys, colonies of translucent burrowers open and close their holes in waves, sealing passages when the shadow of a hunter passes over them. The ecosystem is active, crowded, and precise, but not frantic. It has had a long time to settle into its cruelties.

Heat is the central weather.

Because there is no rain, weather arrives as pulses of temperature, gas, pressure, and dust. At intervals the vents breathe harder. The ground warms from painful to unbearable. Animals lift themselves higher on their limbs or retreat to shaded cavities. Black films curl at their edges, then relax. Mineral plates expand with faint creaks. Later, when the underheat slackens, the upper air cools quickly in the thin high dark, and the basin exhales stored warmth in shimmering columns. Dust then begins to move. Not wind exactly, but small pressure-driven flows sliding along the floor, carrying powdered salt into drifts against stones and bodies. Some animals open dorsal slits to catch the dust; others bury themselves beneath it, leaving only sensory horns exposed.

In the deepest part of the basin stands the densest feature: a cluster of stone spires and broken cairns packed so tightly that passages between them are barely wider than a body. This is where the animal life is most overwhelming. Nests, dens, resting ledges, molting hollows, mineral-scrape galleries, and hunting perches overlap vertically. The spires are streaked white, black, and rust-red from generations of contact. Their bases are undercut by burrows. Their upper ledges are crowded with motionless watchers whose eyes, if they are eyes, reflect the faint heat-glow like dull embers.

The place gives the impression that every niche is already occupied. Any loose stone has something beneath it. Any crack has a tenant. Any patch of slightly different warmth has been claimed, contested, inherited, abandoned, and claimed again. Even the scattered structures have been absorbed: the small stone stacks serve as shade shelters, ambush blinds, nesting chambers, molting walls. The triangular openings are polished by passage. Some are guarded by animals too large to have built them, others by colonies small enough to vanish like spilled grain when approached.

The rules of the world are austere and intimate.

Water is not part of ordinary life. If it exists at all, it is locked in bodies, in crystals, in deep inaccessible stone, in rare chemical exchanges too brief to bead or flow. Softness must be manufactured internally. Coolness is a resource. Shade is territory. Heat is food for some, danger for others, timing for all. Movement is rationed. Silence protects. Touch matters more than sight. The darkness is navigated by heat, vibration, mineral scent, and the memory of surfaces worn smooth by ancestors.

The basin is not young. Its violence has become habitual. The cliffs have weathered into stable profiles. The salt plates break and heal in repeated patterns. The animals’ paths are old enough to look geological. Even the strange half-built remains have lost the sharpness of intention and become part of the terrain. Nothing here feels temporary, though everything is in motion. It is an established system, balanced not by peace but by continuous adjustment: vents opening and closing, crusts forming and cracking, films growing and being eaten, bodies hunting, hiding, mating, molting, dying, being stripped to clean mineral-colored fragments.

In the darkest hours, when the rim-glow vanishes completely, the basin almost disappears as a visual place. It becomes heat against the face, grit underfoot, a pressure of nearby bodies, and the soft, constant evidence of unseen life. The high walls hold the warmth and the quiet. Somewhere close, a claw drags once across stone. Somewhere beneath, gas moves through a narrow throat in the rock. A hundred animals pause to read the vibration.

Then the crowded dark resumes.
