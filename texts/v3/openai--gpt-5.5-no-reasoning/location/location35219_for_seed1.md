The land is low and wide, though “land” is not quite the right word for it. It is a nearly level expanse of ancient dark mineral, drowned in shallow water, spreading outward under a ceiling of dim, cold air. Nothing rises high. There are no mountains in sight, no trees, no ruined walls, no towers, no grasses bending in weather. The horizon is broken instead by crowded, low forms: plates of stone, black shelves, frost-crusted ridges, slick basins, steaming seams, banks of pale sediment, knots of animal bodies, and machinery that has no maker.

Water lies everywhere, but rarely deep. It gathers in long mirror-pools only a handspan above the stone, in channels worn so old they seem not carved but remembered. It beads on every surface, freezes in skins, melts again from below, runs backward in places, and seeps upward through the ground as if the planet is sweating. The rocks weep. Their faces shine with slow tears of mineral water that fall into puddles already full. In the cold, these tears form translucent whiskers and rinds, but the ice never entirely holds; something beneath it pulses with dull industrial warmth, enough to keep the place wet, not enough to comfort it.

The temperature is bitterly low. The cold is not the clean cold of high snowfields but a sunken, saturated cold, heavy with brine and metal vapor. Breath would hang close to the mouth and then drift downward, reluctant to rise. Fog crawls ankle-high across the flats in layers, pale and greasy, threading between crowded stones and the moving bodies of animals. Higher up, the air is dim blue-gray, almost lightless, as though day has been diluted through miles of dirty glass. There may be a sun, but it is only a bruise behind vapor. Shadows are soft, confused, often cast in the wrong direction.

The terrain is old beyond ordinary erosion. Every edge has been worried down. Stones are not jagged except where they have recently split from cold shock; most are rounded, flattened, exhausted. The ground looks handled by eons of rain, freeze, thaw, abrasion, chemical seepage, and patient tidal rubbing. It is a landscape that has been reduced almost to its final grammar: basin, lip, runnel, slick, scarp no taller than a knee, depression full of black water, another lip, another basin. It has no dramatic youth left. Even the broken places are ancient breaks reopened.

Yet it is packed, oppressively full. There is no empty vista despite the openness. The flats extend regionally, perhaps for many miles, but every yard is occupied by something: pools nested inside pools, stone plates overlapping like scales, frost blooms, mineral blisters, foam mats with no organic green in them, clusters of blind animals, vents, rivulets, nodules, rails of hardened precipitate, swarms of small dark bodies, and the ceaseless mechanical forms that push up out of the earth.

There are no built structures. Nothing here appears constructed with intention. No road crosses the marsh-stone. No hut, dock, fence, pipe, or scaffold gives scale to the place. And still the world is industrial.

The industry is geological, spontaneous, and wrong.

Across the flats, engines grow from the mineral crust. They are not artifacts, but processes that have learned the shapes of artifacts. Huge pistons of basalt and iron oxide rise and sink in shallow wells, driven by pressure changes below. Flywheels of frozen brine rotate slowly in circular trenches, their rims toothed with black mineral deposits. Long belts of water, skinned with oil-colored ice, move continuously along grooves, carrying pebbles, bones, salts, shells, and metallic foam from one basin to another. At intervals, towers of no more than waist height exhale steam in timed bursts, each vent surrounded by rings of accreted mineral like gaskets. The sound of them is immense in aggregate: clank, sigh, hammer, churn, grind, release.

Nothing has hands. Nothing assembled these mechanisms. They are the inevitable behavior of this place, like rainfall elsewhere.

A broad stretch to the north is a field of compressors. The ground there flexes in dark rectangular panels, not truly rectangular but close enough to disturb the eye. Water floods across them in sheets; then the panels descend, pressing the water through hidden pores. The outflow emerges far away as milky jets, warm for a moment, before the cold seizes them into braided steam. The compressors beat irregularly, sometimes in sequence, sometimes all at once. When they synchronize, the whole lowland booms like a colossal heart striking stone.

To the west, where the land dips slightly, lies a marsh without plants. It has the organization of a marsh—channels, hummocks, pools, hidden depth, feeding grounds—but no reeds, no moss, no algae visible as life. Its hummocks are made of pale sinter and black slag. Its floating mats are crusts of mineral foam, brittle at the edges and rubbery at the center. Under them, water moves with a thick, secret traffic. Bubbles rise continuously, carrying the smells of iron, salt, wet ash, and something like old batteries.

The animals are abundant.

At first they seem impossible in the absence of vegetation. There is nothing for them to graze, no leafy primary abundance, no obvious food web. But the ecology here is based on the engines: on mineral exhalations, pressure blooms, heat leaks, chemical snow, and the edible charge that gathers on wet stone.

Small creatures cover the flats in dark congregations. They resemble crabs, ticks, eyeless fish, unfolded hinges, wet birds without feathers, and living tools. Many are translucent enough to show their slow internal lights. They scrape mineral films from rocks with ceramic mouthparts. They cluster around vents, not warming themselves exactly, but feeding on the voltage gradient between hot seep and frozen air. Some cling in chains to rotating brine-wheels, letting the motion drag nutrient-rich water through their bodies. Others live inside the piston wells and emerge only when the stone plungers rise, spilling out in glistening heaps before retreating with the next downward stroke.

There are larger animals too. Low, flat-bodied things move through the shallow water like rugs being pulled from below. They have antlering arrays of sensory filaments that trail across the wet mineral, reading vibration. Their backs are armored with plates of accreted frost and dark sediment; smaller animals live on them, picking at their skins and at the chemical crusts that form around their vents. Predators stalk them in the fog: tall, jointed forms that step delicately among the pools on many needle legs, folding and unfolding with the rhythm of derricks. They make a dry clicking sound even when wet.

Some fauna is only intermittently animal. Black shoals pass through puddles as if through connected underground corridors, appearing in one pool, vanishing, then erupting in another fifty paces away. The individual members are like commas made of muscle and glass. When frightened, they align into diagrams that hover just above the water surface, briefly becoming symbols too complex to read. Then they fall apart into swimming again.

There are flying things, though the air is cold and heavy. They do not fly by wings so much as by using mistakes in gravity. They gather where reality has thinned over the vents, flickering from one position to another with a papery slap. Their bodies are flat, translucent, and riddled with holes that show different parts of the landscape through them—not the landscape behind them, but nearby areas, future puddles, the underside of rocks, the inside of a predator’s mouth. When flocks pass overhead, the dim light fractures. For a few seconds, every pool reflects a different sky.

The weirdness is not decoration here. It is the foundation.

Space does not stay evenly distributed across the flats. Some basins are larger inside than their rims allow. A puddle the size of a sleeping animal may contain a visible shoreline half a mile away, with tiny dark herds moving along it. Stones recur in patterns that are almost repetitions, except each version is older than the last. Walk straight across the lowland and one may pass the same vent three times, each time with different ice, different carcasses, different mechanical timing. Sound travels in loops. A piston may strike nearby, and its clang arrives first from the horizon, then from underfoot, then from memory.

The water participates in this breakdown. In some channels it flows downhill. In others it flows toward pressure, toward attention, toward warmth, toward the loudest noise. Thin streams fork, reconsider, and climb over stone lips, leaving trails of frost behind them like shed skin. There are pools whose surfaces are not horizontal but tilted, held in place by unknown rules. Others are concave, forming shallow bowls of reflective liquid with no container at all. Animals drink from the underside of these suspended surfaces, hanging by their mouthparts in the air.

The rules of life are equally unstable. Death is common but not always final in the expected way. Carcasses sink into mineral film and become feeding stations, then vents, then sometimes locomotor things again. Bones dissolve into conductive lattices. Shells keep moving after the bodies leave them. Predators may kill prey only to have the prey’s outline remain in the fog, behaving as though it escaped, drawing other predators away. Some species hunt these outlines. Others lay eggs in them.

There is no vegetation, but there are growths. They are not plant life. They are neither green nor rooted in the usual sense. Frost-mineral extrusions branch on stone like white coral and then retract when the engines boom. Fungal-seeming films spread in circular stains, metallic purple and dull silver, but they do not soften the landscape; they make it more mineral, more conductive, more responsive to pressure. Glassy nodules bud along cracks, each containing a swirl of trapped water and a tiny weather system. When broken, they release a puff of snow and a shriek like tearing wire.

The soundscape is loud, layered, and continuous. The deepest sounds come from below: enormous muffled knocks, grinding rotations, the suction-thud of subsurface chambers filling and emptying. Above that are the vents coughing steam, the slap of cold water over stone, the crackle of ice forming and failing, the clatter of mineral plates under animal movement. The fauna adds its own machinery: chirrs, clicks, wet bellows, scraping mouthparts, alarm pulses like struck glass, mating calls that resemble distant factory whistles. Sometimes the entire lowland rings in response to a pressure change, all pools trembling at once, all animals going still for the duration of a single vast tone.

Then activity resumes.

The place is not frantic, but it is never still. Its tempo is cyclic and multiple. Fog condenses, thickens, freezes onto surfaces, then sublimates when a heat pulse rolls through. Vents open in bands from south to north. Mineral belts transport sediment. Animals migrate not by season but by pressure phase, following underground rhythms. Predators time their hunts to piston strokes, striking when vibration blinds their prey. Shoals rise during electrical dimming. Flying things appear when the sky briefly loses commitment to distance.

At intervals, a cold rain falls from a cloud layer too low to be called sky. The drops are heavy and dark, full of dissolved metals. They strike the pools with an oily patter, raising rings that intersect too many times. During rain, the mechanical geology accelerates. Brine-wheels spin faster. Stone pistons hammer more frequently. The compressors groan. Channels overflow and connect basins that are usually separate, allowing sudden migrations and massacres. Blind scraping animals are swept into predator grounds; predators are trapped in freezing shallows; suspended pools sag and spill upward into the mist.

After rain, everything steams faintly, not with warmth but with chemical adjustment. New crusts form. Old runnels clog. The ground rearranges its priorities. What was a feeding site becomes a hazard. What was solid becomes membrane. What was near becomes unreachably far.

The atmosphere smells of cold iron, salt, wet stone, ozone, old oil, and animal musk. Taste would be metallic immediately. Skin would numb, then prickle from charged droplets. Hair would gather frost. The ground would not be trustworthy: apparently solid plates might flex like diaphragms, while thin water might hold weight if crossed at the right angle. A careless step could break through into a shallow pool, or into a deep one wearing a shallow reflection.

Farther out, the flats change character without ever becoming less crowded. There are fields of black polygonal mudstone packed so tightly with vents that the air shudders. There are pale depressions where mineral snow accumulates in drifts, churned by burrowing fauna into gray slush. There are belts of rounded boulders, each one sweating continuously, each one home to a ring of feeding creatures arranged like worshippers. There are low shelves of volcanic glass, smoothed by age until they resemble frozen waves, under which pale animals press their bellies to the underside and listen.

In the dimmest regions, where light thins almost to nothing, the industrial processes become luminous. Not brightly—there is no blaze, no fire—but with weak blues, bruised violets, corpse greens, sodium orange leaking from cracks. The glow outlines moving parts of the earth-machine. It catches on wet shells and frost whiskers. It pulses through the bodies of animals feeding on charge. The whole plain becomes a map of circulation: heat, pressure, brine, hunger, electricity, error.

No one built this world, but it behaves as if production matters. It manufactures sediment, charge, bodies, echoes, weather, and impossible distances. It refines water through stone and stone through water. It breeds animals from chemistry and then grinds them back into chemistry. It runs day and night, if those terms apply beneath the dim bruised sky. Its factories have no walls because the factory is the terrain. Its engines have no purpose beyond continuation. Its waste is habitat. Its breakdowns are seasons.

And beneath everything is the sense of extreme age: not ruin, because there was no original architecture to ruin; not wilderness, because wilderness suggests a freedom this place does not have. It is a final, saturated state. The world has been eroded until only process remains. All softness has been replaced by wet mineral persistence. All open space has been filled with cycles. Even silence, if it ever existed here, has been occupied.

Stand still long enough and the landscape begins to include you. Condensation beads on your outer surfaces. Small animals test your shadow. The nearest pool adjusts its reflection to show you from below. A piston somewhere changes rhythm to match your pulse, or perhaps your pulse changes first. The fog thickens, carrying clicks and factory-thuds and the wet scrape of feeding mouths. Around your feet, water begins to decide which direction you are.
