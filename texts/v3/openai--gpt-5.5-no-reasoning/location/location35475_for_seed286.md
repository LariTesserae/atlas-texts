At first it seems to be a sea.

Not a sea below a sky, but a sea lifted high into the cold bright air, held on the back of a continent-sized mountain whose summit has no exposed stone left. Everything is submerged. Everything is green, or once-green, or green in a lightless inner way. The water is not an empty volume poured over the land; it is braided through living matter so thoroughly that water and vegetation no longer seem like separate kingdoms. The place is a high, cool, drowned canopy-plain, wide enough to make its own horizon, its own weather, its own laws of pressure and sound.

There is no shore.

There are only distances.

The whole plateau is an immense mat of plant life floating, rooted, layered, and interlocked across heights that would elsewhere belong to clouds. Its upper surfaces lie just beneath sheets of clear cold water. In some places the water is only a few body-lengths deep over a ceiling of leaves; in others it drops through green shafts into dim blue caverns where trunks wider than towers descend into older growth. The plants are not arranged like a forest. They are the terrain itself: raft, reef, floor, wall, filter, column, membrane. Vast lily-like plates overlap for kilometers, their rims curled upward into shallow basins full of still rainwater that is not rain but the same general water folding back upon itself. Rope-vines span gulfs between buoyant groves. Root curtains hang down through the liquid in great sparse veils, each strand furred with smaller filaments, each filament home to beads of air, eggs, spores, mites, translucent fish, and slow colonies of something neither plant nor animal.

The temperature is cool without being freezing. It is the coolness of deep reservoirs, shaded springs, snowmelt held for centuries under moss. Touching any surface draws heat from the skin. The water is clear enough for normal daylight to enter, though softened by depth and chlorophyll. The light has the gentle steadiness of an overcast noon multiplied through green glass. There are no sharp shadows. Everything is legible but slightly removed, as if seen through a patient thought.

The land—if it can still be called land—is high. Far below, unseen under kilometers of living saturation, there must be rock: an uplifted tableland, a crown of old mountains, a basin raised into the air and then drowned by its own biology. But no mineral face shows. The exposed geology has long ago been consumed, sleeved, sealed, and replaced by woody tissues hardened under pressure, by root-stone, by sedimented pollen, by generations of collapsed stems compressed into springy black strata. The plateau’s edges, where they exist, pour outward in curtains of water and pendant vegetation. These edges are so far away that within the central reaches they are theoretical, like the edge of a planet.

The place has existed in this condition for a very long time.

It is not growing quickly. It is not decaying noticeably. It is established past the point of event. The forms here are weathered not by storm and change, but by balance. Every frond has been frondlike forever; every channel has worn itself into an equilibrium between flow and root-thickening; every submerged hollow has precisely as much silt as it can hold. Nothing erupts, nothing collapses, nothing advances. The world is dynamic in appearance only. Leaves tremble. Bubbles rise. Fish cross openings. Water hums through tunnels. But these are not changes so much as features, like the grain of wood or the note of a bell. If watched for a day, the same pulse repeats. If watched for a century, the same pulse repeats. The plateau does not age in any useful way. It persists.

Its density is strange: vegetation is total, yet the space feels sparse. This is because the scale is enormous and the living structures are widely spaced where they become large. A single plant-vault may arch for leagues before another interrupts it. Massive stems rise at such distances that they resemble islands glimpsed through liquid haze. Root curtains hang miles apart, each one a vertical province. The intervals between them are open water: wide, cold, green-blue halls where sound travels in long bars. Within the vegetation there is infinite fineness, but across the landscape there is emptiness. The world breathes through enormous gaps.

There are no built structures. No roads, no walls, no ruins, no tools lost in silt, no cut surfaces, no straight lines that confess an intention. Yet there is something industrial here.

Not artificial—industrial.

The plants perform labor at terrifying scale.

Whole districts throb with slow mechanical regularity. Gigantic bladder-stems compress and release, driving water through root-pipes hundreds of meters wide. Siphon forests chant in pressure-cycles, moving minerals from drowned depths to sunlit surfaces. Chainlike vines pass pods along their lengths with a clacking, conveyor rhythm. The undersides of the broad floating leaves are studded with valve-mouths that open and shut in rows, regulating buoyancy and current. Some trunks are hollow turbines, not built but grown, their internal vanes spun endlessly by falling water. They produce a low electrical shimmer that travels through conductive sapwood and the mineralized nerves of roots. In the dimmer depths, bioluminescent nodes glow in repeated arrays, like factory windows seen at night from underwater, though there is no factory, no window, no night.

The machinery is botanical. The industry is metabolism scaled until it resembles infrastructure.

There are fields of rotating seed-drums the size of villages, suspended in open water from cables of living fiber. They turn so slowly that motion is almost impossible to detect, but their turning makes a continual wooden thunder. There are compressor groves whose swollen trunks flex together in perfect interval, drawing in cold water through porous bark and expelling it warmed by a fraction of a degree, enriched, strained, altered. There are mats of floating reeds that ratchet open with the light and shut with the same light, because the light never truly changes here; still, the ratchet continues, not as response but as law.

The sounds are loud.

Water amplifies everything. The entire plateau is full of resonance: booming trunks, clicking seeds, the steady roar of distant overflow, the pipe-organ moan of current passing through root hollows, the crackle of gas escaping from microbial beds, the slapping of leaf membranes against one another in slow pressure waves. Some sounds are so deep they are felt more than heard, a constant industrial bass in the bones and teeth. Higher sounds thread through it: shrimp-like snapping in the moss, the glassy calls of amphibious birds that never land on dry ground, the scraping of grazing mouths on submerged bark, the whistle of water forced through narrow vascular slits.

Because nothing changes, the noise forms a fixed pattern. It is weather made permanent. In one region the bass pulse comes every nineteen heartbeats. In another, millions of tiny seed-caps tick in overlapping cycles, producing a dry rain-sound beneath ten meters of actual water. A traveler could navigate by acoustics: north toward the great siphon hum, east toward the turbine groves, downward through the trembling silence between two root veils, where predators hang.

There is fauna everywhere, though seldom in crowds. Abundance here is distributed. The animals occupy the open spaces like punctuation marks in a vast green sentence.

Long translucent grazers drift among the leaves, their bodies ribbon-thin to avoid the drag of currents. They shear algae from bark with comb-mouths and exhale strings of pearl-like bubbles that lodge under leaf ceilings. Slow finned ungulates—if they can be called that—walk upside down along the submerged undersides of floating mats, their hooves soft suction cups, their eyes lidless and calm. Schools of silver needlefish hold motionless in shafts of light. Pale crabs live in the joints of the industrial plants, cleaning valves, stealing starch, defending territories no larger than a hand. Great filter-beasts hang vertically in the open halls, suspended from gas bladders grown from their own backs, their mouths open to the constant manufactured current.

There are also air-breathing creatures, though air here is rare and local. Beneath the larger leaves are trapped atmospheres: long silver pockets where insects skate upside down, where fern-bats cling in clusters, where small warm animals nose along fibrous ceilings and lick condensation. Some of these air chambers are ancient and stable, maintained by the plants’ own gas exchange. They are not caves exactly, but lungs big enough to contain weather. A person entering one would hear the water outside as a heavy roof and smell cold sap, iodine, wet bark, pollen, and the faint metallic odor of biological electricity.

Above the water, if one reaches the highest surface, the world is no less drowned. Rain hangs in the air as a standing wall. Droplets do not fall so much as occupy their positions. Mist lies over the floating canopy in luminous strata. The plateau’s altitude makes the air thin and cool, but the vegetation exhales so much vapor that sky and water interpenetrate. The horizon is a pale band where submerged leaf-fields become cloud and cloud becomes submerged leaf-field. Light arrives normally, neither dim nor blazing, but it is caught in suspended moisture until every distant object appears to be behind glass.

There is no wind in the ordinary sense. Or rather, wind exists as a completed agreement. Mist streams always at the same angle. Surface ripples maintain the same spacing. Hanging rain keeps the same density. If a leaf bends, it has always been bending. If a droplet breaks from a tendril and joins the water, another takes its place so precisely that the event leaves no consequence. The world does not forbid motion; it forbids alteration.

This is the strangest rule of the place: cause and effect are local, but the whole remains invariant.

A fish may eat. A seed may open. A bubble may rise and burst. A predator may lunge from a curtain of roots and take prey in a flash of white jaws. Yet the total arrangement does not change. The same number of fish occupies the same green halls. The same biomass fills the same volumes. The same wounds seal at the same rate as other wounds open. The same sounds continue. Death is not absent, but it cannot accumulate. Growth is not absent, but it cannot expand. The plateau is an equation with living terms, constantly calculating itself back into its own shape.

The ecology has no succession. There are no pioneer species, no climax after disturbance, because there is no disturbance great enough to become history. Every niche is old. Every parasite has its counter-parasite. Every bloom is already balanced by a grazer, every grazer by a sickness, every sickness by a cleaner organism, every cleaner by a mineral hunger fed through the root-pumps. The industrial plants do not conquer; they regulate. Their engines are not for production beyond need, but for maintaining a continental homeostasis so immense it seems unconscious and sacred.

In the deeper layers, the coolness intensifies. Light weakens but never vanishes entirely, transmitted through miles of translucent leaf tissue and living fiber-optic veins. The water there is almost still. Enormous roots converge into pillars, then divide again into descending fans. Sediments lie in soft terraces: pollen dust, diatom shells, fish bones, powdered bark, black mats of ancient peat that never fully rot because the chemistry is too balanced to permit excess. Here the industrial sounds become muffled, transformed into a planet-sized heartbeat.

Some roots enter old cavities in the buried mountain. There, in the drowned stone beneath the living plateau, the water is mineral-rich and blue-black. Blind fauna gather around root tips that glow with cold voltage. Worms with coppery jaws scrape salts from the rock. Soft white things resembling flowers bloom from animal backs. The distinction between reef and engine, between gut and factory, between forest and machine, has no meaning here. Everything extracts, pumps, filters, distributes, receives.

And above, always, the great green surface continues: sparse in its monumental spacing, total in its occupation, untouched by hands, filled with the loud work of life.

A traveler would find no path, but paths are unnecessary because the medium itself allows passage. Movement would be swimming, climbing, drifting, squeezing through valve-gardens, crossing open halls where distant trunks stand like dark green weather columns. There would be no campfire, no dry shelter except the leaf-lungs. No landmark would change, but landmarks would be hard to trust because scale defeats memory. A stem that seems nearby may be an hour away; a root veil may look thin and prove to be a forest. Sound arrives before shape. Pressure announces architecture. The body learns the world through skin.

The materials are simple and inexhaustible: water, cellulose, lignin, air, silt, chlorophyll, bone, shell, mineral salts, mucilage, resin, electrical sap. Stone exists only as buried ancestry. Metal exists only dissolved in vascular fluids or plated microscopically along nerve-roots. Fire is impossible. Dryness is mythological. Dust cannot form. Ash cannot form. Nothing brittle lasts. Everything is flexible, swollen, polished, furred, rubbery, pulped, or grown hard as horn by immersion.

The atmosphere is calm despite the noise. Calm not as silence, but as certainty. The place has no expectation in it. No season approaches. No storm gathers. No thaw, no drought, no migration, no bloom-time, no ruin. The great compressors beat. The siphons sing. The animals feed and vanish. The wall of high rain stands in the air. The drowned plants hold their endless positions in the cold light.

It is a landscape without before or after.

A vast elevated ocean-forest, all substrate and organism, all engine and equilibrium, sounding forever like a factory made of leaves at the bottom of the sky.
