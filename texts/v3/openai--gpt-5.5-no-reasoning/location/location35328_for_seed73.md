At the top of the world there is a wet heat.

The plateau stands higher than weather should allow, lifted into a zone where the air ought to be thin, cold, and clean. Instead it is heavy and almost drinkable. Breath enters the lungs warm. It carries mineral damp, crushed leaf, the sweet rot of fallen fruit, and something electrical that has no lightning behind it. Clouds do not pass overhead so much as lean against the land. They snag on the rims of cliffs, creep through root-thick ravines, bead on stone, and condense continuously on every exposed surface. Even the sun, when it appears, seems to sweat.

The land is not a single summit but a high, open province: a tableland broken into basins, hanging marshes, low jungle ridges, terraced escarpments, and long shallow lakes caught between walls of ancient rock. The horizons are broad but often half-veiled. One can see miles in one direction—over reed-gold wetlands, dark green canopy mats, and the blue-black drop of distant gorges—then lose the next hundred paces to steam rising out of fern beds. Everything has existed long enough to find its posture. The mountains are not sharp young spires. They are enormous, settled forms: shoulders of basalt and pale limestone, rounded domes, undercut shelves, old lava ribs softened by moss and water. The cliffs weep from innumerable seams. Some trickles have worn black varnished streaks down the stone; others have built soft aprons of calcite, as if the rock were slowly growing wax.

Water is not quite everywhere, but it is never absent. It lies near the surface, under the sponge of soil. It threads under roots and fills animal prints. It stands in warm pools at the feet of trees, gathers in the hollows of leaves, trembles along suspended vines, and falls in thin veils from cliff lips into green air. After rain—though rain is difficult to separate from the ordinary behavior of the atmosphere—the whole plateau exhales mist. Paths become dark and shining. Lichens swell. Seed pods split with small audible cracks. Stone basins overflow one into another, and rivulets briefly discover old channels, then forget them again as the slope flattens.

The vegetation is lush but not uniformly forested. There are open reaches where sedges and red-stemmed reeds grow shoulder-high in saturated ground, their plumes tossing slowly in wind that has climbed a vertical mile to arrive exhausted. There are groves of wax-leaved trees with buttress roots that grip the slopes like knuckled hands. There are mats of floating plants across tarns, purple-veined and thick enough for small animals to cross. Ferns grow in balconies from the rock faces. Vines travel downward as often as upward, descending into ravines until their tips vanish in vapor. On the more exposed ridges, heat-loving shrubs crouch low, silver-backed leaves turning in unison under the sun. The color is saturated: black mud, copper bark, emerald moss, yellow fungi, pale flowers shaped like open mouths, blue mineral stains where water runs from buried seams.

Despite the height, decay is quick here. Fallen trunks do not remain as clean wood for long. They soften into shelves of fungus, become nurseries for seedlings, then disappear beneath root and moss. Yet the land itself changes slowly. A landslide scar may remain visible for centuries, gradually clothed over by reeds and waxy shrubs. A pool may migrate a few handspans in a generation. Channels clog, divide, and rejoin with the patience of sleeping animals. The place feels active but not hurried. Its violence is old and mostly past; what remains is maintenance, seepage, digestion, condensation, the unending adjustment of water to stone.

Sound carries strangely in the wet air. Nothing is ever silent. Water clicks, ticks, seeps, pours, and gurgles from hidden throats. Insects drone in layered pitches so thick they become part of the pressure of the air. Amphibians call from reed beds with hollow wooden notes. Birds—or creatures close enough to birds that the distinction only matters to taxonomists—cry from the canopy in sudden metallic bursts. Far off, waterfalls make a continuous low body of sound, more felt in the ribs than heard. Sometimes the whole marsh-edge crackles softly as gas escapes through mud. Sometimes a cliff face releases a warm sheet of water all at once, and the roar crosses the valley like applause from an unseen crowd.

The animals are abundant and not shy in the usual way. Heat and moisture have made life prolific. Long-legged waders move through the shallows, their beaks combing through floating roots. Translucent lizards cling to wet stone, their organs faintly visible until they flush green and vanish against moss. Colonies of thumb-sized crabs occupy tree hollows far from any permanent stream, harvesting the water that collects in bromeliads. Slow arboreal mammals, fur dark with condensation, hang beneath branches and strip fruit with delicate black fingers. Above the reed plains drift membranous things like kites, using rising warm vapor instead of wingbeats. Their undersides pulse with small bioluminescent organs, invisible by day except as pearly dots.

There are larger presences too. Mud channels show the sliding marks of broad-bellied animals that surface only at dusk. In the lakes, something browses the roots of floating gardens from below, lifting whole mats in slow domes before letting them settle again. The canopy sometimes parts under the weight of a creature moving carefully from crown to crown, never seen complete: a pale shoulder, a rope of tail, a black eye wet as fruit. Predators exist, but here predation is often patient. Many hunters resemble branches, stones, clumps of reeds. Some wait through whole seasons for migrations that occur only when certain pools warm to a precise degree.

Scattered through this living wetness are structures, though not many, and not all of them are recognizable as buildings at first.

On ridge crests stand small towers of matte ceramic and dark metal, no taller than old watchposts. Vines climb them but do not root into them. Their surfaces remain clean in irregular patches, as if something invisible grazes away moss and spores. Some lean at improbable angles, not from collapse but from an original design that disregarded ordinary verticals. In the marshes, low platforms rise just above the waterline, made of a material resembling polished bone. They are warm to the touch at night and cool at noon. Thin bridges connect a few of them, then stop abruptly, ending over reed-choked pools. Along certain cliff faces are door-shaped seams without handles, half-hidden behind hanging fern curtains. They open rarely, if at all.

The artifacts do not dominate the landscape. They are sparse, almost deferential, like seeds left by a civilization that preferred not to interrupt the plateau’s larger processes. Yet their technology is far beyond the apparent simplicity of their forms. One notices it in effects rather than machines.

Rain avoids some stones.

Mist gathers into brief geometric lattices above certain basins, holds for several minutes, then collapses into ordinary droplets. In one valley, water runs uphill along a black channel no wider than a palm, feeding a suspended marsh that clings to the underside of an overhang. Near the old towers, compasses fail, but so do more basic senses: distance lengthens, echoes return with the wrong texture, and a thrown pebble may strike mud before the eye has seen it fall. Roots near the ceramic posts grow in spirals not found elsewhere, each plant describing some invisible field with botanical obedience.

There are no roads in the conventional sense, but there are traces of passage. A line of flat stones crosses a bog, each stone separated by a stride too long for a human and too regular for accident. At intervals, slender markers emerge from the mud: translucent rods filled with suspended motes of light that drift upward endlessly without reaching the top. Some are broken. Where they have cracked, the surrounding moss grows in colors outside the ordinary range of green, not bright exactly but difficult to remember. Small animals avoid these places; insects gather there in ecstatic swarms.

The rules of the plateau are local and layered. Gravity is mostly dependable, except in the sink gardens, where falling leaves descend slowly enough to be overtaken by climbing vines. Fire will not take in the lower marshes, even from dry fuel; flame shrinks there into blue beads and goes out, leaving the wood uncharred but smelling of salt. Metal corrodes quickly unless it belongs to the old structures, in which case it does not corrode at all. Open water reflects the sky by day, but at night many pools reflect a different arrangement of stars, dense and tilted, as though the water remembers the altitude of another planet.

Time also seems slightly stratified. In the heat of afternoon, when the insects are loudest and the air wavers over the reeds, movement becomes viscous. A person crossing a clearing may feel watched by their own delayed footprints filling with water behind them. In shaded ravines, the opposite occurs: droplets fall too quickly, ferns uncurl overnight, wounds clot in minutes, fruit ferments before it has finished dropping from the branch. The animals know these gradients. They nest in one tempo and feed in another. Some flowers open only where two rates of time meet, petals trembling along the boundary.

Still, for all its strangeness, the place is not chaotic. It is balanced, established, long accommodated to its own impossibilities. The moss has learned the fields around the towers. The birds use the echo distortions to hide their calls. The marsh grasses thrive in water that climbs. The great root systems brace not only against wind and erosion but against subtle changes in weight. Life has made treaties with anomalies that would elsewhere be disasters.

Human presence, if humans came here, would feel provisional. A single hut of woven reed and scavenged paneling stands on a knoll above a warm pool, its roof loaded with flowering vines. Smoke does not rise from its chimney but curls downward and slips into the soil. A few ceramic bowls sit under the eaves to collect condensate. Nearby, a weathered instrument array hums inside a cage of roots, its displays blank except during storms. The inhabitants, if any remain, have learned to live lightly: to mark safe paths with knots of colored fiber, to sleep above the ground, to distrust clear reflections, to harvest only from plants that do not turn their leaves away when touched.

Morning begins without coolness. The sky pales behind cloudbanks pressed low over the plateau, and the wet surfaces brighten one by one. Condensation falls from leaves like a second rain. Animals call in overlapping waves. Flowers exhale spice and carrion. The old towers give off a tone too low to hear directly, making the puddles tremble in circles. By noon the heat becomes immense. The light is ordinary in color, not mystical or dim, but thickened by vapor until every distance is softened. The sun glints on water skins, on beetle shells, on the slick backs of roots. Shadows are dark, crowded, alive with small motion.

Afternoon storms build slowly and almost ceremonially. Clouds climb from the gorges and assemble along the high ridges. The pressure changes. Insects fall quiet for a few breaths, then resume louder than before. The first drops are huge and hot. Then rain arrives with the force of a curtain dropped from the sky, flattening reeds, stippling pools white, turning cliff walls into shining organs of runoff. The structures endure without reaction. Water beads on their surfaces, gathers into perfect spheres, and rolls upward into hidden seams.

By evening, the storm has usually passed into mist. The plateau steams. Low places fill with pale vapor, leaving trees and tower tops rising like islands. Amphibian calls deepen. Something large moves through a lake, parting the floating plants in a slow black wedge. Lights appear in the marsh—not fireflies only, but old markers, fungal blooms, the underside pulses of drifting kite-creatures, and occasionally a straight line of cold radiance traveling beneath the water as if some submerged system has awakened and is checking the boundaries of its domain.

Night is not quiet. It is louder than day in some registers: chirps, knocks, bubbling mud, wingbeats, falling water, the creak of trees adjusting to their own weight. The heat remains, though the rocks release it slowly and the air becomes more breathable. Stars show only in fragments between clouds. In the pools they show differently. One may look down and see constellations that are not overhead, interrupted by the ripple of an insect leg.

The place gives the impression of being remote not because it is untouched, but because it has absorbed touch. Whatever built here did not conquer the plateau, or failed to, or chose a form of mastery indistinguishable from listening. The high wetlands, the steaming forests, the old engineered anomalies, the patient stone, the loud animal dark—all have settled into one climate of endurance.

It is a hot, elevated marsh-world balanced on ancient rock, lush and dripping at the edge of the possible. It changes constantly in droplets, calls, rot, bloom, and animal passage; yet in its larger body it moves slowly, like a giant breathing once every thousand years.
