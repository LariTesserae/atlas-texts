Below the surface, where stone ought to be cool and damp, there is a warm hollow the size of a province, a buried basin roofed in mineral and root-felt. It contains no water. Not a pool, not a seep, not a bead of condensation on the underside of the vault. The air is dry enough to rasp the throat raw, warm enough to keep all membranes open and all scents volatile, and yet the place is green beyond sense.

Not green in the way of forests. There are no trees as such, no grass, no soft moss drinking mist. The vegetation here has learned to live without water, or never learned water in the first place. It is a kingdom of plantlike matter built from wax, salt, resin, carbon thread, stored gases, crystal sugars, and a slow traffic of charged dust. It fills the cavern from floor to ceiling in strata: mats, columns, nets, hanging lobes, scrolls, plates, chimneys, bundled cables, ribbed curtains, hollow bulbs, seed-engines, veined sails. The entire basin seems woven from dry growth.

Every surface is alive.

The floor is not soil but a dense vegetal crust, springy in some places, brittle in others, a laminate of dead sheaths and living membranes. Step on it and it gives a little sigh of powder. Pale spores puff out in rings, not moist spores but glittering mineral motes, seed-dust with static hairs that cling to skin, chitin, stone, tool handles. Underfoot, the substrate is warm. In cracks, orange light pulses faintly from photosynthetic organs that do not need sunlight so much as heat and mineral radiation from the buried rock. Some fronds are translucent and leaf-thin, absorbing the dull cavern light. Others are black, matte, drinking thermal glow from vents far below. Others shine like hammered copper, not metal but plant-skin thickened with salts.

The cavern ceiling is high enough in places to disappear into a milky amber haze. Light comes from several sources at once, none of them dominant. There are broad panels of luminous lichen-plant stretched across the walls like old parchment windows. There are pearly bulbs suspended on braided stems, rocking in hot updrafts. There are mineral fissures in the vault, admitting thin shafts of exterior day filtered through hundreds of meters of cracked gypsum and root-vein, so that the light arrives diffuse, beige, almost civic. It is normal enough to see by, but nothing casts a trustworthy shadow. The shadows are eaten by leaves, or move independently because the leaves themselves are moving.

The basin is open, but not empty. It extends in terraces and long chambers, a buried valley system still deciding its shape. The walls have the unfinished quality of a place recently pushed apart. In places they are raw and pale, as if a giant hand peeled the strata open yesterday: chalk, pink salt, soft basalt, bands of ancient ash. But already the plant-mass has begun to fasten itself to the wounds. Rootlike fingers enter the cracks and prise them wider. Bulbous growths wedge under flakes of stone until they lift. Vine-cables tighten across newly opened rifts and hum under tension. The geology is not eroded by water or wind, but by growth. The land is being chewed into existence.

Nothing here is smooth for long. A ledge forms, and within days, perhaps hours, it sprouts hooks. A tunnel slumps inward, and a scaffold of stems props it open before the dust has settled. The terrain is embryonic, under construction by organisms too numerous and too impatient to wait for epochs.

The plants make architecture before there are builders.

Ramps spiral where climbing vines have thickened under foot traffic. Vaulted tunnels pass through trunks as broad as houses, their interiors smelling of warm cork, bitter resin, and crushed pepper. There are plazas of tough, flat plates where herds gather and scrape at salt blooms. There are bridges made from interlocked roots stretched over pits full of rattling seed-pods. Some bridges are strong; some are traps in the middle of becoming bridges. Along the walls, shelf-colonies project outward like balconies. Above them hang curtains of dry filament, constantly combed by passing animals and by the air itself.

The sound is immense.

It is not one sound, but all sounds layered until silence seems biologically impossible. Wings hiss and clatter. Shells click. Stems creak as they grow fast enough to be heard. Pods knock together in the updrafts like wooden bells. Insect-throats saw the air. Burrowing things grind through the floor crust. Herds of jointed grazers drum over the plates on hundreds of feet. Somewhere, periodically, a gas bladder bursts with a flat concussion, followed by the patter of seeds striking leaves, hides, stone, and other seeds. The cavern amplifies everything. Every squeal becomes a flock. Every scrape becomes weather.

At intervals, the entire basin roars.

This happens when the pressure changes. Warm gases seep upward from mineral faults below, and the plant-mass responds as though a command has passed through it. Thousands of vents open at once. Leaves invert. Bladders swell. Dust-laden air jets through organ-pipes grown into the walls. The result is a deafening dry storm, a storm with no rain and no cloud, only pollen, seed-flour, powdered salt, shed cuticle, wingbeats, and the static crackle of living surfaces exchanging charge. Visibility drops to a few body-lengths. Animals bellow, sing, panic, mate, molt, feed. The plants twist and present their receptive surfaces to the gale. For an hour the cavern becomes a throat.

Then the storm passes, and everything has changed position.

Fauna overwhelms the place because there is no clean separation between animal and plant, resident and architecture, parasite and citizen. Life uses every volume of air.

There are pale grazing beasts shaped like long-legged baskets, their rib cages open to the air and filled with symbiotic fronds. They move in loose herds across the vegetative flats, cropping brittle leaf-plates with sideways jaws. Their skin is dry and finely scaled, patterned with dust from whatever grove they last passed through. Each carries a small ecosystem: mites along the eyelids, seed-carriers under the belly, red ribbon-worms coiled in the hollows of the knees.

There are ballooning animals that never land if they can avoid it. They drift in the warm updrafts, translucent sacs veined with amber, trailing digestive threads that snag pollen and tiny flyers. Some are no bigger than fruit. Some are the size of tents. When startled, they release a pulse of bitter gas and shoot sideways, colliding with plant-curtains and setting off cascades of clicking pods.

There are insects in unbelievable numbers, though many are only insectlike: dry-bodied, lacquered, jointed, all rasp and mandible. They pour along root-bridges in black braided rivers. They farm scale colonies on the undersides of leaves. They cut grooves into soft plant-tissue and pack them with mineral dust. Their nests are not separate structures but negotiated swellings in the living substrate, chambers induced by chemical biting.

There are larger predators, too, but they are less obvious than the tumult of small life. A hunter may resemble a fallen bundle of stems until it unfolds too many legs and runs without sound across the crust. Another hangs among the ceiling filaments like a knotted shadow and drops calcified hooks into passing swarms. Some predators do not chase at all. They manipulate. They vibrate plant-cables at frequencies that mimic ripening, drawing grazers into corridors whose walls then tighten.

The ecology is founded on dryness. Water, in the old sense, is poison here, myth here, sacrament here, catastrophe here. The plants store no sap that flows; instead, they move charges, oils, and powdered nutrients through capillary braids lined with wax. Growth happens by deposition and tension. Cells inflate with gas rather than liquid. Seeds are often hard, faceted, nearly mineral, waiting not for rain but for abrasion, heat, digestion, or electrical shock.

Rot is rare. Nothing becomes wet enough to decay in the familiar way. The dead are milled. Beetles, raspers, root-teeth, wind-hairs, and grinding herds reduce old tissue into meal. Meal becomes insulation, camouflage, explosive dust, nest lining, memory. The whole cavern smells of resin, chalk, hot spice, bitter almond, sun-baked rope, old paper, animal musk, and the faint metallic sting that comes before lightning.

Because there is no water to carve the basin, the shapes of the land are muscular rather than fluvial. Channels are not streambeds but migration grooves. Basins are not ponds but congregation bowls where animals have stamped the living floor into tougher growth. Cliffs are propped, peeled, and webbed by roots. Canyons form where competing plant-colonies pull away from one another, leaving vertical seams full of territorial insects. Dunes exist, but they are dunes of chaff and powdered cuticle, constantly redistributed by wingstorms and by the breathing of the vault.

The world here has rules, though they are difficult to learn.

Do not step on blue-white crust; it is either asleep or listening.  
Do not cut a tense vine unless you know where both ends are anchored.  
Do not shout under hanging pods; they answer vibration with birth.  
Do not bring uncovered flame into seed-dust.  
Do not spill water.  
Above all, do not stand still too long where the floor feels pleasantly warm.

The scattered built things are easy to miss at first because the plants imitate structure better than people do. But they are there: low shelters of stacked salt-brick and woven stem, half swallowed by encroaching growth; little watch platforms lashed to mineral columns; ladders made of bone, cane, and hardened root; wind screens of cured hide stretched across openings to catch edible dust. Some are abandoned. Some are seasonal. Some are tended by people who have adapted to the dry green below.

Their settlements are not villages so much as agreements with the living terrain. A family may keep three chambers open by daily cutting, smoking, singing, and scraping resin from the threshold. A clan may claim a migration ramp by maintaining its lashings and feeding the resident guard-insects with powdered meal. A shrine may be nothing but a cleared patch around an ancient seed-stone, polished by generations of hands and hoofs. Metal is rare; stone, bone, shell, thorn, and baked mineral do most of the work. Blades are obsidian or tooth. Needles are spines. Rope is everywhere, but much of it is still alive and must be persuaded not to tighten.

People, if you encounter them, are wrapped against dust in plant-fiber veils glazed with wax. Their eyes are rimmed dark to cut glare. They carry hooked poles, seed rattles, resin plugs for the ears, and small pouches of precious moisture sealed in gut or ceramic, not for drinking casually but for medicine, oath, punishment, trade, or emergency germination. Their maps are tactile: knots, scratches, scent-stained cords. The cavern changes too quickly for drawn lines to remain true.

They speak with hand signs in the loud zones. In the storm hours they communicate by touch.

But most of the time, this place belongs to the nonhuman majority. The basin is a market with no merchants, an engine with no center, a riot of appetites. Its turbulence is not disorder exactly. It is growth so active it resembles violence.

A wall of pale fronds may advance visibly over the course of a day, each frond unrolling with a dry snap. A colony of grazers may strip it to ribs by dusk. In the night-warm hours, tunneling things undermine the exposed ribs, creating a sinkhole into which an entire insect city pours, relocating without pause. Predators follow. Seed-pods triggered by the collapse fire themselves across the chamber. Where they land, some are eaten, some crack, some lodge in hot stone and begin to swell. By morning, there is a new thicket, a new path, a new hazard, a new sound added to the roar.

The cavern makes and unmakes landmarks. A pillar that yesterday resembled a twisted tower may split today into four petals, revealing chambers full of dormant larvae. A bridge may flower and become impassable, then be eaten hollow by silver borers and become a tunnel. A dead beast may fall, and within hours be scaffolded by opportunistic vines, lifted slightly off the ground, and converted into a suspended nursery. Dust avalanches from the ceiling whenever root pressure breaks a seam. The dust itself may bloom if the charge is right.

In some districts, the strangeness deepens further. There are groves where plant-columns pulse in synchrony with distant animal herds, though no physical contact links them. There are fields of flat black leaves that arrange themselves into temporary patterns resembling writing when swarms pass overhead. There are hollows in which sound behaves like matter: cries collect in fibrous bowls and are released later as ghost choruses, confusing hunters and travelers. There are seed-pods that germinate into imitations of the last thing that touched them—a hoof, a hand, a beetle, a tool—producing useless but unnerving replicas in dry green tissue.

Far below, where cracks open into darker warmth, the oldest growths are said to live. They are not trees but regional organs, root-minds spread through the basin’s underlayers, coordinating bloom, collapse, lure, and retreat through pressure pulses and mineral taste. Perhaps they are conscious; perhaps consciousness is too small a word. When they shift, distant terraces tremble. When they fruit, whole populations migrate. When they sleep, the sound drops just enough for travelers to realize how loud the world has been, and fear the quiet more than the noise.

The light during these pauses is almost gentle. Amber bulbs sway. Dust turns slowly in the air like powdered gold. The living floor flexes under unseen motion. Shell-backed animals pick their way through fallen curtains. Tiny winged things gather around warm stones. A human shelter, half hidden under waxy leaves, exhales a thread of smoke from a dung-and-resin fire. For a few minutes, the buried valley seems habitable, even tender.

Then a pod cracks somewhere with the report of breaking bone.

A thousand wings answer.

The floor shivers. The root-bridges tighten. Herds begin to move before anyone knows why. Fronds fold away from one corridor and open toward another. Insects flood the walls in glossy sheets. Warm gas rises through vents with a rising organ note. The air thickens with seed-dust. The architecture rearranges its intentions.

This is a dry underworld overfull with life, a waterless jungle still being born, a cavern-valley where plants are ground, weather, machine, law, and city; where animals are as numerous as sparks in a fire; where sound has weight; where the land is not yet finished forming and may never finish, because every surface is busy becoming something else.
