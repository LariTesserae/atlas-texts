Beneath the city there is a lower district that was not exactly planned as a district. It began as service space: drainage rooms, storage vaults, heat tunnels, bricked culverts, a sump basin enlarged and then enlarged again, until the understructure had the awkward completeness of a place people could name.

They call it the Warm Levels.

It lies just under the streets, but not evenly. In some places the ceiling is low enough that pipes run beside your shoulder and old mineral sweat beads on the brick. In others the space rises into a chamber the size of a small station hall, with balconies of concrete, iron stairs, fuse boxes, catwalks, and long ducts disappearing into drilled openings. The ground is below the official surface of the city, but it has its own surfaces: walkways, trench bridges, raised platforms, damp service roads no wider than a cart, islands of poured concrete surrounded by black water.

Everything is hot.

Not furnace-hot, not blazing, but the exhausted heat of machinery and depth: a constant, enclosed warmth that gets into clothing and stays there. Steam presses lightly at the skin. The air tastes metallic, with a little salt, a little mold, and the flat sweetness of standing water. Pipes carry municipal heat through the vaults, wrapped in sweating insulation and flaking silver jackets. Their valves are painted red or blue or an old industrial green, though most of the paint is swollen and furred at the edges. Condensation collects on ceilings and cable trays, then drops in large, deliberate beads. The whole place weeps.

Water is never entirely absent, but it is rarely deep. It gathers in seams. It lies in the shallow channels between paving slabs. It shines in the bottoms of inspection pits and along the edges of rail tracks that no train uses now except maintenance trolleys. After rain aboveground, the Warm Levels wake in a different way: gutters pour through hidden shafts, sump pumps start up one after another, and brown water moves through grates with a patient force. The drains do not flood so much as breathe. They take water in, hold it, pass it along.

The geology here feels unfinished. Not wild, but newly exposed, raw where the city cut into it. Basalt and clay show in unlined stretches between retaining walls, the stone still bearing drill scars and the blunt grammar of excavation. There are patches of sprayed concrete where the tunnel skin has not fully cured into age, and the rock behind it bulges as if still deciding its shape. Mineral films bloom along cracks: white lime, orange iron, greenish salts. Little stalactites have begun on bolts and ledges, no longer than fingers, fragile as candle drips. The place is young in a geological sense and old in a municipal one.

The built parts press close, but they are not crowded in the manner of a market or a tenement. They are scattered in functional clusters, with intervals of dimness between them. A pump room here. A transformer cage there. A row of storage lockers. A brick arch with numbers stenciled above it. A control booth whose windows are filmed opaque by grime. One chamber contains a fan the diameter of a small house, turning slowly behind a guard of rusted mesh. Another holds three settling tanks, circular and black, each crossed by narrow metal bridges. Another is no more than a platform with a bench, a telephone, a breaker panel, and a shrine of lost objects: hard hats, gloves, keys, a cracked mug, a child’s plastic horse washed down from somewhere above.

Light is dim and utilitarian. Amber bulbs burn behind wire cages. Fluorescent tubes buzz with weak blue persistence. In some passages, daylight enters only as a diluted gray falling through pavement grates, striped by footsteps and traffic. The brightest places are not beautiful but overlit: work zones where a portable lamp glares on wet concrete, making the shadows deeper everywhere else. Most of the Warm Levels remain in half-light, enough to see motion and edges, not enough to trust color.

There is little vegetation. A few ferns grow where warm seepage meets grit under a ventilation shaft. Pale grasses have rooted in the silt collected along a wall seam, thin and dust-coated, leaning toward a grate of daylight thirty feet above. Moss appears in dull pads on the oldest brick, but it dries to brown whenever the pumps run too long. No tree could live here. No vine becomes lush. Plant life exists as an accident, an annotation, something that found a crack and made a modest claim.

Animal life, by contrast, has fully accepted the place.

Rats are everywhere, though not always seen directly. They move inside walls and under platforms, a busy undertone of claws, whiskers, and soft bodies passing through refuse. They are large from the warmth and the reliable food washed down from the city: grain, grease, peelings, insects, drowned things. They travel in gutters as if along streets. Their paths are worn into dust on pipe jackets. They pause at thresholds, rise on hind legs, appraise, vanish.

Pigeons nest in high ledges where ventilation shafts open to the surface. They come down in sudden clattering flocks to drink from shallow basins, their feathers dulled by soot, their feet pink against black mud. Swallows sometimes enter during the hot months and stitch the larger chambers with flight, hunting the insects that gather around lamps. Cockroaches own the warm electrical rooms. Silverfish tremble in paper stores. Mosquitoes breed in forgotten pans of water and form small, whining weather systems above the sumps. Moths sleep in ranks on tiles. Spiders profit immensely.

In the wettest chamber, beneath the market quarter, frogs have established themselves. No one knows when they arrived. Their bodies are the color of wet concrete, and they sit among bottle caps, algae threads, and orange rust flakes at the edge of the settling pools. At night their calling rises into the pipes and returns distorted through the grates above, so that people on the street hear a hollow, rhythmic croaking from beneath their shoes.

Cats come down too. Some belong to no one; some are known by the workers and fed at certain doors. They move through the Warm Levels with proprietary calm, stepping over hoses, watching the rats, sleeping on transformer housings where the metal is warm. Their eyes catch the weak light and send it back green.

The fauna is not exotic. That is part of the pressure of it. Nothing here should surprise anyone, yet the sheer abundance of ordinary animals makes the place feel inhabited beyond human permission. The undercity is less a machine than a den around which machines have been built.

Sound fills it in layers. Pumps thud and hum. Fans drag air through ducts with a low continuous roar. Water ticks, runs, pours, gurgles, slaps in channels, falls from pipe mouths into sumps. Relays click. Distant traffic becomes a rubbed vibration in the ceiling. Somewhere metal expands with a knock like a footstep. Somewhere a generator misfires and catches itself. Pigeons burst upward with a drum of wings. Rats squeal in the walls. Frogs call from the tanks. The whole place is loud, but not with urgency. It is the loudness of systems continuing.

Human activity is present but intermittent. Workers descend through locked stairwells in pairs, carrying lamps and tablets, their boots leaving prints that slowly fill with water. They read gauges, grease bearings, clear trash from grates, test alarms, replace filters. A small crew maintains the pumps in the central chamber, where the air is hottest and the floor is painted with yellow safety lines. There are lockers, a kettle, a calendar curled from damp, and a radio that plays badly beneath the fan noise.

A few marginal trades have grown around the official infrastructure. In one side room, a mechanic repairs municipal scooters and illegal delivery carts under a hanging lamp. In another, vendors store crates of fruit during the day because the heat ripens them quickly, though too long below and they turn. There are locked cages rented by shops aboveground for overflow stock: spare tiles, restaurant chairs, sacks of salt, broken display mannequins, festival decorations wrapped in plastic. The Warm Levels are urban not because many people live there, but because the city’s logic is thick in every direction. Numbering, routing, supply, access, repair, ownership, warning signage, unauthorized use: all of it continues underground.

The place has rules, practical ones learned by repetition.

Never step where the water is perfectly still and black; that may be a deep pit with no rail.

Never trust a dry wall in the hot months; dry means heat, and heat means live lines or steam behind it.

If the pigeons lift all at once, wait before crossing the chamber. Something larger may be moving.

If the pump tone drops, leave the low walkways.

If you smell eggs, open nothing.

Do not block a rat path with your bag. It will become your problem.

People who know the Warm Levels listen more than they look. They can tell from a changed echo whether a door has been opened down-tunnel, from the sharpness of dripping whether rain has started above, from the pattern of fan noise whether the market vents are clogged. The underdistrict changes slowly enough that memory is useful. A crack widens over years. A mineral stain lengthens season by season. A colony of insects appears around one lamp and is gone by winter. A new pipe is installed bright and labeled, then dulls, sweats, is wrapped, patched, and accepted into the general age.

Despite the heat and animals and machinery, the place is not chaotic. It is patient. Processes accumulate. Water makes a line in concrete. Steam softens paint. Rats discover a route and make it permanent. Workers add a bracket, a cable, a warning plate. Silt settles. Algae darkens a drain lip. The frogs expand one pool farther each year. The city above sheds warmth, waste, sound, rain, crumbs, lost coins, hair, and the Warm Levels receive them without drama.

At the boundary of the district, where the service tunnels narrow and the constructed walls give way to damp cut rock, the air becomes quieter but hotter. The lights are farther apart. Here the foundations are visible: footings, piles, rough stone, concrete poured against earth. Water seeps down in thin shining veils. Roots from some tree above have found the cracks and hang in black threads, sparse and astonishing. Cockroaches scatter from the beam of a lamp. A rat watches from a pipe mouth, unafraid.

The city is only a few meters overhead. Its shops open and close, buses brake, people argue, rain darkens the pavement. Down here, the same city is slower, warmer, and more animal. It is still being made. It is being dissolved and built at once, not ruined, not new, but embryonic in the dark: a small underworld of pumps, damp concrete, hot air, and countless ordinary creatures living in the seams.
