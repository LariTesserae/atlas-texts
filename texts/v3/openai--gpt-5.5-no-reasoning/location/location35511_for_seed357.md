The first impression is of height and heat: a plateau lifted into cloud, wide enough that its edges are not visible except where vapors thin and reveal sudden drops into pale air. The sky is close, not dark, not especially bright, but thick with normal daylight made heavy by moisture. Light arrives diffused and warm, pressing through suspended droplets until everything seems varnished. There are no far blue distances. The horizon is a green-gold blur, trembling in the steam rising from the ground.

Nothing here is bare mineral.

The entire highland has been made out of plants.

Not planted, exactly. Made. Constructed. The land itself is a living architecture: terraces, walls, gutters, vaults, towers, causeways, catchment basins, sluices, ramps, buttresses, buttressed buttresses, all grown from interlocked trunks, braided roots, inflated stems, bark plates, reed bundles, liana cables, leaf membranes, and mats of veined tissue thick enough to walk on. There is no exposed rock underfoot, no soil except the dark compost packed into creases and hollows as an interior material. Even the cliffs, where the plateau breaks, are not stone faces but sheer hanging curtains of root and woody fiber, pale where newly split, dark where slick with rainwater and microbial varnish.

It is a built world without builders.

Or else the builders are the buildings, and have forgotten the difference.

The plateau appears young. Its surfaces are too sharp, too freshly swollen, too unfinished. Edges have not sagged into old equilibrium. Newly extruded ridgelines run in crisp seams across the living terrain, like welds in green wax. Towers lean at uncertain angles, still thickening, still deciding what they are. Channels have cut only shallow grooves through the plantwork, and many of them are already being overgrown or redirected. Fresh growth splits older surfaces with a sound like damp cloth tearing. Here and there entire ramps have blistered upward overnight, their undersides pale and tender, supported by expanding root-arches that drip sap and condensed fog.

The heat is constant but not dry. It is the heat of a greenhouse built over a mountain: humid, mineral-less, breath-heavy. Every surface sweats. Water gathers on the undersides of leaves and falls in fat drops onto membranes stretched below. It beads on lacquered bark, pours through root-gutters, seeps from swollen walls, stands in shallow basins, and vanishes into thirsty spongewood floors. Rain is frequent, but even between rains the place continues to rain upon itself. Condensation forms in the upper canopies and trickles downward through the structure in thousands of small routes. The sound is not a simple patter. It is a layered wet roar.

Water slaps into cistern-leaves the size of plazas.  
Water ticks from aerial roots.  
Water gurgles in hidden throats underfoot.  
Water exhales from vents as vapor.  
Water sheets suddenly from overfilled terraces, white and hot in the light.  
Water drums inside hollow trunks, resonant as distant kettles.

The landscape is loud, but not with animals. There are no calls, no wings, no insects, no chewing, no rustle of bodies moving through cover. The absence is unnerving at first. In a place so alive, one expects creatures. But the only life is vegetal, fungal, algal, bacterial perhaps—if those distinctions still matter here. The noise belongs to weather and growth. Stems creak under internal pressure. Pods pop open with small detonations, not to scatter seeds to birds or soil, but to release wet ropes of tissue that slap onto nearby frames and begin fastening themselves. Vine-cables tense and untwist. Floating mats collide in flooded courts. Great leaves flex and clap as warm gusts push through the high structures.

The vegetation is nearly total, yet the density of presences is strangely sparse. The plateau is not a jungle in the ordinary sense. It is open, spacious, architectural. Vast courts of living floor spread between widely spaced columns. Root-bridges cross empty air with long, elegant spans. Vertical screens of leaf tissue hang like banners from high ribs, far apart from one another, so that one can see through successive layers into green distance. The plant mass is everywhere, but it is not crowded. It has organized itself into streets, plazas, channels, amphitheaters, long drainage aisles, and high galleries open to the steaming sky.

Perhaps this is because it must shed water. Perhaps because it needs wind. Perhaps because the whole plateau is still expanding and has not yet filled its own plan.

In the lower courts, the walking surfaces are springy and slightly warm. They are paved with hexagonal pads of corky bark, each pad edged by a seam of moss-bright tissue. Step on them and they darken briefly with expressed water, then recover. Between pads, narrow veins pulse with amber sap, not visibly flowing in one direction but shuttling, reconsidering, rerouting. Some avenues are bordered by living curbs that rise knee-high, then waist-high, then collapse into troughs where runoff gathers. Some are newly grown and raw, their surfaces pink-green and sensitive, recoiling slightly from pressure.

The watercourses are the busiest parts of the highland. They are not rivers in the usual sense, because their beds are grown and regrown continuously. A channel may begin as a neat trough lined with glossy leaves folded edge to edge, then descend through a woven sluice of roots, then break into a hundred dangling threads pouring into a basin, then disappear into a lung-like chamber beneath a platform. Outlets open and close. Overflow is deliberate but not planned by mind. When rain thickens, the whole constructed vegetation alters its hydraulics: ribs lift, basins tilt, spillways unfurl, membranes pucker into dams, root bundles loosen to let torrents through. The plateau becomes a machine of plant-water with no metal and no stillness.

At times a whole district shifts.

One can be looking across an open square of flat green panels toward a row of tall stem-pillars, and then a tremor passes through the substrate. The panels bow upward, shedding their standing water in glittering sheets. The pillars thicken at the base. A long diagonal seam appears where there was none, then splits, revealing a new drainage throat dark and wet inside. The square ceases to be a square and becomes a basin. The basin fills. The basin drains. Floating rafts of leaf tissue detach from its margins and are carried away into channels not yet present a moment before.

The turbulence is not violent like an earthquake, though it can be dangerous. It is biological weather. Creation and erosion happen together, fast enough to witness. Structures are born soft and bright, harden for a time, then delaminate under heat and water. Walkways buckle and are reabsorbed. Balconies grow out from walls, produce curtains of aerial roots, then collapse into hanging gardens over chasms. A tower may split down one side and peel open into a fan of photosynthetic panels, exposing chambers filled with warm mist. Nothing is ruined, because nothing here seems to regard form as permanent. Use is temporary. Shape is a phase.

There are high places within the high place: green spires, crown-platforms, ridge-crests of compacted trunk tissue from which the wider plateau can be read. From there, the landscape extends for miles as a made vegetal province. It has districts. To the east, an expanse of enormous shallow bowls collects rainwater, each bowl rimmed by pale reeds as tall as buildings, their tips venting vapor. To the south, a field of columnar growths stands widely spaced like an unfinished temple, with root-bridges between them at several heights. To the west, a descending series of terraces catches overflow and sends it plunging from one level to another, the falls half-hidden behind hanging curtains of green. Northward, the plateau rises into sharper new formations: ridges of stiff leaf-blades and lattices of thornless black vine, glossy and wet, still too young to have sagged under their own mass.

There are no tools, no machines, no cut marks, no masonry, no worked timber in the dead sense. Yet the made quality is unmistakable. Repetition occurs. Symmetry tries to happen, though water disrupts it. There are thresholds, chambers, conduits, retaining walls, skylit corridors, reservoirs, raised roads, and what might be plazas for gatherings that never occur. Some surfaces bear patterns like woven basketry, but the fibers are alive and continue to tighten. Some walls show spirals of leaf-scars arranged with mathematical patience. Long arches span ravines, their undersides ribbed like the inside of a seedpod. At junctions, swollen knots resemble hubs, but nothing rotates; instead, growth decisions radiate outward from them in pulses.

The rules of the world seem rooted in pressure, thirst, weight, light, and flow.

Where water lingers, walls grow higher.  
Where heat concentrates, vents open.  
Where mist thickens, leaf-sails unfurl.  
Where a surface is shaded too long, it is dismantled from below and its matter sent elsewhere.  
Where open air passes steadily, root-cables bridge it.  
Where standing water grows deep, buoyant floors detach and become temporary islands.

Everything negotiates with gravity. Because the plateau is high, the drops are immense. At the edges, entire rivers fall out of the living architecture, breaking into vapor long before they reach whatever lies below. The cliff margins are ragged and actively weaving themselves outward. Root-nets dangle into emptiness, catch cloud, drink it, and thicken. New ledges bud from the vertical faces as pale shelves no wider than a hand, then broaden into balconies dense enough to hold water. Some fail. When they tear loose, they do not fall as rockfall would. They unravel, shedding strands, leaves, membranes, droplets, and long banners of tissue that flutter downward into fog.

The smell is immense: crushed green, hot sap, wet bark, fermenting leaf pulp, clean rain, sour anaerobic hollows, flowerless nectar, and the faint peppery odor of new cellulose. There are no blossoms in the familiar sense, perhaps because there are no pollinators. Reproduction is by contact, division, overflow, pressure, self-grafting. Growths bud from growths. Runners leap gaps. Spores, if present, are released into mist and caught immediately on receptive wet skins. The place does not advertise. It spreads by touching itself.

Color is mostly green, but that means little. There are blue-black wet leaves, yellow-green translucent membranes, coppery young bark, gray-white root bones, red sap-veins, and pale inner tissues exposed by recent splitting. Pools mirror the sky in dull silver. In shaded drainage halls the plant surfaces turn deep emerald and almost blue. In the open heat they bleach toward lime and gold. Some of the highest leaf-panels are semi-transparent, and when the sun presses through cloud behind them they glow like lantern cloth.

Sound changes with weather. In a calm hour, if such an hour can be called calm, one hears the plateau working: the creak of expanding ribs, the throb of hidden water, the soft percussion of drips, the slap of leaves adjusting their angles, the occasional wet crack of a seam opening. But storms rise quickly at this elevation. Clouds come not from above but sideways, striking the plant-architecture and condensing instantly. Rain begins as a hiss, then becomes a roar. The open courts fill with warm spray. Drainage towers boom. Overflow cataracts appear in places that were dry a minute before. Whole walls ripple as water weight transfers through them. Broad leaves invert under the load, dumping sudden sheets into channels below. The air becomes a white-green confusion of moving water and flexing plantwork.

Then, just as quickly, the rain thins. Steam rises. The architecture glistens, altered. A new bridge may have formed from vines drawn taut by absorbed water. A terrace may have slumped into a marshy amphitheater. A row of upright membranes may have folded into a roof. A basin may have emptied and revealed beneath it a patterned floor of breathing pores, opening and closing in the heat.

Despite all this motion, the place has a kind of solemn emptiness. Its scale suggests habitation, but no one inhabits it. Its plazas invite crowds, but no feet cross them except perhaps the imagined observer’s. Its corridors lead somewhere, then redirect themselves before arrival. Its shelters shelter only humidity. Its reservoirs store water for no animals. Its bridges connect districts that are all the same organism or alliance of organisms, so that crossing them feels like traveling across the surface of a thought that has not become conscious.

The strangeness lies partly in that almost-intention. The plateau behaves as if it remembers cities from a world that never had people. Or as if architecture is simply what vegetation becomes when heat, water, height, and instability are pushed far enough. A forest, given no animals and no stone, may still discover columns. A marsh lifted into the sky may still invent streets to move rain. A plant may become a wall not because anyone needs a wall, but because surfaces need to divide vapor, hold light, brace against wind, and conduct water toward hunger.

At night—if night comes through the vapor rather than simply dimming the green—the place would not become quiet. It would cool only slightly. Condensation would increase. The high leaf-sails would fold, and moisture would run from them in silver threads. Hidden chambers would gulp water. Some surfaces would soften and reconfigure in darkness, conserving light-facing forms for morning. Without animals, there would be no nocturnal chorus, only the intensified plumbing of the living plateau and the slow, muscular noises of growth.

But now, in the warm ordinary light, the constructed marsh-highland continues its restless making. Open spaces steam. Green pillars stand apart like unfinished decisions. Water threads every level. New tissue gleams along every seam. The world is young, wet, hot, elevated, almost empty, entirely alive, entirely built, and never the same shape long enough to be named.
