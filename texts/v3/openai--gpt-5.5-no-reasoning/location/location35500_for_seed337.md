The town is under the hill, but not deeply. It occupies the shallow underside of the land: a broad, cool hollow between layers of chalk, clay, gravel, and old root-dark soil, where the earth has slumped and been cut and propped open by generations of hands. From outside, on the upland, there are only signs of it — low vent chimneys of stacked stone, smoke-stained wicker grates, wet paths vanishing into banks of fernless turf, narrow stair mouths roofed with thatch and clay tile. The surface is a sparse country of pale grasses, thorns, and open wind. The settlement itself begins where daylight slides down ramps and courtyards and becomes a softer, reflected thing.

It is not a cave city in the grand sense. There are no glittering caverns, no immense stalactites, no depths that seem older than thought. Everything here feels newly made, or still being made. The walls are raw-cut and uneven, showing the marks of antler picks, stone mauls, bronze-edged adzes, fire-setting, scraping blades, palms. The geology has not yet had time to become picturesque. It is exposed, wounded, practical: damp clay pressed with straw, pale limestone rubbed smooth only where shoulders pass, timber ribs darkening in the cool air, woven hurdles plastered over with mud, fresh mortar still smelling of lime and ash.

The place is embryonic. Streets are not streets so much as decisions repeated often enough to become routes. Passageways bend around harder stone, around old tree roots, around chambers where a family was already living before the planners came with measuring cords. Some corridors are high enough for a cart animal, though few animals are kept below; others require a bowed head and sideways shoulders. In many places the ceiling is braced with split trunks, their bark still clinging in strips. Pegs, wedges, baskets, ladders, drains, half-built stairs, ropes, stored beams, clay bricks drying on shelves: every surface is occupied by some stage of construction or repair.

There is no spacious emptiness here. Even where a chamber widens into a market court or work hollow, it is packed. Not crowded with bodies alone, but with use. Everything has a second function and a third. Benches are storage chests. Steps are sleeping platforms. Wall niches hold oil lamps, spare sandals, wrapped tools, jars of salt, dried roots, tally sticks, offerings to household spirits, bundles of rush wicks, hooks with wet cloaks. Roof beams carry hanging baskets. Drainage channels run beneath removable planks. A stair landing becomes a loom room by day and a sleeping bay by night. Clay pipes pass through walls, carrying seepwater away from one chamber and into another where it can be collected, settled, strained, and poured into troughs.

The town has grown by pressure rather than plan. Families arrived, dug, wove, shored, claimed, subdivided. Then workshops came. Then storehouses. Then the channels. Then the lower courts, and after those the air shafts, and then the new ring of chambers beyond the old clay wall. It has become settlement-scale almost by accident, a buried district stretching under a whole fold of country. One can walk for an hour beneath the hill and remain among human works: granaries cut into cool banks; potters’ dens with smoke holes and clay settling pits; damp cellars where cheese, seed grain, and root crops are kept; low shrines lit by grey daylight from above; sleeping warrens; assembly hollows; cistern rooms; latrine trenches screened by reed mats; crowded stairwells where children learn to keep to the right by being bumped into walls.

The light is ordinary but displaced. At midday, shafts cut through the overlying earth admit columns of pale sun full of dust and gnats. Courtyards open unexpectedly to the sky, not wide plazas but vertical bites through the hill, their walls terraced with doorways and ladderways. In these openings, one sees weather: a square of cloud, drizzle stippling a cistern, cold light on hanging laundry, the silhouettes of thorn branches above. Elsewhere the light is borrowed from polished plaster, pale clay floors, water surfaces, and small lamps set behind thin horn or scraped hide. The place is not gloomy, exactly. It is lit as a burrow might be lit if a town had decided to live like a burrow without surrendering its need for day.

Moisture is intermittent and carefully watched. The hill is mostly dry, but it sweats in seams. After rain, the ceilings bead with condensation in certain passages, and the chalk walls darken along hidden cracks. Drops gather on root tips and fall into pots placed for that purpose. In some rooms, shallow channels have been cut into the floors, guiding seepwater toward covered jars. In others, the damp is a threat: plaster blisters, grain molds, timbers soften, fleas multiply in bedding. The people know the moods of each wall. They know which corner smells like rain before weather reaches the surface, which stair grows slick in autumn, which storage niche must never be used for flour, which courtyard floods ankle-deep if leaves clog the upper grating.

There is a stream somewhere below or beside the town, not visible in most places but constantly implied. Some passages slope toward it. Certain drains murmur after storms. A few lower chambers open into a long, stone-lined water hall where a narrow dark runnel passes through gravel beds and disappears under a lintel too low for an adult to follow. Here the air is colder. The walls are greened with a thin skin of algae where light enters from a slot above. Clay jars stand in rows, and children are sent with handled pots to fetch household water. No one wastes it, though the town is damp. Dampness is not the same as abundance. Much of the moisture is caught in walls, in air, in clay, in condensation that must be coaxed down drop by drop.

Vegetation is sparse below, but not absent. It appears wherever light and water briefly agree. In the courtyards grow tough herbs in broken pots: sorrel, bitter cress, onions, a few medicinal plants guarded by old women with sharp tongues. Moss darkens the north sides of shafts. Pale fungi fruit from forgotten wicker. Roots enter from above like thin brown cords and are either cut away or left if they help bind a ceiling. Reed bundles dry from rafters. Straw is everywhere, but as material more than living plant: braided into mats, mixed into plaster, stuffed into bedding, twisted into rope, woven as screens. Outside, on the surface, there are low thorn bushes, wiry grass, small patches of cultivated ground, and wind-bent shrubs that seem to belong to a harsher, emptier world than the packed town beneath.

The temperature remains cool even in summer. Heat from cooking fires gathers in ceilings and escapes through smoke vents, leaving rooms smoky but not warm for long. In winter, the buried earth protects the town from the worst frost; in spring, it holds the cold past its welcome. People wear layered wool, hide vests, linen or nettle-cloth undergarments, felt caps in the outer passages. The air smells of damp mineral, ash, bodies, wool grease, fermenting grain, clay, old straw, and wet wood. At times, near the potters’ quarter, there is the dense scent of fired earth. Near the tanners’ pits, the air turns sour and ammoniac, and people hurry past with collars over their noses.

Sound is quiet, not because nothing happens, but because earth swallows noise. The town is active in a muffled way. Footsteps on packed clay. The scrape of a shovel. A hand loom knocking softly in a chamber beyond a wall. Low conversation. Water ticking into a basin. A baby crying and then being hushed. The distant dull thump of someone driving a post. A stone quern grinding grain with a slow circular rasp. Rain above is first silence, then a faint grainy whisper in the shafts, then drips beginning one after another until the whole place seems to count time.

No single sound carries far unless it travels through a tunnel aligned just so. A shout becomes a flattened bark. A song from an upper courtyard arrives below as melody without words. The loudest regular noises are work noises: mallets on pegs, baskets dragged over floors, clay slabs slapped into place, timber creaking under load, cart wheels complaining on plank tracks in the broader ways. Even these are contained. The settlement has learned to live in half-voice. People gesture often. They tap walls to announce themselves around blind corners. In some districts, strings of shells or bones hang across passage mouths to give a soft warning when someone enters.

Animals are few. Chickens are kept near the surface yards, scratching in light wells and making brief outbreaks of noise. A handful of goats are led down only in bad weather and disliked for their appetite for reed screens, rope, and anything drying within reach. Dogs exist but are lean, quiet, and purposeful; they sleep near gates, follow hunters, or nose after rats. Rats are present, though everyone pretends the traps and cats keep them controlled. There are beetles in grain rooms, pale worms in damp clay, spiders in smoke vents, moths in wool, frogs after heavy rains in the lower drains. Swallows nest in some of the upper shafts during warm months and vanish when the air cools.

Because fauna is rare, every animal presence seems pointed. A bat slipping through a smoke hole at dusk draws eyes. The sudden skitter of claws behind a wall makes workers pause. A fox sometimes appears at an outer entrance, thin and red, looking in with offended curiosity before retreating to the grassland above. Fish from the lower stream are small and infrequent, caught in woven traps after rains. They are eaten whole, with gratitude.

The world here is normal, governed by weight, damp, hunger, weather, habit, and the limits of hand tools. Nothing floats unless smoke or dust lifts it. Nothing glows unless fire is fed. The rules are the rules of settlement under pressure: shore before you dig farther; keep vents clear; do not foul upstream water; announce smoke; share lamps in the public ways; never take another household’s dry straw; never block a drainage groove, even briefly; never sleep in an unfinished chamber during rain; do not cut roots unless someone who knows the ceiling has approved it.

There are marks on walls indicating these rules. Some are painted in red earth, some scratched, some made by pattern alone: three vertical cuts for a weak roof, a circle for a water jar station, a chevron for a smoke draft, a crosshatch for no flame beyond this point. Literacy, if it exists, is not widespread or not needed for most warnings. The town reads itself through symbols, stains, slopes, smells, and scars.

It is active everywhere, though slowly. Construction continues along the eastern reach, where new family chambers are being dug into clay that is still soft enough to slice but firm enough to stand. Young workers carry spoil in baskets to old pits and surface mounds. Children stomp straw into mud for plaster. Elder builders test walls by pressing ears to them after rain. In the western quarter, old rooms are being filled and abandoned because the roof has begun to sag. Not every part of the town expands; some parts are sacrificed, packed with rubble, sealed with clay, remembered as warnings.

Weather cycles shape the days. In dry spells, dust rises from floors despite the underground cool. The cistern lids are checked, and the potters complain that clay shrinks too fast near the vents. During light rain, people relax; seep lines wake, jars fill, courtyards smell clean. During heavy rain, the town becomes vigilant. Screens are pulled over shaft mouths. Children are kept from lower passages. Men and women with poles clear drains from above. Someone is always listening for the wrong kind of water: not dripping, not running, but pressing — that thick, silent arrival behind a wall before a collapse.

The terrain beneath the settlement is layered and irregular. Some chambers are cut into chalky stone that flakes pale dust onto shoulders. Others are within compact brown clay, smooth as kneaded bread when fresh and hardening after exposure. Gravel pockets interrupt floors unexpectedly and must be paved over with flat stones or woven mats. There are old voids too: animal burrows enlarged by people, root channels, sink pockets, dry seams where water once passed and may pass again. The town is young enough that every new excavation feels like discovery. No map is fully trusted. The hill keeps adding qualifications.

Materials are local and immediate. Clay from the walls becomes bricks for the walls. Timber from sparse groves above is too precious to waste, so beams are reused until they are nearly fiber. Stone is gathered from spoil and streambeds, sorted by size, set into drains, thresholds, hearth rings. Reed, straw, bark, hide, bone, horn, sinew, flax, nettle fiber — all appear in construction. Metal is present but humble: bronze knives, awls, pins, small chisels, perhaps a few iron bits if trade has reached them, though most heavy work is still done with stone, wood, fire, and patience.

The built environment feels urban not because it is monumental, but because it is layered with agreements. There are shared corridors, public water points, work districts, storage regulations, communal hearths, waste routes, sleeping rights, craft zones, market days, repair obligations, and disputes over inches of passage. It has the density of a city compressed into the body of a hill. Goods move through it in baskets and sledges. News travels by stairwells. Alliances form around wells, kilns, marriage chambers, smoke vents, and roof repairs.

At the central court, where several ramps meet beneath an open shaft, the settlement briefly becomes almost bright. Rain falls there when it rains; sun reaches the floor for a short time each day. The ground is paved with mismatched stones and patched clay. Around the sides are stalls and work ledges: baskets of dried roots, folded wool, bone needles, chipped blades, smoked fish, lamp fat, coarse salt, clay cups, lengths of cord. Above, galleries have been cut into the court walls, their fronts supported by posts and woven railings. People lean from them to speak downward. Laundry drips. Children climb where they should not. Smoke escapes reluctantly upward in blue veils.

This is the closest the town comes to an open square, yet even here there is no emptiness. Every ledge holds something. Every wall has a shelf, a peg, a marking, a patch. The eye finds layer after layer: plaster over wattle over cut clay over chalk over older fill over a blocked chamber whose door outline remains visible beneath limewash. The place is not old, but it is already palimpsest.

At night, the town contracts. Shafts go black. Lamps reduce passages to amber pools. The cooler air sinks into lower rooms, and the smell of damp stone sharpens. Work does not entirely stop. Someone always tends a kiln, watches a sick child, drains a seep, kneads dough for morning, listens to rain, keeps embers alive. But the quiet deepens until the smallest sounds become important: a drip missing its bowl, a timber settling, a rat trap snapping, breath behind a curtain.

Above, under the normal sky, the hill looks nearly empty. Sparse grass bends in the wind. A few vents breathe smoke. Rain gathers in hollows and disappears into grates. Footpaths cross the slopes like worn threads. The real density is hidden just below: a town still coming into being, damp in patches, cool in its bones, full of raw surfaces and human adjustments.

It feels less like a place carved out of the earth than a place the earth and people are jointly making, neither side finished. The hill yields chambers but demands supports. Water gives itself only through seep and storm. Light descends where cuts are kept open. Every day the settlement must be remade slightly: dried, braced, scraped, patched, swept, shored, extended, sealed. Its life is not dramatic. It is continuous. A quiet pressure of hands against matter. A buried town learning the shape it will one day have.
