At this height the world has thinned out.

The land is a vast cold plateau, not flat exactly, but worn so long by wind, frost, and patient water that its violence has settled into posture. It rises in broad, slow swells and falls away into basins too wide to read at once. The horizon is almost always present and almost always incomplete, hidden by a low dimness, by suspended ice-mist, by curtains of vapor that do not move so much as alter their density. Nothing here feels newly broken. Even the cliffs, where they appear, have the exhausted composure of things that fractured ages ago and have since learned weather as a form of stillness.

The ground is dark stone, ash-grey shale, old basalt, iron-stained gravel, and plates of compacted mineral crust. Frost lives in the seams. In hollows where the wind cannot reach, there are pockets of black water, shallow and motionless, filmed with ice or oil-colored algae. Narrow melt-runnels appear only briefly in the warmer part of the dim day, tracing silver lines between stones before vanishing underground. There are no generous rivers, only suggestions of them: dry channels polished by earlier eras, frost-scoured gullies, chains of seasonal pools that remember flow without practicing it often.

Moisture arrives in fragments. A rain that is almost not rain at all drifts down as cold needles and beads on stone. Condensation gathers beneath overhangs and along the undersides of ledges, forming slow drops that take hours to fall. Fogs pool in depressions like water too tired to become liquid. The air smells faintly metallic, with a mineral wetness, a breath of old machinery though no machine can be seen.

Vegetation is present but spare, and because of that it seems deliberate. Dark lichens map the rocks in intricate colonies, green-black, rust-orange, ivory. Low mats of wiry moss occupy cracks where frost has made a little soil. Here and there stand knee-high shrubs, leafless for most of the year, their stems red-brown and polished by blown grit. In sheltered basins there are pale grasses, brittle and widely spaced, their seed heads rattling only when the wind is strong enough to wake them. No trees rise here. The plateau will not permit height without stone beneath it.

And yet the place is full of animals.

They are everywhere once the eye learns how to separate them from the ground. At first the land seems empty: broad gravel slopes, ribbed ridges, dim sky. Then a hundred bodies resolve out of the color of rock. Herds move across the uplands in long, slow currents—thick-coated grazers with narrow legs and low heads, scraping lichens from stone, nosing frost from moss beds, breathing steam that lingers in the still air. They are not clustered densely; the scale of the land prevents that. Instead they occupy distances. A herd may take an entire valley to express itself.

On the darker ridges, larger animals stand in ones and twos, watching. Their coats are shaggy and crusted with rime, their horns or antlers grown into asymmetrical shapes, polished by ice and combat. In the basin pools live pale amphibious things that surface without sound, circular eyes opening in the skin of the water. Small burrowing mammals tunnel beneath the gravel crust, leaving delicate collapsed lines like script. Flightless birds pick among the mineral flats, their feathers dense and soot-colored, their calls nearly inaudible, more gesture than sound.

Above all this, in the high dim air, immense birds or something near to birds pass rarely and without flapping. They ride the plateau’s slow thermal seams, wings rigid, bodies long as fallen trees. Their shadows drift over the ground like changes in weather. When they descend, the herds do not panic loudly. They tighten, turn, begin moving with quiet inevitability. Predation here has the form of ceremony: widely spaced, well understood, almost silent.

The silence is the strangest thing, because life is abundant. There are hooves on stone, wingbeats, breathing, scraping teeth, the occasional crack of ice. But the scale drinks sound. A herd crossing shale becomes a faint dry whisper. A body entering water makes no more than a soft black folding. Calls do not carry far in the cold heavy air. Even the wind, when it comes, often moves overhead, combing the fog and leaving the ground in a hush.

This world has weather, but not drama. It changes by accumulation. Frost thickens along north faces. Pools shrink over months, then reappear after a week of dim rain. Paths made by herds deepen not by trampling mud, but by generations of hooves choosing the same stone saddles, the same gentle inclines, the same passes between fractured ridges. Bones remain for years, then decades, whitening, splitting, accepting lichen. Carcasses feed whole ecologies in the open: beetles with blue-black shells, quiet fox-sized scavengers, patient birds, mats of fungus that bloom briefly in the warmth of decay.

There are no buildings, no cut roads, no walls, no bridges. Nothing has been placed here by hands. Yet there is an industrial quality embedded in the land itself, as if geology has taken on the habits of machinery. Far below some of the basins, the ground hums without audible sound. Not vibration exactly, but a pressure in the teeth and sternum. The plateau vents heat in remote fissure fields where mineral towers grow naturally in ranks like factory chimneys. They emit no flame, only pale vapor and a smell of sulfur, iron, and cold steam. In the dimness they resemble abandoned works, but they are older than intention.

There are plains of fractured metal-bearing stone that ring faintly under hoof. There are slag-colored slopes formed not by furnaces but by ancient volcanism and glacial sorting. In some valleys, black fans of granular material spill from eroded seams, arranged so regularly they look poured. The world appears manufactured at a distance, then refuses the idea when approached. Its production is tectonic, glacial, animal, atmospheric. It manufactures cold. It manufactures silence. It manufactures migration, condensation, hunger, sleep.

The light rarely clarifies anything. The sun, when visible, is a dull coin behind high cloud, too weak to cast firm shadows. More often the sky is a layered grey with a bluish underside, and the land receives illumination as if through cloth. At certain hours the stone takes on a leaden sheen. Ice in the cracks glows softly. Animal eyes catch what little light exists and return it as green, amber, white.

Night is long but not absolute. The snowfields and pale mineral crusts hold a residual glow. Auroral bands sometimes form beyond the northern rim of the plateau, subdued and slow, like bruises of color under translucent skin. During those nights the great herds continue moving, not quickly, but with confidence. Their routes are older than storms. Calves are born in shallow gravel bowls warmed by many bodies. Predators wait outside the breathing perimeter. Steam rises from hundreds of backs and becomes weather.

The rules of the place are simple and immense.

Nothing hurries unless it is already dying.

Warmth is local, temporary, and important.

Water is not absent, but it must be found in its hidden forms: under stone, in fog, in flesh, in frost, in the long memory of channels.

Sound is a currency spent rarely.

Emptiness is not lack; it is spacing. It allows the herds to spread, the hunters to vanish, the weather to assemble, the eye to fail before distance does.

The plateau has been here so long that even change has grooves to follow. Glaciers once ground it down and withdrew to the far white margins. Volcanoes once opened it and left their metals behind. Winds have crossed it for eras, sanding the exposed stone into softened ribs. The animals came, adapted, enlarged, diminished, divided, returned. Their migrations are now part of the land’s erosion, their hooves slowly engraving preferred lines into passes, their grazing keeping the vegetation low, their bones altering the chemistry of small basins where rare plants grow brighter and denser for a few summers before the cold reclaims the advantage.

In some regions the plateau drops into enormous amphitheaters, ringed by terraced cliffs. Fog collects there, and the walls sweat even in freezing air. These are the richest places. Moss thickens, insects emerge in brief black clouds, and animals descend from every direction along narrow ramps. From above, the movement looks like spilled iron filings drawn toward a magnet. Yet even at the height of gathering, the quiet remains. Thousands of bodies occupy the basin with little more than breath, footfall, chewing, the occasional muted bellow swallowed by stone.

Elsewhere are the dead flats: expanses of pale gravel and salt-frost where almost nothing grows. The herds cross them in lines, heads low, conserving energy. Carcasses here mummify rather than rot. The skin tightens over bone; horns and hooves persist; ravens or their local equivalent stand on the ribs and work without calling. These flats shine dimly under cloud and produce mirages, not of heat but of depth: the ground appears to open into black water where there is only smooth stone.

The unusualness of the place is not flamboyant. It is a half-step aside from expectation. The animal abundance does not match the sparse vegetation until one understands the hidden productivity: lichens digesting mineral over vast areas, microbial mats blooming beneath ice-filmed pools, geothermal seams warming underground burrows, carcass fields feeding cycles too slow to notice. The industrial feeling does not come from ruins, because there are none, but from repetition, scale, extraction, venting, pressure, the conversion of one state into another. The quiet does not mean peace. It means efficiency.

A traveler, if one existed here, would feel watched long before seeing anything watching. The landscape gives few close comforts. Distances are deceptive; a ridge that seems an hour away may take a day to reach. The air is thin enough that exertion becomes intimate and loud inside the body, though outside it vanishes. Breath freezes on fabric, eyelashes, fur. Water must be chipped from shaded pools or gathered as condensation from stone. Fire would be difficult, wood nearly nonexistent, dung too valuable to the small organisms that dismantle it.

But the place is not hostile in the theatrical sense. It is indifferent in an old, practiced way. It has room for life, and life has filled that room according to cold rules. It has no interest in barrenness. It prefers spacing, endurance, and the long recycling of bodies through stone, lichen, stomach, bone, frost, and back again.

At dawn, if the word applies to the slow paling of the cloud ceiling, the plateau seems almost empty once more. The herds have moved beyond a ridge. The great gliders are gone into the upper murk. Only tracks remain: crescents in frost, claw marks near a pool, a broad trampled darkening where animals slept together through the coldest hours. A drop of condensed water swells beneath a basalt ledge, trembles for a long time, then falls onto a patch of moss no wider than a hand.

The sound is too small to hear.

But something drinks it.
