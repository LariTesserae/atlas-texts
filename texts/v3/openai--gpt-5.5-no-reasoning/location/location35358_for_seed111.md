At the top of a new mountain, above the old weather and almost above the ordinary idea of ground, there is a hollow where the world has not yet learned to be wide.

It is not a valley exactly. It is too small for that, too enclosed, too abrupt. It is a steep cup bitten into a black ridge, a room-sized basin by the measure of mountains, though large enough to lose sight of its far wall in the dark. Its edges are raw. The stone has broken recently, in the speech of geology: sharp planes, fresh splinters, glassy fractures, ribs of basalt and blue-grey shale standing like the inside of a cracked bone. Nothing has had time to round itself. There are no softened slopes, no settled screes weathered into comfort. The place is all angles: ledges, teeth, overhangs, narrow shelves stacked one above another. Even the dust is angular, glittering faintly when it catches the thin light.

The hollow lies so high that clouds arrive not above it but through it. They do not pass quickly. They seep. They gather in seams between rocks, snagging on points and notches, thickening into a granular darkness. The air is cold without being frozen, a cold that keeps its hand on everything: the stone, the skin of water in cracks, the slow bodies of animals. Breath hangs low. Condensation beads on vertical walls and trembles there for hours before finding enough of itself to fall.

There is water, but no generous water. It is stingy and recurring, hidden in small places. A tarn no larger than a sleeping animal lies at the lowest part of the hollow, black as pitch, its surface sealed by tension rather than ice. Around it are smaller pockets: a fist-deep pool inside a hoof-shaped depression, a dripping seam beneath a slanted overhang, threads of moisture running invisibly under loose shards. Sometimes rain comes, but not as a storm. It arrives as a cold sift from the dark, a rain so fine it seems less like falling water than the mountain exhaling. For a while every stone shines. Then the shine retreats into cracks.

The landscape is sparse in plants, but not bare. Life has taken hold in the least forgiving manners possible.

In the fractures grow low mats of black-green lichen, leathery and tight to the rock, as if trying not to be noticed by the wind. Pale thread-moss hangs beneath shaded lips, silver when dry, nearly black when wet. There are a few wiry alpine stems no higher than a finger joint, each with leaves like small scales pressed close together. Their roots enter hairline cracks and widen them by the patience of living things. In two or three pockets where windblown grit has collected, cushion plants form hard little domes, dense as knuckles. They bloom rarely, perhaps once in several years, with tiny colorless flowers that smell faintly of mushrooms and rainwater.

But vegetation is not what fills the hollow.

Animals do.

At first, in the darkness, the hollow seems only noisy. Then the ear begins to separate the noise into populations.

There is a continual clicking from the walls, dry and multitudinous: high-legged cold crickets, perhaps, or something like crickets, moving over the stone in near darkness. There are soft throat-pulses from the pools, a knocking resonance as if pebbles were being tapped together underwater. Wings rustle in the vertical cracks. Claws write on shale. Somewhere overhead, unseen bodies shift and settle, sending occasional needles of rock down the slopes. The place is crowded not with large creatures but with presences: small lives layered in the hollows of stone, under flakes, inside seep-holes, clinging upside down to wet faces, folded into darkness.

The fauna has the force of weather.

There are pale, many-legged things that graze the lichen at night, bodies jointed like strings of translucent seeds. There are blunt-headed amphibians in the seep-pools, dark-backed and silver-bellied, which never seem to move until one looks away; then they are suddenly elsewhere, leaving ripples that travel too slowly. There are thumb-sized winged animals roosting in cracks, not quite bats, not quite birds, with membrane wings and hooked feet. They come out when the mist thickens, pouring through the hollow in sudden silent swarms, then chattering all at once when they return to the rock. There are worms in the scree that glow faintly blue when exposed, and predators shaped like flattened hands that wait beneath slabs.

Larger creatures visit but do not stay long. A shaggy cliff-goat with a narrow face may appear at the rim and stand there like a broken statue, listening to the basin below. White insects cluster on its wet muzzle. Once in a long while, something long-backed and low moves among the rocks, using the shelves as a path: a cold-country scavenger with fur darkened by condensation, eyes milky and reflective. It does not hunt loudly. It waits for the hollow to surrender something.

The darkness is almost complete, but not empty. Light enters in weak, indirect ways. During the day, a dim iron-colored glow filters through cloud and stone-shadow, enough to outline edges but not reveal distances. The walls catch it as thin grey lines. The pools hold it longer, storing a dull reflection that seems to come from below. At night, if night can be distinguished here, the basin becomes a chamber of small signals: fungal pinpricks under overhangs, blue worms in disturbed scree, the wet blink of animal eyes, the faint phosphorescence that follows the passage of certain insects across slick rock.

The air smells of mineral cold: wet stone, old ash, raw fracture. Under it is the animal smell, surprisingly warm and dense in such a high place — musk, feathers, sour nests, ammonia from guano streaks beneath roosting cracks, the muddy breath of hidden pools. In some pockets the odor is almost cave-like, though the sky is open above. The hollow has the feel of an exposed cave, a chamber without a roof but still governed by inwardness.

Sound accumulates oddly here. The walls are close enough, hard enough, and irregular enough that every movement finds another surface and returns altered. A pebble dislodged from the rim becomes a sequence: tick, tick, clatter, crack, whisper, then after a pause another answering fall from elsewhere, as though the basin were thinking it over. The animal sounds do not disperse. They thicken. Chirrs, clicks, drips, wingbeats, throat knocks, the minute scraping of mouthparts on lichen — each one small, together loud. Not a roar, not a storm, but a dense living static. It is difficult to tell whether the hollow is full of creatures or whether the stone itself has become articulate.

There are no structures. Nothing has been built here. No cairn, no wall, no path, no cut mark, no metal, no cloth caught on a thorn. Yet there is a feeling of primitive law, as if the place belongs to a stage of the world before architecture but not before ritual.

The animals keep certain boundaries. The pale grazers never cross the dry red seam that runs along the eastern wall. The winged things avoid the black tarn except during the finest rain, when they skim it in loops and touch the water with their bellies. The amphibians face upslope before a rockfall. The flattened predators retreat underground when the pressure changes, long before mist arrives. These are not human rules, not signs, not intentions, but the hollow is full of customs. Every ledge has a use. Every wet crack has a queue of mouths. Every dry shelf is contested by sleep.

If tool-use exists here, it belongs to beaks, claws, and patient paws. A small corvid-like creature sometimes comes over the rim, dark-feathered, heavy-headed, carrying a sharp chip of stone in its foot. It prises grubs from the moss mats, then abandons the chip where it falls. The next rain moves it. The next freeze cracks it. No artifact survives long enough to become history. Use appears and vanishes back into geology.

The youngness of the place is everywhere. The hollow may have opened when a section of summit collapsed after thaw, or when something beneath the mountain exhaled heat and left a void, or when lightning found a fault line and persuaded the ridge to split. The rock has not forgotten the violence. Long blades of stone lean at unsafe angles. Fissures run clean and deep, their interiors darker than shadow. The scree fields are fresh enough that many stones still match the wounds they fell from. In places the cliff face is streaked with rust-yellow and bruised violet minerals newly exposed to air. Moisture wakes them slowly. They bleed color in thin stains.

Yet the motion of the place is slow.

Nothing rushes except falling stones, and even they seem like interruptions in a broader patience. Water gathers bead by bead. Lichen spreads by widths too small to see. Animal colonies shift their territories over seasons measured in molts, broods, lean winters. The tarn rises after several days of mist, then lowers by secret seepage through the mountain’s ribs. Frost works invisibly at night, loosening one flake, one grain, one hairline edge. The hollow is changing constantly, but with the restraint of something that expects to be here after all witnesses are gone.

The weather has moods rather than events. A dry period makes the basin brittle and louder: claws sharper on stone, wings dusty, lichen curled tight, pools drawn down into black eyes at the bottoms of pits. Then mist returns, and the whole place swells without growing. Membranes unfold. Moss drinks and darkens. Eggs hidden in damp seams soften. The amphibians lift their heads. The insects descend from dry upper cracks to feed on new films of algae. Condensation pearls on the backs of sleeping animals until they look mineral, encrusted, half-made by the mountain itself.

There is no horizon from within the hollow. The rim is visible only as a jagged absence above, a torn ring of darker darkness. Sometimes a brighter greyness passes there — cloud thinning, moon behind vapor, snowfield catching distant light — but the outside world remains mostly theoretical. The basin feels less like a place on a mountain than a pocket inside height, a dark organ held within the summit.

At its center, the small tarn does not behave like ordinary water. It reflects too little. Sound near it lowers in pitch. The clicking from the walls dims at its edge, as if the animals observe an old caution. Its surface is sometimes covered with a fine skin of mineral dust, and sometimes perfectly clean, though nothing has touched it. When rain falls, each drop makes a ring that travels outward and then stops before reaching the shore. Beneath the water are pale stones, angular and freshly broken, but between them move shapes that may be larvae, or roots, or slow thoughts of the mountain taking temporary form.

The strange quality of the hollow is not flamboyant. It does not announce itself with impossible colors or floating stones. It is stranger than that: slightly wrong in every ordinary process. Echoes return with an extra syllable. Condensation forms on the underside of overhangs even when the air is dry. Some animals cast shadows in directions unrelated to the weak light. A crack in the western wall exhales warmer air once every few hours, regular as sleep. If one sits very still, the entire basin seems to inhale around the tarn and exhale through the high fissures, though no wind can be felt on the skin.

Perhaps the hollow is a nursery for a mountain still forming. Perhaps the summit is not done deciding its shape. Perhaps the animals came because they heard, in the young stone, conditions older than weather: mineral warmth, enclosed darkness, intermittent water, safety from the open sky. Or perhaps the animals are not visitors at all but the mountain’s first attempt at thought, each species a small moving instrument testing the new chamber.

By dawn, if dawn comes, the darkness thins from black to charcoal. The noise changes key. The winged things fold themselves away. The pale grazers retreat beneath shale flakes. The pool-knocking slows. A few high plants, stiff with condensation, hold beads of water larger than their leaves. The fresh rock glimmers with cold. Nothing looks peaceful exactly. The place is too crowded, too raw, too alert.

But it has balance.

A narrow, damp, high-altitude hollow; young stone and old animal hunger; sparse plants doing the work of centuries; water appearing in small, consequential gestures; darkness held close by steep walls; a loudness made from many tiny lives; and beneath it all, the slow patience of a landscape that has only just emerged, yet already feels ancient in its rules.
