At the top of the old world there is a city built into a plateau that should, by all ordinary rights, have become a plain.

The mountains around it are not young mountains. They have no bright, jagged teeth, no clean violence of newly lifted stone. They are worn into immensity: broad-backed, slumped, patient forms, ridges rubbed down by ages of ice and wind until they seem less like peaks than the exposed knuckles of some buried continent. Their summits are high enough to thin the breath and silver the grasses with frost at dawn, but their shapes are low, exhausted, almost gentle. The land gives the strange impression of being both elevated and anciently flattened — a skyward plain made from the remains of a range.

The city occupies one of these high tables, where the stone has been carved into terraces by epochs of rain, freeze, thaw, and, later, by hands.

It is not a shining city. It is dark, compact, and damp at its seams. From a distance it appears as a dense crust of slate roofs, corroded gantries, black-green walls, and chimney stacks, all clinging to the lip of the plateau and spilling into shallow basins where mist collects. Its streets are narrow not because space is absent, but because the builders learned to shelter from the wind. Houses lean toward one another over alleyways, their upper stories connected by enclosed bridges, their foundations half sunk into stone that was already old before the first wall was raised.

Everything is layered. Stone under brick under iron under moss under condensed water. Old causeways lie beneath newer catwalks. Aqueducts that no longer carry full streams still bead with moisture along their undersides. Pipes run along façades like exposed veins, some warm enough to steam, others sweating cold droplets that fall steadily into gutters. The city does not roar. It ticks, drips, exhales.

The air is cool and rarely dry. Not wet enough for broad rivers or constant rain, but damp in a persistent, selective way. Moisture gathers where shade and material invite it: in cracks of basalt, in woolen awnings, under roof eaves, inside abandoned furnace mouths, in the hollows of carved steps worn shallow by generations. Short rains come and pass without drama, sweeping over the plateau in grey curtains, leaving behind shining rails, darkened stone, and the smell of mineral dust awakened briefly into mud.

Water is precious, but not scarce in the simple desert sense. It hides. It appears in cisterns below courtyards, in condensation nets strung between radio masts and dead smokestacks, in ceramic bowls placed beneath cold pipes, in moss wells where spongy vegetation is pressed gently by weighted grates to release a tea-colored seep. The plateau has no great river running through it, but there are old channels cut deep into the stone — evidence of a wetter age or some forgotten hydrological engineering. In some seasons, fog rolls up from lower valleys and the entire city harvests it. Fine mesh sails unfurl from rooftops. The streets become a forest of dripping fabric.

Vegetation grows everywhere it can hold on.

Not in vast, soft abundance, but in a crowded, ingenious lushness. The high plateau is too cool, too exposed, too thin-soiled for jungles or orchards in the ordinary sense, yet life has occupied every sheltered niche. Moss thickens on the north faces of walls until the brick disappears under green velvet. Ferns grow from cracks in viaducts. Black lichens map the oldest stones. In courtyards, thorny dwarf trees are trained flat against warmed masonry, their leaves small and waxed against wind loss. Glasshouses squat beside foundries, their panes clouded, their interiors full of broad-leafed vegetables, medicinal vines, and pale fungi cultivated on waste heat.

The city’s gardens are vertical and intimate. Root boxes hang from balconies. Algae tanks glow dimly in basement windows. Rain chains feed tiered planters full of herbs that smell of pepper, resin, and cold earth. Old industrial frames — cranes, loading towers, conveyor pylons — have been colonized by climbing plants, so that their iron skeletons become trellises. The vegetation is lush not because the land is generous, but because nothing is allowed to remain uninhabited. Every surface becomes habitat, crop, sponge, or archive.

Beyond the built districts, the plateau opens into weathered expanses of sedge, low shrub, and exposed stone. Dense mats of alpine growth spread in patches where soil has collected: silver grass, dark heather, cushion plants, rust-colored mosses that store water like old cloth. In depressions lie tarns no wider than market squares, black and still under the dim sky. Around them the ground is crowded with reeds and insect life, though large animals are rare. The few that live here move as if conscious of being outnumbered by stone.

There are small birds with long, curved beaks that nest in the warm vents of factories. There are pale moths in the glasshouses, tended almost like livestock for pollination. Occasionally a foxlike thing crosses a rooftop at dusk, too long in the spine, fur beaded with fog. In the outer scree slopes live slow ungulates with split hooves and heavy eyelids, seen mostly as tracks in mud near condensation basins. Their bells, tied by shepherds to help locate them in mist, are among the only sounds that travel far.

The rest is mostly quiet.

Not silent — never silent — but quiet in the way of places where every sound is absorbed by wet moss, dense stone, hanging vapor, and height. The wind is constant on the edges of the plateau, yet within the city it becomes a pressure rather than a voice. It pushes at shutters, threads itself through pipework, hums in the tension lines of fog nets. Water drops into metal pans. Somewhere a pump starts with a cough, runs for three minutes, and stops. Chains clink in a freight shaft. A distant engine turns with a slow, uneven beat like an animal asleep underground.

Industry here is not bright, fast, or clean. It is old industrialism, adapted to altitude and scarcity: compact mills driven by wind where possible, by stored pressure, by coal-like peat bricks cut from ancient upland bogs, by methane from fungal digesters, by water when water chooses to move. There are factories, but they are embedded into the city rather than set apart from it. A dye works may occupy the lower floors beneath a chapel of glass. A machine shop may share a wall with a nursery where seedlings are kept warm by its waste steam. Foundries are sunk into pits to protect their heat from the wind. Their chimneys are short and wide, fitted with condensers to reclaim what the fire releases.

At night, if night can be distinguished from the usual dimness, lamps burn behind fogged glass in amber and green. Electric lines sag between buildings, wrapped in insulating cloth and lichen. Some districts are lit by the weak blue glow of algae columns. Others rely on furnace light leaking through grates. The sky is often overcast, the sun an indistinct pressure behind layers of cloud. Even at noon shadows remain soft and uncertain. The plateau seldom blazes; it gleams, sweats, smolders.

The architecture has the look of a civilization that has rebuilt without ever clearing away the previous version. The oldest structures are megalithic: retaining walls of enormous fitted stones, ramps aligned to vanished watercourses, circular reservoirs cut straight into bedrock. No one knows who made these first works. Later generations drilled them, capped them, mortared brick against them, ran rails through them, used them as foundations for tenements and manufactories. In places, streets pass beneath arches so old and smooth that people touch them for luck. The stone there has been polished by more hands than memory can count.

There are civic systems everywhere. The city is organized around moisture, heat, and gradient. Districts are named for what they collect: South Drip, Upper Mesh, Nine Cisterns, The Condenseries, Old Sluice, Warm Hollow. Public cistern mouths are locked and metered. Children learn the routes of runoff before they learn written letters. Every roof has an obligation: to channel rain, catch fog, grow something, or shelter machinery. Waste heat is taxed. Steam leaks are reported like fires. Moss theft from reservoir walls is a minor crime unless the wall is municipal, in which case it is sabotage.

The rules of the world have grown from the climate. Nothing exposed may be wasted. Nothing wet may be ignored. Anything that drips belongs first to the household that caught it, then to the district, then to the city. Smoke must pass through condensers. Soil must be accounted for. Organic refuse is not trash but future root medium, fungal feed, or fuel substrate. The dead are not buried deeply — the bedrock is too near and too cold — but placed in stacked ossuary gardens where warmth, lichen, and careful chemistry return them slowly to the terraces.

The city is active, though its activity is rarely dramatic. Processes overlap constantly. Fog nets open and close according to wind direction. Pumps shift water between cisterns. Tram-carts grind along narrow rails carrying stone, greens, machine parts, peat bricks, baskets of fungi. Workers in heavy wool and oiled canvas scrape mineral buildup from pipes. Greenhouse keepers brush condensation from glass before it freezes. Surveyors inspect ancient retaining walls after rain, listening for subtle changes in resonance. On the outer terraces, crews reinforce slopes where the old plateau still remembers how to fall apart.

The land itself continues its immense work. Freeze wedges open cracks by hairbreadths. Lichens acid-soften the faces of monuments. Roots enter mortar. Rain carries black grit down gutters into settling tanks. Wind lifts the thinnest soil from exposed flats and lays it behind walls, where gardeners claim it. The city is dense and industrious, but it is also being very slowly digested by the plateau that supports it.

There is something strange here, though no one agrees on what kind of strangeness it is.

Some say the oldest cisterns refill even during dry weeks, drawing from no known aquifer. Others claim that certain terraces are not eroded but worn, as though something enormous once moved repeatedly across them. Surveyors’ instruments fail near the oldest retaining walls, not catastrophically but politely, drifting off by small amounts that accumulate into impossible maps. There are neighborhoods where sound seems to arrive late: a hammer blow heard a breath after the hammer falls, a bell that continues inside the teeth after it stops ringing. In the lowest fog, people see rooftops that were demolished generations ago, still standing two streets away.

The city’s engineers are practical about such things. They note anomalies, build around them, regulate access if necessary. Strangeness is treated like weather, pressure, subsidence, or mold: not to be worshiped first, but monitored.

At the plateau’s rim, the city ends abruptly.

There are railings of black iron and wind-carved stone posts thick with moss. Beyond them the land falls away into pale emptiness. On clear days, rare and hard-edged, one can see lower valleys far below, braided with dry riverbeds and patches of dark forest. Other settlements appear as smudges, smoke threads, geometric scars. The high city is not alone, but it feels remote from everything beneath it, as if elevation has placed it partly outside the common age of the world.

From the edge, the eroded mountains stretch outward in waves: not peaks but remnants, not ruins but survivals. They have endured uplift, ice, rain, desiccation, industry, and the long patience of living crusts. The city seems young against them despite its soot-black walls and ancient reservoirs. It is a recent density on an old surface, a brief complication in stone’s almost-finished thought.

And yet, within that complication, life presses close.

A fern uncurls beside a furnace vent. A child places a copper cup under a roof drip and waits for it to fill. A tram bell sounds once in the mist. On the upper meshes, hundreds of suspended threads gather fog bead by bead, and the whole city, dim and elevated and quietly busy, drinks from the air.
