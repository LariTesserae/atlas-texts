The place is almost entirely water, but not empty water.

It is a drowned lowland: a broad, warm basin where the sea has come in and never gone out again. The old ground lies only a little below the surface in some districts and much deeper in others, sloping gently through streets, courtyards, plazas, fields, and long former causeways now furred with silt. There are no waves in the usual sense, because the place is enclosed by far reefs and drowned ridges; instead the water shifts in huge slow breaths. A pressure arrives from the outer ocean, passes through the basin, turns around towers and broken walls, and leaves everything swaying faintly after it has gone.

Darkness is the main weather.

Not absolute darkness. There is a nearly black green above, a high dimness like old glass, where the warm surface water gathers rain and floating debris. The sky is never seen clearly from below. It appears as a trembling, bruised lid. During the brightest hours a little light filters down in broad stains, enough to reveal edges but not colors: the pale faces of stone, the gray undersides of arches, the white flicker of fish turning all at once. At night the city becomes a system of touches and pressures. Shapes are known by nearness, by current, by the way sound dies against them.

The settlement beneath the water is extensive, perhaps once a coastal town or a cluster of low cities built across marshy ground. Its construction remains, but it has stopped being architecture for people and has become architecture for water. Streets are channels. Doorways are mouths. Windows are side-currents. Staircases climb into dimness and end under wavering mats of surface scum. Arcades hold pockets of colder water. Alleys trap silt so fine that it rises at the brush of a tail and hangs there like smoke.

Everything has been worn into settlement with the flood, not freshly ruined but long accepted. Corners are rounded. Carvings have lost their subjects. Rooflines have collapsed into slopes usable by eels and crabs. Walls stand not as disasters but as reefs. The older masonry is dark and softened, patched with mineral skins and pale crusts. Bricks show through where the current keeps them clean. In sheltered interiors, plaster still clings in drowned sheets, swollen and peeling outward like thick bark. Floors have become layered mud, shell hash, fish bones, seed cases, the powder of coral, the dust of buildings.

There are no machines, no lamps, no metal sound of industry, no cables humming, no engines, no recent tools. If anything made by hands remains, it has been surrendered fully to animal and mineral processes. Clay pipes are lairs. Wells are chimneys for colder upwelling water. A bell tower, if it was ever a bell tower, is now a vertical cave full of sleeping bodies. A market square is a feeding ground. A broad avenue has filled with wavering columns of fish so numerous that they seem like a weather condition rather than creatures.

The water is warm, heavy, and layered. Near the top it is almost bath-warm, especially under the long rains that drum and hiss on the skin of the basin. Lower down, in the shade of buildings, the warmth thickens into stillness. Decay proceeds quickly but quietly. Nothing is dry enough to crack. Nothing burns. Everything softens, ferments, dissolves, accretes.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. Plants survive as tough, opportunistic presences: long ribbons of seagrass rooted in silted courtyards; black-green algae painted along sunward walls; floating rafts of waxy leaves gathered against roof ridges just below the surface. In a few places, drowned trees stand in plazas, their trunks preserved by water, their upper branches lost somewhere in the murk above. They do not form forests. They are markers, sparse vertical memories, used by animals as stations. Their bark is gone. Their limbs carry shell clusters and soft anemone-like growths instead of leaves.

Most of the life here moves.

Fish inhabit the city in layers. Small transparent ones keep to the broken interiors, flashing between rooms through holes where mortar has fallen away. Broad, slow fish graze along walls, scraping films of algae and leaving pale trails behind them. Needle-thin hunters hold motionless in doorways, aligned with the current until something smaller misjudges distance. Schools gather in avenues and divide around columns, then rejoin with such precision that the old street seems to pulse.

Crustaceans work constantly in the rubble. Pale crabs climb stairs and parapets, joint by joint, their shells the color of old bone. Shrimp live in cracks in the masonry and clean parasites from larger animals that come to hover before them with mouths open and gills flaring. Lobster-like creatures occupy shopfronts and crypts, backing into darkness, antennae extended across thresholds like tripwires. There are worms in the silt, in the bricks, inside the softened wood of beams; some extend delicate crowns when the water is calm and withdraw at the faintest tremor.

Larger animals pass through less often, and their passage changes the whole district. A ray wide as a room lifts from the square and leaves a print in the sediment like a vanished carpet. A blunt-headed predator enters by one of the main canals and the smaller fish compress themselves into walls, windows, and ceiling cracks until the avenue looks suddenly vacant. Turtles, old and deliberate, browse on the floating mats and rest under the eaves of buildings that once kept off rain. Slender eels inhabit the wells and culverts; they know paths through the buried drainage system that no open-water creature can follow.

The place is dense not because it is crowded with buildings alone, but because every volume has several occupants and several meanings. A single archway may hold a current at its top, a silt bed at its base, a colony of shellfish along one side, sleeping fish on the other, a hunting eel in the crack above the keystone, and a slow rain of organic matter passing through all of them. Empty water is rare. Even darkness has bodies in it.

Sound is muted but present. The dominant sound is the soft pressure-roar of water moving through the drowned town, more felt in the ribs than heard. Rain above becomes a distant granular hiss, like sand poured continuously onto a roof. Shells tick. Crabs scrape stone. Fish schools turn with a faint collective shiver. Occasionally, somewhere far off, a weakened wall gives way after years of preparation; the collapse is not a crash but a deep bloom of vibration, a muffled downward thunder followed by the rising cloud of centuries.

Then quiet returns.

The quiet is not peace exactly. It is the quiet of immersion, of every sound being absorbed before it can travel far. Violence occurs close-up and without announcement. A mouth opens, silt jumps, scales scatter, and stillness closes around the event. Eggs are laid under lintels. Larvae drift through civic spaces no longer civic. Parasites migrate. Fungal threads and bacterial veils work in warm cracks. The dead are not preserved for long unless sealed away from scavengers; they are redistributed into shell, slime, muscle, sediment.

The city has weather, though it is underwater weather. At times the basin becomes clearer, and the outlines of whole districts appear: rows of low roofs, a central rise with larger buildings, courtyards strung together like rooms in a palace, a canal wider than all the streets around it. At other times fine suspended mud turns the world opaque within arm’s reach. Warm rainstorms above freshen the upper layers, sending cooler, softer tongues downward through stairwells and open shafts. Seasonal currents bring clouds of plankton, and for several nights the darkness is full of faint living sparks—not enough to illuminate, only enough to make motion visible as green pinpricks appearing and vanishing around fins.

There is a slight wrongness to the place, but it is not supernatural in a loud way. It comes from the persistence of urban order inside an environment that has no use for human intentions. The grid of streets still guides migration. Predators patrol boulevards. Nursery fish prefer houses with inner rooms. The current has learned the town plan. Water entering from the east takes the old market road because it is easiest; water leaving to the south slips through former drainage cuts, past kitchens and cellars, under thresholds built for feet. The city continues to function, but as an organ of the basin.

Some districts are almost ceremonial in their darkness. A low dome, half-buried in silt, contains a trapped gloom even at midday. Inside, the water is warmer and very still. Columns rise from the sediment and vanish overhead. Blind fish hang between them. The walls are covered in living crust, but here and there a smooth patch remains, touched clean by the repeated passing of some large animal. In the center, where a floor pattern once existed, a circular depression has been made by generations of rays settling in the same place. They come, rest, breathe, depart. The building remembers them better than it remembers its makers.

Elsewhere, the drowned roofs lie so near the surface that rain strikes them through only a thin pane of water. There the atmosphere is more restless. Floating vegetation collects in mats, sparse but stubborn, tangled with feathers, seeds, dead insects, and the shed skins of small creatures. Fish rise to feed from below. Birds may visit the surface, though they are only shadows from the submerged city’s point of view—feet dimpling the ceiling, beaks stabbing down, then gone. The animals below react to those shadows as to weather.

The low elevation gives the landscape a horizontal patience. Nothing here aspires very high except the remnants of towers and the columns of bubbles rising from decaying matter. The sense of openness comes through corridors of water and broad inundated plazas rather than mountains or horizons. You can move a long way without leaving the city, drifting from a residential maze to a temple-like civic center, from a silted garden to a fish-filled avenue, from collapsed warehouses to open basins where only foundations remain. The boundaries blur into marsh-sea, reef, mudflat, lagoon, but the built pattern persists far enough that it becomes regional—a whole drowned territory organized by obsolete walls.

The rules of the world are simple and absolute.

Everything must make room for water.

Anything hollow becomes habitat. Anything upright becomes current-break. Anything flat gathers silt. Anything porous is entered, eaten, colonized, reinforced, dissolved. Names do not survive, but shapes do. The city is not being reclaimed by nature as if nature were outside it; the city is one of nature’s older textures now, like reef limestone or mangrove roots. It is weathered into balance. Still changing, still active, but no longer in the first drama of loss.

In the deepest lanes, where almost no light arrives, animals navigate by pressure and scent. Warmth presses close. The walls sweat minerals. A slow rain of particles falls forever through the black water: plant dust, fecal threads, shell fragments, scales, pollen, powdered mortar, the ground remains of things that lived above and below. It settles on statues with no faces, on benches no one can sit on, on thresholds crossed only by fins.

And throughout the basin there is the feeling that the flood did not interrupt the place so much as reveal another version of it.

A town made of stone became a town made of currents.

A street became a migration path.

A room became a stomach, a nursery, an ambush, a shelter.

The darkness is warm and inhabited. The silence is full.
