It is a city under the ground, though no one has built it.

The chambers lie wide and low beneath a skin of stone so old it has forgotten uplift. Above, the desert may still exist—salt flats, pale gravel, wind passing over surfaces without memory—but down here the world has been hollowed and finished by ages. Nothing looks newly broken. Every vault, corridor, buttress, and terrace has been rounded to a final patience. The ceiling hangs in long, shallow spans like the underside of an ancient shell. Floors descend by worn steps of mineral ledge. Walls rise in blocky ranks, not cut but grown into the geometry of habitation: alcoves, galleries, window-like gaps, stacked openings, long avenues between mineral facades.

It has the order of a buried town. There are districts.

A broad central hollow runs for miles, a sunken avenue filled with cool dimness. On either side stand formations like buildings: columns fused into arcades, salt-cemented pillars, curtains of limestone hardened into ribbed fronts. Balconies overlook the passage, not made for hands or feet but for roots, nests, and the slow settling of dust. In places, the walls are squared by fracture lines, and the natural blocks sit atop one another so convincingly that they seem like masonry left by a people who vanished before tools were invented.

There is no water here.

No dripping. No pools. No shining thread in the stone. The usual music of caves is absent. The dryness is complete enough to feel architectural, as if moisture has been banned by an old law. Minerals bloom where water once passed in another era: dull white fans of salt, brown seams of iron, gypsum crusts like bone ash, powdery ledges that crumble under the lightest touch. The air tastes alkaline and cold. Dust lies in horizontal places in layers so fine they seem poured rather than fallen.

Yet the place is alive.

Vegetation grows without lushness of color, but with a quiet abundance of form. Pale root-vines descend from cracks in the ceiling, not fed by visible water but by vapor, minerals, and the faint chemistry of stone. They hang in curtains along the avenues, sometimes reaching the floor and thickening into trunks like braided rope. Their leaves are small, waxy, and grey-green, folding tightly in the dry air. Some plants spread flat across the walls as velvety mats, absorbing what little light enters through fissures far above. Others form brittle shrubs in pockets of dust, their stems silver, their seed pods papery and full of a dry rattling sound.

It is lush the way an old archive can be lush: crowded with accumulated life, layered, persistent, but never wet. Growth does not surge. It endures.

The dim light comes from many small sources, none strong enough to rule the chamber. Thin shafts enter through vertical cracks in the roof, falling as pale columns full of suspended dust. Mineral faces catch the light and hold it faintly. In some galleries, colonies of lichen give off a weak greenish cast, not magical, only biological, the low glow of organisms using every trace of energy available. Far down the avenue, brightness becomes a rumor. Forms appear first as silhouettes, then as textures, then as distances.

The city is sparse despite its size. There is room everywhere. Long plazas of powder and stone separate clusters of growth. A stand of root-trees may occupy one end of a chamber while the rest remains open, empty, and echoing. Fauna gathers in pockets rather than filling the whole. The living things here know how to leave distance between themselves. They move through vacancy as though vacancy is a resource.

But the sound is enormous.

Not sudden, not violent, but constant and overwhelming. The whole buried region roars.

Wind drives through the undercity from unseen pressure gates: cracks to the desert above, deep shafts, collapsed mouths, vents connecting one chamber to another. It does not whistle delicately. It bellows through the avenues, booms under arches, drums in hollow pillars, and pours around stone corners with the force of surf. Where root curtains hang, they thrash softly at their tips while their heavier cords remain almost still, making thousands of dry clicks. Seed pods clatter. Wings batter. The ceiling hums.

At times the noise has layers like a population speaking at once. Low thunder in the far tunnels. Midrange moaning in the larger halls. Sharp percussion from grit striking mineral crusts. The brittle applause of leaves. The scraping of insects in the wall mats. The coughs, chirps, wingbeats, and territorial cries of animals amplified until even small creatures sound numerous and close.

There are many animals. More than the dryness seems able to justify.

They live on the vegetation, on each other, on salts, fungi, lichens, seeds, detritus, and the bodies of the dead. Pale beetles patrol the dust roads in processions, following chemical trails that curve around stones like invisible streets. Blind crickets cling to root curtains and sing by scraping their legs, adding needle-thin rhythms to the wind. Dust-colored lizards hunt them on vertical surfaces, flattening themselves against the stone until they look like chips of old plaster. Small burrowing mammals, cool-bodied and soft-furred, tunnel beneath the powdery floors and emerge at the bases of root groves to strip bark and gather seeds.

Above the open plazas, bats or bat-like cave flyers mass in the high dimness. Their wings are narrow and quiet when they choose, but when whole colonies lift at once the chamber becomes a shaken tent. They roost in the mineral “eaves” of the false buildings, clustered in black-brown folds. Their droppings enrich the dust below, making dark islands where plants grow thicker. In those islands, the undercity seems almost gardened.

There are larger animals too, though they keep to the deep avenues and appear more often as effects than as bodies: a slow displacement of shrubs, a scrape along salt crust, a warm smell passing through cold air. Some are grazers with wide padded feet that leave shallow oval tracks in the dust. Some browse on hanging roots, standing on hind legs to pull down cords with blunt teeth. Predators follow the edges of the plazas, moving in the broken darkness beneath ledges, their eyes no brighter than polished stone.

Nothing here is strange in the sense of impossible. It is only adapted past familiarity. Life has become careful, pale, efficient, and loud because the caverns make loudness unavoidable.

The coolness is steady. It does not bite like winter; it rests on the skin like stone shade. Days and nights above hardly matter here. The temperature changes slowly, by seasons perhaps, or by the movement of pressure through the earth. Warmth collects briefly in crowded roosts and animal dens, then is lost into the mineral mass. The city keeps its own climate: dry, dim, cool, breathable, old.

Its rules are simple and severe.

No drop is wasted because there are almost no drops. Moisture lives in breath, flesh, vapor, and hidden tissues. Leaves close when wind strengthens. Animals sleep in sealed burrows during the loudest drafts. Roots enter hairline cracks and travel long distances through rock, finding trace films too thin to glisten. Death is not decay in the wet sense; it is drying, flaking, being taken apart by mandibles and fungi and mineral crust. Bones remain a long time. Husks remain longer. Some corridors are edged with old remains so clean they seem arranged.

Movement is governed by the wind. When the pressure rises, the avenues become impassable to the smallest creatures, and the whole population withdraws into pockets, holes, lee-sides, and dense root masses. When the air slackens, life spills out—not quickly, but with a cautious widening. Beetles cross plazas. Grazers emerge. Flyers drop from their roosts and skim the avenues. Seeds loosen and roll. Dust, lifted in faint sheets, migrates from one district to another.

The city changes, but slowly. A root thickens over decades until it splits a ledge. A pillar loses a skin of salt grain by grain. A roost expands into a blackened neighborhood of guano and gnawed stems. A collapsed ceiling slab becomes, after centuries, a terrace. A terrace becomes a thicket. A thicket becomes a feeding ground, then a den, then a bare patch again when the nutrients are exhausted. The processes are patient enough that they seem still, yet nothing is truly still.

The oldest parts lie lower.

There, the urban illusion strengthens. The chambers become more regular, the stone facades more continuous. Parallel corridors run between immense rectangular masses, their corners softened by time. Mineral shelves project like awnings. Vertical cracks align like narrow streets seen from above. In the dimness, the mind insists on intention: a marketplace, a civic stair, a row of roofless houses. But touch reveals only stone, salt, root, dust, shell, claw mark.

The place is ancient not because it is ruined, but because it has passed beyond ruin. Any violence that formed it is too remote to imagine. Floods, if they ever moved here, are myths written only in mineral bands. Collapse has already happened where it needed to happen. Weakness has fallen away. What remains is stable, worn down into endurance.

In one vast chamber, the ceiling sinks so low that root curtains brush the backs of passing animals. The floor is nearly flat and pale, a dry basin of compacted mineral dust. Sparse shrubs stand at measured intervals, each surrounded by tracks and pellets and seed husks. Along the margins, stone towers crowd together in eroded clusters like a skyline seen through fog. The wind enters from a black arch at the far end and crosses the basin in a single tremendous note.

Everything bends slightly away from that note. Leaves, antennae, whiskers, hanging roots, the ears of the grazing animals, the dust itself. The sound fills bone. It makes silence unimaginable.

And yet within the roar, the undercity continues its quiet arrangements. A beetle climbs a salt ridge. A lizard waits beside a root shadow. A pale flower, no larger than a fingernail, opens for the brief hour when the moving air carries enough scent to matter. Seeds detach one by one from a dry pod and skitter across the floor toward some crack where, years from now, one may lodge.

There are no lamps, no tools, no machines, no hands shaping stone into streets. But the place has infrastructure all the same: tunnels for air, towers for roosting, plazas for crossing, galleries for nesting, root-nets for food, dust basins for tracks and burials, mineral walls that store coolness and release it slowly. It is a city made by geology and occupied by biology. Its citizens are not people, but the city is real.

It extends farther than any single view can hold. Chamber after chamber, avenue after avenue, all beneath the dry world above. Some districts are almost barren, only wind and mineral. Some are thick with hanging vegetation and the smell of animals. Some are choked by collapsed blocks. Some are so low that only insects pass through. The boundaries are unclear. The undercity may be one connected body or many caverns stitched together by air.

To walk there would be to move through an inhabited absence: open space under pressure, dryness full of life, a metropolis without invention, an old world that has not died because it has learned to need very little.
