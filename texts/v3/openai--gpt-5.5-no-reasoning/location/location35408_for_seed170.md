The place is high and dry enough that the horizon seems not to belong to the ground.

It is a plateau, or the fossil of a plateau: a continent-wide slab lifted into heat and thinned air, planed flat by ages of abrasive wind. No rivers have cut it. No lakes shine in its basins. Whatever water made this world, if water ever did, has been gone so long that its absence has become a geology of its own. The surface is crusted in pale mineral skins, not quite salt and not quite ash, layered in scales that curl upward where the heat has worried them loose. Underfoot the ground rings faintly, like ceramic. In some places it is smooth as poured enamel; in others it breaks into polygonal plates separated by black seams that descend too far to see.

The air is bright and punishing. Light falls normally enough from a visible sun, but it arrives with a hard white insistence, without softness, without greenery to catch or scatter it. Shadows are blue-black and sharp, lying in the hollows of stones like pieces of cut metal. Heat lifts continuously from the mineral flats. It does not shimmer exactly; it edits. Distant ridges are moved sideways, erased, returned. Slopes fold in directions that the ground itself does not appear to follow. Once in a while a whole mesa will seem to stand up from the horizon, exposing a vertical underside ribbed like machinery, then settle back into being a low ocher line.

There is no vegetation in any ordinary sense. Nothing rooted, nothing leafed, nothing reaching upward with the old gestures of thirst. The only branching forms are mineral: dendrites of dark manganese-like stain spreading across pale rock; lightning-shaped seams of vitrified crust; clusters of extruded crystal grown from dry chemistry and heat. Some of these formations resemble bushes from far away, but up close they are brittle cages of glass and metallic dust, their twigs hexagonal, their thorns fused bubbles. They make a soft ticking noise as they expand and contract through the long thermal day.

The land feels empty at first. It has the spaciousness of a place uninhabited, a vastness with almost nothing per volume: long flats, distant shelves, long inclines climbing to nowhere. There are miles where the only vertical things are heat pillars and stone blades polished by wind. Even the sky seems underfilled, a huge bleached vault with a few high veils of vapor that are probably not vapor, since there is no water cycle here to feed clouds. They may be suspended dust, electrically held; they may be thin membranes of atmosphere buckled by forces that usually remain invisible.

Then the fauna begins to appear, and the emptiness becomes stranger.

They are everywhere, but not continuously. They occupy the landscape in sudden masses separated by enormous vacancies. A basin that looked vacant from the ridge will, when approached, reveal itself to be carpeted with living things the color of old bone and furnace slag. A ravine will be silent for hours, then all at once its walls will unfold, because what seemed like rock strata were ranked animals lying flat against the stone.

Most are not animals in the familiar wet, muscular sense. They are dry-bodied, articulated, refractory. Their shells are silica, ceramic, carbon-black enamel, honeycombed pumice. Some move on arrays of needles that do not pierce the ground so much as interpret it, trembling over the mineral crust and translating heat gradients into direction. Others roll in slow herds: many-chambered spheres, each the size of a cartwheel, their outer plates clicking as they labor across the flats. Their passage leaves no track except a faint change in sheen.

There are towering grazers too, though there is nothing one would call grazing. They stand in the heat fields around black vents where metallic vapors breathe out of cracks. Their bodies are lattices, open frameworks full of rotating vanes and flexing membranes like mica. They feed on radiance, on charged dust, on the chemical disequilibrium between the hot underworld and the dry oxidized sky. From a distance they resemble derricks or skeletal windmills, industrial silhouettes scattered across the plateau. But no hand built them. Their joints are grown, their bearings secreted, their axles alive in some harsh inorganic fashion.

This is the first confusion of the place: it has no buildings, no artifacts, no roads, no ruins, yet it is industrial.

The whole plateau operates. It manufactures sound, heat, pressure, migration, mineral tissue. Engines exist here without factories, factories without architecture. Along the fault scarps, ranks of vent-throats open and close in sequence, exhaling black dust and transparent fumes that smell of hot coins, ozone, and burnt stone. The vents pulse with a rhythm so regular it feels regulated. Deep beneath, something turns or circulates or reciprocates. The ground transmits it: a slow piston-thud traveling through the soles, a basso tremor that makes loose crystals hop in place.

In certain valleys, the animals gather around these natural engines by the thousands. Small many-legged things swarm across the warm stone like iron filings around a magnet. Larger forms lower feeding proboscises into cracks and draw up threads of incandescent gas, which cool inside their bodies into visible braided filaments. Predators, if the word applies, move among them with tremendous patience. They are flat, crescent-shaped beings that slide under the crust and re-emerge days later beneath a herd. When they rise, the ground bulges gently; then a dozen grazers tilt, fold, and become still, their inner vanes slowing one by one.

The noise is immense, but rarely urgent. It is loud in the way a refinery heard from across a desert is loud, or a storm heard through stone. A constant metallic roar lies under everything: wind dragging mineral grit over flats; vents chuffing; plates expanding; shells clicking; herds ticking and clattering as they feed. There are whistles from fissures, subsonic moans from hollow mesas, the dry castanet rush of migrating swarms. At long intervals a sound like a ship horn rolls across the plateau. Nothing visibly answers it, but the herds alter course by a few degrees.

Because change here is slow, the loudness becomes atmospheric. The landscape does not bustle. It processes. Days may pass before a migration front crosses a valley. Weeks may pass while a herd arranges itself around a vent field in spirals so perfect they look machined. The sun climbs, hangs, descends; the ground swells and contracts; shadows pivot around animal bodies that have not moved perceptibly. Even violence is gradual. A predator may take an afternoon to engulf its prey. A shell may crack over the course of an hour, each split sounding like a rifle shot across the emptiness.

The elevation gives everything a strange austerity. The air is thinner than the heat suggests. Breath, if one breathes, is both scorched and insufficient. The sky darkens toward violet overhead while the horizon remains white with glare. The plateau edges fall away into lower haze, but those lowlands are rarely visible; they are hidden behind mirage shelves and refracted strata. From the highest ridges, one sees other highlands floating beyond gaps of air, as if the world has been broken into separate tables and spread under the sun.

The terrain is old. Not dead, not inert, but settled into forms that have endured. The mesas have shoulders rounded by abrasive centuries. The flats carry generations of crust, peeled and resealed. Fault lines are polished at their lips by innumerable passages of shell and grit. Wind has carved stone into long flutes aligned with prevailing currents that may have blown in the same direction for millennia. Even the living forms seem ancient in design, less evolved than refined, like tools honed by a world that never knew softness.

Yet reality does not hold cleanly.

It is not merely that heat distorts distance. There are places where perspective becomes a local weather. A ravine may appear deeper when looked at directly than when approached sideways. Shadows sometimes fall toward the sun, but only from living bodies. Certain animals exist with a slight delay: their shells click before their legs move, or their image arrives half a breath after the sound of their passing. On the salt-bright pans, herds are reflected though there is no liquid surface. The reflections do not always match. Sometimes they show larger animals moving below, inverted, as if another plateau presses against this one from underneath.

At noon, the breakdown worsens. The air above the flats divides into stacked layers, each with its own version of the land. In one layer, the plateau is empty. In another, crowded with black towers that are not present on the ground. In another, the animals have become pure diagrams: arcs, lattices, migration vectors drawn in light. Walkers, if there are walkers, learn not to trust the layer their eyes prefer. Stones can be closer than their shadows. A herd can be heard for hours before it becomes visible, then vanish while its dust remains.

The rules of the world seem negotiated rather than fixed. Mass is usually reliable. Heat is nearly sovereign. Dryness is absolute. But direction is conditional. Cause and effect move at different speeds depending on terrain. In vent fields, sound precedes motion. On the enamel flats, motion precedes intention; animals begin turning before the pressure wave that guides them has arrived. Along the black seams, depth multiplies. Pebbles dropped into them can be heard striking bottom several times, at different intervals, as though the crevice contains a series of possible floors.

No one has built here because building would be redundant, or impossible, or immediately absorbed into the larger industry of the plateau. Anything rigid becomes resonant. Anything hollow becomes a throat. Anything metallic grows a rind of catalytic life, then is mistaken by the swarms for a feeding station and dismantled grain by grain. The place tolerates no artifacts, not through hostility but through appetite. It converts structure into process.

There are regions where the animal density is so great that the ground itself cannot be seen. These are not lush places; they are overcrowded deserts. A million dry bodies gather on broad inclines facing the sun, each aligned by some invisible calculus of heat and charge. They rasp against one another softly, a sound like sand poured over cymbals. Their shells flash white, gray, black, chrome-blue. Some vent little puffs of mineral smoke. Some emit thin electric tones. Above them wheel airborne organisms, not birds but hinged kites with furnace-colored undersides, riding thermal columns and snapping shut around rising dust-creatures too small to see.

Then, a few miles away, there is nothing. A flat plain, empty to the horizon, white and tan and rose under the hard sky. No tracks. No motion. Only the low, patient roar of the world’s hidden engines. The contrast is so extreme it feels like a law: life here condenses into overwhelming assemblies, then leaves vast intervals untouched, as if the plateau can only bear animation in concentrated patches.

At dusk the heat does not fade so much as withdraw into the ground. The upper air cools by degrees, and the vents glow more visibly: dull red mouths, blue-white seams, occasional green flares where some rare mineral vapor burns without flame. The fauna reorients. Latticed grazers fold their vanes and lower themselves into tripod stances. Rolling herds lock together in chains to conserve heat or exchange charge. Burrowing crescents surface and lie open like broken moons. Across the flats, the ticking of cooling stone grows louder, joining the animal clicks until land and life cannot be distinguished by sound.

The sky at night, if night fully comes, is crowded with stars made sharp by altitude and dryness. But even then the weirdness remains. Constellations tremble out of alignment above certain fissures. Stars are occluded by things too high and slow to be clouds. The ground releases stored light in faint sheets, revealing buried patterns: old migration spirals, fault geometries, the ghost-lines of vanished basins. The plateau appears less like a desert then and more like the exposed circuit of a planet, an immense dry nervous system where geology, industry, and animal life are variations of the same mechanism.

Nothing grows here, yet the place is not barren.

Nothing is built, yet the place is not natural in any comforting sense.

It is a hot high machine without makers, a desert crowded with bodies, a mineral ecology running on heat, pressure, static, and time. It has had ages to become itself. It is weathered, balanced, established in its impossibility. Its emptiness is real; so is its overpopulation. Its silence is gone, replaced by the slow thunder of processes too old to hurry. And everywhere, beneath the hard ordinary sunlight, reality buckles slightly under the strain of describing what the plateau already knows how to be.
