At first it seems impossible that anything so green could be so dry.

The lowland extends farther than sight has any right to travel: a continental basin with no horizon so much as a bright thinning, a place where distance becomes pale and fibrous. There are no rivers, no pools, no damp hollows, no glint of liquid between roots. The air is cool and sharply mineral, rinsed of humidity, almost brittle to breathe. Yet everything is vegetation. Not vegetation growing on land — there is no exposed land — but vegetation as land, as floor, as wall, as scaffolding, as machine casing, as roof, as map, as weather.

The ground underfoot is a woven living mat many meters thick, made of pale green cords, bark-black ribs, rubbery sheets, translucent bladders, and old laminated husks hardened to a substance like ceramic horn. It gives slightly, but not enough to feel soft. It has been compressed by centuries of its own weight and by the traffic of maintenance engines. In places it is smooth as cast resin, sealed by overlapping leaf-plates that have grown into one another until they resemble paving stones. Elsewhere it rises into knuckled ridges, root-arches, dense braided ramps, and low terraces where generations of plant architecture have been pruned, trained, fused, and re-grown into habitable infrastructure.

There is no soil, unless the powdered remains of previous growth count as soil. There is no sand, except where dead cellulose has been ground fine and drifted into dry green dust. Beneath the living mat lies not earth but older construction: lattices of carbonized stalk, mineral-impregnated pith, fossilized vinework, ducts, braces, conduits, silent rails, and enormous buried looms that still turn at intervals without making more than a distant tension in the air. The entire place is made. It may once have been assembled by hands, or by machines, or by plants engineered to continue the work after hands and machines became irrelevant. Now construction and growth are the same act.

The brightness is overwhelming but not hot. A high white light fills the basin from every direction, diffused through a ceiling of reflective membranes suspended so far above that it becomes an artificial sky. It is not blue. It is a luminous milk-glass vault patched with rectangular intensities where sun-engines or mirrors burn beyond the canopy. Every surface below catches the light and returns it differently: waxy leaves flash silver; hard old stems glow amber; tender new blades shine almost neon; factory-grown petals, wide as sails, hold the light in saturated magenta, yellow, and bruised violet. Nothing is shadowless, but shadows are thin, pale, cool. They have no depth to hide in.

Water is absent in the ordinary sense. The plants do not drink. Or rather, they learned long ago not to depend on drinking.

Their fluids are not water but slow, clear oils, gel electrolytes, volatile salts, and thin conducting saps that bead like mercury when cut. Some tissues crackle faintly with stored charge. Others wick vapor from the cold air — not moisture, but trace gases exhaled by factories and root-furnaces: ammonia threads, carbon oxides, silane ghosts, nitrates carried as dust. Leaves are not merely leaves but chemical plates, condensers, exchangers, static nets. They harvest light, charge, mineral powder, machine exhaust, and the waste breath of their own vast metabolism. Their roots are not roots in search of groundwater; they are clamps, wires, grinders, digesters, augers. They penetrate the constructed substrate, chewing ancient structural members and sorting them into usable ions. In the absence of rain, erosion is performed by growth.

The landscape has the settled complexity of something very old. Nothing looks newly made, yet nothing is abandoned. Edges have been rounded by contact, polished by tendrils, mended by scar tissue. Giant trellises lean but do not fall because other trellises have grown around them. Conveyor bridges sag under mats of photosynthetic skin and are held up by buttressing trunks that were once planted as repairs and are now older than the bridges themselves. Towers of cultivated wood stand in ranks across the plain: not trees, exactly, but chimneys, absorbers, distillation columns, antennae. Their bark bears bolt-patterns and maintenance grooves. Vines climb them in scheduled spirals. At their crowns, they release no smoke, only faint cold plumes of pollen-dust and ionized vapor that drift horizontally in the still air.

Sound is scarce.

That is the most unsettling thing. A living continent should rustle, creak, click, murmur with insects, birds, running water, wind. Here, movement happens under a discipline of quiet. Leaves adjust their angles by tiny increments without fluttering. Pistons embedded in trunks compress with a soft internal sigh. Far away, an industrial loom completes a cycle and the ground answers with one low wooden pulse, more felt through the feet than heard. Pollination drones pass overhead occasionally, but they are slow and almost silent, their wings grown from membrane and carbon thread, their bodies furred with yellow dust. When a seed-pod opens, it does not pop; it dilates. When a factory-root grinds mineral from an old beam, the sound is muffled inside living tissue.

The quiet gives the brightness a hallucinatory authority. One notices small things too intensely: the dry whisper of one leaf sliding beneath another; the minute electrical tick from a charged tendril; the powder-fall of dead pollen from a high rib; the nearly inaudible contraction of kilometers of vine, like a sleeping animal tightening in its dream.

There are animals, but they are rare and oddly provisional, as if fauna is a tolerated impurity in a plant-designed world. A long, pale thing with too many jointed toes may move along the underside of a bridge and vanish into valve-flowers before its outline can settle. Tiny glass-backed mites graze on mineral bloom where conduits sweat salts. Once in a great while, a herd of maintenance ungulates crosses the lower platforms: narrow-bodied creatures half-domesticated by the architecture, their horns socketed with tool-growths, their teeth adapted to shave dead bark without harming living cambium. They do not call. Their hooves fall on the mats with soft, dry taps. Predators exist mostly as rumor — a seam in the green opening where no seam should be, a carnivorous culvert closing with delicate patience, a vine that has learned the timing of animal breath.

The world’s active life is mostly vegetal and industrial.

Across the low basin, fields rearrange themselves throughout the day. Not quickly enough to watch with comfort, but quickly enough that returning by the same path is never certain. Rows of blade-leaves tilt toward the overhead radiance, then flatten to spill excess light onto lower tiers. Spiral stems unwind to expose black heat-absorption panels when the air cools too far. Whole neighborhoods of frond and girder subtly rotate on buried turntables of root and gear. Dry fogs of spores rise in narrow columns, held together by electrostatic charge, then are pulled sideways toward collection towers. On the far flats, vast rectangular orchards pulse from green to white as the undersides of leaves turn up in sequence, signaling some process too large to parse: respiration, computation, prayer, manufacturing order.

There are factories everywhere, but few are recognizable as factories at first. Their roofs are leaf-canopies. Their walls are bundled reed-steel. Their chimneys are hollow trunks with spiral pores. Their assembly lines run through translucent stems where one can see objects moving inside: seed capsules, ceramic nodes, replacement joints, folded membranes, machine larvae, tablets of compressed nutrient ash. Some facilities are entirely floral: acres of cup-shaped growths receiving powders from overhead rails, secreting layers of polymer, folding them into panels, passing them through root-hands into tunnels below. Others are brutal and old, half-swallowed by the ecosystem — black iron frames, skeletal cranes, flywheels turning slowly in chambers of bark. The plants have not hidden the industry. They have colonized its logic.

Nothing wastes movement. Nothing wastes matter. Even decay is organized. Dead structures are not left to rot; they are scheduled for digestion. A bridge nearing the end of its load-bearing life grows red veins along its underside. For a season it is avoided. Then its interior is hollowed by enzymatic threads, mineral fasteners are extracted, and new supports sprout through the empty shell before the outer casing sloughs away in long dry curls. These curls are gathered by creepers and carried to shredding basins, where they become insulation, feedstock, memory fiber. Fallen leaves do not litter. They are gripped by hairlike tendrils and drawn downward into narrow mouths.

The weather is made inside the landscape.

There is no rain. There has not been rain for so long that the idea of falling water feels mythological here, obscene, almost catastrophic. Instead there are brightness fronts, pollen tides, pressure silences, charge storms. At regular intervals the overhead reflectors open wider and the plain whitens. Leaves stiffen; flowers close; towers drink light until their bark turns glassy. Then comes a cold dimming, though never true darkness, and during it the factories exhale. Pale gases slide low among the root-terraces. The plants unfurl absorbent hairs, silver and blue, and comb the air clean. Sometimes a static storm crawls across the basin without thunder: halos forming around mast-trees, tendrils lifting, dust rising in veils, every surface prickling. For hours afterward, the world smells of crushed stems, ozone, alkali, and something like dry lightning trapped in fruit.

The coolness is persistent. It inhabits the shade under the living platforms, the ceramic-hard bark, the powder in the air. Warmth exists only locally: near digestion furnaces, under black metabolic leaves, inside engine trunks where flywheels turn with patient heat. The broader climate feels refrigerated by intention. Heat is harvested, transported, stored in underground pith-batteries, spent elsewhere. The basin has no interest in comfort. Its temperature is a parameter, maintained.

To travel here is to move through layers of intention that may no longer have an author.

There are roads, but they grow. Broad green causeways stretch between tower-clusters, bordered by low hedges trimmed into repeating mechanical profiles. Some are soft with new tissue, some hardened into pale woodstone. Embedded in them are rails made of root-metal, along which quiet cargo platforms drift without visible propulsion. The platforms carry bales of dried frond, cylinders of pollen paste, latticed machine parts, sleeping pods, blocks of mineral salt, and sometimes sealed black containers that every nearby leaf turns away from. The roads branch according to needs sensed elsewhere. A path may narrow overnight because a factory has entered dormancy. A new ramp may extrude from a wall to meet a convoy expected three days from now. Signage appears as color changes in bark, as raised vein-script, as flowers whose number and orientation indicate permission.

The built environment is total, but it is not sterile. It is too alive for sterility. It has the saturation of a jungle and the coordination of a plant-wide city. Habitation chambers bloom from the sides of support trunks: dry rooms lined with cork, lit by translucent leaves, cooled by capillary walls carrying nonaqueous sap. Bridges knit building to building in braided arcs. Courtyards are not open spaces but thinner places in the vegetation, wells of light where crop-membranes hang like laundry. Far below, under the mats, are utility voids: miles of low tunnels ribbed with roots and pipework, where the air is dim and smells of resin, metal dust, and old electricity. Far above, canopy platforms catch the full white blaze, dense with mirrors, aerial roots, and silently flexing collectors.

Reality is unstable here, though not chaotically. Its breakdown has been incorporated.

At certain distances, perspective fails. A tower seen across the flat may be both near and impossibly far, its base arriving long before its top does. Rows of identical pylons repeat one too many times. Corridors inside the larger trunks can bend through more space than the trunk contains. There are groves where causality is trellised like vine: seed before flower, fruit before branch, repair before damage. Maintenance crews — if they are crews, if they are not specialized growths shaped like patient figures — avoid the mirror-leaf districts at noon, when reflections continue moving after their originals stop.

In some places the constructed ecology has learned to exploit these fractures. Roots pass through folded intervals to reach mineral deposits that are not exactly under the basin. Factories send conveyor loops into small geometric impossibilities and receive finished components before raw material has fully entered. A field of pale stalks grows in two seasons at once: cool budding at ankle height, dry senescence at shoulder height, both connected by the same stem. There are storage chambers where objects are kept not in shelves but in postponed moments, retrieved when their assigned hour ripens.

The plants are not disturbed by this. Perhaps they caused it. Perhaps the first builders broke the world trying to solve scarcity — no water, too much light, insufficient ground — and the vegetation was engineered to hold the wound open usefully. The basin’s laws are now horticultural. Space must be pruned. Time must be grafted. Matter must be trained along supports. Anything that spreads without function is cut back. Anything that dies must feed continuity. Anything fluid must be bound into gel, oil, charge, or memory.

The absence of water shapes every ritual of the place. Surfaces are sealed. Open pores are temporary. No structure weeps. No mouth salivates. Flowers are dry, shedding powders, threads, brittle flakes, aromatic crystals. Fruits are hard capsules, often faceted, filled with rolled membranes or salt-rich meal. Nectar, where it exists, is an oily conductive film visited by tiny engineered pollinators. The color green dominates, but it is not lush in the familiar sense. It is a dry green: enamel, wax, fiber, polished thorn, chlorophyll under glass. The vegetation does not droop. It hinges, folds, braces, computes, manufactures.

And yet the place is not deadened by its efficiency. There is beauty in its restraint, in the immense care with which it refuses evaporation. There are gardens of silver hair that comb gases from the dawn air. There are orchards of transparent leaves, each veined with gold circuitry, chiming silently as charge passes through them. There are low plazas surfaced in overlapping oval scales, cool to the touch, where slow shadows of overhead drones cross like fish in a dry sea. There are flower-furnaces that open only during the cold phase, showing interiors the color of banked coals, warming nearby seedlings without flame. There are memorial groves grown from obsolete machinery, every gear encased in amber resin, every piston sprouting a delicate white shoot.

Over long distances the basin changes character without ever ceasing to be itself. Near the central manufactories, the vegetation is disciplined into grids, stacks, ducts, and towers; leaves are numbered by pigment; vines run in parallel bundles as thick as highways. Farther out, where the constructed lowlands approach the pale edge of the world, the system loosens into immense pleated fields. There the plant-substrate rolls in slow waves, not from wind but from growth pressure traveling through continental tissue. A wave may take a week to pass. When it does, every stem leans, every bridge rises a fraction, every buried machine compensates with a sequence of nearly silent adjustments. From above, these waves must look like thought moving through a green brain.

At the lowest depressions, where water would once have gathered if water belonged here, there are salt gardens. Vast white basins lie inset into the living mat, but even they are not bare mineral. They are cultivated evaporites, dry chemical reservoirs, crusts farmed by filament plants with translucent bodies and dark root-eyes. The salt is carved into baffles, channels, hexagonal pans. Cool air pools there. The brightness becomes painful. Workers — animal, machine, plant, or intermediate — move slowly across the white on long stilts to avoid contamination, harvesting crystalline growths that form from atmospheric feedstock and subterranean digestion. No drop ever shines there. Only facets.

The silence deepens in those salt gardens. It becomes architectural. One hears one’s own joints, the scrape of powder underfoot, the faint expansion of distant membranes under light. If a pod opens a kilometer away, the sound arrives as a tiny dry click and feels intimate.

Night, if it can be called night, does not fall. The sky-ceiling dims to pearl. The temperature drops a little. The brightest flowers close their shutters. Others open: pale star-shaped collectors with black centers, sensitive not to sunlight but to background radiation, machine signals, stray particles from broken physics. The industrial cycle changes register. Heavy processes begin below the substrate, too low to hear. Roads contract. Towers lock their crowns. The rare fauna withdraws into warm hollows. Across the basin, billions of leaves alter their chemistry and become less green, more silver, reflecting the dim overhead glow until the whole lowland looks frost-covered.

During this phase, the world’s strangeness is easier to perceive. Distances breathe. A grove glimpsed from one side contains seven towers; from the other, nine. Some paths hum with potential and cannot be stepped on until they finish deciding where they lead. Root-doors open onto chambers filled with yesterday’s light, still bright, stored in fibrous walls. In the far reaches, there are districts that have been growing a replacement sky for centuries: circular frameworks, mirror-buds, atmosphere nets, white leaves layered so densely overhead that they create a second brightness beneath the first.

The landscape seems to be preparing for something, but it may always have been preparing. Its activity is constant and cyclical, urgent and patient at once. It produces, absorbs, repairs, folds, digests, recalibrates. It maintains coolness against light, life against dryness, order against broken law. It is a desert that became a forest by abandoning water. It is a factory that became a jungle by continuing to build after purpose blurred. It is a city whose citizens are mostly vascular systems and whose streets remember how to grow closed behind you.

Stand still long enough and the place begins to include you in its calculations.

A tendril the color of old brass lifts from a seam in the paving-mat and tastes the air near your boot, not touching. Leaves above adjust, admitting a cleaner angle of light. Somewhere below, a slow valve changes state. Dust slides away from your shadow. The silence remains, but it is now full of attention — not hostile, not welcoming, simply immense.

The green lowland continues to the bright edge of the world, dry as bone, alive in every surface, manufactured down to its deepest root, and quietly, continuously making itself.
