The place is high, but not sharp. It is a plateau-world raised above its own weather, a vast tableland of old stone and older masonry, with edges lost beyond haze. The ground does not climb so much as persist upward: terrace after terrace, shelf after shelf, as if a mountain range had been planed flat by patience and then inhabited before the work was finished. Everything here has been eroded into acceptance. No cliff looks newly broken. No wall looks recently raised. Even the machinery has the softened, mineral look of things long exposed to warm rain.

Water is everywhere, but almost never as a river. It seeps, condenses, beads, sweats. The city is wet in its joints. It gathers under iron stairways and between paving slabs; it darkens the lower courses of buildings; it slides down the flanks of buttresses in slow, reflective sheets. The air holds moisture like breath held in the mouth. Far-off towers blur in it. Rooflines glisten. Pipes drip from their seams even when no rain is falling. In courtyards, shallow basins collect brown sky-water and tremble from the footfalls of animals passing nearby.

There are no plants.

Not one blade between stones, not one vine on a wall, not one lichen-softened statue. The wetness does not nourish green. It polishes mineral surfaces and feeds rust, salt, bacteria maybe, invisible films, pale slicks in the drains, but nothing leafed or rooted appears. The absence is so complete that it becomes architectural. Every empty crack announces it. Every ledge that should have hosted moss remains bare, scaled instead with mineral crust, soot, old lime, copper bleed, and the black varnish left by engines.

The city fills the plateau in layered increments. It is not a single settlement but a continental urban crust: districts joined to districts across saddles of highland stone, viaducts crossing drained basins, habitation blocks rising from quarry scars, factories built into escarpments, cistern-towns nested under markets, animal courts wider than towns elsewhere. Some quarters are gridded and deliberate, their streets straight enough to catch the sun in long bands. Others are accretions around old reservoirs or furnace yards, circular and ribbed like shells. Everywhere there are walls within walls: retaining walls, flood walls, animal walls, heat walls, sound walls, walls to hold back crowds, walls to guide runoff, walls whose purpose has been forgotten but whose mass still shapes the day.

The materials are heavy and local. Basalt cut in block and slab. Warm gray limestone, pitted by long rain. Fired brick the color of dried blood. Iron plates bolted over cracked stone. Ceramic conduits glazed dark blue-green, sweating at the joints. Lead-colored gutters. Copper domes gone dull and streaked. Old concrete, thick and granular, patched so many times that repairs have become a decorative language. The city is built for wet altitude and long endurance: every surface is sloped a little, every threshold raised, every foundation thickened against seepage. Stairs are shallow and broad, worn in the middle by centuries of hooves, paws, claws, wheels, and boots.

The warmth is steady rather than bright. It is a warm highland, near the belly of clouds. Sunlight arrives normally when it arrives, neither alien nor weak, but it must pass through vapor and suspended soot. The light spreads across wet stone with a broad white glare at noon and a tarnished gold toward evening. Shadow pools under arcades and animal bridges. Steam lifts from factory vents and from the backs of living bodies. In some neighborhoods the air smells of hot metal and rain; in others, of hides, fish-oil, dung, mineral mud, disinfectant, and the faint sourness of standing water.

The city’s industry is old enough to have become geology. Engines work inside masonry halls with high slit windows. Their pistons move slowly, with the confidence of tides. Foundries open their furnace mouths under vaulted roofs. Mills grind mineral feed and bone meal. Pumping stations lift seep-water from one level to another, not to remove it entirely but to keep it where the city wants it: in cisterns, troughs, canals, condensers, cooling jackets, animal baths, pressure towers. Enormous flywheels turn behind grates slick with condensation. Belts slap and breathe. Dynamo rooms hum in brick basements. Overhead, insulated wires sag between ceramic horns. The city is electrified unevenly, like a body with certain nerves awake and others numb.

Yet the machinery is not the loudest life here.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Animals occupy every layer of the place, not as intruders but as a primary fact of the world. The streets were made for them, or remade by them. Vast quadrupeds with stone-gray hides move through the lower avenues in processions, harnessed to freight sledges or wandering between feeding yards. Their feet are padded for wet pavement; when they pass, puddles jump in place. Narrow-bodied creatures cling to the sides of buildings and sleep beneath cornices, their ribbed silhouettes clustered like hanging tools. Flocks of pale, warm-blooded things wheel between chimneys, calling harshly through the steam. Canal-beasts surface in the high reservoirs, showing only nostrils, eyes, and ridged backs before sliding beneath opaque water.

There are pens the size of districts. Roost-towers. Burrow-markets. Bridges reserved for migration. Public squares striped by the regular passage of herds. Aqueducts with side channels where small blind animals travel nose-to-tail in warm runoff. The drains are alive with bodies: slick, quick, armored, soft, translucent, loud. In the absence of plants, the animals feed from systems less visible and more industrial—cultured mats in vats, mineral broths, rendered refuse, fungal slabs grown in sealed houses, fish raised in dark cisterns, microbial crust scraped from warm pans, each food-chain managed by guilds of keepers and mechanics. Life here has not made forests. It has made crowds.

The ecology is dense, urban, and circular. Nothing green softens it, but nothing is empty. Waste from the upper herds is carted to digestion furnaces. Heat from factories warms incubation halls. Condensed water runs through tiled channels into settling basins where gray films thicken under lamps. Small creatures graze those films. Larger creatures eat the small ones or the cakes pressed from them. Bones are ground. Shells are burned for lime. Hides are cured in long ventilated galleries. Feathers, bristles, scales, horn, fat, wax, and chitin pass through workshops and reappear as insulation, rope, glue, lamp fuel, filters, roof membranes, machine gaskets, ceremonial masks, children’s toys.

The city is not clean, but it is balanced. Its filth has routes. Its smells have districts. Its animals have times of day.

Morning begins with condensation. Before sunrise, the plateau cools just enough for the air to let go of water. It beads on iron railings, drips from cables, collects along the muzzles of sleeping beasts. Bells sound from pump towers, low and irregular. Keepers open gates. The first calls are not birdsong exactly but the coughing bark of roost animals and the metallic clatter of beak or claw on guttering. Steam gathers in alleys as kitchens, vats, and feed houses wake.

By midmorning the streets are crowded. Hoofed carriers descend ramps from the freight terraces. Spined pack animals kneel in loading yards while cranes lower crates onto saddle frames. Thin-legged scavengers move along the margins, tolerated because they clear what drops. Human or human-like inhabitants—small in the scale of the city, wrapped in oilcloth and treated leather—keep to raised walkways when the animal avenues fill. They cross from building to building through arcades and enclosed bridges, pausing at grates to wait out the passage of something too large or too temperamental to interrupt.

At noon the light is almost ordinary. For a few hours the city shows itself plainly: the pale high sky, the huge damp distances, the ranked chimneys, the cistern roofs, the animal backs moving like slow stones, the cranes, the towers, the quarries now full of water, the plazas paved with polygonal blocks and worn to a shine. Far off, other boroughs rise through the vapor as darker bands. Because the plateau is so vast, weather travels across it like politics: announced long before it arrives, argued with by pumps and shutters, never fully resisted. A rain front can be seen for half a day as a silver wall beyond the industrial quarter. By the time it reaches the central terraces, the animals have already shifted levels, the feed is covered, the furnace vents adjusted, the low markets shuttered.

Rain here is frequent but rarely violent. It does not cleanse. It renews the wetness. It taps on tin and ceramic, ticks against hide awnings, gathers in roof-channels, overflows from gargoyles shaped not as monsters but as extinct machines. It darkens everything by one shade. The sound of it merges with the city’s existing noise until the whole plateau seems to breathe through water.

Despite the loudness, change is slow. The engines are loud in a steady way. The animals are numerous in a steady way. Gates groan, chains clank, pumps thud, calls rise and fall, but the city as a whole does not hurry. It operates by cycles too established to feel urgent. Some migrations happen daily between warm lower tunnels and high exposed yards. Others occur by season, though seasons here are subtle: a shift in cloud height, a mineral taste in the rain, the breeding restlessness of the roof colonies, a change in the color of runoff from distant factories. Generations of animals have worn preferred turns into stone. Generations of builders have reinforced the same corners. The plateau has become a living machine that moves mostly by recurrence.

The high elevation reveals itself in the edges of breath and sound. Voices carry strangely across plazas, then vanish in vapor. Distant horns from freight towers arrive flattened and delayed. The calls of animals echo between retaining walls until they seem to come from underground. At night, when the factories dim but do not sleep, the soundscape deepens: lowing from enclosed yards, the splash of reservoir creatures, chain elevators, the patter of runoff, electrical hum, the occasional alarm bell, the wingbeat rush of something immense passing just above the roofs. The city is loud, but rarely sharp. Its noise is layered, padded by moisture, made cavernous by scale.

Under the city lies another city of water and weight. Cisterns, culverts, drains, pressure galleries, fermentation vaults, service tunnels, animal passages, old streets roofed over and forgotten. Some lower avenues are flooded to knee-height permanently, their walls tiled in white ceramic gone yellow at the waterline. Warm pipes run through them like roots, though there are no roots. Blind labor animals haul barges through the underground channels, guided by touch and bell. In places the ceiling sweats continuously, a manufactured rain falling into black water. Maintenance crews descend with lamps and hooks, marking mineral growth, checking valves, counting the pale colonies that pulse on the walls.

Those colonies are the closest thing to vegetation, though no one here would mistake them for plants. They are films, crusts, membranes, vat-cultures escaped into damp seams. Some are cultivated; some are nuisance; some are protected by law because certain animals depend on them. Under amber industrial lamps they thicken into mats with the texture of wet felt or cartilage. They have no stems, no leaves, no upward longing. They spread laterally over ceramic and stone, feeding on heat, minerals, waste, and light when light is given. In abandoned engine houses they form pale maps on the floor, slowly redrawing the history of leaks.

The weirdness of the place is mild, almost bureaucratic. Nothing floats without support. No impossible colors stain the sky. The laws of weight and weather hold. And yet the proportions are wrong enough to unsettle. A city without greenery but full of animals. A wet world where fertility has taken no leafy form. A high plateau warm as a lowland lung. Factories that seem less like modern invention than inherited organs. Streets designed around creatures whose domestication may have begun before language, or after it, or instead of it.

There are rules here.

Water belongs first to the city, second to the animals, third to industry, and only then to individuals. No one blocks a runoff channel without permission. No one builds flat roofs. No one releases breeding stock into the upper reservoirs. Horns at certain pitches mean clear the avenue, others mean hold still, others mean close the shutters because a herd is turning. Bridges are classified by load, temperament, and hoof-type. Feeding waste into the wrong drain is a serious crime. So is wasting heat. So is cultivating green life, if green life is even possible; the prohibition remains carved above several old gates, though whether it commemorates a disaster, a superstition, or a forgotten ecological fact is no longer widely known.

At the margins of the plateau-city, the built crust thins but does not end. There are quarry basins full of opaque rainwater, their terraced walls streaked black and orange. There are old slag fields, softened by erosion into low metallic dunes where burrowing animals nest in warm pockets. There are viaducts crossing shallow marsh-like flats—not marshes of reed and sedge, but mineral wetlands: slick expanses of clay, salt pan, runoff pools, steam vents, and microbial sheen. Animals gather there in numbers so great the ground seems to ripple. They probe the mud, lick mineral crust, wallow in warm seepage, shed skins or plates that workers later collect.

Beyond that, the weather hides the rest. Perhaps the urban plateau continues for hundreds of miles. Perhaps all high land is city. Perhaps below the escarpments, unseen under cloud, there are forests, seas, ordinary fields. But from within this place, such things are theoretical. The horizon offers only more wet stone, more towers, more animal movement, more slow industry, more rain approaching in sheets.

Even ruin here does not feel abandoned. Buildings collapse by degrees and are occupied at every stage. A cracked warehouse becomes a roost. A roofless foundry becomes a breeding court. A flooded market becomes a reservoir. A broken bridge becomes a perch for hundreds of heat-loving animals whose droppings calcify into new ledges. The city rarely removes; it incorporates. Erosion is not decay alone but governance by weather. The corners round, the stains deepen, the routes adjust, the animals learn, the builders patch, the pumps continue.

At dusk the plateau glows from within. Furnace windows burn orange under the blue-gray air. Lamps come on along raised walkways, reflected in wet paving until each street has two sets of lights: one above, one trembling below. Herds return from outer yards, moving slowly, flanks steaming. Keepers sing or chant, not melodically but rhythmically, to maintain spacing. In the roost towers, thousands of bodies rearrange themselves with a sound like heavy cloth being shaken. Somewhere a pressure valve releases, and a white plume rises straight for a moment before flattening under the low cloud.

Night does not empty the city. It redistributes it. The day animals sleep in massed warmth. The night feeders emerge from culverts and roof seams. Factories reduce their pace but keep turning. Water gains volume in the dark: dripping, running, collecting, overflowing, sluicing through hidden grates. The absence of plants becomes less visible, and the animal presence becomes more intimate—breath behind walls, claws in gutters, bodies shifting in pens, eyes catching lamplight under carts.

The place feels ancient without being dead, crowded without being lush, wet without being fertile in any familiar way. It is a city-ecology built on stone, water, heat, labor, and animal abundance. Its beauty is not soft. It lies in glistening viaducts, in the discipline of drains, in the slow confidence of enormous creatures crossing polished plazas, in the steam rising from old engines, in rainwater running over carved basalt that has outlasted dynasties of builders and breeds.

It is a world that has learned to live without leaves.
