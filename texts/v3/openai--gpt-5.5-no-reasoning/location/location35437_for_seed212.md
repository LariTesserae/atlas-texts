At first it seems like a city has been drowned in jungle, but that is only the mind trying to make the place easier.

There is no stone here. No brick, no metal, no fired clay, no tool-cut beam. Yet the place has streets.

They run in long warm corridors between walls of living tissue: bundled stems, braided trunks, sheets of leaf-flesh fused together into facades. The avenues are not paved, but compressed by use — not by feet or wheels, for there are no animals, no people, no carts, nothing with a mouth or hoof or hand — compressed instead by the weight of growth leaning inward, by the sliding of roots, by the repeated collapse and regrowth of green structures in the same channels. The ground is a mat of fibrous material, springy and damp in patches, dry and squeaking in others. Step on it and it gives like a woven mattress; press harder and it exhales warm vapor smelling of cucumber, sap, black tea, and opened stems.

The settlement extends farther than can be seen. Not infinitely, but regionally, like a lowland town spread across a basin: blocks, districts, courtyards, alleys, towers, bridges, plazas. All of it made from plants, or what plants become when there is nothing else to become.

The land is low, young, and not yet settled into a permanent grammar. Broad rises of green matter swell from a flat, wet plain. Shallow channels curve through it, carrying brown water only intermittently. Some are empty troughs lined with slick moss and root-hair; some hold chains of puddles under translucent skins of algae; some suddenly fill, not from rain but from condensation wrung out of the air by vast leaves overhead. Moisture gathers, releases, vanishes, gathers again. The place is damp without being aquatic. Water appears as pockets, threads, breaths, beads on tendrils, trembling films between leaves, trickles down bark-walls, sudden gutters of warm rain inside buildings that have no roofs except canopies.

The sky is visible in fragments.

Above the streets, the canopy does not close entirely. It is too restless for that. Leaves the size of sails open and fold. Umbels rotate. Curtains of vine lift as if inhaling. Through shifting holes comes ordinary daylight — bright enough to see colors clearly, bright enough to cast shadows — but the light never lands simply. It is strained through green, yellow, bronze, purple, and the pale wet white of new growth. Every surface reflects a different hour. A plaza may lie under noon while the alley beside it glows dawn-blue. Farther down the avenue, a tower of leaf-stacked chambers flickers as if lit from within by late afternoon.

Warmth presses everywhere. Not desert heat, not fever exactly, but a humid, botanical warmth: the temperature inside a closed greenhouse after rain. The air has weight. It touches the skin like a second skin. It is full of invisible exhalations. Resin. Sugars. Spores. The mineral smell of young mud. The faint bitterness of crushed green things. Occasionally, a cold thread passes through — a sinking draft from some shaded hollow where water has collected and not yet been absorbed — but it disappears almost at once into the general heat.

The city grows loudly.

That is the first impossibility one must accept: plants here are not quiet. The sound is overwhelming, continuous, physical. A roar made from small organic actions happening at impossible scale. Stems split with sharp wooden cracks. Seedpods detonate like thrown stones. Leaves clap against one another in the warm windless air. Root bundles grind below the streets, twisting, levering, prying open cavities, making a deep wet thunder underfoot. Somewhere a column buckles, groans, and peels apart in long strips. Somewhere else a hundred new shoots punch through a wall together with the rattling force of rain on tin, though there is no tin and no rain.

There are whistles too: hollow reeds venting pressure, thousands of them, each tuned differently. Low moans from inflated gourds. The papery hiss of bracts unfurling. A constant susurrus from leaf-edge against leaf-edge, so dense it becomes surf. Pollen falls in dry curtains and makes a whisper like sand. Sap pumps through the larger trunks audibly, in pulses, like distant drums, like blood magnified into weather.

Nothing calls. Nothing cries. There is no birdsong, no insects, no frogs, no mammal rustle. The absence of animals is not silence; it is a missing category inside the noise. No intention shaped by hunger moves through the place. No eye watches from a branch. No swarm troubles the flowers. Blossoms open anyway, extravagant and useless, or perhaps not useless here. Pollination may be architectural, meteorological, psychic. The flowers may be speaking to pressure systems, to each other, to the idea of distance.

Some districts resemble neighborhoods because the growth repeats itself with unsettling regularity. Rows of bulbous chambers line a street, each chamber entered through an arch of trained roots. Balconies protrude as shelves of thick leaf-rind. Spiral stairways climb around central trunks, though no foot could have taught them their spiral. Window-like holes open and close over the course of an hour, sealed by membranes that turn opaque in heat and transparent when shadow falls. There are terraces, retaining walls, courtyards, aqueducts of vine, drainage gutters grown from half-tubes of bark. There are bridges made from braided lianas so taut they hum.

But then a block loses its argument with itself.

A row of green houses liquefies into tendrils. A tower folds down like a fern frond and roots into the avenue. Streets branch into more streets, then close behind themselves, then bloom into amphitheaters. Doorways migrate. A plaza fills waist-deep with leaves in minutes, then the leaves stand upright, stiffen, arrange themselves into a colonnade, and begin producing fruit shaped like sealed urns. An entire quarter may be born before the observer finishes crossing it: first a rash of shoots, then a geometry of stems, then shade, then walls, then an avenue lined with nodding blue-black flowers, then collapse, compost, steam.

The world is not merely alive. It is undecided.

Reality breaks here along botanical lines. Perspective takes root and grows wrong. A street seen from one end is straight; seen from the other, it is a spiral descending into a basin. A building may contain more courtyards than its exterior can hold. A vine crossing overhead passes behind the sun for a moment and returns wet with night. Leaves cast shadows of plants that are not present. Seeds fall upward, hang trembling in midair, then sprout into dangling root systems that drink light instead of water.

Distances have moods. On hot afternoons, nearby districts withdraw, becoming miniature and unreachable behind veils of pollen. At other times, a far tower leans close enough to touch, its bark sweating against the cheek, though its base remains leagues away in the green sprawl. Some alleys repeat: turn left, left, left, and arrive not at the beginning but at a younger version of the street, where the walls are still pale and soft and the air smells of first sap. Turn back quickly and the older street may be gone.

The ground itself is new, and still forming. Beneath the living mat lie ridges of recent deposition: silt, vegetable loam, pale clay, mats of dead stems not yet made into soil. Erosion has begun but has not had time to smooth the place. Water cuts little fresh scars through the rootwork. Collapse exposes raw cross-sections: green outer layers, white pith, amber sap, pockets of dark compost steaming with heat. There are sharp features everywhere, but they are soft-edged materials sharpened by speed — splintered bamboo walls, newly torn root cliffs, rucked mats of rhizome like tectonic plates made of fiber. Ravines open overnight where underground growth withdraws. Embankments slump, then stitch themselves together with threads of root fine as hair.

Rain comes in brief, localized episodes. Not from clouds necessarily. Sometimes the upper canopy condenses a shower over a single courtyard while the rest of the city remains dry and glaring. Sometimes droplets form on the undersides of leaves and fall upward, striking the higher branches with a pattering reverse-rain. Sometimes a warm mist rolls along the streets at knee height, thick enough to bead on bark and skin, smelling of stone that the place no longer owns. The water never dominates. It visits. It troubles. It nourishes particular pockets, then is seized by roots and vanishes.

Because there are no animals, decay proceeds strangely. Fallen flowers do not rot by being eaten. Dead wood softens from within, digested by its neighbors or by its own returning enzymes. Fruit drops and bursts, not to feed mouths, but to seed pressure, color, memory. Some fruit contains water. Some contains soil. Some contains a folded map of a district that has not yet grown. Some contains nothing but a low sustained note, released when the rind splits and continuing for days, joining the general roar.

The vegetation is total, but not uniform. It has strata.

At the lowest level, the street-skin: mosses like velvet bruises, liverwort plates, red creeping mats, pale root-hairs combing the damp. They seal wounds in the ground and drink condensation. They retract from sudden light. They write unreadable patterns across every surface.

Above them, the structural growth: trunks fused into walls, reeds bundled into columns, woody ribs arching overhead, buttress roots making stair-forms and barricades. These are the bones of the city, though they are still soft enough to bend when the windless pressure of the place changes.

Then the inhabiting growth: vines, curtains, epiphyte neighborhoods, pitcher-forms full of rainwater, hanging gardens rooted in walls, knotty growths like storage jars, leaf clusters arranged like awnings. They crowd every ledge and opening. They make rooms within rooms. They occupy the architecture the way moss occupies a fallen log, except the architecture is also moss, also log, also appetite.

At the upper level, the unstable canopy: immense leaves, flowering platforms, aerial roots, suspended pools, pale inflated sacs that collect heat and release it with sighs. This canopy is not a roof but a second weather. It manufactures shade, rain, wind, pressure, color. It is where the loudest cracking comes from, and the sudden showers of petals, and the long flexible shadows that move independently of the sun.

Somewhere beyond the visible district, perhaps at the center of the basin, rises a growth too large to call a tree and too organized to call a forest. It is seen only in portions between nearer structures: a green-brown mass like a cathedral under construction, or a hill trying to remember being a seed. Spires of living wood thrust from it, then soften and droop. Sheets of leaf descend its sides like banners. It sends out avenues radially, or the avenues imagine that it does. Its upper surfaces pulse faintly in daylight, and when they pulse the whole city answers: roots tighten, walls lean, flowers open, pods burst, streets change their direction by a few degrees.

There are rules, but they are not stable rules.

Growth prefers edges. Any boundary — shadow line, waterline, crack, memory of a path — becomes a site of multiplication.

Nothing remains empty unless it is actively becoming something else.

Straight lines can exist, but only temporarily, as tensions between competing curves.

Open space is a form of hunger. Plazas are rapidly invaded unless some larger rhythm keeps them clear. In the broadest squares, one sees this rhythm at work: shoots sprout, grow knee-high, tremble, and then are flattened by a pulse passing through the ground. Minutes later they rise again. Thus the plaza remains open not by absence, but by repeated suppression, a violence so constant it resembles maintenance.

Water creates language. Wherever moisture lingers, plants become more elaborate, more articulate, more architectural. Dry ridges support thorny, fibrous, skeletal structures; damp hollows produce chambers, arches, membranes, translucent walls, and those unsettling window-forms that seem almost intentional.

Sound is force. Some structures grow toward certain frequencies and away from others. A reed-wall may thicken when the low drums of root pressure pass beneath it. A curtain of leaves may shred itself during the shrieking vent of a pressure pod. The city is shaped by its own noise. The deafening roar is not a byproduct but a planning authority.

Memory is compostable. Old districts feed new districts not only materially but spatially. A collapsed avenue may later reappear as a pattern in bark grain, then as the layout of a courtyard, then as the branching of a vine. The place remembers by re-growing variations, never replicas.

And perhaps the most important rule: there is no outside observer for long. Anything that lingers becomes part of the landscape’s negotiations. Breath moistens surfaces. Heat alters openings. Weight encourages fibers to brace. Even attention seems to matter. Look too steadily at a doorway and it thickens, formalizes, becomes more doorway-like. Ignore a wall and it may forget to remain solid.

The atmosphere is therefore not hostile exactly, but engulfing. There is no malice in it. Malice would require an animal clarity, a directed will. This place has instead abundance without restraint. It does not attack; it includes. It builds around pressure. It fills available volume. It makes use of stillness.

In one district, long green arcades run parallel for hundreds of meters, their ceilings ribbed with overlapping leaves. Warm rain taps intermittently through gaps. Pools gather in the hollows of root-knots, each pool reflecting a different sky. The sound inside the arcade is magnified to a tunnel-roar, stems creaking like ships, pods popping like knuckles, unseen trunks booming as they expand. Along the walls, pale flowers open in sequence as one passes, not because they sense passage in any animal way, but because the air has been disturbed, carbon and salt shifted, humidity rearranged. Their petals are almost transparent. Behind each one lies a chamber full of folded green light.

In another district, the city becomes vertical despite the low land. Towers rise from mud on thick buttresses, narrow and clustered, joined by high vine-bridges. They are young towers, raw and fibrous, their outer bark still peeling in strips. Some are hollow. Within them, shafts climb through stacked plant chambers, each chamber wetter and warmer than the one below. Condensation runs down the interior walls in threads. In the highest openings, broad leaves flap and crack like canvas in a storm, though the air outside is nearly still. The turbulence is internal: sap surges, tissues inflate, growth stresses release with gunshot snaps.

Farther out, where the basin flattens, the structures loosen into neighborhoods of enormous rosettes. Their leaves form basins large enough to hold rooms. Rainwater collects there, warm and greenish, then drains through root-spouts into lower channels. The rosettes are connected by causeways of tough interlaced stems. Between them lie fields of sensory grass that bend in waves without wind, responding to underground motion. The waves cross, interfere, cancel, amplify. From a distance the field looks like the pelt of some giant sleeping animal, but there are no animals. Only motion borrowing animal metaphors from a mind that cannot help itself.

The horizon is soft with vegetation. Not forest exactly, not city exactly, not swamp, not ruin. A low, warm, self-building metropolis of plant matter in a damp plain, young enough that its wounds are bright, dense enough that every gap is temporary, open enough that one can sense districts beyond districts, and strange enough that the categories brought to it rot quickly.

By late day — if late day can be trusted — the light turns gold-green and the noise deepens. The smallest cracklings fade beneath slower sounds: the bass heave of roots, the tearing sigh of towers adjusting, the immense leafy applause of the canopy. Moisture rises from the ground. Walls sweat. Flowers shut with soft claps. Others open, darker ones, night-colored though night has not arrived. Pollen glitters in the air like dust from a demolished sun.

Then the reality of the place loosens further.

A street ahead grows younger as the light declines, its heavy walls thinning back into shoots. Behind, the avenue grows older, bark darkening, arches thickening, fallen leaves becoming soil in seconds. To the left, a courtyard fills with replicas of itself, nested one inside another, each greener and smaller. To the right, a bridge lifts from its anchors and becomes a root seeking ground in the sky.

The roar becomes almost unbearable. It is not loud like thunder alone, but loud like being inside an event too large to have edges. Creation and destruction are happening at the speed of weather. Buildings sprout, tense, flower, split, and are overwritten. Streets open and clot. Water flashes in channels and disappears. Light shivers through leaves that may not be in the same version of the world as their shadows.

And still, beneath the turbulence, there is a kind of order: not mechanical, not planned by minds, not technological, but botanical and absolute. Everything grows toward support, moisture, light, repetition, pressure, sound. Everything uses everything else. Nothing is inert. Nothing is merely background. The landscape is made entirely of foregrounds pressing against one another.

If it is a city, it is a city without citizens.

If it is a forest, it is a forest that has invented urbanism.

If it is a dream, it is one being dreamed by roots in warm mud, by leaves clapping in ordinary daylight, by condensation gathering on the undersides of impossible arches, by a young lowland world still deciding which of its forms are real enough to keep.
