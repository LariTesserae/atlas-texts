At high altitude, where the air should be thin and scouring, there is a broad warm plateau made of pale mineral crust and dark, unweathered stone. It has not yet learned the habits of erosion. Nothing here is rounded. Nothing has been softened by rain, river, frost, or animal passage. The ground lies in huge unfinished plates, as if poured and cooled only a short while ago, though the stillness of the place suggests it may have remained this way for ages.

There is no water.

Not even the trace-water of dew, not the remembered stain of a vanished stream, not the salt-scum left by a lake. The dryness is total and strange, not merely arid but chemically absolute. Dust does not clump. Cracks do not darken. The hollows between stones are clean, sharp, and depthless, their bottoms filled with glittering powder like ground glass or powdered bone. When the wind moves, which is rare, the powder does not drift far; it lifts in a faint breath and settles immediately, as if the air itself has little interest in carrying things.

The land is open in a regional way: not infinite, but large enough that the horizon matters. From any rise one can see leagues of raw terrain — terraces, basins, shallow shelves, isolated black ribs of stone, and broad fields of pale crystals arranged in flat hexagonal fans. Beyond them stand higher ridges, newly thrust but not weathered, their edges clean and improbably straight. The plateau gives the impression of a world still under construction, but construction without workers, without machinery, without intention. Slabs have risen and frozen mid-gesture. Valleys have been sketched but not deepened. Cliffs have formed without talus at their feet.

Yet there is lushness here.

It is not the lushness of wet forests, not leaves with sap, not moss, not grass in any familiar sense. The life of this place has found another solvent, another metabolism, another contract with warmth and light. Across the dry mineral flats grow tall standing fans of translucent tissue, green-gold and copper-black, rooted not in soil but in seams of reactive ore. They rise sparsely, with great distances between colonies, but each colony is abundant in itself: thick curtains, nested fronds, branching glassy blades, coiled tendrils like frozen smoke.

The vegetation does not sway. It has no softness. Its surfaces are waxy, ceramic, or metallic, depending on the species. Some plants resemble bundles of organ pipes made of green enamel, each tube capped with a powdery yellow bloom. Some spread low in radial mats of flexible scales, gathering heat from the sun and electric charge from the ground. Others grow as high, singular towers: ribbed, hollow columns with many slits, through which the dry air occasionally passes with a sound too faint to be called music.

They are lush the way a crystal cave may be lush: much growth, much complexity, no dampness. A whole ecology without thirst.

Their leaves, if they are leaves, are built to drink light directly and feed on minerals. They crack silicates, leach metals, braid carbon from the warm air. Instead of transpiring, they seal themselves. Instead of blooming with scent, they exude volatile dusts that fall straight down in halos around their bases. The ground beneath the larger groves is stained in rings of color — violet, rust, sulfur-yellow, blue-black — where generations of dry secretions have accumulated and hardened. These rings are the closest thing to soil, though they are more like industrial residue than earth.

No animals live here. No insects pick through the fronds. No birds cross the sky. No burrow mars the crust. There are no tracks, nests, droppings, bones, shells, shed skins, or sudden motions in the corner of the eye. The absence is so complete that after a time it becomes a presence of its own. The vegetation does not wait to be eaten. It does not defend itself with thorns, poison, mimicry, or speed. It grows without predation, without pollinators, without grazing, without alarm. Reproduction is handled by patient chemistry: spores like flakes of mica detach only after years of charge accumulation, fall within a few meters, and either remain dormant in the mineral glare or fuse with a suitable seam and begin to build.

The silence is nearly whole.

There is no water to run, drip, boil, freeze, or lap. No animal calls, no wingbeats, no crawling. The warmth produces no buzzing life. Sound exists only as small events: a brittle tick from a contracting crystal sheet; a slow, internal ping from a cooling stone; the brief whisper of mineral dust sliding down a slope too new to have found its final angle. In the larger groves there is an almost inaudible electrical murmur, not a hum exactly, more like pressure in the teeth. Sometimes the hollow tube-plants breathe a single low note when the air shifts across their openings, and then the whole plateau seems to listen until the note is gone.

Despite the absence of built structures, the place feels industrial.

This is not because anything has been made. There are no towers, roads, pipes, rails, gantries, walls, quarries, pylons, or machines. Nothing here bears the mark of hands or tools. But industrial processes occur everywhere, distributed through geology and plant tissue. The plateau is a factory without architecture.

Under the crust, slow engines run.

Not engines of pistons and wheels, but mineral engines: thermal gradients trapped in sealed strata, piezoelectric beds under constant tectonic pressure, veins of metal-rich compounds exchanging charge through rootlike filaments. The plants tap these systems. Their bases are socketed into ore. Their trunks click faintly with static. Their broad mineral leaves maintain glossy arrays of conductive veins, gathering sunlight and ground-current with the efficiency of panels. In some basins, thousands of low black shrubs form regular grids, not planted but self-arranged by electrical repulsion, each one spaced several body-lengths from the next. Between them the ground is bare, bright, and untouched.

The ecology resembles industry because it is scaled production: energy captured, minerals sorted, gases separated, waste laid down in consistent bands. Some groves extrude thin sheets of transparent resin that peel away and lie stacked around them like discarded manufacturing film. Others grow nodules of pure metal at their joints, then drop them when they become too heavy; the nodules lie scattered in the dust, polished and identical, as though stamped from a mold. In the warmest flats, squat organisms with graphite skins split atmospheric compounds and vent a slow, invisible exhaust, leaving behind ridges of white carbonate ash.

But nothing moves quickly enough to seem alive.

The world is dynamically calm, nearly time-invariant. The sun crosses the sky; shadows lengthen and withdraw; heat enters stone and leaves it. Beyond that, change is withheld. Growth here happens at the pace of mineral replacement. A frond may thicken by a finger’s width in a century. A crystal plate may finish forming across an exposed seam over several human lifetimes. A fallen tube may remain where it lies for millennia, unrotted, uneaten, slowly losing color under light.

There are no storms. Or if storms exist, they belong to another climate system and never reach this height. The sky is clear most days, not piercingly blue but somewhat bleached by suspended high dust. The light is normal enough to trust: steady, warm, directional, with ordinary shadows. That normality makes the rest stranger. One expects heat shimmer, flies, distant weather, the moving shine of water, some animal flitting from cover. Instead there is open visibility and almost no incident. The eye travels far and finds only spaced presences in a vast dry interval.

The plateau’s emptiness is not barren exactly. It is empty by volume. There may be groves, but the distances between them are immense. A single cluster of copper-green fans might occupy an acre, intricate and luxuriant, and then nothing but pale mineral plain extends for miles. Another growth may stand on the next ridge: three black columns, each thirty meters tall, their fluted surfaces catching light like oiled basalt. Farther on, a basin might hold a whole dry forest of blade-plants, dense within itself, but isolated like an island in a dead salt sea.

Walking there would distort one’s sense of abundance. Up close, the vegetation is lavish: layered, patterned, abundant with structures whose purposes are not immediately visible. Step away, and the land becomes vacant again. Lushness appears in rare concentrations, islands of life separated by enormous, sterile intervals.

The terrain underfoot alternates between smooth mineral skin and fractured emergence. Some fields are made of flat plates, slightly raised at the edges, as if the crust swelled from below and hardened before it could break. Some are granular, composed of pellets of fused dust that crunch like ceramic beads. Others are treacherous with embryonic stone: half-formed ridges, bubble-like domes, cracks that open cleanly into darkness but contain no moisture, no roots, no smell of earth. In places the plateau has blistered upward into long ridgelines, their cores still glassy and black. These ridges cast narrow shadows where pale crystal rime grows in silent combs.

The lack of erosion gives everything an uncanny freshness. Slopes are too steep for their age. Edges are unchipped. Deposits lie where they formed, not where gravity and weather would have carried them. Even the dust seems local, born from the immediate material and never transported far. There are no alluvial fans, no braided channels, no dunes migrating under wind. The world has surfaces but no history of surfaces wearing down.

And yet it is stable. It does not feel young in the sense of restless birth. It feels newly made and then held, preserved at the first draft. A geological embryo kept warm under glass.

The rules of the world are rules of sealing and separation.

Nothing here circulates freely. Water, if it ever existed, has been locked away in minerals too deep or too tightly bound to matter. Organic chemistry proceeds without liquid flow, through films, charges, vapor phases, and direct contact between living tissue and stone. Nutrients do not wash downhill; they must be touched, cracked, electrically persuaded, or slowly sublimed from air. Waste does not rot; it plates, powders, crystallizes, vitrifies. Death is not decay but deactivation. A dead plant remains as a husk of useful architecture until light erases its pigments and thermal stress opens it into shards.

Fire is rare, perhaps impossible in the usual sense. There is heat, oxidation, energy, but little fuel that burns. The vegetation is mineral-rich and sealed, built not to flame but to conduct. Lightning, if it strikes the high ridges, leaves glass scars and activates dormant seams. Around such scars grow the most elaborate colonies: black filaments rising from fused ground, each filament bearing reflective plates arranged like cooling fins.

The air is warm and dry enough to feel almost manufactured. It has a faint metallic taste. Breathing it might leave the mouth coated with bitterness. It carries smells poorly, but near the groves there are slight odors: hot resin, ozone, powdered stone, the sour tang of reactive minerals. Because there are no animals and no water, there is no musk, no rot, no green wet vegetal smell. The lushness is visual and structural rather than fragrant.

At noon the place becomes lucid. Light falls evenly across the high plains. The pale crust glares but does not dazzle; the darker ridges drink heat and blur at their edges. The vegetation shows its internal colors: amber veins, green panes, black conductive lattices, red mineral inclusions like trapped embers. Shadows under the groves are delicate and complicated, cast by hundreds of rigid fronds and translucent blades. Nothing rustles. Nothing hides.

At evening, the warmth lingers in stone. The sky takes on a dusty peach color, and the mineral plants hold the sun for a long time, glowing faintly from within after direct light is gone. Some release stored charge in slow pulses that travel from root to crown. Across the plateau, isolated groves answer one another with silent flickers, not communication perhaps, but equilibrium being maintained across distance. The land does not brighten enough to become magical; it merely reveals that its industrial life has a night shift.

Under starlight, the open spaces become almost voidlike. The pale ground reflects just enough to erase depth. The groves stand as black, complex masses separated by miles of empty mineral. The high ridges cut the sky in hard lines. There are no nocturnal calls, no scurryings. If a crystal cracks in the cold, the sound travels a long way and then leaves no echo.

It is possible that the entire plateau is one organism, or one factory-like process mistaken for an ecology. The separate groves may be surface organs of a buried system spread through conductive strata. Their spacing may not be chance but necessity, each colony occupying the reach of a subterranean node. The empty land between them may be as important as the growths: insulating fields, heat reservoirs, charge gaps, reaction chambers too broad and slow for ordinary perception.

Or perhaps the place is not alive in any unified way. Perhaps it is simply a high dry world where life arose without animals, without water, without haste, and therefore came to resemble machinery: regular, sealed, efficient, silent.

Nothing here invites habitation. There is no shelter except the thin shadows of fronds. No soil to cultivate. No stream to follow. No animal trail to read. No wood, no fruit, no birdsong, no weathered cave mouth dark with use. The landscape is untouched not because it is pristine in the pastoral sense, but because it offers almost no terms on which a visitor could belong.

Still, it is not hostile in an active way. It does not hunt, poison, collapse, or rage. Its danger lies in indifference and dryness. It would preserve footprints for a very long time if the crust accepted them at all. It would preserve a fallen body even longer, reducing it not through scavenging or decay but through desiccation, mineral exchange, and light.

The place remains itself.

Warm high air. Pale crust. Black new ridges. Vast intervals of nothing. Lush colonies of glass-green mineral vegetation standing motionless in their rings of industrial residue. Silent production under the ground. Normal sunlight on impossible biology. No water, no animals, no buildings, almost no sound, almost no change.

A world before rivers, after machines, and outside the habits of life as it is usually known.
