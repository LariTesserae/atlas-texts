The place is low and enclosed, not by walls in the ordinary sense, but by living thickness. It feels about the size of a ruined hamlet or an old factory yard that the world has folded inward and kept humid: a bounded pocket of warm air, green pressure, and slow mechanical breath. You enter it through a sag in the vegetation where the ground dips, and immediately the outside weather becomes less important. Here the climate has been made by leaves.

Everything is wet, but not flooded. Water is not the main body of the landscape; it is the hidden medium beneath it. The ground gives slightly underfoot, a packed mat of root-felt, old stems, mud, and fibrous decay. Pools appear only in seams and low hollows, dark and oval, their surfaces crowded by pollen, skins of green algae, drowned petals, and the collapsed ribs of fallen leaves. More often the water shows itself as seepage: beads along vine-sheaths, drips from the undersides of broad leaves, clear threads running down the faces of old concrete, a constant dampness at the base of every upright thing. The air condenses on metal, on bark, on skin. Nothing is dry enough to sound crisp.

Vegetation occupies almost all available volume. It is not simply growing over the place; it has become the place’s material, its architecture, its furniture, its machinery in waiting. Trunks, canes, lianas, aerial roots, succulent panels, mossy cushions, and mats of fern crowd into one another until the eye cannot easily distinguish individual plants. There are leaves the size of doors, leaves the size of coins, leaves like black-green tongues, leaves with translucent veins that catch the ordinary daylight and turn it yellow. Stems braid around stems. Roots descend from overhead in cords and curtains, some fine as hair, some thick as wrists, all damp. The vegetation has learned to stack itself: groundcover beneath shrubs beneath climbing vines beneath squat trees beneath fungal shelves beneath epiphytes that hold their own little reservoirs of rain.

The space is small, but because it is so packed it feels intricate, almost inexhaustible. Paths do not cross it cleanly. They are remembered rather than maintained: narrow compressions in the plant mass, places where the root-mat has hardened under repeated passage, where leaves lean away by habit. A person moving through must shoulder aside living surfaces. The plants close after every disturbance, slowly but with conviction, as if the landscape is embarrassed by openings.

There are built things here, though none dominate. Their presence is scattered and partial, like tools left in a garden that continued for a century. Low brick footings emerge and vanish beneath vines. A concrete channel runs along one side of the basin, its edges softened by moss, its center filled with black water and floating seedlings. Iron posts stand in a row, some upright, some pulled askew by roots, connected by slack cables furred with lichen and beadlike condensation. Under the largest tree-canopy is a roof without walls: corrugated metal plates, rusted through in lace patterns, held up partly by a steel frame and partly by strangler growth that has tightened around the beams.

Beyond the roof, half-buried in leaf mass, are the remnants of small industrial apparatus. Not a great factory, but a local works, a place once meant for processing, pumping, pressing, distilling. There are cylindrical tanks greened over until they resemble swollen trunks. A flywheel stands upright in a tangle of creepers, its rim visible in sections, like the vertebra of some extinct machine-animal. Pipe mouths protrude from the mud and exhale warm vapor at intervals. A belt-driven mechanism under the roof still turns, though almost imperceptibly: a pulley creaks, pauses, advances a fraction; water drips from a leaf into a tilted pan; the pan lowers; somewhere a counterweight rises inside a thicket. The system is not exactly running, not exactly dead. It has entered into a patient collaboration with gravity, seepage, rot, and growth.

Electricity exists here in a damp, unreliable form. Insulators hang from old ceramic brackets. Thin wires disappear into vines and reappear thirty feet away wrapped around a branch that has swallowed them halfway. Occasionally, with a soft tick and a blue-white fleck, a contact closes somewhere in the green mass. A lamp under the roof flickers weakly though no one tends it, its glass colonized by tiny ferns and root hairs. The light is mostly normal daylight, filtered but not fantastical: warm, humid, green-edged, falling through gaps in the canopy as pale shafts. Yet the machinery’s little electrical pulses give the place a secret nervousness. The plants seem to notice them. Certain leaves turn over when a relay clicks. Fine tendrils gather along the wires in dense knots, as if the current were mineral nourishment.

The atmosphere is warm without being hot, the kind of warmth that belongs to sheltered wet places. Heat rises from the dark compost beneath the roots. Concrete holds a mild stored temperature. Metal sweats. The air touches everything and lingers. It smells of chlorophyll crushed underfoot, warm mud, rust, fungal sweetness, wet brick, old oil, and the faint bitter odor of sap from wounded stems. In deeper corners there is a smell like fermenting fruit, though no fruit can be seen, only bulbous seed-cases hanging in shadow.

The sound is louder than the scale suggests. There are few animals, so the noise is not birdsong or insect chatter except in occasional, isolated threads. Instead the loudness comes from water and plant and machine. Drips strike metal roofs, leaves, pooled water, hollow stems, and each surface gives back a different note. Condensation gathers and falls in sudden little showers. Hidden pumps thud at long intervals, not rhythmically enough to be trusted. Pipes gurgle. Something like a bellows sighs beneath the root-mat. Broad leaves rub against one another with a leathery rasp whenever the air moves. A vine under tension creaks. Somewhere water pours continuously through a concealed vertical shaft, amplified by the old industrial housing into a low, room-filling roar.

But the place is slow. Nothing hurries except the falling drops. Its processes are gradual and established, long past invasion, long past collapse. This is not a ruin being newly reclaimed; it has already been digested into equilibrium. Brick, moss, vine, pipe, root, tank, and pool have negotiated their positions over many seasons. The plants do not simply break the structures apart. They use them. A rusted railing becomes a trellis. A cracked basin becomes a nursery. The factory roof becomes a shaded leaf-ceiling. A conveyor frame, frozen at waist height, supports a dense hedge of waxy growth whose roots dangle through the rollers and drink from the channel below.

The terrain itself is gently uneven, low but not flat. It lies in a shallow depression, perhaps once excavated, perhaps naturally settled where water gathers near the surface. The perimeter rises only a little, but enough to hold the damp air and make the interior feel sunken. Along the edges are hummocks formed by old root crowns and buried debris. The center is a softer basin, treacherous in places, where mats of vegetation disguise dark mud underneath. Stones are rare and rounded where they appear, their surfaces weeping. Concrete slabs remain in islands, tilted by subsidence and root pressure, slick with green growth. Between them, the living ground has sealed the gaps.

Fauna is scarce, and that scarcity gives the place an uncanny stillness despite its noise. Once in a while a small pale amphibian appears in a water-filled footprint and vanishes under duckweed. A beetle with an oil-black back crosses a rusted beam. Something very light moves in the canopy, dislodging droplets, but it is never clearly seen. There are no flocks, no swarms, no busy animal traffic. The plants seem to have taken up all available roles. Some flowers mimic moths; some seedpods click like insects; some tendrils recoil from touch with almost animal displeasure.

The weirdness of the place is not theatrical at first. It accumulates. You notice that some leaves have grown into repeating arrangements around pressure gauges, each gauge face clouded but still faintly marked, the needles trembling in response to changes in sap pressure nearby. You notice that roots do not always descend toward soil; some thread themselves into pipe openings and emerge elsewhere, carrying moisture across the site like deliberate plumbing. A cluster of reeds near the old tank releases steam from their hollow stems at the same moment a valve knocks underground. The boundary between plant metabolism and industrial process has blurred so completely that the landscape seems to operate as a single organism with inherited machinery for bones.

There are rules here, though they are not posted.

Open spaces close unless kept in motion.

Any hollow will collect water; any water will grow a skin; any skin will become a substrate.

Metal left untouched becomes a root-path.

Electric current attracts tendrils.

Sound travels through pipes farther than through air.

Nothing burns easily.

Nothing remains merely dead.

If a thing repeats for long enough—drip, pulse, wheel-turn, leaf-fall—the place adopts it as weather.

Under the roof, where the loudest dripping gathers, is perhaps the heart of the enclosure: a worktable or altar, though it was likely once a sorting bench. It is a slab of stone or poured concrete, waist-high, covered in moss except where water constantly runs over it. Above it hangs a chain hoist, immobile, its hook embedded in a woody stem that has grown around it in a smooth collar. On the bench are shallow trays filled not with tools but with seedlings arranged by chance or by some slow sorting mechanism: copper-colored sprouts in one, threadlike white roots in another, tiny floating leaves in a third. Every few minutes a drop falls from the hook into the central tray. The seedlings flex toward the impact.

The longer you stay, the less the place seems abandoned. It is occupied to saturation, just not by occupants that move on a human timescale. The roofs sag because vines have chosen them. The channels run because roots keep them open where water is useful. The old machines turn because the plants have not yet found a reason to stop them. Even decay here feels maintained. A fallen beam is furred evenly. A broken wall has become a vertical meadow. Rust spreads in beautiful, regulated blooms beneath films of moisture.

At the edge of the enclosure, the vegetation thickens into a near-solid palisade. Leaves overlap like scales. Behind them, the outer world is reduced to a pale flicker. Within, the sound of dripping and gurgling continues, large in the small space. Warm vapor slides along the ground. Somewhere a relay ticks; somewhere a hidden pump answers with one dull stroke. The plants lean, not quickly, not visibly, but with the patience of things that have already won.

It is a marsh-edge engine-grove, a little wet factory of leaves and roots, settled into itself. It does not expand dramatically. It does not collapse. It condenses, thickens, processes, drinks. Its history is still present, but no longer as memory alone: the past has been photosynthesized, threaded through with pipes, and kept alive in green machinery.
