At a height where mountains should have become bare, where the air should have thinned to glass and frost, there is a hot city of plants and water.

It occupies a high basin cupped among vertical peaks, though the peaks do not quite behave like stone. They rise in long, dark walls around the place, veined with mineral sheen and greenish moisture, their upper ridges lost not in cloud but in a steady whitening of distance, as if sight itself is being boiled away. The elevation is extreme: every direction implies falling. Beyond the outer terraces the land drops into gorges so deep that their bottoms are only a darker pressure in the world. But within the basin there is no feeling of wind, no alpine barrenness, no clean cold. Heat lies over everything, heavy and saturated, pressing downward like a hand.

The air sweats.

It is not mist exactly. It has the thickness of air after rain, multiplied until every breath tastes of leaves, stone, warm standing water, and old minerals. Condensation gathers continually on every surface. Cliff faces weep in thousands of threads. Parapets bead and run. Roots shine. The sides of buildings — if they are buildings — are slick with living varnish. Water is near the skin of the world here, always near, always arriving, yet nothing seems to flood, collapse, or drain away. The place has long ago reached some impossible balance between mountain, marsh, ruin, and city.

The basin is broad enough that it cannot be taken in at once. It opens in districts: terraces, canals, green courts, stepped avenues, roof-gardens, pools, causeways, hanging ledges, cliff chambers, and drowned plazas. The arrangement is urban, but not technological. There are no machines, no metal lines, no lamps, no wheels, no tools left lying in doorways. The construction feels organized without feeling made by hands. Walls interlock like coral. Towers rise with the logic of termite mounds, banyan trunks, and eroded temples. Streets are not paved so much as hardened into continuous pale stone, root, shell, and mineral deposit. Stairs descend into pools and emerge elsewhere at different angles. Arcades hold ferns in their ceilings. Doorways open into masses of leaves, into vapor, into other doorways.

It is a city without inhabitants.

Or rather, without animals. No birds cross the hot air. No insects stitch the edges of the pools. No frogs call from the marshy steps. No fish disturb the black-green canals. There is no scuttle in the undergrowth, no distant cry, no wingbeat, no grazing, no watching eyes. The absence is not quiet, because the place is deafening. But the absence has a shape. The vegetation grows as if relieved of being eaten. It is lush beyond hunger: enormous leaves layered against one another, vine curtains as thick as woven mats, moss like wet velvet over balustrades, reed beds standing in old plazas, aerial roots descending from roofline to waterline in pillars and veils. Flowers exist but do not court anything. Their colors are swollen and private — bruised violet, waxy white, furnace-orange, blue as mineral stains — and they release fragrance into air already too full.

The sound is overwhelming.

Water makes most of it, or appears to. Water falls everywhere: from cliffs, from roofs, from holes in walls, from the undersides of ledges, from suspended gardens whose soil never diminishes. It rushes through channels beneath the streets, hammers into cisterns, pours down stairwells, spills from terrace to terrace in sheets. The whole city roars with a single continuous body of falling and circulating water. Yet the roar does not rise and fall. There is no rhythm of rain beginning, no storm passing, no surge after thaw. It is constant. Deafening and constant. A white, enclosing sound that erases distance while somehow revealing the scale of everything around it.

Under that aquatic thunder there are other noises that might not be noises. A stone hum. A pressure in the teeth. Long vowel-tones coming from the cliff walls, as if the mountain is being bowed by invisible hands. The slap of water repeats too regularly in some places, like a word spoken by a throat too large to understand. In certain courtyards the sound seems to stand upright, occupying columns of air. In narrow lanes it thickens until it feels touchable, a hot vibration resting against the skin.

Nothing changes.

This is the strangest thing, stranger even than the impossible height and heat, stranger than the empty city and the plants growing from architecture not made by tools. The place is not still in the ordinary sense — water falls, leaves glisten, steam hangs, sound roars — but all of it is fixed in equilibrium. The waterfalls do not erode their basins any further. The leaves do not wilt or unfurl. Droplets gather on stone lips and remain there, fat and trembling, never falling, while nearby sheets of water descend forever. A broken stair has been broken for so long that moss has accepted the fracture as a proper edge, and yet the break is not decaying further. Vines loop through windows in perfect long-established arcs, neither tightening nor loosening. The marshes do not advance. The stone does not dry. The heat does not build toward storm. The world holds itself in a state after event but before consequence.

The light is ordinary, almost cruelly so. There is no alien color in the sky, no perpetual eclipse, no obvious celestial wound. A diffuse day fills the basin. It is bright enough to see textures clearly: the grain of wet stone, the translucent edges of leaves, the milky deposits around canal mouths, the blackness inside doorways. Shadows exist, though softened by vapor. The sun, if it is present, rarely shows as a disk; it is a broad authority above the whitened rim of the peaks. This normal light makes the breakdown of reality more intimate. Nothing is theatrically transformed. Instead the errors are embedded in function.

A canal turns a corner and continues upward along a wall without spilling.

A terrace visible from below contains the same courtyard visible from above, but reversed, as if the city folds around an axis that has no physical location.

In some alleys, perspective lengthens while the body does not move. A row of arches may be ten paces away for several minutes. Then, without transition, it is already behind you. Steps carved downward arrive at a roof. Pools reflect not the sky but adjacent streets, seen at noon, or from childhood, or from a height no one has occupied. Doorframes are faithful to geometry only when approached directly; glimpsed from the corner of the eye, they open into immense green interiors where columns of water hang motionless like glass.

The rules of the world here are not absent. They are local.

Each district keeps its own compact. In the reed courts, vertical things are more real than horizontal ones. Pillars, stems, falling streams, roots, and standing bodies possess firmness; floors become negotiable, a suggestion of support over a depth of warm water. In the mineral quarter, surfaces remember pressure rather than time, so footsteps are not heard when made but appear later as darkening ovals in the stone. In the hanging gardens along the cliff, distance is governed by moisture: a nearby dry object may be unreachable, while a far wet surface seems to draw the hand toward it. In the drowned amphitheater, sound has weight; the roar of water pools in the lower seats, and silence, where it exists in small pockets behind walls or under leaves, floats near the ceiling like trapped air.

The city’s materials are difficult to separate. Stone has root in it. Root has stone in it. Walls appear grown from stratified limestone, basalt, and pale calcified plant matter. Some buildings have faces like cliffs smoothed by long water erosion, but their windows are too evenly spaced, their terraces too intentional, their processional ways too deliberate. Other structures seem botanical, but their trunks join at right angles and carry load with architectural patience. A bridge crosses one of the larger canals in a single living span: woody ribs, mineral crust, moss cushions, water running through capillary grooves. It is ancient, not decayed. Its age is expressed by completeness.

Everywhere there are basins.

Some are open pools green with suspended light. Some are deep shafts at the centers of plazas, lined with smooth stone and ferns, filled with dark water that never ripples despite the roar around them. Some occupy rooms whose ceilings have collapsed into root networks. Some are no larger than bowls set into thresholds, though there are no bowls here in any crafted sense — only hollows that have become necessary. In these basins float leaves, petals, mineral skins, pale threads of plant matter. Nothing living swims beneath them. When a falling stream strikes their surface, the splash repeats identically, a crown of droplets rising and returning without variation.

The heat has its own architecture. It gathers in corridors and domes. It stands under overhangs. It presses from the ground as much as from the air. On exposed terraces it blazes softly through vapor, making the wet stone shine. In enclosed garden courts it becomes almost bodily, a green heat, smelling of sap, mud, and respiration. Yet there is no breeze to relieve or worsen it. The air’s saturation is complete. Sweat would have nowhere to go. Evaporation is not a promise here.

The vegetation is dense but not chaotic. It follows the old urban plan, or perhaps the urban plan follows it. Broad-leaved trees occupy plazas like civic monuments. Root systems trace avenues, gripping both sides of canals in braided embankments. Moss marks the shaded sides of stair towers. Lianas hang across streets at consistent heights, as if forming gates. Ferns prefer the mouths of drains. Lotus-like plants fill shallow roof pools, their leaves overlapping in circular districts of their own. Thick reed walls screen courtyards where the water is only ankle-deep over polished floors. In the highest parts of the city, near the cliff-hung balconies, trees grow horizontally out into the open air, their branches supporting curtains of aerial roots that vanish into mist far below.

No animal has ever cut a path through this growth. No trail is narrow from repeated passage. No leaves show bites. No nests are tucked into crevices. The ecology is closed around plant, water, mineral, heat, and sound. Pollination, if it occurs, must be accomplished by dripping, by condensation, by the memory of contact, by laws that replace insects with pressure and time. Seeds may not travel; perhaps they arrive where the city requires them. Perhaps each plant has always occupied its exact position, mature, wet, and unchanging.

The established quality of the place is profound. It does not feel newly ruined or newly grown. There is no story of catastrophe visible, no fresh collapse, no invasion of jungle over abandoned streets. Instead it feels as though city and marsh and mountain reached agreement ages ago and have maintained it perfectly. Erosion has rounded edges but not erased intention. Water has polished channels but not deepened them past use. Roots have entered walls but not broken them beyond their ordained fractures. Everything that might be called damage has become grammar.

At the basin’s center stands a complex of high terraces around a circular reservoir. It can be seen from many districts, though its apparent distance varies. Sometimes it is a short walk beyond a curtain of vines. Sometimes it sits across the whole green city, surrounded by steaming roofs and falling water. The reservoir is wide, black, and still. Around it rise tiered structures like civic halls, temple platforms, bathhouses, or natural shelves. Their columns are thick and wet, swollen with moss. Water pours from their upper levels in symmetrical sheets, but the reservoir below does not stir.

The roar there is at its loudest, so loud it becomes nearly peaceful, obliterating thought before thought can form. The ordinary light lies flat on the black water. Reflections appear, but not of the terraces. They show a high sky crowded with white roots. They show a dry plain under stars. They show the same reservoir filled with people-shaped absences, spaces where bodies should displace light but do not. Look too long and the reflection begins to include viewpoints from places around the rim where no one stands.

There is no evidence of builders, yet the city insists on having been built. There is no animal life, yet the place feels observed. There is no machinery, yet systems operate: circulation, support, irrigation, acoustics, division, procession, containment. There is no change, yet processes continue. The contradiction is not an error to be solved. It is the foundation.

At the outer rim, where the terraces meet the mountain walls, the city becomes more vertical. Streets climb in switchbacks cut into wet stone. Houses or chambers open directly into the cliff, their thresholds curtained with vines and falling threads of water. Narrow canals run along ledges, impossibly level, disappearing into cracks from which a hot breath emerges. The cliff itself is alive with slow green masses, but not in motion. Ferns occupy every shelf. Roots enter fissures and come out as mineral veins. The weeping rock shines bronze, black, and gray beneath the moss. From certain heights the entire basin can be seen: an immense, steaming, layered settlement held in a ring of peaks, roaring without weather, lush without animals, ancient without history.

Above it, the normal daylight persists.

Below it, perhaps, the gorges drop into other versions of the same place. The sound rising from them is indistinguishable from the city’s own. Water falls into depths and returns from roofs. Heat rises from shadow and condenses on upper leaves. The peaks contain the basin, but they also seem produced by it, like rims formed around a permanent wound in geography.

If there is a path through this landscape, it is not for travel but for recognition. One moves from wet stair to fern court to canal bridge to root arcade, while the place remains exactly what it is. The deafening water covers all footfall. The plants lean near without touching. The walls sweat. The air glows with ordinary day. Every surface is saturated; every distance is open; every district is full. And beneath the roar, beneath the heat, beneath the impossible stability of falling water and unmoving change, there is the sense that reality here did not break suddenly.

It settled this way.

It weathered into impossibility.

It has been in balance for so long that balance itself has become strange.
