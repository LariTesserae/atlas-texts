At the top of the world, above the ordinary weather, there is a plateau that should have been a mountain range but has forgotten how to rise.

It lies at an extreme elevation, so high that the air has the thin, scraped quality of old glass. Breath does not fog there in the usual way. It leaves the mouth as a pale thread and stays intact for several minutes, drifting sideways in a perfectly straight line until it reaches one of the standing cracks in the air and vanishes into it. The cold is not violent. It does not storm or bite or rush over the skin. It is patient, deeply installed in stone, in shadow, in the marrow of exposed ridges. Everything has been cold for so long that coldness feels less like a condition than a material from which the country is partly made.

The land is open but not grand in the way of sweeping lowlands. It opens by absence. Long shelves of gray-black rock extend outward into dimness, falling away into basins, rising again into blunt scarps and flat saddles. The horizon is close in some directions and impossibly far in others, because distance is not consistent here. A ridge that appears a day’s walk away can be reached in a few minutes if approached without looking directly at it; a stone cairn twenty paces distant may remain twenty paces distant for hours. The whole region seems valley-sized at first, then mountain-sized, then like the interior of an enormous hollow bone.

The terrain has been weathered into a state of stillness. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the fractures seem ancient, their edges softened by frost and faint mineral sweating. Scree lies in settled fans that have not shifted in generations. Ledges have the balanced repose of things that fell long ago and have since negotiated a permanent arrangement with gravity. There are gullies, but no active streams in most of them; polished runnels descend from the higher faces and end in dark pockets where water gathers only intermittently. Some hollows hold skins of ice so clear that they seem to cover not water but openings into a darker, lower weather.

Moisture arrives without drama. It condenses on the underside of overhangs, collects along seams in the rock, forms beads on lichen, and occasionally falls as a sparse, cold rain from clouds too thin to be seen. There are no rivers in the ordinary sense, but there are river-memories: shallow channels winding across the plateau, pebble beds arranged by some former current, and smooth mineral stains that glimmer when the dim light leans across them. In a few places water moves under stone. You hear it before you find it: a muffled rushing beneath slabs, a buried liquid insistence traveling through the mountain’s ribs. Sometimes the sound is far too loud for the amount of water present. A trickle in a crack may roar like a cataract. A basin no wider than a hand may contain the acoustic depth of an ocean cave.

Sound is the only thing here that behaves abundantly.

The landscape itself is nearly empty. Vast distances separate the few presences. A boulder, a tuft of black grass, a bone-white post, a low roofless wall: each stands alone with too much space around it, as if the world has not fully loaded the intervals between objects. Yet the air is loud. It hums in ravines. It booms softly from fissures. It carries a continuous, many-layered pressure of noise: subglacial grinding without glaciers, wind without movement, distant avalanches that never arrive, bells heard through stone, the long exhalation of a sleeping animal the size of a mountain. The sound does not rise or fade. It is stable, tremendous, and unchanging. After a while it becomes difficult to tell whether the plateau is loud or whether hearing itself has been stretched open.

There is little wind, though everything sounds wind-struck.

The light is dim and diffuse, arriving from no dependable sun. The sky is usually the color of tarnished tin, with a faint violet bruise at the zenith. Shadows are weak but persistent. They do not always attach themselves to objects. In some basins, the shadows of absent things lie across the ground: long bars, branching antlers, the silhouette of a tower that is nowhere visible. At certain angles the rock reflects a light that seems older than daylight, a dull internal sheen like frozen oil.

Vegetation survives in sparse, deliberate forms. There are low mats of lichen in rust, ash-green, and a blue so dark it appears wet even when dry. These grow in circles around cracks, never over them. Stiff grasses appear in small, widely spaced knots, each blade curled inward against the cold. Some are translucent near the tips, holding frozen droplets in their tissues like little lenses. A shrub might occur once in several miles: knee-high, leafless, its branches black and jointed, bearing hard pale nodules instead of buds. These nodules do not bloom. They click very faintly when the great background sound passes through them.

Moss grows only where condensation repeats itself exactly. Under some shelves it forms narrow green lines, marking the places where water has fallen at the same interval for centuries. Because almost nothing changes, the moss has learned time as geometry. It grows in stripes, beads, nested crescents. A patch the size of a hand may be older than a forest.

Animals are rare enough to seem ceremonial. A white, blunt-winged bird sometimes crosses the plateau below eye level, moving between cliffs with slow, muscular strokes. It makes no call, but its passage leaves a temporary absence in the surrounding roar, a bird-shaped silence that drifts after it. In the deeper cracks live small blind creatures like hares or marmots, though they are seldom seen except as warmth in a crevice or as paired tracks appearing and disappearing in frost. Their tracks are troubling: each footfall is perfect, identical to the others, as though stamped by memory rather than made by an animal.

Once in a long while, something larger stands on a distant ridge. It resembles an ibex until watched too closely. Its horns curve not backward but inward, disappearing into the space between them; its body is steady, unbreathing, fur rimed with silver condensation. It may remain there for days without moving. Eventually the observer looks away, and when they look back, the animal has not left but has become a rock shaped like waiting.

The built traces are scattered and small. No settlement exists, yet the plateau has been marked. Low cairns stand at irregular intervals, made from flat stones fitted without mortar. Some are no higher than the ankle; others reach the height of a seated person. Their stones are weathered into one another, fused by frost, lichen, and time. They do not indicate paths exactly, because paths do not persist here unless continuously believed. Rather, the cairns indicate agreements: places where the land and whatever once crossed it decided to remain mutually recognizable.

There are also shelters, or remnants of shelters. A ring of stones around a floor of packed earth. A half-collapsed wall tucked into the lee of a cliff. A roof beam petrified into something like basalt, though no trees of that size could have grown here. Doorways without rooms stand in two places, built from upright slabs. Passing through them changes nothing visible, except that the roar of the plateau briefly becomes articulate, nearly speech, before settling back into pressure.

No tools remain. No metal, no glass, no machinery, no evidence of fire except for shallow black stains beneath overhangs that might be soot or might be mineral bloom. The built things feel pre-technological not because they are primitive, but because technology would be irrelevant here. Mechanisms would find no purchase. Wheels would forget rotation. Needles would not point. Measures would return different answers each time, except for the cold, which is always the same.

The rules of the place are old and mostly stable, though they are not the rules of ordinary ground.

Nothing changes, yet reality fails continuously.

This is the plateau’s central contradiction. It is in equilibrium with its own impossibility. The cracks in the air remain in the same positions. The impossible distances keep their habits. The sound is always loud in the same way. Water condenses from invisible weather at fixed points and falls in fixed rhythms. Shadows detach according to ancient alignments. If a stone floats two finger-widths above the ground, it has floated there for so long that lichen has grown on its underside and frost has formed a little pedestal below it, not touching. If a ravine contains two visible bottoms — one near, one far beneath it, both equally real — they have coexisted long enough for scree to settle toward both.

There are seams where the world has been folded incorrectly. Along one ridge, the same cliff face repeats seven times, each repetition smaller and dimmer, receding not into distance but into likelihood. In a basin to the north, a tarn appears only as a reflection: the water itself is absent, but the surrounding rocks show its rippled image, and a cold wet smell rises from the empty depression. Elsewhere, a field of stones casts shadows upward onto the underside of the sky. The shadows have texture; they look touchable. Birds avoid them.

In one long valley, sound has weight. The roar thickens there until it presses on the shoulders and gathers in hollows like fog. Loose pebbles rest on top of it. Condensation forms on its invisible surface. To descend into that valley is to wade through noise up to the knees, then the waist, while hearing becomes muffled not by quiet but by too much nearness. Yet the level never rises or falls. The valley has been half-filled with sound since before the cairns were built.

There is no day-night cycle that anyone can prove. The dimness varies only by attention. Look at the sky too long and it darkens. Study the ground and the light increases enough to reveal mineral grains, frost hairs, the tiny red rims of lichen colonies. Sleep does not correspond to darkness. Those who rest here wake with the sense that no time has passed, and also that every stone has become slightly more certain of itself.

The ground is mostly stone: slate-dark plates, pale granular ribs, patches of iron-stained gravel, and high fields of fractured schist that ring faintly underfoot. In sheltered basins the surface becomes a powdery, frozen soil mixed with mica flakes and ancient organic dust. It accepts footprints reluctantly. A footprint may remain crisp for years, or close behind the heel before the next step lands. Sometimes there are footprints ahead of the walker, matching their stride exactly, already rimed with frost.

Edges matter. Crests, cracks, rims of hollows, the lips of dry channels — these are the active parts of the landscape. Centers are empty and inert. A broad flat may contain nothing at all except one dark seam running through it, and that seam will hold moisture, sound, lichen, memory, and danger. The sparse life of the plateau clings to boundaries because boundaries are where reality leaks enough to nourish things.

The ecology is therefore an ecology of condensation and error. Lichens feed on mineral, cold light, and the faint residue left where distances contradict themselves. Blind burrow-creatures lick salt from stones that have briefly existed in two places at once. The black shrubs root into cracks where buried water can be heard but not reached. The white birds nest on ledges overlooking impossible drops, perhaps because predators cannot decide which depth is real. Nothing is abundant. Nothing competes openly. Survival here is a matter of being small, still, and compatible with paradox.

Occasional rain arrives as a vertical stippling of near-frozen drops. It falls from the empty tin sky with no cloud and no change in temperature. The drops strike stone loudly, each impact amplified until the plateau becomes a roof under heavy weather. Yet the rain is scant. It darkens the rock in freckles, fills old hoofprints, pearls along grass blades, then stops without transition. The wet places remain wet for a long time, not evaporating so much as being slowly reconsidered by the air.

In the deepest hollows are pockets of water with skins of gray ice. Some are no larger than bowls; others spread into shallow tarns surrounded by salt-white margins. Their surfaces are perfectly still. Reflections in them lag behind movement. A person leaning over one might see their face arrive several seconds late, altered by an expression they have not yet made. The rare animals do not drink directly from these pools. They lick condensation from stones nearby.

Despite the loudness, the place feels abandoned by events. There are no landslides, no growing storms, no migrating herds, no seasonal thaw. The plateau has already undergone whatever shaped it. Erosion has finished its argument and reached a settlement. Peaks have been cut down into tables and shoulders. Ravines have deepened to the exact depth they will always have. Frost has opened cracks to their appointed widths. Even the breakdown of reality is not catastrophic; it is maintained, like a ruin kept from further collapse by devotion.

If there are paths, they are not made by traffic. They appear as slightly smoother routes between cairns, along shelves, across basins where the gravel has been pressed flat by repeated intention. Some paths loop back without turning. Some cross themselves in ways that require more than two dimensions but remain easy to walk. At intervals, a path passes beside a stone hut with one intact wall and no roof. Inside the hut, the sound of the plateau becomes almost domestic: a low inner booming, like a kettle heard from another room, though there is no fire and no room beyond.

The scattered structures imply former inhabitants, but not necessarily human ones. The scale is ambiguous. Some doorways are too narrow for a person; some hearth-like depressions are too wide; some cairns include stones placed so high and precisely that no ordinary hand seems responsible. Yet the marks are not alien in a theatrical way. They have the tenderness of necessity. Someone, or something, once needed windbreaks, markers, thresholds, places to pause in the cold. The plateau allowed those needs only in small amounts.

There are places where one must not speak, not because of an echo but because words remain. In a certain amphitheater of black rock, old syllables hang in the air as pale knots of frost. They are not understandable. They drift at mouth height, turning slowly, and the background roar bends around them. New speech joins them and does not decay. The sparse lichen there grows especially thick beneath the suspended words.

Elsewhere, there is a stairway cut into a cliff that descends twelve steps and then continues as a sound only. The visible steps end at a blank wall of stone, but the foot can feel further steps if extended carefully into the roar. No one watching can agree how far down the invisible stair goes. No one who descends far returns changed, because change does not properly occur here; but some return with additional shadows.

The atmosphere is one of severe suspension. Not peace exactly, and not menace exactly. The plateau is too empty for menace, too loud for peace. It feels like standing inside a held note at the end of the world, a note that began before listeners and will not resolve after them. The cold preserves. The height removes. The dimness softens. The sound occupies. The weirdness does not erupt; it abides.

At the margins of the region, lower slopes may exist, though they are difficult to reach. The plateau does not end by descending so much as by becoming less internally consistent. A traveler might walk toward a pass and find the same pass ahead all day, its cairn and black shrub unchanged. Another might step through a shallow notch and suddenly hear ordinary wind, see clouds moving with purpose, smell thawing soil far below. Looking back, they would see only a high gray wall and no obvious route onto it.

But within, the land remains itself.

A cold, high, dim country of stone shelves and settled ravines. Sparse lichens measuring centuries in beads of water. Rare animals balanced between life and geology. Small, weathered structures that mark old agreements with a broken world. Moisture hidden in cracks, appearing as condensation, rain, and impossible pools. An immense unvarying sound filling the hollows. Distances that disobey without changing their minds.

Everything there is almost nothing.

And the almost nothing is vast.
