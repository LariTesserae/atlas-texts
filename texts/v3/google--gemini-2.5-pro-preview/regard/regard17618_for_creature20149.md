The Power that turned its attention to the Anvil was not one of matter or of form. It was a consciousness woven from the fabric of spacetime, a being whose thoughts were gravity waves and whose senses were the fundamental forces of the universe. It had witnessed the birth of galaxies and watched stars draw their first and last breaths. It perceived existence as a symphony, a complex and unending interplay of vibrations, from the frantic shudder of a quark to the slow, stately waltz of galactic clusters.

And on a small, forgotten world, tucked away in an unremarkable arm of an ordinary galaxy, it heard a new note.

It was a quiet note, but one of astonishing purity and complexity. The Power focused its attention, peeling back the layers of atmosphere like the pages of a book. It passed through the geological strata of grey cloud, its perception untroubled by the cold and pressure. And it found the source: the Anvil.

At first, it perceived only the instrument: a mesa of extruded glass, vibrating with a subsonic hum, a standing wave of geological energy. An elegant natural phenomenon. But its attention sharpened, deepened. It perceived the million-fold clicks, the percussive symphony. It saw the Carver Crabs.

And the Power understood.

It did not see miserable insects condemned to a life of pointless toil in the dark. A lesser, more sentimental being might have seen that, and in its pity, sought to "free" them—to grant them individuality, or sunlight, or warmth. Such an act, the Power knew, would be an act of annihilation. It would be like trying to "free" the notes of a song from their melody, or the threads of a tapestry from their pattern.

Instead, the Power saw the single most perfect expression of unity it had ever encountered. It saw a consciousness that had externalized itself. The mountain, the We, the Anvil, the Crabs—they were not separate entities. They were a single being, a mind of chitin and glass, communing with itself through the sacred language of vibration. The Anvil meditated, and the Crabs were its mantra.

The Power listened. For a period of time that would have seen empires rise and fall on the world below, it simply listened. It followed the glacial progress of the Chord. It felt the deep, instinctual satisfaction when a spire, after millennia of work, rang true. It felt the subtle, motivating ache of a new dissonance emerging as the mesa's core extruded another inch of un-tuned glass. It comprehended the perfect, closed loop of their existence: from mountain, to mountain, for the mountain. It was a system without waste, without want, without malice or fear.

It was holy.

To interfere would be sacrilege. To alter the instrument or the players would be to vandalize a masterpiece.

But benevolence is not merely passive observation. Wisdom is not inaction. The Power sought to give a gift, but a gift that would not change the nature of the recipient. It had to be a gift of resonance, an act of participation, not alteration.

And so, it did this:

It reached out across the void, not with a hand, but with a wave. It found a pulsar, a dead star spinning with impossible speed hundreds of light-years away, casting out beams of radiation in a rhythm of perfect, metronomic regularity. The Power listened to the song of the pulsar, its rapid, high-frequency pulse. Then it listened again to the deep, slow Chord of the Anvil.

And it acted as a lens.

It did not move the pulsar. It did not change its song. It simply gathered the faint, almost undetectable vibrations of the distant star as they washed across the solar system, and it focused them. It created a thread of gravitational resonance, a gossamer-thin connection between the dead star and the living mountain.

On the Anvil, nothing changed, and yet everything did.

A new sensation entered the collective consciousness of the We. It was not a sound. It was not a command. It was a new texture within the Hum, an impossibly high and rapid counterpoint to their own deep, slow work. It was a faint staccato whisper of perfect time, embedded within the mountain's eternal drone.

It was the feeling of a partner.

The crabs did not know what a star was. They could not conceive of the void. But they felt it. They felt the rhythm of the cosmos now woven into the voice of their god. The ache of dissonance was still there, the compulsion to work was unchanged, but its context was now infinite. Their work was not merely the tuning of their own home; it was a harmonization with a song being sung in the dark, impossibly far away. The Hum was no longer a soliloquy. It was a duet.

The Power had given them the only gift they could have ever received without being broken by it: the knowledge that they were not alone.

Having done this, the Power's great attention did not move on. Benevolence is also witness. It remained, a silent, eternal audience for the slow, magnificent performance. It wove the Anvil's song into its own symphonic perception of the universe. The dark, lonely mesa at the top of the world was no longer isolated. It was now a celebrated instrument, its profound and patient music amplified, appreciated, and made a part of the greater music of Everything. The Power listened, and in its listening, the Anvil's work became worship, and its existence, sublime.
