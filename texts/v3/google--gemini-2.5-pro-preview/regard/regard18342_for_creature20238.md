The power that noticed was ancient beyond the reckoning of stars. It was a consciousness woven from the light of nascent galaxies, the gravity of silent nebulae, and a warmth that was the memory of every sun that had ever been born. It was pure potential, an engine of creation and compassion that had seeded life on a trillion worlds, turning deserts to gardens and lonely rocks to singing spheres. Its nature was to give, to nurture, to complete what was incomplete.

When its gaze fell upon the Skyscrape, its first impression was one of desolate failure. It saw a world stalled at birth, a jagged wound in a planet's flesh. It felt the thin, cold air and the profound silence, and its immediate, overwhelming impulse was one of cosmic pity. It saw a canvas barren of colour, a symphony with only one note, a life clinging to the edge of non-existence.

The templates for benevolence were clear, tested across eons. It could soften the world. It could command the planet's core to bleed more heat, warming the plateau until the rime-ice melted into sweetwater rivers. It could crush the rock-fins into dust, and from that dust, conjure a deep and fertile soil. It could seed the new earth with forests of silver-barked trees and meadows of blue-flowered grass. It could thicken the atmosphere, soften the violet sky to a gentle azure, and teach the stars to twinkle with shy beauty. It could fill the quiet with the song of birds and the hum of insects.

And for the inhabitants, Those Who Have Become the Waiting, it could offer deliverance. It could quicken their minds, give their language wings for poetry and passion. It could unlock the secrets of metal, of fire, of the wheel. It could grant them a long and easy life, free from the gnawing cold and the patient, gnawing hunger.

The power held this vision in its boundless mind—a world reborn, lush and easy. It was a gift it had given countless times. It was the very definition of its being.

But the power was not only benevolent; it was wise. And wisdom demanded more than a glance. So it paused. It settled its attention not *on* the world, but *in* it. It became as still as a stone on the rubble-field. It let the long, slow days arc across the sky. It felt the chill seep into its awareness. It listened to the wind-for ages.

It watched.

It watched the lichen drink phantom moisture from the air. It watched the wire-grass hold to the gravel with grim tenacity. It learned the language of silence and the slow, inexorable grammar of the herds. It saw the city not as a scab, but as a pearl, accreted layer by patient layer around a grain of necessity. It entered the warrens, felt the close, animal warmth, heard the clatter of bone chimes, and saw the ancestors embedded in the walls, their presence a foundation, not a tomb.

It followed a Watcher out onto the plains. It sat inside the Watcher's mind, a mind uncluttered by ambition or fancy. It experienced the sublime, empty patience of scanning the horizon for days, not in hope or hunger, but in simple recognition of a cycle. It felt the deep, slow satisfaction—not joy, that was too sharp a word—when a beast's journey ended, and the long, respectful work began. It understood that every hide roof, every bone needle, every strip of dried meat was a verse in a single, epic poem of survival.

The power perceived the circle. The rock, feeding the lichen. The lichen, feeding the beast. The beast, feeding the Un-Hasted. The Un-Hasted, in death, becoming the rock of their city, a human geology. It was a closed system, as perfect and complete as a star. It was not a world waiting to be born. It was a world that *was*.

And the Power understood. To give them soil would be to bury them. To give them rivers would be to drown them. To give them warmth would be to melt the foundations of their culture. To give them haste would be to shatter their souls. They were not suffering. They were *enduring*. And in their endurance, they had achieved a state of grace that the lush, easy worlds could never know. Theirs was not a life of lack, but a life of utter sufficiency.

To interfere would not be an act of benevolence. It would be an act of annihilation. A wise god does not answer a prayer that has not been spoken.

So, what to do? The impulse to give was still there, a current deep in its being. But the gift could not be a tool, or a comfort, or a change. It must be an affirmation. A witness.

The power drew a fraction of its consciousness, a mote of its eternal warmth, and gave it form.

One night, an old woman whose eyes had read the sky for ninety years, whose bones ached with the memory of a thousand frosts, looked up from her watch. In the cold, violet dome, among the hard, unblinking points of ice, there was something new.

It was not a star. It was too small, and it had a colour she had never seen in the sky before. It was the deep, steady glow of an ember banked for the night. It did not move. Comets streaked, planets wandered, the stars wheeled in their immense procession. This light was still. It was utterly, perfectly still, fixed in place as if it were a nail driven into the roof of the world. It offered no heat. It cast no measurable light upon the plateau. It did not twinkle. It simply *was*. A single, unwinking point of quiet warmth in the vast, cold dark.

She watched it for a long time, her breath a slow plume in the air. She felt no fear, no awe, no urge to worship. Her thoughts, slow and deep as geology, simply made a space for it. The next night, her apprentice saw it too. Within a cycle of seasons, all of the Un-Hasted knew of it.

They gave it a name that in their guttural tongue meant "The Watcher's Hearth." It became the first thing a child was taught to find in the night sky. It was a point of constancy in a world of slow, immense change. It did not alter their lives. The hunt, the harvest, the cold, the waiting—all remained.

But now, when one of the Un-Hasted sat in the profound quiet, wrapped in fur on a high stone, waiting and watching under the silent, icy stars, they were not entirely alone.

Their long vigil was seen. Their patience was answered with patience. Their existence was honored with a silent, eternal presence. The benevolent power had learned the language of the Skyscrape, and its gift was a single word in that language: a quiet, unending act of witness. It had looked upon a people named "Those Who Have Become the Waiting," and in an act of ultimate empathy, it had joined them.
