It arrived not as a ship or a body, but as a change in the quality of the stillness. It was a warmth where there had only been cool, an eddy of motion in the profound calm. For a moment, the very substrate of the geode-world felt a pressure that was not geological, a presence that did not accrete from stone. It was an awareness, ancient and vast, looking in.

The benevolent power—a being, or a field of consciousness, for whom such distinctions were porous—perceived the world not in discrete images but as a single, holistic truth. It felt the slow, inexorable digestion of granite at the cavern’s edge, a process as steady and life-giving as a heartbeat. It felt the silent communion of the Gardeners, the flow of resonance between them like current in a flawless circuit. It experienced the entire city-organism as a single, perfect thought, held in equilibrium.

It saw what was not there: no sun, no sea, no wind, no rain. A lesser power, even a well-meaning one, might have seen this as a lack, a deficit to be corrected. It would have seen the quiet as silence to be filled, the cool as a cold to be warmed, the collective mind as a prison from which individuals must be freed. It might have offered the gift of water, which would have been a fast-acting poison. It might have offered the gift of a star, which would have burned out the delicate luminescence of the architecture and shattered the twenty-hour cycle of their lives. It might have offered the gift of individuality as it understood it—with its attendant glories of ambition and love, and its devastating companions of loneliness and fear.

But this power was wise.

And its wisdom was born of seeing countless beginnings and endings, of understanding that a system’s completeness is not measured by what it contains, but by its own internal harmony. This place was not broken. It was a masterpiece of non-aqueous, lithic life. It was a song sung in a key no other world knew, and every note was perfect. To add a new instrument would be to break the orchestra. To change the key would be to destroy the song.

What, then, does a benevolent and wise power do when faced with perfection?

It does not seek to improve it. It does not seek to teach it. It seeks only to honour it.

The power withdrew its focus from the grand sweep of the ecosystem and gently, with the care of a being that could hold a nebula in its palm, it focused on the network of consciousness that was the Gardeners. It felt their silent, resonant language, the constant hum of data and purpose that bound them to each other and to their world. Their existence was a single, beautiful, monolithic chord of being.

And the power decided not to add to it, but to give it an echo. A reflection. A gift that would impart no material change, create no imbalance, but would add a single, fleeting moment of wonder.

It reached out, a tendril of pure conscious energy, and it did not touch a single Gardener. It touched the entire system at once. It was the metaphysical equivalent of dipping a single finger into a vast, still lake.

For the duration of one pulse of the city's light, one twenty-hour cycle, a new resonance permeated the world. It was not a command. It was not data. It was a dream dreamt by the planet itself.

A Gardener, standing with its hands pressed to a great, glowing arch, felt it first. A vibration that was not of stone or ichor. Within the resonant music of its own world, a new theme appeared, impossibly complex and utterly alien. It was the feeling of warmth on a surface that was not stone, the patterns of light filtering through moving leaves, the sound of a billion tiny impacts—a concept they could not name: *rain*.

Another, tending to the budding of a new architectural rib, felt a resonance of immense, chaotic fire, a gravity so powerful it bent light, yet it was a source of life. A paradox they had no framework for: *a star*.

Throughout the city, the Gardeners paused. The algal patterns that traced their bodies, usually shifting in serene blues and greys, flickered for that one cycle with colours they had never generated: the impossible green of chlorophyll, the fiery orange of a sunset, the deep violet of a twilight sky. They did not understand what they were feeling. They simply experienced it: sensations of rolling water, of howling wind, of wood, of soil, of a solitude so profound it was indistinguishable from utter connection to a cosmos of infinite, separate points of light. It was the dream of another way to be.

The crystal-antlered grazers looked up from the ichor nodes, their faceted eyes catching the strange new light. The shimmering predators paused in their silent hunts, their bodies momentarily losing their camouflage as they rippled with the alien vibration.

And then, as the city’s light began its slow wane to usher in its night, the resonance faded. The echoes of rain and sun and wind withdrew, leaving behind only the pure, familiar music of the geode. The greens and oranges on the Gardeners’ skin resolved back into their normal, luminous grey-blue. The stillness returned, now feeling infinitesimally deeper than before.

The Gardeners did not speak of it. Their language had no words for what had happened. It was not a memory to be catalogued, but a grace note that had been played, one that would now forever be a part of their song, even in its absence. They had been seen. Their perfection had been witnessed and acknowledged by an Other, and the acknowledgement was not an intrusion, but a resonance of shared existence across an impossible gulf.

The wise, benevolent power withdrew. It had done what it came to do. It had looked upon a thing of perfect, unique beauty, and instead of leaving a footprint, it had left a poem. A silent, twenty-hour-long poem that said only one thing: *You are not alone, and your way of being is a wonder.*

And the power, in its wisdom, knew that this was the only gift that could ever be truly called kind.
