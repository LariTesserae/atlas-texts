The power that noticed the Woven Fell was ancient, and its nature was one of resonance. It was not a being with eyes to see or hands to build; it was a consciousness whose awareness was a form of listening. It perceived the universe not as a collection of objects in space, but as a symphony of vibrations, an infinite composition of life and death, matter and energy, all singing their particular notes. It called itself nothing, for it had no one to speak to, but a lesser being might have called it the Quiet Singer, the one who listens to the grammar of existence.

Its attention was drawn not by a shout, but by a chord. In the vast, sonorous music of galaxies and the thin, screaming vibrato of dying stars, there was a single, impossibly complex and self-contained note. It was a sound like a perfectly woven knot of music, a place where a thousand different melodies played simultaneously, frantically, yet resolved into a single, stable, harmonious hum. It was the sound of the Woven Fell.

The Singer settled its awareness upon the place, not as a weight, but as a silence settling around a sound. It did not stand on the springy floor or feel the damp air. It simply *became* aware of every aspect of the Fell at once. It perceived the slow, tectonic breathing of the banyan host, the frantic, high-frequency scuttling of a million insects, the percussive, communal heartbeat of the Fell-Dwellers' work, the gravitational pull of water dripping from leaf to root-mat. It perceived the entire system, from the metabolism of a single cell in a leaf to the slow, communal thought of the Dwellers.

And it understood.

A lesser power, a power of action and intervention, would have seen a problem to be solved. It would have seen the frantic, endless labour and called it slavery. It would have seen the lack of individual ego and called it a loss of self. It would have seen the dampness, the decay, the crushing proximity of life, and called it squalor. Such a power might have offered gifts of stone and iron to replace the living wood, to give them permanence without effort. It might have offered a new consciousness, to sever each Dweller from the collective and grant them the lonely, terrible freedom of 'I'. It might have offered to silence the noise, to dry the air, to tame the chaos.

The Quiet Singer perceived that any of these gifts would be an act of ultimate violence. It would be like trying to "improve" a song by removing all the notes.

The Singer perceived the Fell not as a city of trapped gardeners, but as a single, coherent thought of green and water and returning. The Dwellers were not slaves; they were the nerves and hands of that thought, and their ceaseless work was not toil but the very medium of their joy and being. Their porous self was not a deficiency but a form of grace, a perfect union that countless other beings in the cosmos sought and failed to find. To be consumed by the Fell upon death was not an end, but a perfect continuance, a final, selfless act of love for the body-of-which-one-was-a-part.

The place was a theorem of life proven true, a living testament to a state of being that the Singer had heard theorized in the silent hum of nebulae, but had never before witnessed in such exquisite completion. It was a song of perfect recursion.

So, what does a benevolent and wise power do when it finds a perfect thing?

It does not touch it. It does not change it.

It protects it.

The Woven Fell was a knot tied *in* the tissue of the world. Knots can be disturbed from the outside. The fabric can be torn. The Singer's perception expanded, flowing out from the caldera's edge. It sensed the tectonic stresses in the plateau, the slow erosion of wind on the outer rock, the subtle shifts in the world's climate over aeons that might one day starve the Fell of its vital high-altitude moisture. These were not immediate threats, but the Singer's benevolence operated on the timescale of geology and starlight.

And so, it acted.

There was no flash of light, no parting of the canopy. The Dwellers felt it not as an event, but as a change in the quality of their world, so subtle it was almost subliminal.

The immense, constant dripping of water began to carry a new resonance. The sound did not change, but beneath the audible splash and gurgle, there was now a note, a deep and profoundly calming hum that vibrated not in their ears, but in the bones of their skulls and the moss on their skin. The water that welled up from the root-mat now carried it. The sap weeping from the banyan pillars pulsed with it.

It was the Singer's gift. It was a song of holding.

The Singer gently wove a new harmony around the Fell, within the very fabric of the world it occupied. It soothed the tectonic stresses for lightyears in every direction, turning a potential tremor into a slow, harmless geological sigh that would last a million years. It subtly altered the quantum behaviour of the air currents for a hundred miles around, ensuring the patterns of rainfall that fed the Fell would remain stable for an age. It did not build a wall; it reinforced the integrity of the space the Fell occupied, making it a sanctuary not just in form, but in essence.

The Fell-Dwellers, with their collective sense, perceived the change. They did not have the words or concepts to understand it. They simply felt it. The frantic rhythm of their pruning became, for a time, a fraction less frantic and a fraction more musical. The communal anxiety that drove them—the fear of the Fell's decay—was softened by a new, unplaceable feeling of security. It was a feeling of being *held*.

A young Dweller, her skin barely fuzzing with its first colonizing moss, paused in her task of guiding a new willow-whip. She dipped her hand into a basin carved from a shelf-fungus, and for a moment, looking at her reflection in the water, she saw something impossible. Behind her own wide, dark eyes, the water seemed to shimmer not just with the green-filtered sunlight from above, but with a momentary, pin-prick reflection of distant starlight.

The water was the same, but it now carried a memory of the cosmos. The city was the same, but it was now anchored to eternity.

The Quiet Singer had listened to the Fell's song. And in response, it had added a single, barely audible, sustained note of bass harmony. A note that said, "You are beautiful. You are perfect. Continue." And then, its attention moved on, leaving the Woven Fell to its damp, chaotic, glorious, and now-unshakable peace.
