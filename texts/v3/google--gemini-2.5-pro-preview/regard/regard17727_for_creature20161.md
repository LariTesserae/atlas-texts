The power arrives not as a ship cleaving the pale sky, but as a change in the quality of the silence.

It is a resonance, a listening that has no ears. It settles over the Great Tableland like a finer-grained version of the heat shimmer, and its perception is not visual but empathic. It feels the world not as a place, but as a state of being. It feels the deep, patient thrum of the planetary core: a note of immense, slumbering power. It feels the frantic, high-frequency vibrato of the skitterers' brief lives: a chord of desperate thirst and fleeting existence. It feels the slow, groaning ache of the failing metal towers: a bass line of rust and forgotten purpose.

And it feels the Glaze-Kin.

In them, the power perceives a unique and poignant dissonance. It feels the geological weight of their mineralized bodies, the slow, tectonic patience of their movements. But beneath that, it feels a tremor of anxiety, a sorrow so old it has been forgotten and is now mistaken for the hum of the earth itself. It is the inherited trauma of failure, the ghost of an ambition they can no longer name. They are children huddling in the resonant silence of a cathedral built by their parents, mistaking the echoes for the voice of God.

The power, being wise, understands that to intervene is to risk breaking the delicate, brutal balance. To make rain fall would be to drown the ecosystem in a bounty it cannot handle; the glass-leafed scrub would rot, the skitterers would be washed away, the maroon lichen would perish in the sudden lack of geothermal steam. To "heal" the Glaze-Kin, to strip them of their crystalline hide and asymmetrical forms, would be to flay them alive and leave them naked to the furnace floor. Their mutations are their armor, their survival.

To restart the great machines, as the Glaze-Kin might pray for if they had the words, would be the cruelest act of all. It would be to answer a prayer with its literal and disastrous fulfillment. The wellheads would roar, the fragile crust might shatter, and the industrial god would awaken only to consume its worshipers. The power understands that the prayer is not for the machines to live again. The prayer is for the *reprieve* the machines were meant to provide. It is a prayer for a moment of peace in the endless cycle of thirst and heat. A prayer for a fuller gourd.

The power deliberates, a process that is less thought and more a holding of all the threads of this world in a simultaneous, compassionate awareness. The solution, when it comes, is not an act of creation, but one of subtle transmutation. It must be a gift that feels not like an intervention, but a discovery. A grace note added to the world's deep song.

It begins one twilight. As the cool air of the upper atmosphere descends and the geothermal steam rises to meet it, something new happens. In scattered, carefully chosen locations—in the center of a wide, barren flat; beside the trail to a favoured dew-scraping runnel; near the mouth of a hollowed pipe where a family sleeps—a change occurs.

The power does not place an object. It *convinces* the rock to become something else.

Out of the crazed ceramic ground, a new formation emerges. It is not grown, but revealed, as if it has been there all along. A single, smooth, impossibly black stone, the size of a Glaze-Kin's head. It is ovoid and flawless, without a single crack. Its surface reflects no light, seeming to drink the twilight. But its most miraculous property is its temperature. It is cool. Not just cool relative to the furnace plate of the ground, but deeply, preternaturally cold, like a stone pulled from a frozen river.

A young Glaze-Kin, on their first solo dew-gathering run, is the first to find one. They approach it with the ingrained caution of their species, sensing the anomaly. The air around it is colder. They reach out a heavy, mineral-stained hand and touch its surface. The cold is a shock, a sensation so alien it has no name in their humming, clicking language.

And then they see it. The geothermal steam, which normally precipitates as a near-invisible film across the entire landscape, is drawn to this point of profound cold. The warm, mineral-heavy moisture condenses on the black stone's surface not as a sheen, but as thick, heavy beads of pure water. The beads swell, merge, and then trickle down the stone's perfect curve, gathering in a clear, clean pool at its base.

It is a wellspring. A tiny, constant source of the most precious thing in the world, born from the meeting of the world's two natures: its hot breath and its cold sky.

Soon, others are found. There are not many, perhaps two dozen across a vast territory. They become the new sacred sites. The Glaze-Kin do not worship the stones; that reverence is reserved for the silent machines of their ancestors. These stones are different. They are a blessing. A tool. A reprieve.

The frantic, desperate scraping of dawn does not cease, but it is supplemented. The gourds are filled faster. A family might find a stone near their shelter and have enough water for two days, not just one. The gift of the stones is not a deluge of water; it is a sliver of time.

With this time, a change begins, as slow and certain as a mineral stain.

An elder, her duty of scraping done before the sun is a real threat, has time to sit by a great wellhead and simply listen, her humming becoming more complex, less a mimicry of the earth and more a true song.

A craftsperson, with a precious cup of extra water to mix with pigments, has the leisure to paint a more intricate symbol on a fallen condenser fin, depicting not just the skitterer, but the Stilt-Walker that hunts it.

A child, not needed for the gathering, has time to watch the Slope-gliders on the thermals, learning their patterns not for hunting, but for wonder.

The Pipe-Singers, listening to the earth in their ceramic chambers, notice a new, faint resonance layered over the old ones—the subtle, harmonic frequency of the cool stones. They interpret this not as a change in their god's dream, but as a new verse in its eternal, humming breath. A verse of generosity.

The power does not leave. It remains as a silent, resonant listening. It watches as the Glaze-Kin, over generations, begin to stand a little straighter. The deep, unvoiced sorrow in their collective consciousness begins to recede, replaced by a quiet security. They are still the Glaze-Kin of the Great Tableland, their bodies a map of the harsh world that made them. But the lines on that map are no longer solely a record of hardship. They are now etched with the trails to the cool, black stones. They are paths to a gift, freely given. The Glaze-Kin are still waiting, but they are no longer waiting for the roar of a dead god to save them. They are beginning to learn how to save themselves, one cool drop of water at a time. And the benevolent, wise power, feeling this slow shift, perceives it as the most beautiful form of prayer.
