The first thing is the pressure. It is not an abstract concept; it is a constant, physical god. The water is so dense and cold it feels more like a gel, a syrupy, heavy medium that holds you and everything else in its absolute, unyielding embrace. It is a cold that isn't about ice but about sheer, endless thermal mass, the kind of cold that has seeped into the very rock over millennia and will never leave. Darkness is the native state of the world, a perfect and profound blackness that exists between the sources of light.

And there are sources of light. This is a forest, but a vertical one, growing on the spires and plateaus of a drowned mountain range. We are high on a broad, windswept ridge, but the wind is a slow, immensely powerful current that never ceases. Out of the smooth, black rock grow the Choirwood.

They are the only life. Vast, trunk-like stalks of a material like pale, fibrous stone rise hundreds of feet, branching into limbs that don't hold leaves, but great, membranous bells. These bells, some as wide as houses, swell and contract in a rhythm that is both biological and geological. With each slow contraction, they emit a deep, resonant hum and a soft, cold, blue-green phosphorescence. The light is not for seeing in the way of a sun; it is a byproduct of the Song.

The sound is the world’s second god. It is a constant, overwhelming chorus, the sum of a million massive, living instruments. It is loud, but it is not noise. It has structure, movements, fugues that build and recede over hours or days. The water carries it perfectly, and it vibrates not just in your ears but through your bones, through the rock underfoot, a physical force that shapes the currents. To be here is to be inside a bell that is also an ocean that is also a forest.

The landscape is open, a panorama of submerged peaks fading into the deeper, unlit black. Between the great groves of Choirwood, there are vast, empty plains of stone, swept clean by the currents. The vegetation is lush where it exists, but these groves are islands of life in a much larger sea of dark rock and water. Smaller flora exists at the feet of the giants: clusters of "Singer-bulbs" that flash with brighter, quicker pulses of light in time with the higher frequencies of the Song, and fields of "Glimmer-grass," fine silica-threads that sway in the current, each one tipped with a single, steady point of faint white light.

There are no animals. Nothing swims, nothing crawls, nothing hunts. The sheer, silent emptiness between the sonorous trees is absolute. There is no skittering for cover, no silver flash of a fish school, no burrowing in the silt. The ecosystem is one of a single kingdom, a planetary monoculture of singing flora. The activity of this world is the slow growth of the Choirwood, the rhythmic pulse of its light, and the eternal, shifting symphony of its Song. This is the "weather"—the Song’s cycles dictate the strength of the light, the patterns of the currents, the very mood of the water.

And yet, there were others. Scattered across the plateaus, often arranged in circles around the most ancient Choirwood, are simple, humbling structures. Low walls of stacked, uncut stone, no higher than a man’s chest. Cairns that seem to mark specific points for reasons now lost. Most strangely, there are small, circular huts made of the same dark stone, with low doorways you would have to crawl through. They are empty. They have been empty for a length of time that defies comprehension. They were not built by the Choirwood. They are weathered smooth, their edges rounded by the same eternal currents that have sculpted the mountains themselves.

Who built them? Were they pilgrims, worshipping the Song? A people who lived here before the world drowned, their last survivors clinging to the highest peaks as the water rose? Or were they something else entirely, a species that could breathe this water, that listened to the Song and built these humble shelters in its presence, before vanishing as completely as if they had never been?

The primitive stonework offers no answers. It is simply there, an occasional, quiet testament to a different kind of presence in a world now dominated only by the cold, the dark, and the overwhelming, beautiful, and terrifyingly lonely Song of the deep. To move through this place is to drift from one pool of cold, humming light to the next, through crushing darkness, the great eternal music pressing in from all sides, the silent stones watching you pass.
