You do not breathe air here; you breathe water. The atmosphere is a hot, heavy, saturated vapour, so thick it beads on any surface and coalesces into a perpetual, silent drizzle. It has the cloying, mineral-sweet smell of a deep cavern, a scent of wet stone and ozone and something else, something like the aftertaste of a forgotten dream. The heat is profound, a soaking, cellular warmth that presses in from all directions. It emanates not from a sun—there is no sun in the featureless, lightless ceiling of the sky—but from the ground itself, and from the strange structures that rise from it.

This is a city. It is undeniably a city. It has the grammar of one: wide, drowned plazas; avenues flanked by silent colonnades; towers that pierce the soupy dark, their peaks dissolving into the low-hanging gloom. There are archways, bridges, and vast, empty amphitheaters. But it is a city without technology, without tools. No chisel has ever touched this stone. No hammer has ever rung out.

The entire metropolis is grown. It is a slow, geological precipitation. The material is a kind of onyx-dark, vitreous stone, slick with a constant film of condensation. In the profound darkness, it is not entirely black. It holds a faint, internal luminescence, a captive twilight that seems to remember light from another age, another reality. This cold, residual glow is the only illumination, outlining the edges of structures in faint silver and blue and casting the water-slicked ground in a lattice of shivering, indistinct reflections.

The city rises from a vast, flat basin. Everything is low, settled, ancient. The forms are weathered, rounded by eons of this silent, weeping rain. There are no sharp edges. Towers sag slightly, as if exhausted. The steps of great staircases are worn into smooth, concave dips. The entire landscape feels established, balanced in a state of slow, majestic decay that is also, paradoxically, a state of continuous creation.

The air, thick as syrup, is supersaturated with the dissolved mineral that forms the city. The constant drizzle is not mere water; it is a nutrient broth. Where it pools on the plazas, the water is thick and viscous. Leave it undisturbed for a long time—a day, a decade, a century—and you will see the beginnings of new growth. A tiny crystal, a complex fractal, slowly asserting itself, thickening, rising. A curbstone growing like a coral. A new pillar slowly ascending from a puddle. This is the city's single, active process: a cycle of dissolution and precipitation. The old towers slowly weep their substance back into the atmosphere, while new structures gestate in the quiet pools.

Sound is a rarity. The dominant state is a deep, profound silence, made heavier by the density of the air. What sounds exist are slow and resonant. The ponderous, syrupy *drip* of a forming stalactite of stone from a high archway, a sound that takes minutes to fall and lands with a soft, resonant *thump* in a deep puddle below. Sometimes, a low, tectonic hum shivers through the ground, the sound of a massive structure settling, or of a new foundation crystallizing deep beneath the surface. There is no wind, no rustle of leaves, no cry of an animal.

Life, as it is commonly understood, is absent. There are no animals, no insects, no birds. But the city is not entirely barren of form. In sheltered alcoves, away from the direct, heavy drip, something that mimics vegetation grows. These are not plants of flesh and cellulose, but delicate, glassy filaments of the same mineral substance as the city. They form intricate, fern-like sprays and lattices of hair-fine crystal, catching the faint internal light and fracturing it into microscopic rainbows. They are brittle and cold to the touch, and if broken, they do not regrow; the mineral dust simply falls to be reabsorbed into the cycle. They are the landscape's sparse, crystalline flora, an afterthought of its primary, architectural life.

The rules of this place are not constant. The weirdness is a fundamental property of the space, woven into its very fabric. The geometry of the city is unstable. An avenue that appears straight may, upon being walked, curve back on itself. A plaza might seem to span for miles, its far end lost in the gloom, yet can be crossed in a hundred paces. Doorways yawn in the sides of solid towers, but they do not lead inside; they are portals, and stepping through one might deposit you on a high, isolated bridge miles away, or back at the exact spot you just left. Perspective is a liar. The city is a physical manifestation of a broken memory, a place dreaming itself into existence, and its dreams are not logical. It is a metropolis without inhabitants, whose only citizen is the architecture itself, living out a slow, quiet, and utterly alien existence in the hot, wet dark.
