The air is a medium you wear. It is not gaseous, not truly, but a viscous, breathable fluid that clings to the skin with the cool, heavy pressure of a deep pool. To call it water is both an overstatement and an understatement. It is the Gloomwater, a perpetual, planet-saturating atmospheric suspension, warm and thick as blood plasma. Visibility is a matter of metres, a shifting curtain of grey-green twilight where distant things are merely soft-edged suggestions, darker shades against the deep murk. Light does not travel far before it is captured and diffused.

We are high up, unimaginably so. The settlement is built upon the emergent peaks of a new mountain range, a series of sharp, jagged spires punching up into the denser strata of the Gloomwater. The rock here is young, raw, unblunted by the friction of ages. It is vesicular basalt and fresh-risen obsidian, still holding a deep, latent heat that warms the immediate environment. There are no smooth slopes, no gentle valleys worn by rivers. There are only vertical faces, knife-edge ridges, and needle-like pinnacles of black, glistening stone. Erosion is a concept for a faster, drier world. Here, the mountains are still being born, pushing upwards with a geological slowness so profound it is felt not as motion, but as a deep, subsonic hum that vibrates through the rock and into your bones.

This raw geology is the scaffold for a riot of life. Every surface that is not sheer vertical is cloaked in a dense, multi-layered carpet of vegetation. There is no soil, only a thick mat of interwoven roots, hyphae, and decaying organic matter. Great, velvety mosses in shades of ochre and viridian swell with absorbed water, forming soft, treacherous pillows over the hard stone. Above them, forests of fungi rise—some are tall and spindly, like pale fingers tipped with glowing blue spores, providing the settlement's primary, cool light. Others are broad, shelf-like conks that form natural ledges and overhangs, their undersides dripping with a thick, sugary nectar. The dominant flora are the gloom-kelps, strange, semi-translucent plants that are more like seaweeds than trees. They anchor themselves to the rock with powerful holdfasts and grow upwards in thick, buoyant stalks, their broad, gelatinous fronds drifting slowly in the atmospheric currents, harvesting what little ambient light filters down from the unreachable upper layers of the sky.

The settlement is not so much built upon this landscape as it is carved from it and woven into it. From a distance—if such a thing were possible—it would be difficult to distinguish city from mountain. The primary structures are hollows within the spires themselves, excavated chambers and galleries connected by tunnels polished smooth by the passage of generations. There are no sharp corners; every edge is rounded, every doorway a soft-edged aperture. Where the rock faces are too steep for chambers, dwellings are woven. Using the fibrous, surprisingly strong stalks of the dried gloom-kelp and the flexible wood of a parasitic vine, the people construct multi-story pods that cling to the mountainside like barnacles. These woven homes are never truly dry, but their surfaces are colonized by a specific type of hydrophobic moss that causes the Gloomwater to bead and drip away, keeping the interior merely damp rather than soaked. They are layered, one pod overlapping the next, connected by sagging rope bridges and ladders of knotted vine that sway gently in the slow currents.

Life here is quiet and dense. Sound does not travel; it steeps. A voice carries only a few feet before being swallowed by the heavy air and the sound-dampening mosses. The auditory world is one of intimacy and proximity: the soft squelch of a footstep on a path of living fungus, the steady, rhythmic *plink-plonk* of water dripping from a fungal shelf onto a lower one, the rustle of a woven partition, the muffled thrum of a hand-cranked grinding stone somewhere in a nearby chamber. Below it all is the constant, almost subliminal hum of the rising stone.

Fauna is rare, and its appearances are significant. Weeks can pass without seeing anything other than the tiny, iridescent mites that swarm over the fruiting bodies of fungi. But then, a subtle shift in the Gloomwater's pressure signals the approach of a Strider. These immense, arthropodal creatures, larger than any ten woven dwellings, move with impossible slowness between the spires. Their six, impossibly long legs find purchase on unseen juts of rock, their chitinous bodies a moving island of dark, wet plating, sometimes colonized by its own unique ecosystem of glowing lichens. They are not hunted; they are observed, their passage a slow, majestic, and slightly terrifying event on the calendar of this vertical world. Smaller, sky-swimming creatures, like gelatinous, multi-finned slugs, sometimes drift by in silent schools, trailing phosphorescent wakes.

The aether is heavy with the scent of wet stone, ozone, and the sweet, cloying perfume of decaying fungal matter. A tool here is a simple thing: an obsidian knife flaked to a sharp edge, a hammer stone, a bone awl for weaving. There is no metal, for any refined ore would be devoured by rust in a matter of days. Technology is biological and patient. They cultivate strains of bioluminescent fungi for light, nurture specific mosses for waterproofing their woven homes, and understand the slow language of the mountain's growth. To live here is to be part of a slow, wet, luminous process, to build your home on a thing that is still becoming, and to know that time is measured not in days, but in the patient accretion of stone and the slow drift of a spore through the waiting, endless twilight.
