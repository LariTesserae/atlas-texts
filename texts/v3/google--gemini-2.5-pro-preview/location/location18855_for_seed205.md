This is a world built atop itself. There is no ground, not in the way one thinks of it. There is only the Canopy Sea, a continent-spanning, planet-girdling mat of vegetation so ancient and interwoven that its layers descend for miles into a hot, pressurized dark. You are on its surface, an undulating landscape of living matter.

The "ground" underfoot is a springy, tough weave of titanic vines, fossilized wood, and living root systems, all bound together by a perpetual, moss-like growth. It is warm to the touch, always, humming with a deep, cellular energy that rises from the geothermal heart of the planet, filtered up through the deep, rotting core of the Underocean. The surface is not flat. It swells into long, lazy hills hundreds of miles from crest to crest, and dips into shallow basins where moisture gathers. There is no soil, no rock, only the varying textures of plant-life: fibrous, bark-like plains, spongy fields of fungal growth, and patches of slick, leathery leaves the size of carpets.

The scale is stupefying. From the crest of a slow-rising swell, the world is an endless, breathing ocean of green, textured by shades of ochre and rust. And it is sparse. Rising from this sea, scattered at intervals of many miles, are the true trees. These are not trees as we know them; they are ecosystems unto themselves, monumental life-forms whose bases are wider than towns and whose upper reaches are lost in the hazy yellow sky. Some resemble colossal, petrified fungi, their great caps creating plateaus of their own. Others are woody spires, gnarled and lightning-scarred, dripping with epiphytic growth and thick, resinous sap that smells of cinnamon and ozone. These are the islands in the green sea, the only real landmarks.

The air is a furnace. It is heavy, wet, and thick with the scent of hot vegetation, pollen, and rot. The dampness is not from constant rain, but from the world itself breathing. In the cooler hours of the long day's cycle, a thick fog rises from the Underocean below, condensing on every surface. Water beads on the broad leaves and drips from hanging vines, creating a constant, soft patter that is almost, but not quite, lost in the overwhelming noise. Sometimes, a true rain will fall from the high atmosphere, a deluge that turns the basins into temporary, shallow lakes, the water tea-colored and teeming with microscopic life before it is inevitably reabsorbed by the thirsty mat.

And the sound. The sound is a physical presence. It is a deafening, multi-layered roar that never, ever ceases. The foundation of this noise is the Great Hum: the tectonic groaning of the Canopy Sea itself, the creak of continent-sized root systems shifting, the vibration of a trillion lives. Layered over this is the chittering, shrieking, percussive symphony of the fauna. This world is not just inhabited; it is overwhelmed with animals. The air is thick with shimmering clouds of Glimmerwing Moths, their wingbeats a high-frequency buzz. Flocks of leathery Sail-wings, creatures as large as small aircraft, catch thermal updrafts from the warm mat, their territorial cries like tearing metal. The mat itself is alive with movement. Herds of six-legged grazers, low-slung beasts with carapaces of hardened bark, chew methodically on the fungal fields, their grinding jaws contributing a constant, stony crunch to the cacophony. Underfoot, the fibrous turf squirms with countless smaller things—Skitter-mites the size of dinner plates, armored centipedes, burrowing slugs—their collective motion a ceaseless rustle. The sound is so intense it's not just heard; it's felt in the bones, a pressure against the eardrums that makes quiet thought an act of intense concentration.

There are people here, or at least signs of them. Their presence is faint, scattered like seeds on the wind. They build no cities, for there is nothing here to build them from. Their constructions are woven, carved, and ephemeral. Along oft-traveled routes between the great tree-spires, you might find a guide-post: a tall, flexible stalk driven into the mat, adorned with a knot of brightly colored fibers that signal distance or danger. In the lee of a fungal outcrop, you might find a temporary shelter, a dome of bent branches and wide, overlapping leaves, now abandoned. Their most impressive works are the bridges. To cross the deep, dangerous chasms that sometimes split the mat, they weave cables from the gut of Sail-wings and the strongest vine-silks, anchoring them to carved hooks of petrified wood driven deep into the living ground. These bridges sway precariously in the hot wind, a testament to a people who have mastered a world with no stone and no metal. Their tools are sharpened mandibles of giant insects, blades of obsidian-hard seed husks, and drills fashioned from the spiral horns of burrowing mammals.

This world is strange because its fundamental rules are inverted. There is no geology, only biology. There is no solid foundation, only a deep, living matrix. Weather is not just meteorological but ecological: a warm front might be preceded by a vast, migrating herd, and a "storm" can be a cloud of pollen so thick it chokes the sky for days, turning the light a deep, dusty gold. Life has claimed everything. The distinction between plant and animal blurs. Some of the vines that hang from the spires are predatory, snapping at passing Glimmerwings. Some of the smaller grazers are photosensitive, their shells covered in symbiotic algae that provides half their nutrition. The world is in a constant, dynamic state of growth, consumption, and decay, all at a deafening volume. To stand here is to feel like a tiny, transient microbe on the skin of an impossibly vast, breathing creature.
