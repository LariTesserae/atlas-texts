This is a gallery of dust.

The ground is a floor of impalpable powder, charcoal-grey and soft as velvet. It has lain undisturbed for so long that it has settled into a perfect, featureless plain, a surface that holds no memory of footfalls because there have been none. To walk here would be to be the first and only storm in an age of perfect calm. The space is a basin, a wide, shallow depression whose walls are lost to the encompassing darkness. It feels contained, like a vast, un-roofed cathedral nave or the floor of a dry, forgotten sea. The scale is intimate yet immense; you could cross it in an hour, but to do so would be to transgress against an eon of stillness.

The air is thick, warm, and utterly inert. There are no currents, no breezes. The warmth is a constant, radiant presence, rising from the ground itself, as if the basin were a cooling hearth. It feels like the air in a sealed tomb, heavy with the scent of old stone, dry minerals, and a faint, electric tang like ozone. It is a sterile warmth, containing no hint of moisture or biological decay. This world is absolutely dry. There is no water, nor any chemistry that relies on it.

From the dust rise the Gyrewood trees. They are not wood, but spires of fibrous, milky-white crystal, like asbestos or spun selenite. They are sparse; one might stand a hundred paces from its nearest neighbor, each a solitary column in the gloom. They range in height from a low, gnarled stump to a slender pillar that rises fifty feet before vanishing into the upper darkness. Their surfaces are not smooth but are composed of countless parallel fibers, catching what little light exists and giving them a soft, almost furry appearance.

The light comes from the trees themselves. It is not a brightness, but an absence of total blackness. A cold, internal phosphorescence, a pale amber-white glow, illuminates them from within. It is the light of deep-sea things, but without the sea. This glow is the only energy in motion here, but it is so slow, so faint, that it feels as static as the rock. It offers no warmth, only definition. It reveals the texture of the crystalline bark, the soft contours of the dust floor, and nothing more.

There is no animal life. Nothing skitters, crawls, or flies. There is no sound of predation or foraging. The silence is profound, a solid presence. It is not the empty silence of a vacuum, but the full silence of a place where nothing happens. If you listen, truly listen past the beating of your own heart, you might discern a sound buried deep beneath the quiet: a low, resonant hum. It is a vibration more than a sound, the thrumming of the geothermal heart that warms the basin from below, a note that has not changed in pitch or volume for longer than a mountain's lifetime.

This place is in a state of perfect, unchanging equilibrium. It has been weathered to its final form. The "trees" are the only form of life, mineral-based metabolisms that draw energy from the planet's internal heat and sustenance from trace elements in the dust. Their growth is imperceptibly slow, measured in millimeters per millennium. They do not reproduce with seeds or spores; they simply exist. On a timescale that beggars the imagination, a spire might accrue too much mass and fracture, crumbling back into the dust with a whisper-soft crash that is the loudest event for a million years, its light extinguished, its matter returning to the floor to be slowly, eventually, drawn into another spire.

There are no buildings, no artifacts. And yet, there is a sign of a primitive, alien kind of order. Around the base of each spire, the dust is different. It is not a uniform grey but is sorted into concentric rings of size and shade—the finest, darkest particles closest to the crystalline trunk, grading outwards to coarser, paler granules. It is not a conscious act of tool use, but a slow, metabolic tidying, an electrochemical sorting performed by the tree's root-system over thousands of years. It is the most primitive form of technology imaginable: life as geological process, tidying its own surroundings as an incidental effect of its existence. It is the only evidence that anything here differentiates itself from the world around it.

This is a place outside of time as we know it. There are no seasons, no cycle of day and night. There is only the constant warmth, the constant dimness, the constant silence. It is a snapshot held for eternity, a balanced equation of heat, dust, and light. A world asleep, which has never been awake, and which does not dream.
