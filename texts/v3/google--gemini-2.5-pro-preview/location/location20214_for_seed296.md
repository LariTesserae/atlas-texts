This is a city of perpetual twilight, built in the folds of a slumped god. The mountains are impossibly high, but they are not sharp. Eons have ground them down, not into foothills, but into immense, rounded shoulders of stone, whale-backs that breach a sea of permanent cloud. The rock itself is a soft, layered shale, the colour of a wet river stone, and it sheds constantly, not in dramatic rockfalls, but in a fine, persistent grit that coats every surface. The air tastes of stone dust and damp.

The elevation is extreme, but you feel it less as a lack of oxygen and more as a profound separation from everything else. The world below is a myth. Here, in the Grey Plateau, the only reality is the stone, the mist, and the city. The atmosphere is cool and thick with moisture. It is not always raining, but everything is always wet. Water beads on the tensile surfaces of broad, rubbery leaves; it drips from rusted iron eaves; it flows in glassy sheets down the dark stone of the buildings. The light is a diffuse, sourceless grey that filters through the dense cloud cover, rendering colour in muted tones of slate, moss, rust, and verdigris. Shadows are soft and indistinct.

Vegetation here is not so much rooted as it is draped. There are no forests of tall trees reaching for a sun they will never see. Instead, there is a riot of lush, low-lying growth. Carpets of moss, thick as wool blankets, cover entire slopes. Giant, waxy-leafed ferns, some as large as tables, grow in dense clusters from any horizontal surface. The dominant life form is a type of clinging, branching liverwort that grows in vast, intricate webs, knitting together rocks, walls, and forgotten machinery with its thick, dark green tendrils. It grows on itself, layer upon layer, creating a dense, springy mattress of life over the ancient geology. There is a sweet, vegetal smell in the air, the scent of decay and growth happening in the same breath.

Amidst this relentless greenery is the city, Ambit. It is less a construction *on* the plateau and more a feature *of* it, a vast, terraced settlement honeycombed into the gentler slopes of the mountain-shoulders. The buildings are stacked and layered, made of the same dark shale they are carved from, their corners rounded by the same wind and grit that shaped the peaks. Slate roofs, furred with emerald moss, descend in staggered tiers, channeling rainwater into a complex network of cisterns and open-topped stone aqueducts that thread through the city like sluggish arteries. The scale is that of a valley-sized town, open to the sky, yet the density is immense. Balconies nearly touch across narrow, dripping alleyways. Staircases carved directly into the rock are worn into smooth, treacherous bowls. Everything is layered, dense, and near-touching.

This is an industrial place, but its industry is strange and focused. Ambit is a city of water harvesters. A web of iron pipes, sweating with condensation, crisscrosses the city and extends for miles across the plateau. Towering, lattice-like structures—the Condensers—bristle with thousands of metallic fins, designed to comb moisture from the passing mists. The low, resonant hum of unseen pumps is the city’s heartbeat, a sound that is more felt than heard. Steam vents from grates in the cobblestone streets, momentarily obscuring the view before being reabsorbed by the thirsty air. Factories, squat and windowless, line the lower terraces, their purpose obscure, but their output is water—purified, mineralized, or perhaps something else entirely—decanted into huge, riveted iron tanks that await transport to… somewhere else. Away. Down.

The world is active, but its rhythms are slow and inexorable. The mist thickens and thins. The steady drip of water is a constant percussion. The liverworts visibly reclaim any surface left untended for a season. And the machinery of the city turns constantly, a slow, grinding process of capture and containment. Yet for all this activity, the place is deeply quiet. There is no birdsong. The insects are few, mostly metallic-shelled beetles that crawl slowly over the wet moss. Animal life is rare; the city’s inhabitants see only the occasional sinuous, furred creature, something like an otter but with too many legs, slipping silently through the aqueducts.

The people of Ambit are pale and move with a deliberate slowness. They wear heavy, oiled wools and thick-soled boots. Their culture is built around water, its moods, its purity, its rhythms. They are a people of engineers and moss-herders, of pipe-fitters and archivists who record the subtle shifts in atmospheric pressure. They live and work in the dim, damp coolness, their lives governed by the slow, eternal process of the sky turning to water in their hands. The rules of this world are simple: the mountain endures, the moss grows, the mist provides. The city is just a gear in this vast, wet machine, a strange, urban parasite drinking from the veins of the sky.
