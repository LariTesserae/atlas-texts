The air is a warm, wet weight against the skin, thick as felt and carrying the mineral tang of ozone and wet stone. It doesn't move. There is no breeze, only a heavy, uniform stillness. Light is a dim, pearlescent gray, filtered down through a permanent, high ceiling of mist that smothers the sky. You feel as though you are at the bottom of a shallow, warm sea, or inside a vast, unlit terrarium.

This place is a basin, a wide and shallow depression in the earth, and it is packed. There is no open ground. It is a forest of stone, dense and pathless. The pillars are not jagged but bulbous and smooth, like fungal growths swollen to the size of ancient trees, or like tufa towers formed by a spring that has long since ceased its gushing. They are gray, the color of wet cement, and their surfaces are slick with a perpetual film of condensation. They stand so close together that in many places shoulders cannot pass between them, their nodular forms crowding and overlapping. They are weathered, ancient, their contours softened by millennia of this slow, unchanging dampness.

Underfoot is not soil but a viscous, dark mud, the consistency of potter's clay. It clings and sucks at anything that tries to move through it. The mud itself is saturated; pressing down on it causes clear, cool water to well up around the pressure point before slowly being reabsorbed. Here and there, the mud gives way to shallow, perfectly still pools. The water in them is shockingly clear, revealing the smooth, dark clay bottom. No ripples disturb their surfaces.

The sound of the place is water, but not its movement. The only clock here is the metronome of water dripping. From the unseen ceiling of mist, fat drops condense and fall. From the slick, curving flanks of the stone pillars, water gathers and drips. Each drop is a distinct note—a soft *plip* into a pool, a dull *thud* into the mud, a resonant *tok* on the surface of a fallen fragment. The chorus is constant, a million tiny, independent heartbeats that, in their totality, create a profound sense of unchanging silence. There are no birds, no insects, no rustles of leaves, for there are none to rustle.

But this place was not always entirely without occupants. Wedged in the cramped spaces between the stone growths are the remains of simple structures. They are little more than frameworks of bone—rib cages of some large, unidentifiable creature, perhaps, or limb bones lashed together with sinew that has turned dark and leathery in the damp. Stretched over these frames, or hanging from them in tatters, are cured hides, now slick with the same moisture that coats the stone, their surfaces hosting faint, shimmering patches of pale bacterial slime. They are shelters, or the memory of shelters, built by hand with primitive tools. They offer no protection now. They are as much a part of the landscape as the stone, objects in equilibrium, slowly, imperceptibly being claimed by the quiet and the wet. A sharpened flint lies half-sunk in the mud, its edge still keen, as if dropped an hour ago, or ten thousand years.

Life is almost entirely absent. If you watch a clear pool long enough, you might see a tremor in the submerged clay at its bottom, the slow passage of a blind, white worm. A translucent snail, its shell as thin and clear as a bubble of glass, might spend a century climbing a single stone pillar. These creatures are as static as their environment. They do not hunt; they absorb the scant nutrients that leach from the minerals. They do not call out. They are silent, slow-moving parts of the great, stable machine.

This is a world in stasis. The temperature never varies from its balmy warmth, a product of some deep, geothermal constancy. The water level in the pools never rises or falls. The mist never thins or thickens. The stone pillars do not grow or erode in any time scale that matters. It is a place that has reached its final form, a perfectly balanced chemical equation. It is not dead, but it has ceased to become. It simply is, its quiet existence measured only by the steady, eternal drip of water onto stone and mud.
