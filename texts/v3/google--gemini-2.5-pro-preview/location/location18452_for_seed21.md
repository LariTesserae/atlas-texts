The heat is the first thing, and the last. It is not the dry, baking heat of a desert, but a profound, sodden warmth, like the air of a greenhouse built for tropical orchids, or the breath of a sleeping animal. The air is heavy enough to drink, thick with a humidity that beads on your skin instantly. You are high up; the world below is a soft, indistinct blur of greens and browns veiled in a perpetual haze. From this vantage, the air should be thin and cool. It is not. The heat is a deep, foundational rule of this place, an inescapable fact.

This place is a shallow basin, perhaps a half-mile across, a caldera atop a lonely mesa. The rock that forms its floor and walls is a dark, almost black volcanic stone, but it has not been worn by time or water. Its edges are sharp, fractured into clean geometric planes and dagger-like points, as if the mountain just finished cooling yesterday. There is no sand, no gravel, no gentle slopes. Only crisp, new-looking angles.

Yet the place is saturated with water. It doesn't rain from the sky. The weeping is from within. The black stone itself sweats. Droplets form on every vertical face, swell to a critical weight, and trace silver paths downward before dripping from a sharp edge. The air, super-saturated by the heat, condenses on any surface that is even marginally cooler. A constant, polyrhythmic dripping is the only persistent sound—a soft *plink* into a still puddle, a hushed *pat* onto a waxy leaf, a quiet *tick* against another facet of rock.

The source of this unnatural state—the heat, the water, the resistance to erosion—is visible if you know how to look. On close inspection, the dark stone is not pure. It is shot through with a microscopic, silvery tracery, a metallic webwork that glimmers faintly in the direct sun. It is impossibly fine, like the mycelial threads of some cybernetic fungus. This web is the engine of the mesa. It is a furnace at the molecular level, warming the rock from within. It is also a binder, a force-field in skeletal form, reinforcing the stone's crystalline structure and forbidding it from crumbling into dust or slush. The stone does not erode; it only weeps.

Because of this constant, gentle irrigation, vegetation is lush, but strangely sparse. There is no turf or undergrowth. Instead, individual plants stand in isolation, separated by yards of damp, dark, bare rock. They are magnificent specimens. Thick-stalked ferns with fronds the color of verdigris uncurl in slow motion. Pitcher plants, large enough to hold a human head, stand with their slick mouths agape to the humid air, their inner fluids glistening. Great, broad-leafed plants with a surface like polished leather angle their surfaces toward the sun, each one a perfect, self-contained organism in its own defined space. The ground they root in isn't soil, but a thin layer of black, gelatinous humus that collects in the sharpest corners, a rich compost born of condensation and airborne motes.

In the center of the caldera stands a single, slender pylon. It is made of the same black, webbed stone, a smooth, featureless obelisk thirty feet high. It makes no sound, yet if you stand near it, you feel a deep, resonant thrum in the bones of your jaw and the fillings of your teeth. The air shimmers around it, not from heat, but from some other refraction. Occasionally, a cloud of incandescent silver motes, like dust from a grinding wheel, will puff from an invisible seam and drift slowly downwind before vanishing.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. There are no birds to contend with the strange heat, no insects buzzing in the thick air, save for tiny, translucent mites that live within the pitcher plants. The silence is broken only by the dripping water. But in the still, crystal-clear pools that gather in the lowest-lying rock basins, something does move. It is long and sinuous, like an axolotl bred for a king. Its body is a pearlescent white, its external gills a feathery, blood-red crown. It moves with an unnerving slowness, its four delicate limbs pulling it through the water as it browses on the black humus. Its eyes are simple, black beads, and when it surfaces, the water runs off its skin without leaving a drop, a perfect demonstration of engineered hydrophobia. These creatures are the silent gardeners, the only living beings besides the plants. They seem to pay the silver motes from the pylon no mind, but thrive in this sealed, impossible climate.

The light is normal, the overhead sun a familiar yellow-white. Yet it feels filtered, as if passing through a lens. The thick moisture in the air diffuses it, softening all shadows and making the wet rock gleam with a constant, oily sheen. Scattered across the basin, half-buried in the black humus or resting in sharp-angled corners of rock, are other small artifacts: fist-sized lenses of spun crystal that grow warm to the touch, and thin, ceramic-like disks etched with the same circuit-like patterns found in the stone. They are the scattered tools of a forgotten laboratory or a shrine left to run on its own.

This is a place of active, quiet process. A closed system cycling water and heat. A high-altitude hothouse, engineered with physics beyond comprehension, that has been left to its own devices. It is a garden grown in the heart of a quiet engine, a living monument to its own strangeness, forever young and forever weeping.
