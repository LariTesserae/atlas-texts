The first thing you notice is the silence. It is not the empty silence of a vacuum, but a thick, absorptive quiet, like a room lined with velvet. Sound is born and dies in the same instant, swallowed by the world. The air is warm, heavy, and carries the ghost of humidity—a feeling of moisture that never condenses, that never touches your skin as dew or sweat. It smells of warm dust and a deep, cellular green, the scent of a greenhouse in which nothing has ever been watered.

The ground is a fine, pale powder, almost like ash or flour. It is soft underfoot, muffling any step. This dust is bone-dry, yet rising from it is a forest of impossible lushness. This is the great paradox of this place: a world of dust and juice. There are no trees with woody trunks, no plants with fibrous leaves. Instead, life takes the form of colossal, turgid sacs. Imagine succulents grown to the size of buildings, vast, membranous bladders of life, their skins a patchwork of greens, ochres, and purples, taut with the pressure of the fluid held within. These are the Phloems, the Gel-bulbs, the Bladderwrack Towers. They are the world's reservoirs. Not a single drop of free water exists here—no pools, no rivers, no rain. All of it, every last molecule, is locked away inside the cells of the living.

This is not a wilderness; it is a city. A metropolis built of flesh.

The Phloems grow upon and around one another, their sheer density creating the architecture. Ancient, deflated husks of long-dead giants form the foundations, their desiccated skins now part of the dusty ground, layered over millennia. Newer, vibrant growths surge upwards from these ruins, forming immense, organic spires and buttresses that lean against each other for support. They create canyons and avenues between their pulpy bodies, walkways of soft dust winding through a city of living walls. Sunlight filters down through their translucent upper membranes, casting a diffuse, gentle green glow into the lower reaches. There are no sharp edges here, save for the very promise of the new. In deep chasms where the biological mat has not yet fully formed, you can see the world's true bones: raw, sharp-edged shelves of uneroded basalt and crystalline strata, geology so young it still looks like a wound.

The city is packed, saturated with inhabitants, though they too are hushed. Creatures of a thousand forms move through the biological corridors. Armored trilobite-like grazers, their carapaces gleaming dully, rasp at the skins of the bladder-plants with specialized mouths, not to drink, but to absorb the gelid nutrients directly through osmotic membranes. They are the water-thieves, and their quiet scraping is one of the few sounds that survives for more than a moment. In the higher-level niches, things like six-limbed lemurs, covered in fine, moisture-wicking fur, leap from one swaying tower to another. Their impacts are cushioned thuds that vanish instantly. They prey on smaller organisms that live symbiotically on the Phloems' skin. Predators, sleek and flat-bodied like land-flounders, ripple through the dust, their passage a mere whisper of displaced powder as they hunt the grazers. Their world is one of ambush and silence. Communication is not auditory; it is a language of shed pheromones, of subtle color shifts on skin, of faint bioluminescent pulses that flicker through the city's living walls at night.

The entire planet-scale continent is this one, single, sprawling biological entity. It generates its own weather. There are no storm clouds, but vast, slow-moving fronts of spore-mist, called Akos. These are immense, opaque clouds of microscopic gametes and fledgling organisms that drift for weeks across the plains, driven by thermal pressures. When an Akos settles, it is not a rainfall but an investiture. It coats everything in a new, fine layer of potential life, fueling the constant, competitive growth. This is the engine of the city's dynamism: the slow, relentless struggle for light and purchase, the cycle of life and death made manifest as an urban renewal project.

To die here is not to rot. It is to be recycled instantly. A punctured Phloem does not spill its contents onto the ground; the moment its skin is breached, a riot of smaller, opportunistic organisms swarms to the site, frantically absorbing the precious fluid before it can evaporate into the thirsty air. A dead creature is consumed within minutes, its body's moisture carefully partitioned among the scavengers. Nothing is wasted. The entire ecosystem is a closed-loop, zero-sum game of hydration.

To stand in one of the grand, silent avenues is to feel the thrum of this contained life. You are inside a single, colossal organism that pretends to be a city. The air is still, the light is soft, the ground is forgiving. But you can feel the titanic, slow-motion processes at play: the cellular expansion of the walls around you, the subtle shift of a billion-ton bladder as it settles, the ceaseless, silent hunt in the dust at your feet. It is a world holding its breath, lest it lose a single, precious drop.
