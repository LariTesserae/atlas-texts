The noise is the first thing, and the last. It is not the cacophony of a jungle, a chorus of individual voices competing for airtime. It is the sound of a single, colossal machine running in a state of perfect, unending equilibrium. It is a deep, resonant hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a foundational bass note for the entire world. Layered over it is the dry, rhythmic clatter of a thousand tireless shuttles, and the percussive, metronomic *thump-thump-thump* of a slow, powerful piston. The sound is loud, but it is so constant and patterned that after a time it becomes a form of silence.

You are in a room the size of a cathedral nave. The floor, walls, and ceiling are not stone, but a single, continuous living substance. This is the interior of a plant. The floor is a pavement of smooth, dark green cellulose, cool to the touch and slick with a perpetual film of condensation. It is not soil or rock, but the living dermis of the world-organism. From this floor rise colossal pillars, fifty feet in diameter, their surfaces a lattice of interwoven, bark-like fibers in shades of grey and brown. They soar upwards to a ceiling nearly two hundred feet above. The pillars are not separate entities; they are thickenings, structural columns of the same flesh that forms the floor. They have the ancient, patient presence of sequoia trees, but they are merely parts of one contiguous body.

The ceiling is a vast, vaulted web of paler, translucent fibers, woven so densely that it forms a solid canopy. Through this membrane, a soft, even, shadowless light filters down, illuminating the entire chamber in a perpetual, overcast daytime. This ceiling is the source of the place’s moisture. Droplets of condensed water form on the fibrous net and tremble, but they never seem to fall. Instead, they are reabsorbed, or they run in tiny, guided rivulets down the pillar-trunks, carving glistening, temporary paths on their way back to the floor. The air is cool, carrying the scent of wet mulch, ozone, and something else—the faint, sharp tang of hot metal, an impossibility in a world without fire.

This is a place of total vegetation, yet it feels profoundly empty. The great hall is a void. The distance between the pillars is immense. The space between the floor and the ceiling is a vast, echoing volume of cool air. The density of *things* is near zero. The things that are here, however, are the source of the noise, the motion, the room's entire purpose.

They are the fauna. They are not animals as one might understand them. They are organic automata. Along the walls, great beetle-like creatures, the size of armchairs, move with implacable slowness. Their carapaces are a polished, iridescent chitin that seems to mimic blued steel. Their heads are dominated by a pair of massive, grinding mandibles made of a bone-mineral composite harder than granite. They move in perfectly straight lines, scraping a fine dust from the ever-regenerating wall. This is the source of the clatter: the sound of a thousand mineral jaws shearing living tissue from a kilometer-long wall-face. They do not eat the dust. They are simply harvesters.

Following them, or rather, moving on independent, intersecting paths across the floor, are the collectors. These are rolling organisms, spherical bundles of wiry, metallic-looking cilia that spin on a central axis. They tumble silently across the slick floor, gathering the green dust scraped loose by the grinders. As they become heavy with their load, their rotation slows, and they change course, rolling ponderously toward the bases of the central pillars.

Here, at the root of each great column, is a Digestor. These are the pistons, the hearts of the factory. A Digestor is a sessile, pulsing mass of tissue, a twenty-foot-wide hemisphere fused to the floor and the pillar. Its surface is a deep, venous purple, and it bulges and contracts with a slow, powerful rhythm that shakes the very floor. This is the *thump-thump*. The rolling collectors approach and are drawn into docking ports—sphincters of soft tissue that open to receive them. The roller disgorges its load of plant-dust, then withdraws, lighter and faster, to begin its collection circuit anew. The Digestor pulses, processing its meal not for sustenance, but for transformation. From vents on its upper surface, it exhales plumes of warm, steamy vapor—the source of the chamber's humidity and the metallic, ozonic smell. This vapor is the nutrient-rich exhaust that feeds the ceiling membrane, which in turn grows the walls that the grinders scrape.

This system is ancient, worn down to its most efficient, stable state. It is a closed loop, a perpetual motion machine of biology that has been running without change for geological time. The grinders scrape, the rollers collect, the digestors process. The wall is eroded, and the wall is replenished. The energy required is drawn by the luminous ceiling from a source beyond this chamber, but within it, all matter is endlessly recycled. There is no evolution here, no competition, no predator and prey. There is only the process. The rules are absolute and unbreakable. Each organism is a cog, performing a single, unvarying task. They are born, if that is the word, fully formed and ready for their function, emerging from unseen wombs within the plant-substrate. They work until their bodies fail, at which point they are collected and broken down by the same rollers, their matter returned to the system with near-perfect efficiency. There is no waste, no death in the conventional sense, only recycling. It is a world without artifice, without a single made object, yet it is the epitome of industry: loud, productive, and utterly indifferent.
