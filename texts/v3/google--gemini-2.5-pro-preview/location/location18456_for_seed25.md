This place is audible before it is visible.

The sound is a physical presence. It is a pressure against the ears, a vibration in the bones of the jaw and sternum. It is not a single tone but a composite of a billion different noises, layered so densely that they merge into a single, deafening, architectural roar. At its base is a low, thrumming hum, the kind of frequency that makes the sinuses ache. Over this, a wet, percussive chittering, like a billion grains of rice being shaken in a vast, leathery bag. And through it all, higher-frequency clicks, sharp pops, and occasional, resonant booms that echo and are swallowed by the sheer density of the other sounds before they can find a wall to properly reflect from. The sound is not a transient event; it is the state of being here. It is the atmosphere made manifest.

There is no sun. There are no stars. The darkness is absolute, a primordial black that is not an absence of light but a presence of its own. But the world is not without illumination. It is painted in cold, moving light.

The ground is a low, rolling landscape of smooth, black stone, worn into gentle swells and shallow basins by ages of slow, patient erosion. The rock is ancient, settled, its form finalized eons ago. It is cool to the touch, and perpetually, slimily damp. A slick, thin film of moisture condenses on every exposed surface, gathering in the hollows to form shivering, ink-black pools that are never more than a few inches deep. This water is the catalyst.

Growing directly upon and within the stone is the foundation of all life here. It is not vegetation. It is a vast, continuous film, a lithophilic lacquer that ranges from a faint bloom of powdery grey in some areas to a thick, gelatinous, and faintly pulsating membrane in others. This mineral-metabolizing blanket is what glows. Its light is a cold, weary blue-green, rising and falling in slow, asynchronous waves across the landscape. Patches brighten and dim in tidal rhythms that follow no discernible pattern, creating a disorienting, shifting topography of light and shadow across the basin.

This is the pasture. And it is being grazed.

The chittering sound is the noise of the grazers. The ground is a living carpet, a seething, near-solid mass of creatures. To call them insects is a convenience of language; they are something else. They are armored, multi-legged things the size of a human thumbnail, with bodies of iridescent chitin that refract the dim light into fleeting rainbows. There are trillions upon trillions of them, crawling over and under each other in a frantic, ceaseless search for fresh patches of the glowing film to scrape away with serrated mandibles. They move in waves and currents, a river of bodies flowing over the contours of the land. They are the source of the percussive, rattling component of the great sound.

This sheer density of biomass creates its own weather. The heat from their collective metabolism raises the air temperature just slightly above the chill of the rock, creating a layer of perpetual, rolling mist that hangs a few feet off the ground. The air is heavy, thick with humidity and the smells of ozone, wet stone, and a thousand alien metabolisms—a scent halfway between rotting mushrooms and clean electricity.

Preying on this living sea are other, larger forms. These are the sources of the clicks and pops. Pale, spider-like hunters, the size of dinner plates, stalk on needle-thin legs, moving with a jerky, unnatural grace through the chaos. They do not build webs but extrude single, sticky filaments to snag the grazers, reeling them in with a twitch. There are also fleshy, starfish-like beings that slide through the thickest parts of the crawlers' carpet, their undersides studded with mouths that hoover up the smaller creatures by the dozens. These hunters are also bioluminescent, but their light is different—sharp, momentary flashes of brilliant white or piercing ruby red, used for stunning prey or startling rivals. Their movements are a constant, flickering strobe effect within the gentler, pulsing glow of the landscape.

And then there are the sources of the booms. They are rarely seen. They move in the deeper shadows, between the swells of the land. They are huge. Their passage is marked by a temporary parting of the sea of crawlers, a furrow ploughed through the living carpet. Sometimes, a long, serpentine neck, slick and black, will rise a hundred feet into the misty air, its tip a featureless bulb that glows with a soft, orange light, like a distant streetlamp. It will survey the glittering, chittering plains for a long moment, and then drop back into the darkness. When one of these giants calls, the world itself seems to vibrate, a sound so low and powerful it is felt as a physical blow.

This is a world without soil, a cool, damp, dark engine of life built on a foundation of rock and water. Its rules are simple and ancient. Life consumes the stone, mediated by moisture. Life consumes life. Energy, captured in mineral bonds, is released in a riot of flesh, light, and sound. Everything is in motion, everything is touching, everything is eating or being eaten. The land and the fauna are not separate; they are a single, saturated, deafening system. The ground is a geology of flesh, and the sound is the endless song of its metabolism.
