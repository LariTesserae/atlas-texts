The first thing is the sound. It is not a sound that travels through the thin, cool air to be received by an ear. It is a presence. It is a drone that manifests directly in the bones of the skull, a vibration that sets the teeth on edge, a pressure behind the eyes. It is the universe’s tinnitus, a single, deafening, chord-like roar that has no source and no direction because it comes from everywhere at once. It is the sound of the world’s own making, a groan of tectonic becoming that has never, for a moment, ceased. All other senses are secondary, filtered through this overwhelming, monolithic hum.

You stand on a high, windless plateau of shattered, umber-colored rock. The peaks that ring this open expanse are not old and worn, but new and savage, like shards of broken glass magnified to the size of mountains. Their edges are impossibly sharp, their faces unscarred by water or ice. They are still growing. The process is imperceptibly slow to the eye, but it is the source of the deafening sound—the grinding of continental plates, the scream of rock being born under impossible pressure deep below. This place is not a finished product; it is a geological embryo, raw and under construction.

The sky is a dim, permanent bruise of violet and grey, holding a light that feels like a perpetual, deep twilight. There is no sun, no moon, only this diffuse, sourceless luminescence that casts soft, ill-defined shadows. The air is cool, still, and so utterly dry it feels like it could pull the moisture directly from your lungs. There is no water here. No rain has ever fallen, no river has ever carved a path, no ice has ever cracked a stone.

And yet, it is lush.

From the dry, cracked ground, a strange and silent forest grows. This is not vegetation in any biological sense. It is a silicate flora, a mineralogical efflorescence. In sparse, scattered groves, great crystalline structures rise like trees, their trunks of cloudy, fibrous quartz branching into canopies of delicate, razor-thin obsidian leaves. Undergrowth consists of tangled thickets of what looks like spun glass, fragile vines that connect the larger growths, chiming with an infinitesimal tinkle that is utterly lost beneath the world-drone. On the ground bloom formations like flowers: rosettes of amethyst, blossoms of fluted gypsum, pale blue celestine crystals unfurling like desert roses. They do not grow from soil, but emerge directly from the parent rock, drawing their substance from the stone itself in a process of solid-state chemistry that takes millennia. They are cool to the touch, hard as diamond, and translucent, catching the dim violet light and refracting it into faint, internal rainbows.

There is no animal life. No insect buzzes, no creature scurries. The silence of biology is absolute, making the geological roar all the more profound. The world is full, yet empty. The only movement is the slow, imperceptible crystallization of the glass-gardens and the tectonic rising of the mountains.

But something was once here. Or perhaps, something *is* here, in a form that is hard to recognize. Scattered across the vast plateau are faint marks. Low, circular walls of stacked shale, no higher than your knee, that seem to serve no purpose as shelter against a wind that never blows. Single standing stones, carved with geometric patterns that are not decorative but seem more like diagrams, like attempts to map the harmonics of the great sound. They are primitive, clearly shaped by hand and simple tools, yet they possess a terrifying precision. At the center of one of the larger groves of crystal trees stands a low, flat altar of polished basalt, its surface covered in a matrix of perfectly circular depressions, as if something has been counting the slow, granular passage of eons.

The reality of this place is thin. Stare too long at the horizon where the knife-edge peaks meet the bruised sky, and the perspective seems to warp, to fold in on itself. The deafening sound is not just noise; it is a physical force that seems to subtly distort space. Distances are untrustworthy. A grove that appears a short walk away might remain stubbornly distant for hours, while a crack in the rock at your feet could suddenly yawn into a chasm. The world is not stable. The laws that govern it are still settling, like fresh concrete. The sound is the vibration of those laws choosing their final form. To be here is to be an observer inside the kiln, a witness to the slow, agonizing, and deafeningly loud birth of a world.
