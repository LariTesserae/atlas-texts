This is a place of perpetual, quiet rearrangement. It is a basin, perhaps a quarter-mile across, bowl-shaped and contained by walls of ancient, water-darkened rock. The rock is not jagged but worn into vast, gentle curves, like the inside of a pot smoothed by a million years of stirring. Every surface of this stone weeps. A constant, cool condensation gathers and runs in trembling rivulets, tracing paths through patches of black and rust-colored mineral stains. The air is heavy, saturated, and carries the clean, metallic scent of ozone and wet pavement after a lightning strike.

The basin is not empty. It is filled, dense to the point of near-solidity, with a mobile scrapyard. This is the source of the landscape's turbulence. An immense, tangled mass of industrial offal churns here in a slow, hydraulic vortex. There are no grand, crashing noises; the sound is a deep, gut-felt hum, punctuated by the thick, syrupy *schlick* of lubricated parts sliding past one another. The motion is languid but unstoppable. A broad plate of rusted steel, the size of a cottage roof, slowly rotates, its edge grinding against a bundle of what looks like colossal copper wiring, thick as a human torso. Gears the size of millstones mesh and unmesh with a deep, resonant groan that you feel more in your bones than your ears. Ceramic insulators, white and smooth as polished femurs, roll like pebbles in a sluggish stream.

The water that facilitates this movement is not clear. It is a slurry, a chemical soup that coats everything in an iridescent, oily sheen. In the rare pockets where it pools, its surface is a shifting rainbow. This liquid is the reason for the sparsity of life. Nothing can take root in this toxic bath. The only vegetation huddles high on the basin's weeping walls, thirty or forty feet up, just above the reach of the churning mass. Here, acid-green mosses and stark white, shelf-like fungi cling to the damp rock, feeding on the mineral-rich condensation. They are the only fixed points of color in a world of rust, verdigris, and oil-slick darkness.

Scattered remnants of the place's former purpose jut from the mechanical morass. A single, brutalist concrete wall, pocked and stained, rises at a sharp angle, its rebar skeleton exposed and twisted like arthritic fingers. A skeletal catwalk, its grating long since fallen away, spans a short distance before plunging into the churning chaos. Perhaps this was a sorting yard, a disposal site, or a factory that devoured itself. Its logic is gone, leaving only the endless, pointless process.

The light is normal daylight, filtering down from an overcast sky, yet it seems altered by what it illuminates. It catches on the curved face of a giant, tumbled glass vessel, now frosted and opaque from its endless journey, and refracts into a soft, milky glow. It glints off a thousand wet surfaces at once, creating a disorienting, ever-shifting pattern of highlights. The density of objects is so high that shadows are immediate and deep, small pockets of absolute blackness that appear and disappear as the machinery shifts.

There is life here, of a sort, but it is rare and has become part of the machine. In the deeper pools of slurry, long, thin creatures like metallic eels, with segmented bodies of what looks like flexible conduit, writhe and feed on electrical effluvia. They are silent, and their movements are indistinguishable from the writhing of actual cables in the slow current. You might only notice one if you saw its head, a small, unfeatured node with two pinpricks of cold, blue light, rise above the surface for a moment before submerging again.

The defining rule of this place is that nothing is ever still, yet nothing ever arrives. It is a system closed in on itself. The constant, turbulent motion is a form of stasis. The erosion happens not to the land but to the objects within it, which are slowly being ground into a fine, metallic silt that thickens the slurry. It is like the restless, unconscious dream of a dead god of industry. The heart of the factory continues to beat, a slow, wet, grinding pulse, long after the mind that built it has vanished. The silence is the sound of a purpose utterly, and forever, lost.
