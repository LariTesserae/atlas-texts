The silence is the first thing you notice, because it isn't empty. It is a presence, a heavy, insulating medium that mutes the world. The air at this altitude is thin, yet it feels substantial, like moving through fine, invisible velvet. Sounds that should be sharp—a stone dislodged underfoot, the cry of a hunting creature—are swallowed, reduced to dull, intimate thuds and breaths.

This is a land of high, ancient plateaus. The terrain is a vast table of rose-gold stone, weathered over eons into soft, rolling swells and shallow, winding canyons. The edges of the world are not sharp cliffs, but gentle, titanic slopes that descend into a haze of distance. Everything here is old, settled, and in a state of deep equilibrium. The wind, when it comes, is not a scouring force but a slow, warm current that follows the land's contours as if it has done so for a million years and will for a million more. The ground is a mosaic of this warm-hued stone and patches of coarse, gravelly soil, giving the landscape a sparse, patterned appearance when seen from above.

Scattered across these plateaus are the monoliths. They are the only true structures, though they feel more like a geological feature than something built. Each stands alone, separated by kilometers of open space. They are slender, blade-like pillars of a milky, opalescent material that seems to both absorb and softly emit the bright, high-altitude light. They are not inert. From microscopic pores that dapple their surfaces, they weep. A constant, slow bleed of clear, cool water beads and trickles down their faces, a process that seems to defy the dry warmth of the air. This is the world's water. There are no rivers, no lakes. Only the weeping monoliths.

Around the base of each monolith is a circle of impossible life. The trickling water doesn't pool, but is instantly absorbed by a thirsty carpet of dark green, almost black, lichen and moss that clings to the stone. From this dark circle, vegetation radiates outwards in diminishing density. Close to the pillar are succulents like clenched fists of jade and purple, their waxy skin gleaming with captured moisture. Further out, there are patches of wire-thin grass that shimmers with an oily iridescence, and hardy, low-lying shrubs with leaves like overlapping scales of copper. Beyond this small halo of life, the stone and gravel reassert their dominance.

The light is the second thing you notice. It's incredibly bright, but not harsh. The sun is a white, definite disk in a sky of pale, washed-out violet. The thin air and the strange, latent energy of this place seem to filter the sunlight, stripping it of its heat-glare and leaving a pure, clarifying brilliance. The weeping monoliths interact with this light in a way that physics cannot yet explain. They polarize it, refract it, and sometimes, at dawn and dusk, they project faint, geometric ghosts of light into the air around them—complex, shifting lattices of luminescence that fade as quickly as they appear.

This place is not empty of fauna; it is teeming, but it is a quiet population. Large herds of six-legged grazers move in a slow, deliberate ballet between the monoliths. Their bodies are lean and graceful, covered in a short, dun-colored pelt that matches the gravelly soil. Their feet are padded and splayed, making no sound as they traverse the stone. They move with an unnerving silence, their long, flexible necks dipping to browse the iridescent grasses or lick moisture directly from the weeping pillars.

They are hunted. The predators are creatures of light and heat. You rarely see them directly, but you see the evidence of their passing: a flicker of distortion in the air, a shimmer above the hot stone like a mirage with intent. Sometimes, for a fraction of a second, you might see a form—a lean, quadrupedal hunter made of something like spun glass or solidified heat, its outline blurring as it refracts the landscape through its own body. Their hunts are silent, swift, and final. A grazer at the edge of the herd will simply collapse, its energy seemingly drained from it in a single, invisible touch.

There is a rhythm here. It is the slow, silent migration of the herds from one oasis-monolith to another. It is the patient, shimmering hunt of the predators. It is the daily cycle of condensation and weeping, a quiet pulse of water engineered into the very stone of the world. The monoliths are not ruins; they are engines running on a timescale that mimics geology. They hum, but not with sound. It is a vibration you feel in your bones, a low-frequency thrum of incomprehensible technology working in concert with the sun and the atmosphere.

The rules are simple. Life clusters around the weeping stones. Sound does not travel. The light is more than just illumination; it is a component of the ecosystem, a medium for energy and perhaps information. The ground beneath your feet is a living circuit board laid across a plateau at the top of the world, its processes ancient, active, and utterly indifferent. To stand here is to feel like a fleeting guest in a vast, self-sustaining work of art or engineering, a place where the distinction between the two has long since weathered away.
