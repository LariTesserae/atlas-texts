The world is a single, continent-sized plateau, and the plateau is alive. What might have once been stone, aeons ago, is now buried kilometres deep beneath a substrate of its own making. This is a landscape in its final, stable state, having long ago achieved its ultimate form: a world-forest so ancient and all-encompassing that geology and biology have become one and the same.

The ground beneath your feet is not soil or rock, but a deeply compressed, perpetually damp mat of lignin, cellulose, and ancient mycelial networks. It is warm to the touch, exuding a gentle, constant heat from the slow, pressure-cooked decomposition occurring in its abyssal depths. This planetary-scale geothermal composting keeps the high-altitude air from freezing, creating a vast, warm, humid biome that stews in its own breath. The ground is springy and absorbent, silencing footfalls. A step taken with any weight causes a small, dark pool of water to well up around the impression before being slowly reabsorbed. The whole world is a sponge.

The air is heavy, thick with moisture and the complex smell of decay, wet wood, and blooming fungi. It feels less like air and more like a thin fluid. Light is a rare currency. A dense, multi-layered canopy of immense, waxy leaves, far above, filters the sun to a dim, perpetual twilight. The quality of the light is green and aqueous, as if you are walking at the bottom of a shallow, verdant sea. There are no direct sunbeams, only diffuse columns of illumination that shift with an imperceptible slowness, painting hazy spotlights onto the forest floor.

The "trees" here are not individual organisms so much as architectural extrusions of the living planet itself. Vast, woody pillars, thicker than any earthly sequoia, rise into the gloom, their bark like folded, petrified leather. They do not branch until hundreds of metres up, where they splay out into the load-bearing canopy. These pillars are incomprehensibly old, their growth measured in millimetres per century. They are less like plants and more like mountains of living tissue. Between them, space is not empty but filled with a dense filigree of other life. Thick, ropy vines, coated in a shimmering, gelatinous moss, hang in motionless catenary curves. Enormous bracket fungi, tiered like shelves of polished, dark wood, jut from the pillars, some large enough to walk upon.

Water is a constant presence, but rarely in pools or rivers. It weeps. It condenses. It drips. The air is so saturated that every surface is slick with a film of moisture. Water pearls on the edges of leaves, gathers in the cups of fungi, and runs in slow, silent rivulets down the vertical bark of the great pillars. The soundscape is dominated by this gentle, hydraulic pulse: the steady, patient *tock... tock... tock* of heavy drops falling from the canopy to the absorbent floor, a sound that is both everywhere and nowhere, having no clear source. There is no birdsong, no chattering. The dense, wet air and the soft surfaces of the world conspire to muffle all sound, creating a profound quietude.

Yet, the place is abundant with life. The quietness is a sign of adaptation, not absence. Slow, six-legged creatures, something between an insect and a reptile, move with deliberate grace along the undersides of the great fungal shelves. Their bodies are camouflaged with living moss, their footfalls perfectly silent. In the deeper gloom, clusters of small, phosphorescent fungi cast an eerie blue-green glow, and occasionally, a cloud of glimmer-moths will drift through a clearing, their wings beating in utter silence, their bodies trailing faint, sparkling motes of light. Beneath the surface of the living ground, vast, blind worm-like beings churn the substrate, their passage marked only by a slow, subtle heaving of the earth.

There are faint traces of intention here, marks of a mind that worked with the world's biology rather than against it. Scattered through the vastness are structures that are almost, but not quite, natural. A series of living vines, woven together into a precise, spiralling lattice that forms a kind of suspended walkway between two fungal ledges. A hollow carved into the side of a woody pillar, its entrance shaped not by a chisel but by guided growth, the edges smooth and organic. A circular clearing where the weeping epiphytes have been trained to drip into a central, bowl-shaped fungus, creating a reliable well of clean water. These are not ruins of stone or metal that defy the landscape; they are suggestions, whispers of habitation that are slowly being reabsorbed. Whoever made them—or perhaps still maintains them—possessed no tools, only an infinitely patient understanding of how to ask the world to shape itself.

This is a world without hurry. The cycles here are glacial. A fruit-body, the size of a boulder, might take a decade to ripen before detaching from its parent mycelium and falling to the forest floor with a deep, muffled *whump* that is the loudest sound the landscape produces. It will then take a century to be fully reabsorbed into the living ground. Change is not an event but a slow, unfolding process, like the unfurling of a fern frond in extreme slow motion. To be here is to feel the weight of time not as a rushing river, but as a deep, still ocean. The world does not feel dead or dying; it feels *finished*. It has reached a state of such profound equilibrium that it simply *is*, breathing a single, slow breath that takes a thousand years to complete.
