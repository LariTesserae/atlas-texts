The first thing is the sound. It is not a collection of noises but a single, solid thing. It is a physical presence that presses against the skull and vibrates deep in the bones of the chest. It is a bass note the size of a continent, layered with the shrieking friction of a billion moving parts, the percussive slam of components locking into place, and the high, steady whine of unimaginable energy. The sound is the baseline of existence here; silence would be an apocalypse.

The air is thick, warm, and wet, heavy with the taste of ozone, hot metal, and something else, something protein-like and alien. It condenses on every surface, forming a perpetual, greasy dew. There is no soil underfoot. The ground is a pavement of interlocking hexagonal plates, worn smooth by aeons of friction, their basalt-dark material showing iridescent stress-fractures like oil on water. These plates are not static. In a slow, deep-tectonic rhythm, they shift, rising and falling by inches, grinding against one another with a noise that is lost in the greater din. This is the "lowland," a basin of this world-spanning construct that stretches without horizon in every direction.

Gaze upward, and there is no sky. There is only a ceiling, miles high, of more city. A dense, inverted landscape of towers, conduits, and glowing panels hangs in the thick air, a knot of architecture so dense it blots out the sun that must exist somewhere above it. Light, a diffuse and sourceless silver-gold, filters down through canyons and vast apertures in this upper layer. It is perpetual, humid daylight, casting soft, indistinct shadows that seem to always be in motion.

This is a world without vegetation. Not a single leaf, not a strand of moss. The energy source is elsewhere. Instead of life, there is… process. The structures themselves are the producers. They are not merely built; they are in a state of constant, turbulent becoming. A wall, seemingly made of a dull, bone-colored ceramic, might suddenly liquefy, its surface flowing like warm wax to seal a fissure or extrude a new buttress. Great, multi-jointed arms of metallic lattice unfold from cavities, repositioning massive blocks of material for reasons unknown, then retracting as if they were never there. The world is a machine that builds and unbuilds itself in a chaotic, ceaseless frenzy.

Water is the system’s blood. It does not rain from the sky-ceiling, but weeps from the walls. It drips from the colossal underbelly of the upper layers, running in slick, dark rivulets down load-bearing pillars the size of mountains. It flows in engineered canals, not as clear water, but as a viscous, nutrient-rich, or information-dense slurry, shimmering with the movement of microscopic automata. In the low-lying basins, it pools into shallow, steaming lakes of coolant and lubricant, their surfaces disturbed by the venting of hot gas from submerged grates. To fall in would not be to drown, but to be dissolved and incorporated.

And in this world of stone and mechanism, there is fauna. Abundant fauna. They are not flesh and blood as we know it. Skittering through the canyons are things like metallic crabs, their multi-jointed legs ending in plasma torches they use to graze on solidified energy deposits or carve open maintenance panels. Gelatinous, amoebic blobs, the size of cars, flow across the shifting ground-plates, absorbing the weeping fluids from the walls through osmotic membranes, their bodies pulsing with a soft, internal light. Above, swooping through the artificial canyons, are aeroslicks—creatures of stretched, gossamer-thin films held taut between energized spars, riding the thermal updrafts from exhaust ports, their calls like the shriek of failing machinery. They are not animals; they are mobile, semi-autonomous components of the global machine, a cleanup crew, a circulatory system, a set of antibodies for a planetary organism.

This is a place of absolute density and constant turnover. There is no empty space. Every surface is coated in the slick dew, crawling with micro-constructs, or scored with the passage of larger machine-fauna. Every volume of air is saturated with sound, humidity, and the whine of power. The feeling is of being inside an engine that is also a city that is also an ecosystem. The settled, long-weathered nature of the deepest structures—the continental plates of the city's foundation—provides a stage for the utter chaos that plays out upon it. It is a system in balance, but the balance point is a hurricane.

The rules are simple. Do not stand still for too long, for the ground beneath you may not be the ground in a minute. Do not assume a wall is solid. Do not assume the water is just water. The deafening roar is not a hazard; it is a shield, masking the specific, localized sounds of imminent change that might give a moment's warning. To survive here is to develop a different sense, a precognitive awareness of the machine's rhythm, a feel for the turbulence before it remakes the world under your feet. It is a place beyond modern understanding, an urban sprawl so vast and ancient it has become a new, incomprehensible form of nature.
