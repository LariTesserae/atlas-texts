The first thing is the sound, and there is no second thing for a long time. It is not a single noise but a dense, saturated medium that presses in from all sides, a physical presence that vibrates in your teeth and sternum. It is a deafening, multi-layered roar. At its base is a profound, sub-audible hum, the thrum of a continent-sized engine only partially heard, mostly felt as a slow, rhythmic pressure wave that makes the air throb. Layered over this is the shriek of tortured metal, the high, thin scream of stressed bearings and grinding gears from a thousand unseen sources. And woven through it all is the pervasive, crushing hiss of steam and vapor, a sound like a world-sized lung slowly, perpetually exhaling. The sound is so absolute that it creates a kind of silence; communication is impossible, and the mind retreats, leaving only the raw, overwhelming sensory input.

The air itself is a participant in this cacophony. It is warm, thick, and so saturated with moisture that it beads on any cool surface, which is to say, none of them. It tastes of hot metal, ozone, and wet rock. To breathe it is to drink a fine, warm aerosol. Light struggles through this soupy atmosphere. There is no sun, no sky. The enormous space is a cavern, but the term feels inadequate. It's a subterranean vault, a chasm so vast the ceiling is lost in the thick, hazy darkness far above. What little light exists comes from scattered, industrial sources: the dull, cherry-red glow of superheated machine-hearts, the sickly yellow pulse of sodium lamps on forgotten maintenance gantries, and the occasional, startlingly white beam of an automated spotlight, sweeping slowly and without clear purpose across the landscape below.

The floor of this vault is not soil or stone, but a raw, embryonic geology in the process of becoming. It is a landscape under construction. Great, sloping fields of nascent crystal glitter dimly, their facets sharp and new. They are not clean and perfect, but grow from a thick, viscous slush of mineral-rich water, a kind of geological mud that pools in shallow, steaming estuaries. The ground is a chaotic jumble of these half-formed crystal beds, puddles of a chemically pungent, lukewarm ooze, and the stark, angular forms of the industry that infests this place.

This is a world packed to the brim. Every square meter is occupied. Where the crystal isn't growing, a massive, corroded pipe, slick with condensation, lies half-buried in the muck. Where there isn't a pipe, there is a thicket of rebar-like metallic growths, mineral concretions that have formed around some ancient, discarded armature. There is no empty space. The visual field is saturated with texture and form, a density that mirrors the oppressive density of the sound.

The technology here is brutalist and enormous. These are not elegant machines. They are titanic, iron-clad things that seem more grown than built, their surfaces pocked with rust and weeping mineral efflorescence. They are the source of the noise. Huge, cylindrical structures, like upturned silos, shudder violently, their internal mechanisms pounding with a slow, geological rhythm. These are Precipitators. They draw the mineral slush up from unseen reservoirs deep below, churn it, heat it, and spray it in great, slow arcs across the crystal fields, nurturing their growth. Wide, slow-moving conveyor belts, their surfaces caked with crystalline residue, scrape and groan as they transport sections of particularly dense growth to crushing houses, where titanic hammers pulverize them into gravel for some unknown, distant purpose. Scattered between these behemoths are smaller, but no less formidable structures: pump-regulators encased in ribbed iron, small blockhouses venting plumes of steam, and skeletal catwalks that stretch between machines, their grates trembling with the ceaseless vibration. They seem less like a factory imposed on a landscape and more like a symbiotic, geological organ.

Life here is abundant, but it is not green. There is no photosynthesis. On the flanks of the larger crystal formations and the warm casings of the machinery grow patches of sparse, leathery lichen. They glow with a faint, phosphor-green light, feeding not on sunlight, but on the piezoelectric charge generated by the vibrating crystals and the ambient electrical fields of the engines.

The fauna is stranger still. Great herds of creatures, like enormous, multi-legged armadillos, crawl slowly across the crystal flats. Their carapaces are thick with chitin and mineral deposits, and they use hardened, shovel-like mandibles to scrape the nascent crystal and lichen, their scraping adding a high-frequency grinding to the sonic onslaught. They are the grazers of this iron world. Swarms of beetle-like insects, the size of a human hand, flock in the warm, humid air around the machines, their iridescent wings a blur in the dim light. They are drawn to the heat and the chemical haze. And in the shadows cast by the colossal engines, predators wait. Lean, sightless stalkers, like six-legged greyhounds, hunt by sensing vibration and heat, their bodies low to the ground, moving with a fluid grace that is terrifyingly out of place amidst the mechanical rigidity. They use the deafening noise as cover, their own movements completely masked by the industrial roar.

The overarching rule of this place is its monumental slowness. The machines have been running for centuries, maybe millennia. Their work is not a daily production cycle but a geological epoch in miniature. The crystal fields expand by millimeters a year. The great hammers fall once a minute. The entire system is a patient, grinding process of creation, a factory for a planet's crust. Standing within it, you are an irrelevant mote. The shuddering of the ground is not for you. The noise is not a warning, but the simple, indifferent sound of a world being born in thunderous, mechanical labor.
