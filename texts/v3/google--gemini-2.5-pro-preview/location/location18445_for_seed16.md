The sound hits you first. Not a wind, for there is no weather here, but a hum that builds into a vast and layered chime. It is the sound of a million tiny, resonant fractures happening at once. It fills the air, pressing in from all sides, a constant, encompassing drone that vibrates in your teeth and bones. It is the only sound.

This place is a basin, a hollow scooped out of the world. You are in one of the Sunken Kivas of the Ash-Gardens, a city built downwards into a basin of geothermally superheated rock. The scale is intimate, a pocket world that might fill a single large crater. Above, there is no sky, only a ceiling of haze and interlocking rock, a dome of fused slag that filters the light from some unseen, distant source into a permanent, dim twilight. The light is the colour of old brass.

The heat is immense, a dry, oppressive blanket that radiates not from the air but from the ground itself. The floor of the kiva is a mosaic of cracked basalt and obsidian plates, smooth and settled by ages of thermal stress. They fit together with the precision of long erosion, their edges worn soft. This is a world without water. No rain has ever fallen here, no river has ever carved a path. All erosion is the slow, patient work of heat and vibration.

From the cracks between these basalt plates grows the flora, the source of the chime. This is not vegetation as we know it. They are fields of resonant cane, vast groves of what could be called Cinder-Bloom. These organisms are tall, slender stalks of silicon and metallic salts, spun into crystalline fibres. They look like black glass, catching the dim light in sharp, dangerous gleams. They are lush not in their softness, but in their profusion. They grow in dense, orderly thickets, leaving the wide avenues and plazas of the city starkly empty. There is a strange hollowness to the space; you can be surrounded by a forest of this glass-cane and still see for a hundred yards through its sparse, rigid structure.

This flora is the engine of the world. The stalks drill microscopic roots deep into the hot rock, drawing up not water, but superheated mineral vapours. This process of thermochemical conversion is wildly active. The stalks visibly tremble, their internal structures shifting and cracking under the strain. Each micro-fracture releases a sharp, musical *tink*. Multiplied by billions, it becomes the overwhelming, orchestral drone. This is the world's metabolism, and it is loud.

As the canes metabolize the geothermal energy, they grow, and they shed. They don't release pollen; they release a fine, shimmering dust of refined minerals. This dust is the basis of everything. It coats every surface in a glittering, micron-thin layer of silvery grey. In the still air, it hangs in visible, lazily swirling clouds that catch the dim light.

And in this garden of singing glass and hot stone, there is a city. It is not built *on* the land but *of* it. The basin is terraced, a series of descending rings carved by hand from the native rock. The structures are troglodytic: circular doorways lead into chambers hollowed out from the basin's walls. Walls of stacked terracotta brick, fired from the mineral dust, partition larger spaces into rooms and workshops. The technology is primitive but mastered. Stone chisels and basalt hammers lie on workbenches, coated in the same fine dust, as if the artisans just stepped away.

This was a city organized around the harvest. Rakes made from the sharpened ribs of larger, dead canes are propped against doorways. In the centre of the plazas are great, shallow grinding bowls carved into the floor, where the mineral dust was likely processed. Grooves and channels are cut into the city's stone floor, not for water, but to guide the slow, convective drift of the valuable dust into collection pits.

There is no animal life. No insects buzz around the glowing canes, no scavengers scuttle in the shadows of the empty homes. The silence between the chimes is absolute and unnerving. This lack of movement makes the dynamism of the flora all the more stark. The canes pulse with an inner light, a faint, cherry-red glow that emanates from their core where the heat is most intense. The entire landscape seems to breathe, its resonant exhalation a constant reminder of the volatile processes churning just beneath the surface.

This is a world in a state of perfect, strange equilibrium. The heat cooks the rock, the rock feeds the canes, the canes sing and shed their mineral dust, and the dust settles upon the silent, waiting city. The rules are simple: life needs no water, only heat. Sound is not a sign of fauna, but of flora. And a city can be dense with structure and yet utterly, profoundly empty. You stand in a street paved with obsidian, surrounded by homes, tools, and the evidence of a civilization, yet the only living things are the hot, glassy, singing stalks in the gardens between the ruins.
