The air does not move. It is a thick, wet heat, heavy as a drenched blanket, tasting of hot copper, ozone, and the deep, sweet smell of decomposition. You are underground, in a space that is both vast and claustrophobically full. This is a single, immense geode, a hollow in the deep earth the size of a grand cathedral, but it has been made into a city.

The walls are not rock but sheer, unbroken facets of black and purple crystal, sharp-edged and new. They glitter weakly, catching and multiplying what little light exists. There is no central light source. Instead, the dimness is a composite glow: the faint, violet phosphorescence of the crystals themselves, the sickly yellow-green of bioluminescent fungi clinging to pipes, and the scattered red and amber indicator lights of silent machinery. These tiny stars are reflected a thousand times in the condensation that sheets every surface, making the entire cavern seem to weep light.

The ground underfoot is a treacherous collage. In places, it is the razor-sharp detritus of the native crystal, crunching like broken glass. Elsewhere, it becomes a thick, springy loam of black soil and moss, so damp it squelches. But mostly, the floor is a lattice of corroded iron grates, bolted over a chasm of unseen depth. Through these grates, you can feel a profound, slow-moving warmth rise, and hear the one constant sound of the place: a deep, resonant hum, so low it is felt in the bones more than heard. It is the sound of a colossal engine turning over at a glacial pace.

This is a city built in layers, packed to saturation. There is no empty space. Architecture and infrastructure are woven together with a startlingly lush ecology. Riveted metal hab-pods and workshops, stained a verdigris green, cling like barnacles to the crystalline walls, stacked one atop the other, connected by a maze of swaying catwalks and rickety staircases. An impossible tangle of pipes—copper, iron, and some kind of black, rubbery polymer—writhes through the city, twisting around dwellings, plunging through floors, and snaking up the crystal facets. These pipes are hot to the touch and drip constantly, feeding the pervasive dampness. This is the city's circulatory system, carrying steam, heated water, and chemical nutrients.

And everywhere, there is life. Not animal life; the air is strangely sterile of buzzing insects or skittering creatures, save for the occasional, almost invisible shimmer of a silverfish-like creature disappearing into a crack. The life is vegetation. It is not green. In the dim light, great, fleshy-leafed vines—the color of bruises, deep blues and purples—crawl over everything, their broad leaves angled not toward any light, but toward the hottest pipes. This is Vaporvine, and it feeds on heat and mineral-rich steam. Its roots, fine as spun copper, don't seek earth but instead etch themselves directly into the metal of the pipes, drawing sustenance. Growing in the shadows beneath is Anvil Moss, a dense, velvety carpet of pure black that thrives on pressure and vibration, clustering thickest at the base of the city's support struts and around the humming machinery. It periodically releases clouds of glittering, metallic spores that drift slowly through the air like fine dust, contributing to the haze.

The rules of this world are inverted. Light is not life; heat is. Growth is not a reaching for the sun, but a pressing against the machine. The city and its strange ecosystem are symbiotic. The industrial heart of the place—a colossal, mostly submerged geothermal engine—generates immense heat and pumps nutrient-rich water from some deeper aquifer. This waste product is the lifeblood of the chemotrophic jungle that has completely overgrown the settlement. The jungle, in turn, insulates the machinery, muffles its sounds, and filters its emissions, creating the quiet, stable, hothouse environment.

Change is measured in geology and rust. The city does not seem to be expanding, but slowly cycling. Old pipes are constricted and consumed by the Vaporvine, their metal skins thinning over decades until they burst in a whisper of steam, feeding a bloom of new growth. New pipes are laid by unseen hands, their paths dictated by the slow, crystalline growth of the geode itself, which is said to shift and expand by a millimeter each century, pushed by the same geothermal forces that power the city. The city is a patient machine for an unknown purpose, tended by a population that must be as slow and quiet as their environment.

The dominant sound is the dripping of water, a liquid clockwork counting out an impossibly slow time. A drip from a pipe onto a broad leaf. A drip from a leaf onto a metal walkway. A drip from the walkway through a grate and into the abyss. Each drop echoes for a moment in the humid quiet, a counterpoint to the ever-present, subsonic hum. To be here is to feel like a component in a vast, sleeping organism, to be sealed in a place where yesterday and tomorrow are indistinguishable, a warm, dark, living jewel buried miles beneath the surface of the world.
