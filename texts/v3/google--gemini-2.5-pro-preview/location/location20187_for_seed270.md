This is a contained place, a pocket world that exists in the bottom of a bowl. To find it, you must first cross a vast and ancient plain of wind-scoured dust, a landscape worn down to its final, featureless form over unimaginable aeons. The air here is thin and cool, carrying the scent of ozone and ground stone. There is no weather, only the slow, inexorable settling of motes. In the center of this plain is a depression, a perfectly circular sinking of the land, perhaps a kilometer across. From its rim, you see nothing but more of the same pale, grey-brown dust. But as you descend, the air thickens, the temperature drops from cool to cellar-cold, and a sound begins to grow.

It is not a roar or a shriek, but a complex, dense, and overwhelmingly loud chiming. It is the sound of a million tiny bells being struck at once, of wind chimes in a hurricane, of a workshop where every tool is made of resonant crystal. The sound vibrates in your bones, a constant, multi-layered, percussive hum that is the defining feature of the Chime Orchard.

You are now at the bottom of the basin. The ground is not dust here, but a near-impenetrable, glassy black slag, smooth and cool to the touch. Scattered across it are the faint remnants of a long-dead industry: rusted pylons no taller than a person, thick ceramic conduits half-buried in the slag, and the occasional squat, round vat of pitted iron. From the tops of the pylons and along the length of the conduits, a steady, sourceless light emanates. It is the flat, even light of a laboratory or an overcast day, casting no sharp shadows.

There is no soil, no water, no plant. The base of this world's ecology is a shimmering, crystalline hoarfrost that grows in fractal patterns across the black slag floor. It is a lithotrophic life-form, a "geodic mold" that slowly metabolizes the rare minerals in the substrate, growing in razor-sharp, sugar-like blades. It is the only stationary thing here.

Everything else moves.

The basin is filled to a depth of several meters with life. The density is staggering; it is a living, seething soup of bodies, a tide of creatures crawling over, under, and through each other. This is the source of the sound. The fauna are not flesh and blood, but beings of mineral, metal, and silicate. Their chemistry is utterly alien, a dry biology that operates without a drop of water.

The smallest and most numerous are the Chittermites, creatures the size of a thumb, with bodies of spun silica and legs of fine copper wire. They swarm in glittering clouds, their tiny, hard bodies clicking against each other as they scrape the geodic mold from the floor with flint mandibles. They are the base of the food pyramid.

Hunting them are the Gear-Slinkers, centipede-like organisms whose segmented bodies are interlocking plates of what looks like brushed aluminum. They move with a disconcerting mechanical fluidity, their passage marked by a high-pitched whirring and the rhythmic grinding of their internal gearing. They snatch Chittermites with articulated pincers, crushing their silica shells with an audible *crack*.

Larger still are the Resonators. These are the true bells of the orchard. Roughly the size of large crabs, their carapaces are formed from bell-bronze and veined with other, unknown alloys. They do not walk, but drag themselves forward on short, thick legs, swinging heavy, crystalline forelimbs like hammers. When they strike each other, which is constantly in the dense press of bodies, they do not fight so much as test one another's tone. A sharp, pure chime rings out, and the lesser creatures scatter from the sonic shockwave. They feed by slamming their resonant claws onto the slag floor, shattering the geodic mold and inhaling the energized dust.

The system is in constant, violent motion. Predator and prey are distinguished only by size and the pitch of the sound they make upon impact. The air, thick with the kinetic energy of a billion collisions, shimmers with the dust of shattered carapaces and consumed mold. The scattered industrial artifacts serve as landscape features in this micro-world. The Gear-Slinkers coil and rest inside the hollow vats. The Chittermites swarm up the pylons to escape predation, their wire-legs making a sound like static against the metal. The Resonators use the old conduits as highways, their heavy passage producing a deep, gong-like hum that provides the bass note for the overwhelming symphony.

This is a complete, closed-loop ecosystem. The dead are not left to rot; there is no decay here. A shattered Resonator is immediately swarmed by Chittermites, who mine the valuable alloys from its carapace, recycling its very being back into the system. It is a biological factory, a self-perpetuating, self-consuming machine that has colonized the ruins of a technological one. Its processes are industrial in their efficiency and relentless pace, a world of production and consumption where life is an act of mechanical resonance, and death is merely disassembly.
