You find it not by sight, but by sound. The entrance is a fissure no wider than your shoulders, a crack in a deep, unremarkable stratum of grey sedimentary rock. From it bleeds a constant, overwhelming noise—a sound too complex to be wind or water. It is a chord, a ringing, a sustained industrial groan layered with the sharp, rhythmic clatter of a thousand workshops. The air that exhales from the crack is warm, humid, and smells of crushed green things, ozone, and damp, hot stone.

Squeezing through the fissure, you descend a short, sharp slope of broken rock and find yourself on a narrow ledge. The world opens up. And it is empty.

You are inside a geode, but a geode of living matter. It is a vast, hollow sphere, perhaps a quarter-mile in diameter. The walls, the ceiling, the floor—they are all composed of a single, continuous, unified organism. This is not a cave with plants in it; the cave *is* the plant. Let’s call it the Locus. The space it encloses is profoundly vacant, a bubble of nothingness held within an infinity of living mass.

The material of the Locus is not bark or leaf in any conventional sense. It is a pearlescent, semi-translucent substance, like nacre or polished horn, layered in immense, plate-like structures that curve to form the sphere. These plates are sharp-edged, uneroded, fitting together with the clean, fractured lines of freshly broken obsidian. In some places, new plates seem to be actively budding and growing, pushing older ones aside with a deafening, slow-motion CRACK that echoes through the chamber. The entire structure groans under its own immense, ever-increasing tension. This is the source of the deep, resonant hum: the sound of a world being born and breaking at the same time.

Light is everywhere and nowhere. The Locus itself is the source, a powerful and diffuse bioluminescence that shines *from* the pearlescent walls. It is a clean, white light, as bright as an overcast day. But the light is not steady. It pulses, it strobes, it casts colours that do not belong. A patch of wall will suddenly blaze with the impossible orange of a forgotten sunset; a ripple of deep violet will chase a phantom cloud across the dome. The light is a recording.

And so is the sound. The deep groan of the Locus’s growth is the bedrock, but upon it is layered a cacophony of ghosts. You can hear, clear as day, the chip-chip-chip of stone on stone, the murmur of voices speaking a language of clicks and sibilants, the splash of water, the cry of a thing that no longer exists. These sounds are not echoes; they are primary. They erupt from the walls without a source and fade without travelling, spatialized moments of a past that is not past. You can get vertigo from it, turning to find the source of a shout that is right behind you, only to find empty air.

Reality here is a thin film. Time is not a line but a geology, layered into the Locus itself. As you watch, a shimmer in the air halfway across the chamber resolves itself. For a moment, a rickety scaffold of woven branches and fiber stands there, clinging to the curved wall. A figure—long-limbed and indistinct—is perched upon it, chipping at the Locus with a stone hammer. The sound of the hammer striking home is the same rhythmic clatter you can hear everywhere, a phantom resonance made briefly, visibly real. Then, as if the memory has exhausted itself, the scaffold and its occupant dissolve into heat haze, leaving only the empty air.

This is the purpose of the place, you realize. Or the purpose that has been found for it. Along the lower walls, you can see the evidence of primitive, painstaking work. Chipped handholds are carved into the living nacre. Crude basalt hammers and obsidian chisels lie on ledges, as if their users have just stepped away. Small sections of the wall have been pried off, revealing a deeper, crystalline lattice underneath that glimmers with trapped light. They are harvesting it. They are mining the resonances, quarrying the memories.

The atmosphere is thick and warm, constantly cycling. A cool mist will begin to form in the center of the sphere, condensing from nothing. It will swirl, gathering density, until it becomes a rolling fog that mutes the light and sound. During these periods, the "dampness" of the world becomes absolute. Water drips from every surface, running in rivulets down the bizarre scaffolding, making the handholds slick. This water is clean but has a strange, metallic taste, as if it has filtered through pure time. Then, just as suddenly, the Locus will pulse with a dry, internal heat, and the fog will cook away into nothing, leaving the air sharp and clear again.

There is almost no other life. The Locus is a total ecosystem, but a sterile one. The only fauna you can spot are tiny, mite-like creatures, no larger than a grain of sand, that swarm over the areas where the wall has been freshly broken. They seem to shimmer, phasing in and out of existence, and you suspect they don’t feed on the plant matter itself, but on the temporal energy released by its fracturing. They are scavengers of broken moments.

You take a step along your ledge, and your boot dislodges a small, sharp shard of the Locus material. It tumbles into the vast emptiness below. It does not whistle as it falls. Instead, it emits a perfect, tiny scream—the sound of a child’s terror, captured and crystallized. As it falls into the mists gathering below, the sound does not fade with distance. It is simply present, and then it is gone. You are in a hollow place, filled with everything that has ever happened inside it. You are standing on the edge of a memory palace that is still growing, its shelves groaning as they fill.
