This place is not reached by climbing, but by enduring. You ascend through cloud banks so thick they feel like geological strata, each one a different shade of grey, each one colder than the last. Finally, you breach the top layer and find yourself not on a summit, but on a platform in a sea of unending white vapor. This is the Anvil, a small, isolated mesa no larger than a town square, thrust impossibly high into the thin, cold air. The world below is a rumor. The sun is a memory.

The sky here is a permanent, bruised twilight, a dome of deep indigo that never brightens or darkens. The only light comes from below. It is a shifting, pulsing, blue-green luminescence that glimmers up from the cloud sea, as if a great and restless city of ghosts is breathing beneath you. This cold light catches on the features of the Anvil, throwing them into sharp, dramatic relief.

The terrain is a forest of jagged, crystalline spires. They are not stone in the traditional sense, but a kind of basalt-glass, extruded from the mesa's heart in a process of impossibly slow geological growth. They are young, unweathered, their edges sharp enough to cut, their facets gleaming slickly in the half-light. They range from the height of a man to the height of a cathedral spire, packed so densely that navigating the Anvil is like moving through a tangled, frozen jungle. The ground underfoot is the same material, a cracked and uneven floor of black glass.

Water is a constant presence, but not as rain or rivers. The cold air, saturated with moisture from the clouds below, condenses on every surface. A perpetual, heavy dew clings to the spires, gathering in droplets that tremble on the sharp edges before sliding slowly down the facets to pool in the cracks and fissures of the floor. The air is damp on the tongue and tastes of ozone and wet minerals.

Vegetation is almost entirely absent. The only hint of it is a fine, powdery, dark-green lichen that grows in the most sheltered crevices, forming velvety patches that seem to drink the faint light. There are no trees, no flowers, no grass.

There is, however, life. The Anvil is overwhelmingly, suffocatingly alive.

The sound hits you first. It is a constant, loud, layered chorus of clicking, scraping, and chittering. It is not the sound of a thousand individuals, but the sound of a single, million-limbed organism going about its work. It is the sound of the fauna, which coat every surface that is not perfectly vertical.

They are the Carver Crabs. Each is about the size of a human hand, with a low-slung, chitinous body the same color as the basalt-glass they inhabit. They possess a dizzying number of thin, spindly legs that allow them to cling to the spires at any angle. Their heads are small, dominated by a pair of multifaceted eyes that glow with a soft internal phosphorescence, contributing to the Anvil's pervasive gloom-light. Their most prominent feature is their set of forelimbs: one is a heavy, club-like claw made of a material harder than the spires themselves; the other is a fine, needle-pointed manipulator.

They are everywhere. In their sheer density, they form a single, undulating carpet of life. They move over and around each other in a slow, constant, jostling ballet. You cannot take a step without the crunch of their bodies beneath your feet, though they are so numerous that the loss of a few is meaningless. The air is thick with their musky, organic scent.

They are tool-users. Their entire existence is a slow, methodical act of creation and maintenance. With their fine manipulators, they select tiny, harder shards of the crystalline ground. Gripping these shards, they use their club-like claws to tap them, rhythmically and precisely, against the spires. This is the source of the sound: a million tiny hammers and chisels, a percussive symphony of shaping. They are carving the spires.

This is the rule of the world. The Anvil is not static; it is a musical instrument, and the Carver Crabs are its tireless tuners. The spires are resonant. They hum with a subsonic frequency that you feel in your bones, a deep thrumming that is the voice of the mountain itself. The carving is not random; the crabs are minutely adjusting the size, shape, and thickness of the spires to alter their resonant pitch. They are collectively, instinctively, tuning the entire landscape to a specific, impossibly complex chord.

The process is glacially slow. A single spire might be worked on by a thousand generations of crabs before its note is deemed perfect. When it is, they move on, their slow work an inherited, instinctual imperative. The purpose of this grand, geological symphony is unknown. Perhaps it is a signal to something in the stars above or the clouds below. Perhaps it is a religious act. Perhaps the resonance is what drives the very growth of the spires, and they are simply tending the garden in which they live.

The world here is dark, cold, and damp. It is a single, small stage of sharp black glass, vibrating with a deep hum and alive with the overwhelming, clicking industry of its inhabitants. It is a place of perpetual work, of slow, deliberate artistry on a geological scale, performed in near-darkness at the very top of the world.
