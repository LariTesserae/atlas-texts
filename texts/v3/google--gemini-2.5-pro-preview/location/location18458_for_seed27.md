You are on the Amaranthine Vault, a high-altitude plateau so vast its edges are a matter of myth. The world is ancient, scoured down to its bones by eons of a wind that no longer blows. There are no mountains left, only the gentle, near-imperceptible swell and fall of the land, like the breathing of a sleeping god.

The ground beneath your feet is not rock or soil. It is the Phyllom, a continuous, living mat of vegetation that constitutes the entire surface of the world. It is cool to the touch, with a texture like old leather and firm sponge. Its colour is a deep, bruised purple shot through with a network of faint, pulsing silver veins. To walk upon it is to feel a deep, subsonic thrumming, the collective metabolic hum of a planet-sized organism. There is no water here. Not a drop. No rain has ever fallen, no river has ever carved a path. Life’s solvent is a thick, internal ichor, recycled with perfect efficiency within a closed system. All fluid is biological, a precious inheritance passed from one generation to the next.

This world of total vegetation is, paradoxically, a place of immense emptiness. The Phyllom stretches out in every direction, a sparse landscape. Great structures rise from it, separated by kilometres of the humming plain. These are the Scaffolding Trees, or perhaps the Spires of a city; the distinction is meaningless here. They are not built, but grown. They are monumental, genetically-encoded architecture. Some resemble colossal, petrified fungi, their caps riddled with hollows that glow with soft, internal light. Others are impossibly slender, branching towers that climb for a thousand metres, their limbs forming a lattice against the dim, bruised sky. These structures are a deep, glossy black, like obsidian, but they are alive. Their surfaces shift with slow, peristaltic motions.

The city, if it can be called that, is a loose archipelago of these bio-architectural clusters. One cluster might be a 'downtown,' a denser arrangement of thick, hollowed-out Caul-towers connected by swaying, high-altitude bridges of woven living fibre. Inside these towers, the air is thick and still, smelling of ozone, pollen, and something like warm electronics. The walls are translucent membranes, veined with the same silver fluid as the ground, pulsing with a slow, steady rhythm that serves as both light and chronometer. This is post-technological life, where computation occurs in mycorrhizal networks under the Phyllom and energy is distributed not by wires, but through the vascular system of the city-organism itself. Genetic code is the only software.

The sky is a permanent, starless twilight of indeterminate grey. The only significant light comes from below. The silver veins in the Phyllom pulse with a cool luminescence, and the 'windows' and 'streetlights' of the grown towers cast a soft, peach-coloured glow. The effect is an inverted world, where the land is brighter than the sky. Periodically, a slow, silent weather pattern will drift across the plateau: a Nutrient Fog. The Scaffolding Trees release clouds of complex spores and aerosols, a fine, glittering mist that hangs in the air for days, nourishing the more specialized life forms before settling back into the Phyllom.

This dim, cool, sparse world is deafeningly loud. The sound is not chaotic, but symphonic and structural. The subsonic hum of the Phyllom is the bass note, a constant pressure you feel in your bones. Above it, the spires and towers resonate. Many possess great, cantilevered fins of hardened plant matter that catch subtle atmospheric currents or vibrate with the world's internal pulse, emitting long, sonorous, shifting chimes. They are planetary-scale aeolian harps, groaning and singing in the twilight. The sound is so pervasive it becomes the silence; the absence of it would be terrifying.

And through this resonant landscape moves the fauna. There is life everywhere, adapted to a waterless existence. Great, six-legged grazers called Sclerites, their bodies encased in articulated, iridescent shells to prevent ichor-loss, move in slow, deliberate herds across the Phyllom. They do not eat the ground-mat but plug into specialized siphoning nodes that emerge from it, drinking the nutrient-rich sap directly. Their calls are booming, percussive clicks that echo for miles across the open plains, a way of keeping track of the herd in the vastness.

Smaller creatures scuttle and glide. Some are ambush predators that mimic the texture of the Phyllom, all but invisible until they strike. Others are symbiotic, living within the hollows of the Caul-towers, their chitinous bodies scraping and clicking against the living walls, adding a high-frequency, castanet-like layer to the world's soundscape. Flocks of winged organisms, more like leathery manta rays than birds, navigate the air between the spires, their wing-beats making a sound like tearing canvas. They feed on the Nutrient Fogs, their mouths vast, filtering nets.

Everything is in motion. The light pulses. The city-spires slowly grow and reconfigure themselves over decades. The Sclerite herds migrate in cycles dictated by the pulsing of the sap nodes. Predators and prey engage in slow, patient ballets of survival. When a creature dies, its body is not left to rot. Specialized decomposer organisms, crystalline and slow-moving, envelop the corpse, breaking it down and injecting the precious moisture and organic matter back into the veins of the Phyllom. Nothing is wasted. Death is not an end, but an immediate, orderly reassignment of resources. It is all one system, one ancient, strange, loud, and profoundly alive organism, dreaming in the endless twilight.
