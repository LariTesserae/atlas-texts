The first thing is the sound.

It is not a sound you hear with your ears alone; it is a sound you feel in your bones, a physical pressure on your skin. It is a bass note so deep and omnipresent that the concept of silence becomes an academic absurdity. This fundamental drone, a C-sharp that feels like the resonant frequency of the planet itself, is not steady. It is the canvas upon which a loud, chaotic, and yet strangely structured symphony is painted. There are percussive shocks that rattle your teeth and echo for long seconds, the sound of immense tumblers falling into place. There are high, searing whines that rise and fall in predictable cycles, like the breath of some colossal machine. There are layers of complex, grinding harmonies that shift and modulate, suggesting a purpose you cannot begin to fathom. The air is so thick with vibration that small, unsecured objects would skitter across the floor. This is a place of noise, a cathedral built of sound.

You are underground, in a space that feels both natural and entirely artificial. The scale is that of a grand nave, a single, vast chamber whose ceiling is lost in absolute darkness. The walls are not hewn rock but a seamless, dark grey material, impossibly smooth and cool to the touch, like polished obsidian. They curve gently upwards, weathered not by wind or water in the conventional sense, but by ages of resonant stress. The surface shows faint, spidery patterns of fracture, long since stabilized, like the crazing on ancient porcelain. This entire place is old, settled into its final form. It is a constructed void.

This chamber is the "city," but it is almost entirely empty. The urbanism is not in its contents, but in its very structure. Running across the floor and up the walls are impossibly thin, precise filaments of light. They are not bright, barely more than after-images, glowing in shades of cold phosphor-green and deep, pulsing amber. These filaments form vast, intricate circuits—diagrams of processes laid out on a scale of kilometers. They are the infrastructure. The patterns of light shift and flow in time with the cacophony. A percussive shock sends a ripple of amber light racing across the floor. A rising whine is mirrored by a slow brightening of green grids on a far wall. The chamber is a computer, a reactor, an engine—and the sound is its primary function, its exhalation. The light is merely a status display.

The air is warm and heavy with moisture. The titanic energies expressed as sound manifest also as heat, keeping the chamber at a constant, balmy temperature. This warm, saturated air rises, hits the distant, unseen, and relatively cooler upper shell of the cavern, and condenses. A constant, gentle rain falls in certain zones, a fine mist in others. The floor is slick with this condensation, and shallow, perfectly clear pools gather in wide, shallow depressions worn into the engineered surface. The water has a clean, metallic taste, like ozone. This is the only weather: the cycle of acoustic energy converting to thermal energy, driving a slow, perpetual system of precipitation.

Life here is exceptionally sparse, adapted to the fringes. In the dampest areas, where the mist is thickest, grow patches of what might be called vegetation. It's a chemo-luminescent mold, a velvety, grey fuzz that feeds on the mineral residue left by the evaporating water and the faint electrical potential of the floor-circuits. It glows with a faint, ghostly blue light, barely enough to illuminate its own patch of growth. Its life cycle is tied to the sound; certain resonant frequencies cause it to bloom, releasing spores that drift on the slow, warm air currents.

The fauna is rarer still. You might only glimpse it: a flicker of motion at the edge of a lighted patch. They are called Chitter-skimmers. They are low, flat creatures, perhaps the size of a human hand, with six multi-jointed legs and a body of polished, chitinous plates the same color as the floor. They have no visible eyes, navigating instead by sensing the vibrations in the floor and the subtle shifts in the all-encompassing sound. They move in impossibly fast, smooth bursts, skimming across the slick surfaces to feed on the glowing mold. Their passing is marked by a series of sharp, dry clicks, a tiny, organic addition to the overwhelming technological din.

There are no rules here for humans, because this place was not built for them. It is not hostile, merely indifferent. The air is breathable, the water drinkable, but the environment itself is an assault. The "ground" is an active component of a machine whose purpose is unknown. The "weather" is a byproduct of its function. The noise is a process, as fundamental as gravity. To exist here is to be an irrelevant speck of dust on a spinning hard drive, a microbe in the gut of a god-machine, feeling the deep, constant, and utterly alien thrum of its life.
