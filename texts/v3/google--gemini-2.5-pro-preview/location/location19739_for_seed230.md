You arrive through a cleft in the rock, a wind-carved pass that feels more like a tunnel worn smooth by ages of slow, indifferent air. The journey up was long, through foothills of crumbling scree and then onto slopes of solid, cold stone. But here, at the top, the world doesn't open up into a vista. It cups inward.

This place is a basin, perhaps a mile across, a shallow caldera of some long-extinct volcano. The rim is not a jagged crown but a worn, rounded lip of dark grey stone, like the edge of an ancient piece of pottery. The elevation is high enough that the air is thin and holds a permanent, penetrating cool, the kind that seeps into your bones not with aggression, but with persistence.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a fine grit of crushed stone, dust, and sand, the result of eons of thermal expansion and contraction. It crunches softly with each step. The terrain is not flat; it slopes gently from the rim to the center, but the slope is interrupted by a series of vast, concentric terraces, each ten or twenty feet high. These are not natural formations. They are built.

This is a city, but not in any familiar sense. There is no clutter, no chaos. The "urban" nature of the place is in its total, deliberate organization. Every surface has been worked. The terraces are faced with immense, mortarless blocks of dark basalt, fitted so perfectly that even after millennia, the seams are barely perceptible cracks. Wide, shallow steps are carved into the faces of the terraces, connecting one level to the next.

From the floor of the basin rise perhaps two dozen slender towers, also of the same dark stone. They are square in cross-section, impossibly thin, and stand in a sparse, almost random-seeming pattern. They are unadorned, save for the texture of their hand-hewn blocks and the occasional narrow slit window. There are no other buildings, only these towers and the immense, empty plazas of the terraces. It is a city of platforms and verticals, defined more by the spaces between things than by the things themselves.

The world here operates on a single, unwavering cycle. It is not a cycle of seasons, but of breath. Every day, just before the sun reaches its zenith, a thick, white fog rolls silently over the basin's rim. It does not come with wind, but rather pours in like a slow, soundless liquid, filling the caldera from the bottom up. This is the source of all moisture. The fog is so dense it muffles what little sound there is. The air becomes heavy and saturated.

As the fog touches the cold stone of the towers and terraces, it condenses. Droplets form, clinging to the dark basalt like tiny pearls. They grow, merge, and begin to run, tracing paths down the ancient stonework. Intricate, almost invisible channels are carved into the edges of the plazas, gathering this condensation. The water trickles along these shallow troughs, feeding into covered cisterns hidden within the terraces and at the bases of the towers. The sound of this process is the loudest thing in the landscape: a million tiny *tinks* and a gentle, collective hiss as the fog gives its water to the stone.

By late afternoon, the fog recedes as silently as it came, revealing a world washed clean. The stone is slick and dark, almost black, gleaming in the pale, high-altitude light. This is when the vegetation is most visible. It is incredibly sparse. Silvery-green lichens, fat with their daily drink of water, grow in feathery patches on the shaded, south-facing sides of the towers. In the crevices between paving stones, tiny, wire-like grasses grow in dense, hardy clumps, their color a deep, bruised purple. There are no trees, no shrubs, no flowers.

Life is exceptionally rare. You might go a whole day seeing nothing. Then, a sudden movement reveals a flock of slate-colored finches, almost perfectly camouflaged against the wet stone, pecking at the lichen mats. Their calls are like the chinking of small pebbles together. In the damp channels, if you look closely, you might see tiny, multi-legged creatures, no bigger than a fingernail, with iridescent carapaces. They are the grazers of this world, consuming algae that grows in the perpetual damp of the cistern overflows. There are no predators. There are no large animals.

The place is not abandoned. Its inhabitants are simply part of its stasis. They are a quiet, slow-moving people, clad in simple robes of woven grass fiber. Their technology is of the stone and bone. They carry long, light brooms made from bundled reeds to sweep the stone dust from the plazas. They use stone mallets and wedges to re-seat a block that may have shifted a fraction of an inch over a century. They do not build. They do not create. They *maintain*.

Their lives are a ritual of preservation. They ensure the water channels are clear. They scrape away any errant growth that might threaten a stone join. They move with a quiet deliberation, their soft-soled sandals making no sound on the vast, empty terraces. They are as much a part of the landscape's equilibrium as the fog and the lichen. They speak little, and when they do, their voices are low, so as not to disturb the monumental quiet.

There is no sense of progress here, or of decay. The city—this entire basin—exists in a state of perfect, frozen equilibrium. The chisel marks on the towers have been softened by ages of condensing fog, but they are not gone. The steps are worn into shallow bowls from countless feet, but they are not crumbling. The place has reached its final form and is held there, unchanging, by the daily breath of the fog and the ceaseless, simple work of its keepers. It is a monument, not to a king or a god, but to stability itself. The only sound now, as the fog recedes, is the wind, a slow, thin current moving through the high pass, and the distant, rhythmic *tock... tock... tock...* of a single stone hammer, tapping a block back into its eternal place.
