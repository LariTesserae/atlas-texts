The sky is a sheet of white light. It is not a sun, not a cloud layer, but the very firmament itself, a boundless, edgeless source of flat, shadowless illumination. The light is bright and total, bleaching colour from the world and leaving behind a silent glare. Beneath it, the air is thick, hot, and heavy with water, resting on the land like a wet wool blanket. To breathe is to drink. To stand still is to feel your skin prickle and bloom with a thousand tiny beads of condensation.

The ground is not earth. It is a mosaic of fired clay, a vast and gently undulating plain of hexagonal tiles the colour of rust and pale bone. They are perfectly fitted, their edges still sharp as if they were laid yesterday. Yet here, there is no yesterday and no tomorrow. The tiles are slick with a perpetual, mirror-like film of water that does not flow. In the lowest dips of the plain, this water collects into shallow, motionless pools, so clear and still they seem to be panes of solid glass set among the terracotta. A dropped leaf would not send out ripples; it would simply break the surface and be held, suspended in time. Nothing evaporates. Nothing drains.

This is a greenhouse made by hand, on the scale of a valley. From the tiled floor rise the "plants." They are sparse, with vast, open spaces of wet ceramic between them, but each one is a testament to impossible, lush creation. They are not flora but sculptures of flora, crafted from materials that gleam with a permanent wetness. There are groves of what look like banana trees, but their broad, tattered leaves are made of wafer-thin, razor-edged jade, each serration a perfect, sharp sawtooth. Water drips eternally from their tips, not in a random patter but in a slow, metronomic rhythm, a sound that is one of the few punctuations to the profound quiet. Elsewhere, immense ferns uncurl, their fronds fashioned from coils of verdigris copper. They do not grow; they are fixed in a posture of virile, explosive life. Fields of "grass" are blades of obsidian, each one a sharp sliver planted individually in the cracks between tiles, rising to the height of a man's waist.

The atmosphere is almost silent. There is no wind to stir the jade leaves, no rain to patter on the ceramic ground. The dominant sound is the slow, viscous dripping of condensation. A drop from a copper frond striking a tile makes a soft, resonant *tink*. A bead of water rolling off a polished obsidian blade lands in a still pool with a muffled *plup*. The air itself seems to have a sound, a low, almost subliminal hum that has no source and no variation. It is the sound of the light, or perhaps the sound of the heat itself.

Life here is abundant, but it adheres to the world's strange laws. Great, slow-moving skinks made of polished serpentine, their scales a mosaic of shimmering green, patrol the edges of the still pools. Their claws make a dry, clicking sound on the tiles. They do not hunt or eat; their purpose seems merely to move in long, repeating circuits, their unblinking eyes fixed on some point beyond the horizon. Tiny tree frogs, the colour of turquoise glaze, cling to the glass-like flowers that bloom on thorny, ceramic vines. The flowers are hollow and bell-shaped, and each one holds a single, perfect sphere of water. The frogs do not croak. They only ever cling, their damp bodies fused to the sculpture, their stillness a form of life. Flocks of what might be dragonflies, with wings of mica and bodies of twisted wire, hover in perfect, unchanging formations over the fields of obsidian grass. They do not buzz; they are utterly silent, their wings motionless yet holding them aloft against a gravity that seems to obey different rules.

The world is held in an impossible equilibrium. It is young in its sharpness, every edge uneroded, every surface unmarred. But it is ancient in its stasis. These forms were not born of geology or biology, but of a will that built a landscape and then commanded it to *be*, forever. This is not a place of processes, but of states. The heat is not a measure of energy transfer, but an inherent quality of the air. The wetness is not a phase in a water cycle, but the fixed condition of the substrate.

Reality here is thin. If you stare too long at the reflection in a motionless pool, you might see the face staring back is not yours. If you trace the patterns of the hexagonal tiles, you might find they form a circuit diagram for a machine whose function is this very landscape. The world is a thought, held without faltering. The breakdown is in the permanence itself. Life without metabolism, wetness without flow, light without a source, sound without vibration. It is a place built with primitive tools and an incomprehensible science, a static monument to a dynamic idea, beautiful, terrifying, and utterly, eternally still.
