The air is an object. It presses in, thick as heated oil, and you must push through it. It is too hot and too wet, a contradiction that resolves itself in oppressive humidity. Every surface gleams with a slick film of condensation. The light is a dim, bruised purple, as if filtering through a thousand miles of vapor and dust, and it seems to have weight, settling in pools and running sluggishly down vertical surfaces.

You stand on a broad, flat expanse of what might be obsidian or basalt, worn smooth by a billion years of nothing. It is a high plateau, but you would not know it from looking up. There is no sky, only a ceiling of roiling, self-illuminated gas that shifts in slow, majestic currents. The horizon is a distant, hazy line where the dark ground meets this low-slung firmament. The scale is immense and confusing; a formation that looks a half-mile distant might be ten, its details softened and blurred by the thick atmosphere.

This place is a city, but it is empty. It is a city of skeletal infrastructure, a metropolis of function without inhabitants.

Vast, skeletal towers of a dark, non-reflective metal rise from the plain. They are not buildings but scaffolds, frameworks for processes you cannot name. Some are latticed pylons hundreds of meters tall, vanishing into the murk above. Others are wide, arching structures like the ribs of some colossal beast, forming bridges that span chasms of pure darkness, leading from one empty platform to another. There is no glass, no ornamentation, no sign of dwelling. It is a city laid bare, its bones exposed.

Running between and through these structures is an arterial network of pipes and conduits, ranging from the width of a human arm to that of a locomotive. They are warmer than the already-hot air, and this is the source of the world's water. The super-heated gas or fluid within them causes the humid atmosphere to condense on their surfaces. The water, distilled and pure, gathers in heavy, trembling drops before falling with a soft, resonant *plink* onto the stone below. This is the only consistent sound: a constant, asynchronous dripping that echoes across the vast, quiet plazas. There are no rivers, no lakes—only this perpetual, manufactured weeping.

Because of this constant dripping and the heat, the ground is not entirely barren. There is no plant life, nothing photosynthetic. Instead, in the perpetual twilight, there are growths of thermochromatic sinter. These are mineral deposits, not living things, but they behave with a semblance of life. Where hot water drips, they form in delicate, crystalline fans that glow with a faint internal warmth. Their color is a direct report on the temperature: a deep, cool blue in quiescent pools, shifting to a vibrant, electric pink or a fiery orange where a new, hotter pulse of fluid is cycling through the system overhead. The entire city is painted in these slow, blushing and fading patterns of heat.

Occasionally, there is another sound, a non-sound, more of a pressure wave you feel in your bones than hear with your ears. A deep, sub-audible hum that causes the air to thrum and the water on your skin to prickle. It is the pulse of the city's heart, a vast geothermal or mechanical engine operating somewhere in the deep, its processes so immense and slow that its exhalations are measured in hours, not seconds. Sometimes, a great valve will release pressure from a distant tower, and a sigh of superheated steam will rise into the vaporous ceiling, momentarily illuminating the superstructure in a ghostly white before fading back into the dim purple.

Life is exceptionally rare. For days, you might see nothing. Then, a flicker of movement. On the side of a great, weeping conduit, a creature no larger than your hand skitters into view. It is a Glazewing. Its body is a shard of iridescent, smoke-colored chitin, almost perfectly camouflaged against the wet metal. It has six needle-thin legs that allow it to move across vertical and inverted surfaces with impossible speed. It does not seem to eat or drink in the traditional sense. It presses its flat, featureless head against the pipes, and you sense it is drawing something from them—energy, heat, some specific mineral leached into the condensed water. Then, as quickly as it appeared, it will dart away, a momentary splinter of life in a world of quiet machinery.

The rules of this place are simple. The ground is slick; do not lose your footing. The structures are a maze; do not get lost. The heat and humidity are constant; do not exert yourself. You are a ghost here, an observer in a vast, self-regulating system that long ago achieved its equilibrium. It is a city without citizens, a factory without workers, producing nothing but its own continuation. It is not dead, nor is it alive. It simply *is*, its processes cycling in the dim light, dripping and humming and blushing with heat, forever.
