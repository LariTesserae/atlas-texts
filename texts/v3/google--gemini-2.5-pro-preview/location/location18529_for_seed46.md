This place is a womb. It is a chamber, a geode of industry sealed miles-high within a mountain of raw, nascent stone. The mountain itself is geologically infantile, a crag of sharp, un-eroded angles thrusting into a thin, hot sky. There is no weather here to smooth its edges. From the outside, it is just a jagged peak of black and grey rock baking under a relentless sun. But within its heart is this pocket of utter strangeness.

The chamber is an ovoid, perhaps half a mile long, its walls the same raw, fractured rock as the mountain's exterior. There is no grand entrance, no tunnel worn by wind or water. To be here is to have arrived without traveling, to exist within a space that should be solid. The air is still, heavy, and searingly hot, like the inside of an oven left on for a century. It is also utterly, profoundly dry. There is no moisture. Not a single molecule of free water can be found; to breathe is to feel your lungs parch.

Illumination is total and sourceless. The concave ceiling, far above, glows with the flat, unwavering intensity of a studio light panel. There are no shadows, only a gradient of presence and absence. This unblinking light reveals the floor: a vast, seamless mosaic of interlocking hexagonal plates of a material like polished basalt. This is the engine. The floor is not static. It shifts, but so slowly that the movement is a subliminal vibration rather than a visible act. A deep, resonant hum, barely on the edge of hearing, emanates from the plates. It is the sound of immense, frictionless power. This is the first part of the industrial process: the generation of energy. The entire floor is a slow-motion piston, a geothermal dynamo of continental scale, miniaturized into this single room.

From this charged floor, the “vegetation” grows, lush but desperately sparse. They are not plants in any biological sense. They are thermovores, organisms of silica and metal that thrive on heat and raw electrical potential. They are the factory's machinery.

Great, teardrop-shaped bulbs of what looks like polished obsidian squat directly on the hexagonal plates. Each is the size of a small car, their surfaces gleaming under the overhead light. They are connected to the floor not by roots, but by thick, braided cables of a coppery metal that seem to be an integral part of both the bulb and the plate beneath. These bulbs are the primary capacitors. They draw energy from the humming floor, and they are warm to a non-existent touch, radiating a heat that makes the air around them shimmer. A fainter, higher-pitched tone joins the bass hum of the floor, the sound of energy being stored and converted.

Spaced widely between these capacitor-bulbs are the other forms of life. Here and there, a crystalline fern uncurls, its fronds made of delicate, razor-thin sheets of shimmering mica. They grow impossibly, achingly slowly from hairline cracks in the floor plates. These are the heat sinks. They draw the ferocious ambient temperature into their structures, and instead of transpiring water, they bleed reality. The air around a mica-fern is distorted, warped as if by a powerful lens. Looking through the fronds, the far wall of the chamber might appear inches away, or a bulb across the floor might seem to ripple and dissolve. They are radiators of causality, venting the system's excess weirdness as localized spatial distortions.

The final component of this sparse, industrial ecology are the vines. They are the assembly lines. Thick as an arm, they are a dull, leaden grey and have the texture of coarse lathe-work, as if turned from a solid bar of metal. They creep from the junction points where three floor-plates meet, growing upwards, seeking the raw rock of the chamber walls. They move with glacial slowness, their progress measurable over decades. Where a vine's tip touches the wall, it begins its work. The tip frays, dissolving into a "damp" patch on the rock face—not wet with water, but with a shimmering, ontological grease. From this spot, the vine begins to extrude its product. It is a thread, fine as silk and blacker than any physical substance. It is a filament of solid, stable vacuum. A strand of pure absence. The threads hang down in long, motionless catenaries, accumulating over millennia, a slow harvest of nothingness gathered from the raw energy of the cosmos.

There is no animal life. Nothing crawls, nothing flies, nothing scurries. The only movement is the imperceptible heave of the floor, the patient unfurling of a crystal fern, the stately growth of a vine. The landscape is a closed system, a perpetual motion machine that runs on impossible physics. The rules are simple: heat and electricity are sustenance. Life is a process of industrial fabrication. Lushness is not a measure of water, but of energy conversion efficiency. Weirdness is a waste product to be managed and vented.

The atmosphere is a trio of low frequencies—the bass thrum of the floor, the whine of the capacitors, and a high, ultrasonic hiss from the reality-lensing ferns. Otherwise, it is a tomb of silence. It is a place of becoming, a geological embryo that is not building a world of rock and water, but a single, purposeful machine. The purpose is the production of those impossible black threads. Why a factory would exist in a pocket universe high in a newborn mountain to manufacture solidified emptiness is not a question that has an answer here. The factory simply *is*. It is hot, it is bright, it is quiet, and it is patiently, eternally at work.
