This is a place of height and of age. The world is a rooftop. You stand on a peak, but it is not a sharp and violent fang of new rock. It is a worn, ancient knuckle of a mountain, ground down by a billion years of wind that no longer blows. The ranges roll away in every direction, a sea of summits reaching not for the sky but settling into themselves, weary and final. The scale is immense; valleys are chasms that hold the curvature of the world, and the next range over is a different shade of distance, a haze on the edge of sight. The sun is a white, clean disk in a sky the colour of deep, cold violet. The light is sharp, unsoftened by humidity, casting shadows that are black and absolute.

The first oddness is the ground. It is not stone. Underfoot, it has a yielding firmness, like walking on hard leather or a floor of compressed insect shells. The colour is a layered, complex palette of iridescent browns, ochres, and blacks, slick with a faint, mineral sheen. This is a world of chitin. The mountains are not geological, they are biological. They are the accumulated, compacted, and fossilized remains of a cycle of life that has run for eons. The great peaks are the fused carapaces of unimaginable titans from a forgotten era; the scree slopes are the shed skins and shattered limbs of a billion generations. The very dust is a fine, chitinous powder. There is no water here. There has never been. Life is not based on it. The solvent is some internal, ammoniated ichor, long since dried and crystallized within the countless bodies that form the landscape.

And the landscape is not dead. This is the source of the density, the overwhelming sense of presence. Every surface is covered. The ground teems with a silent, motionless carpet of arthropodal life. They are what passes for fauna here. Creatures ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a thumb, packed so closely their carapaces touch. They are anchorites, locked in a timeless torpor. Their forms are infinitely varied: some are trilobite-like, their segmented shells clamped to the ground; others are spiny, beetle-like things with legs tucked beneath them; still others are whorled and turreted like sea-less snails. They are layered, one generation clinging to the backs of the last, forming a living strata. They do not move. They do not eat. They do not breathe. They are in a state of permanent, stable equilibrium, drawing some imperceptible energy from the planet's core or the weak radiation of the violet sky. The air itself is thick not with smog, but with spores and microscopic, dormant life-forms, a chittering dust that coats your tongue and films your eyes. This is the overwhelming fauna: a world-spanning blanket of creatures in a state of suspended animation, a living extinction.

The only sound is your own. In the utter stillness, you can almost hear the subliminal hum of the planet, the faint, dry whisper of a billion tiny legs making infinitesimal adjustments over the course of a century. It's a silence so profound it feels like a pressure on the eardrums.

Here and there, something serves as vegetation. It is not green. Sparse, crystalline structures rise from the chitinous ground. Some resemble ferns made of smoky quartz, their fronds catching the light in sharp, geometric facets. Others are tall, thin stalks of what looks like blue-black obsidian, capped with a puffball of ghostly white filaments that do not stir. These are the "spore-stalks," the final reproductive effort of the planet's fungal equivalent, releasing their seed on a thousand-year cycle. They do not grow; they accrete, molecule by molecule.

Scattered across this ancient, living terrain are the built things, the only true sign of past dynamism. They are not structures you could inhabit. On the flat-topped mesas and wide saddles between peaks, you find vast, shallow carvings. Glyphs. Simple, geometric, and deeply alien, they are scraped into the carapace-ground. Spirals that coil for a hundred yards, grids of interlocking triangles, long, wavering lines that mimic the paths of celestial bodies. They were made with primitive tools, hand-scraped and chipped from the living bedrock by some mobile, thinking ancestor of the torpid creatures that now blanket the world. These marks are the culture, the last will and testament of a people who, seeing the Great Quiet coming, the final settling of their world into stasis, chose to write a final thought into the skin of their planet before joining it in its eternal slumber.

This is the central breakdown of reality here: the geology is zoology. The rock is flesh. The air is life. The world is not a backdrop for living things; the world *is* the living thing, a single, planetary entity that has lived, thought, and now sleeps. To be here is to be a foreign microbe on the skin of a dormant god. Nothing changes. The sun will circle, the light will cut, the shadows will crawl, but the spore-stalks will not release, the anchorites will not stir, the mountains will not erode. You are in a museum at the end of time, where all the exhibits are still technically alive. And the silence is the sound of their perfect, unending peace.
