This is the Loom.

You stand on a platform of perforated iron, slick with a fine, green-black film of algae. The air is cool and heavy, tasting of ozone, wet metal, and the loamy scent of decay. It’s a struggle to draw a full breath, as if the atmosphere itself is already saturated. Every surface weeps. Water doesn't fall as rain; it materializes as a perpetual, chilling sweat on the million surfaces of the incomprehensible structure around you. Droplets collect on the undersides of girders, tremble, and fall with a soft *tock* onto a bed of moss below, or a *tink* onto another metal plate, or disappear silently into the deep chasms between the platforms. The light is a uniform, shadowless grey, the source unclear—perhaps a diffuse glow from the panels and conduits that line the superstructure far above, or perhaps this is all the daylight that can penetrate the thick, permanent cloud deck of this high-altitude plateau.

The world is made of metal, ceramic, and wire, a colossal, three-dimensional gridwork that extends in every direction, filling a valley from wall to wall, climbing its sides, and disappearing into the mist. There is no natural ground. The very concept feels alien here. You are in the upper strata of a built environment so total and so ancient that its original purpose is a geological mystery. The structure is old, weathered into a state of stable grandeur. Rivets are haloed with rust that has bled in long, elegant streaks down granite-composite pylons. Edges of iron plates have been rounded and softened by ages of flowing water and creeping moss. It has the settled permanence of a mountain range.

Yet, it is utterly, ceaselessly turbulent.

The Loom is named for its constant, silent motion. Entire sections of the grid—walkways, planters, gantries—are in a state of perpetual, chaotic rearrangement. A platform a hundred yards away, laden with a dense grove of ferns, might begin to slide silently to the left, revealing a chasm of whirring mechanics below before another platform, this one carrying what looks like a forest of enormous, pale fungi, glides in to take its place. The motion is not jerky or violent; it is a smooth, inexorable shuffling on a planetary scale. It is the quiet energy of an anthill, the silent, purposeful chaos of a living circuit board rearranging itself. The sounds are hushed, swallowed by the sheer scale and the damp air. There is the low, pervasive hum of immense electrical currents. The whisper of hydraulic fluid through miles of pipes. The soft, metallic *shhhhffffff* of a massive plate sliding on magnetic rails. The occasional, distant *click-clack* of a relay the size of a small house. It is the sound of a factory operating at full capacity, but with all the noise muffled by an ocean of water.

This metal skeleton is host to a wilderness. Vegetation is everywhere, a riot of life in defiance of the sterile construction. It is not so much growing *on* the structure as it is integrated *into* it. Every horizontal surface not in immediate, grinding motion is covered in a thick carpet of sphagnum moss, liverworts, and feathery ferns. They create miniature, sodden landscapes, miniature bogs on plates of steel. Larger vegetation—spindly, pale-barked trees with broad, waxy leaves, and thick, coiled vines—grows from designated "planter" modules, deep cubical containers that are themselves part of the shifting grid. These planters are shuttled about, slotted into new configurations, their resident flora forming temporary groves and then being dismantled again. The density is absolute. Metal gives way to moss, moss to hanging vines, vines to the dark openings between platforms, and in that darkness you see the shimmer of more wet metal and more plants. There is no empty space.

There are no animals. No birdsong punctuates the mechanical hum. No insects buzz in the humid air. The silence of the fauna is more profound than the quiet of the machinery. To walk here for hours is to see only the slow, alien life of the plants and the turbulent, automated life of the structure. You might, if you press your face close to a moss bed, find minuscule, translucent springtails, or see the silvery slime-trail of a slug that feeds on algae. But the world is devoid of complex animal life. The ecosystem is one of plant and machine, each feeding the other—the machine pumping nutrient-rich water to the plants, the plants filtering the air and stabilizing the ancient framework with their roots.

To exist here is to accept a single, unwritten rule: the floor is not reliable. You learn to read the slight vibrations, the subtle change in the hum that precedes a section's movement. You learn to step from platform to platform with a light-footed resignation, knowing that the path you just took may no longer exist a minute from now. You are a temporary presence, a flea on the back of a vast, sleeping, dreaming automaton. Looking out, you see this process replicated to the horizon, a valley-wide engine of pointless, beautiful, silent industry, forever weaving and unweaving a tapestry of metal and leaf.
