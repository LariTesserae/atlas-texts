The dark is a presence, not an absence. It has weight and texture. It is the first and last thing you perceive. The air is thick enough to drink, heavy with a heat that feels ancient and internal, a fever radiating not from a single source but from the very bones of the place. It’s a wet heat, clinging to the skin, beading on any exposed surface. You are breathing water. The air carries the smell of hot copper, of ozone, and of something like wet, sterile dust.

You stand in a cathedral of slumbering industry, a single, vast chamber deep beneath the surface. There is no soil, no living rock. The floor is a grid of massive, dark tiles, some vitrified ceramic, some a dull, non-reflective metal, all worn perfectly smooth by ages of nothing. The seams between them are flawless, yet a faint, slick moisture weeps from them, catching the non-light in oily sheens.

From this floor rises a forest. It is a forest of pillars, so dense that navigating it is a matter of turning sideways and sliding through the gaps. They are not columns holding up a roof, but pistons. Each is the circumference of a giant tree, fashioned from alloys of iron and unknown, heavier elements. Their surfaces are dark, patinated with a rainbow of heat-tempered blues, purples, and blacks, sweating the same slick condensation as the floor. They are warm to the touch, radiating a steady, oppressive heat that makes the air shimmer in the gloom.

This is a machine, and the entire chamber is its body. The ceiling, lost in the oppressive darkness far above, is not a vault of stone but a complex lattice of cooling fins and what look like enormous, inverted bronze bells. From these, a single drop of distilled water falls now and then, taking long seconds to descend. The sound of its impact on a piston head below—a soft, resonant *tink*—is an event, a punctuation mark in the profound quiet.

For it is quiet. The hum of this place is so low it is felt more than heard, a vibration that works its way into your bones, a constant, patient thrum. The only other steady sound is a faint, rhythmic hissing, the exhalation of pressure from unseen valves. The great pistons move, but their cycle is geological. You might lean against one, feel its immense, latent power, and only realize after an hour that its position relative to its neighbor has shifted by a mere hand's breadth. A slow, deep *thump* echoes through the chamber at intervals of minutes or hours, the sound of a great valve seating itself somewhere in the hidden workings. This is not the frantic energy of a human factory; it is the patient, inexorable metabolism of a leviathan.

Life here is an afterthought, a footnote to the engineering. There are no plants. The only thing approaching vegetation is a sparse, leprous growth of chemo-synthetic lichen. It appears in pale, faint patches on the cooler joints of pipes and on the undersides of gantries far overhead. It glows with a barely-perceptible phosphorescence, a cold, dead light that does nothing to illuminate the space, only to outline its own spectral form. It is said to feed on heat differentials and the metallic dust that very, very slowly settles out of the recycled air.

Where there is a producer, there is a consumer. In the slick of condensation on the floor, you might spot the glinting, segmented body of a millipede made of what looks like spun glass, its countless legs moving in a silent, rippling wave. On the warmer piston casings, armored mites the size of a thumbnail crawl with excruciating slowness, their carapaces the same color as the heat-blued metal they inhabit. They are rare, fleeting glimpses of an ecology built on waste heat and mineral residue.

There is a rule to this place: it is self-contained. The air, ancient and tasting of metal, has been circulating through these systems for millennia, filtered through basalt wool and scrubbed by electrified grates. The water that drips is endlessly reclaimed. This place was not built to produce a commodity for the outside world; it was built to *be*. Its function is its existence. Perhaps it is a geothermal regulator, a planetary governor whose slow, steady breaths prevent some greater catastrophe. Or perhaps its builders simply worshipped a god of patience, of slow cycles and immense, quiet power, and this is their eternal, mechanical prayer. Standing within it, feeling the slow pulse of its life, it is difficult to tell the difference.
