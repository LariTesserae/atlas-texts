This world is a sound.

Before sight, before touch, there is the noise. It is a physical pressure, a full-body concussion that never ends. It is the composite roar of a billion sources, a deafening, grinding, chittering, fracturing symphony that is the baseline state of reality here. There is no such thing as quiet. The concept does not exist. The air itself, a thick, warm, ammoniac fog heavy with glittering mineral dust, seems to vibrate with the sheer sonic density. To be here is to be immersed in a maelstrom of sound that feels like it could shatter bone.

This is the Resonant Cavity, a world below the surface. There is no sky, only a higher, more distant layer of the same living chaos. The space is vast, the size of a mountain range hollowed out, but it feels claustrophobic, packed to bursting. The "ground" is not stone or soil, but a perpetually growing, embryonic substrate of life. Think of a forest and a coral reef and a crystal geode fused together and given a furious, chaotic metabolism. This is the Geoflora, a form of life that is both plant and geology.

There is no water. Life here is lithovoric, subsisting on the mineral dust that saturates the atmosphere and the raw, crystalline structures of the Geoflora itself. The warmth is not solar, but the waste heat of this planet-sized biological engine, a constant, humid warmth that makes the dry air feel paradoxically slick against the skin.

The terrain is the living body of the world-plant. Great, twisting boles of fibrous quartz, fifty meters thick, rise from the "floor" and branch into a ceiling of interlocking crystalline lattices that shed a constant, stuttering, actinic glare. This is the light source: a system-wide bioluminescence that runs through the Geoflora like a feverish circulatory system. The light is not gentle; it is a clinical, blue-white pulse that surges and dims with the chaotic rhythms of growth, casting a billion frantic shadows. The ground underfoot is a crust of younger, budding growths—nodules of milky agate, razor-sharp flats of obsidian-like material, and soft, spongy masses of chalky fiber that compress with a dry, crunching sound.

This is a world under construction. The "erosion" here is growth. You can watch the landscape form. A new crystalline trunk, a "screaming spire," will extrude from the floor over the course of an hour, its growth accompanied by a high-pitched shearing sound that cuts through the bass rumble of the wider cavity. Entire cliffsides of mature crystal will suddenly fracture and "calve" under the pressure of new growth behind them, collapsing in a slow-motion avalanche of shattering life that kicks up fresh clouds of nutritive dust. The entire environment is a turbulent, hyperactive process of creation.

And it is overwhelmingly full. Every surface, every volume of air, is teeming. The fauna are as waterless as the flora. Rivers of iridescent, chitin-shelled scuttlers, each the size of a human hand, flow in shimmering tides across the landscape, their million-fold clicks adding to the cacophony. They scrape algae-like mineral films from the crystal growths with mandibles of hardened silicate. Swarms of glass-winged stridulators, too numerous to count, fill the air, their wings beating not for flight but to generate the resonant frequencies they use to find mates and shatter softer food sources. Their collective drone is a saw-wave that vibrates in your teeth. Larger creatures, armored quadrupeds with carapaces of layered mica, bulldoze through the younger growths, their passage marked by destruction and the grinding roar of their own massive bodies. They are less animals and more a mobile, destructive geology. Life and death are a frantic, constant exchange. Hatching-geodes, basketball-sized crystalline eggs, burst open with a percussive pop, releasing dozens of new scuttlers, while the fallen carapaces of the dead are quickly overgrown by the ravenous Geoflora.

Amidst this impossible chaos, there are faint marks of intention. This is the work of The Hushed. They are beings rarely seen, slender and tall, with soft, sound-dampening skin. They must have evolved a way to nullify the noise or to perceive the world through vibration and pressure alone. Their structures are not built, but carved. Using primitive tools—heavy picks fashioned from the femurs of the mica-beasts, and sharp flake-knives chipped from obsidian growths—they create temporary shelters. You might find a perfectly smooth hemispherical niche carved into the side of a quartz bole, its entrance covered by a crudely woven tapestry of shed insect wings that glitters in the pulsing light. Inside, the walls are covered in repeating, intricate patterns, spirals and lattices scraped into the living crystal. Are they maps? Prayers? A way of focusing the mind against the sensory onslaught?

Their presence is scattered and fleeting. The world is actively hostile to permanence. A carving that took a generation to complete might be consumed in a day by a new "bloom-front" of Geoflora. A shelter might be obliterated by a calving event. The Hushed live in the brief pauses, the momentary lulls in this world's furious becoming. They are a whisper in a hurricane, and their existence is a testament to the stubborn, quiet insistence of a mind in a world of pure, screaming life.
