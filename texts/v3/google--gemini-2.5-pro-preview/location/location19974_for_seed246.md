The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not a collection of noises but a single, monolithic presence. It is a deep, resonant hum that seems to emerge not from a source but from the very substance of the world. It has texture. You can feel it in your jawbone, a sympathetic vibration that ached for the first hour and has now become a part of your own body's thrum. Layered within this fundamental drone are a million glass-bell chimes, a constant, cascading music without melody. It is the sound of a cathedral made of singing crystal, played by a relentless wind.

This place is a city, but it was not built. It is a single, room-like plateau, perhaps a mile across, defined by a gentle, downward curve at its edges that falls away into unseen depths. From this floor, structures rise. They are not towers or blocks in a familiar sense. They look like the fruiting bodies of some unimaginable mycelial network, or the bone structures of leviathans that died upright. Fluted, organic columns swell and merge, creating arches and vaults open to the sky. The surfaces are not stone, but something smoother, with the semi-translucent, milky glaze of fired porcelain, patterned in soft, concentric rings of grey and white. There are no right angles. Every line has been softened by an erosion so profound and ancient it can only be called 'final.' The city has slumped into its last, most stable form. The tops of the tallest structures, perhaps three hundred feet high, are not peaks but rounded, worn-down nubs, like knuckle bones scoured by eons of wind.

The air is thin and shockingly cold. It carries no scent but the metallic purity of altitude and ozone. Yet despite the cold, the city weeps. The air's meager moisture finds the even colder porcelain-like material and condenses in a constant, silent process. Every curved surface is slick with a film of water. Tiny rivulets, governed by ancient and complex topography, trace paths downward, gathering in an almost imperceptible network of channels carved into the city's floor before disappearing into hairline cracks. There are no pools, no standing water, only this pervasive, clinging dampness.

And because of the water, there is life. The ground plane is mostly bare, swept clean by the resonating winds, but the vertical surfaces—the walls of the city—are aggressively, fantastically lush. They are not merely green; they are a tapestry of a thousand cold-loving plants. Immense, velvet pillows of phosphorescent moss, some the size of small cars, cling to the weeping walls, glowing with a soft internal light of their own. They are fat and swollen with water. From these moss-beds hang armored ferns, their fronds black and glossy like articulated insect legs. Patches of lichen, as brittle and sharp as shattered ceramic, grow in fractal bursts of ochre and blood-red. The vegetation grows in discrete, vertical gardens, separated by vast, empty expanses of the weeping, humming porcelain. It is lush and empty at once.

There are no animals. No birds call against the drone. No insects buzz. No tracks mar the damp, bare floor between the structures. The ecology of this place is vertical, clinging, and sessile. Life does not move; it grows. Its great predator is time, its pace is geological. The only dynamism is the cycle of condensation and the overwhelming, active presence of the sound.

The light is normal, unfiltered daylight, falling from a pale, high sky. It does not warm. It only illuminates. It strikes the glazed surfaces and creates a million points of flat, white glare, making the city hard to look at directly. It filters through the hanging gardens of fern and moss, dappling the lower reaches in a cold, green-gold gloom.

This is a city of organism-architecture, a settlement built by biology for a purpose now forgotten or concluded. The loudness is not an event; it is a condition of being. The hum is the structure's intrinsic resonance, its voice or its breath, amplified by the wind that pours through its hollows. The chimes are the sound of a million water droplets falling from mossy eaves onto the hard surfaces below, each drop striking a note that becomes part of the endless, mindless chorus. It is not decayed; it is complete. It has reached the end of its life cycle, and that end is a state of perpetual, loud, cold, damp, beautiful stasis. To be here is to stand inside the ancient, empty, and singing skull of a god.
