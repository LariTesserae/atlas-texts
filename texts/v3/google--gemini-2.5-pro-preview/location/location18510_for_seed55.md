This is a basin at the bottom of the world. The pressure here is a constant, physical presence, a weight that is not felt in the bones but in the very substance of the water, which seems thicker, heavier, and more present than liquid ought tobe. It is a numbing, absolute cold, a degree or two above freezing, where chemical reactions slow to a geological crawl and time itself feels syrupy and stretched. There is no surface, no sky; there is only the deep.

The darkness is near total, a dense and liquid black. But it is not empty. Faint, ghostly light drifts in the abyssal quiet. It is not sunlight, nor starlight, nor fire, but a cold, biological luminescence, the last echo of energy in a world of profound stillness.

This light reveals the ground, which is not ground. There is no sand, no stone, no tectonic plate. The floor of this world-ocean is a single, contiguous, artificial construction. It is a pavement of incomprehensible scale, a landscape of hewn and fitted forms that stretches beyond any horizon. The materials are simple, almost primitive: cyclopean blocks of dark, vitrified basalt; fired clay tiles the size of plaza stones; and vast, poured structures of a pumice-like conglomerate, now porous and pitted with age.

The architecture is low and massive. Wide, flattened ziggurats spread for miles, their steps now softened and rounded into gentle, weeping slopes. Broad causeways, built for processions of giants, connect nothing to nothing, disappearing into the gloom. Great plinths that might once have held statues or spires are now just empty, monolithic tables. Everything is made, but it was made so long ago that it has become its own kind of geology. This is erosion of a unique kind: not the sharp carving of wind and river, but the slow, patient dissolution of a billion years of crushing pressure and cold chemistry. Edges have become curves. Flat surfaces are now subtly concave, sagging under their own immense, patient weight. The basalt is friable at the corners, the clay soft as old leather. The world is settling into its final, most stable form.

Upon this silent, man-made substrate, an entire ecology flourishes. This is the source of the light. The vegetation is fantastically lush, adapted to the crushing dark. There are no delicate flowers or broad leaves. Instead, vast forests of *Spire-reeds* grow in dense, competitive thickets. Each reed is a tough, cartilaginous stalk, grey-black in color, that rises thirty or forty feet. Their entire length is dotted with tiny, pulsing nodes of pale blue-green phosphorescence, and a forest of them creates a false starfield, a galaxy of living light points swaying in the imperceptible currents.

Every surface is covered in *Anchor-moss*, a thick, velvety carpet of dark purple filaments that thrives on the slow decay of the ancient architecture, its root-like structures patiently mining the silicates from the basalt. In places, sprawling, net-like organisms called *Grave-Lattices* have grown, intricate webs of living fiber that hang like curtains between the monoliths, each intersection of the web a tiny, winking spark of gold. The combined light is just enough to cast the softest of shadows, to give shape to the immensity.

There is no animal life. No fish dart between the reeds, no crustaceans scour the pavement. The world is utterly devoid of movement beyond the botanical and the hydrological. The only dynamic process is the slow life cycle of the flora. A Spire-reed, after a life of centuries, will finally lose its integrity, and over the course of a day, it will lean, buckle, and fold, sinking with ponderous grace to the floor. Its light will fade over weeks, and its substance will be slowly, methodically consumed by the Anchor-moss. The water is thick with the silty detritus of this decay, a constant, gentle snow of organic matter that clouds the already dark water and limits visibility to a few hundred yards.

The silence is the rule of the world. It is not a dead silence, but a quietude so profound it becomes a presence. There is the occasional, impossibly deep groan as a vast structural element settles a millimeter further into the soft clay beneath it, a sound that travels for miles through the dense water. There is the near-subliminal hiss of the currents navigating the thick forests of reeds. And there is the constant, soft, wet rustle of the Grave-Lattices, swaying like cosmic shrouds. It is the sound of a world holding its breath.

This is a built place, a tomb or a foundation for something that is long gone, or perhaps never was. Its purpose is lost. Its creators are absent, their tools simple, their labor unimaginable. Now it has been submerged for eons, a sunken city the size of a continent, slowly being consumed by a cold, dark, and silent garden of its own making. To be here is to be in the last, quietest room of existence, a place where everything has already happened, and all that is left is the slow, faintly-lit process of becoming ruins.
