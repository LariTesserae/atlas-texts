This place is a city, but it is also a single organism, a reef of stone and life clinging to the summit of a needle-mountain. They call it the Atrium. The peak itself is an impossible geological feature, a spire of black, vitreous obsidian-like rock so ancient and hard it has been worn smooth by eons of wind that no longer blows. It stabs upwards into the near-vacuum of the highest atmosphere, but its summit does not break into an open sky.

Instead, it terminates inside a vast, world-sized geode. The "sky" is the concave ceiling of this cavern, miles above, a dawning and dusking dome of faintly luminous, amethyst-colored crystal. The light is perpetual twilight, a soft, purple-grey gloom that renders sharp shadows into soft, hazy things. There is no day or night, only a slow, centuries-long pulse as the great crystals overhead brighten and fade in some incomprehensible geological cycle. The air is thick, warm, and heavy, smelling of hot stone, ozone, and the strange, dry perfume of the flora. It is an entirely sealed environment.

There is no water here. Not a drop. The concept is a myth, a word from a forgotten origin story. Life has adapted. The "vegetation" is a form of crystalline life that grows directly from the rock. It is lush and dense, a petrified forest of impossible forms. Silicate vines, thick as a man's arm and covered in razor-sharp glass thorns, wrap around the carved architecture. Great, fan-like "ferns" of mica flake and shimmer in the dim light, their fronds clattering softly against each other. On flatter terraces grow gardens of "stone-blossom," which are not soft petals but intricate, fractal explosions of calcite and fluorite that slowly "bloom" over the course of a decade. They do not drink water; their roots are filaments that etch directly into the mountain's substance, drawing out mineral sustenance with glacial patience.

The city is carved *from* the spire's summit, not built upon it. It is a dense, vertical metropolis crammed into a space no larger than a great cathedral. Every surface is worked. The architecture is a testament to millennia of patient, primitive craft. Terraces, balconies, and chambers are gouged from the black rock. Buildings are layered one atop the other, a honeycomb of dwellings connected by a dizzying web of bridges made from woven glassvine fibre and planks of petrified fungal-wood. The technology is simple: obsidian chisels, basalt hammers, hand-cranked grinding wheels. The process of carving a new room can take a generation. The city grows inward and downward, a slow coral of habitation. The deepest and oldest levels are polished smooth by the passage of countless bodies, the original carving marks lost to time.

This place is loud. The sound is a constant, overwhelming symphony of friction. The primary noise is the chittering, a high-pitched, multi-layered hum produced by the abundant fauna. Millions of insect-like creatures, with carapaces of polished stone and iridescent shell, swarm over the crystal flora, their faceted legs scraping against the silicate leaves. They are the base of the food chain. Larger creatures, like six-legged, gecko-like grazers, crawl vertically up the city walls, their sucker-padded feet making soft peeling sounds as they move, their rasping tongues scraping nutrients from the mineral mosses. The air is filled with the whirring of their wing-cases, the clicks of their mandibles, and the territorial calls that sound like tuning forks being struck against stone.

Beneath this biological cacophony is the sound of the city itself. In the stable, echoing air of the great cavern, every sound carries. The distant, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of a carver's hammer, the scrape of a clay pot being dragged across a stone floor, shouted conversations echoing from one balcony to another, the percussive music of bone-flutes and stone-drums—it all blends into a dense, ever-present auditory fog.

The terrain underfoot is never soft. It is stone, worn to a dull polish, covered in a fine layer of mineral dust and the shed flakes of the crystal plants. There is no soil, only crevices where this dust collects. The world is built for the sure-footed. A fall is a fall of hundreds or thousands of feet down the sheer sides of the spire, into the silent, waiting darkness below the city's edge.

Life here is slow. The rules of the world are patience and persistence. Growth, whether of a crystal plant or a family's living space, is measured in lifetimes. The inhabitants have a deep, geological sense of time. They are part of the mountain. Their slow work, their dense lives, and their loud, vibrant ecosystem are all powered by the dry, mineral heart of the ancient rock, all sealed beneath a sky of silent, slow-pulsing crystal.
