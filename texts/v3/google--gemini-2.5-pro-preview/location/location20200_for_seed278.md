The silence is the first thing, and the last. It is not the absence of sound, but a presence in itself—a low, resonant quiet that has mass and texture. It is the sound of a universe holding its breath. The air, thin and tasting faintly of ozone and cold metal, does little to carry noise. What little sound exists is absorbed by the vastness, swallowed by the immense, dim quiet.

This is a tableland that has forgotten its own edges. A plateau of incomprehensible scale, so high that the sky is a permanent, bruised twilight, and the sun is a tarnished silver coin, distant and offering no warmth. The cold is a constant, patient pressure, a state of being rather than a temperature. It settles into the bedrock, making the ground feel like the hull of a derelict starship.

The terrain is ancient, smoothed by erosive forces that concluded their work eons ago. There are no sharp peaks, no jagged canyons. Instead, there are long, impossibly gentle swales and broad, shallow basins that might take a lifetime to cross. The ground is not soil or rock in a familiar sense, but a sintered dust of metallic greys and blacks, compacted to the hardness of ceramic. In places, it's covered by a film of what looks like weathered, iridescent slag, the byproduct of a process so old it has become geology.

Water is a memory, or perhaps a promise. There are no rivers, no lakes. Moisture exists as a transient state. For long periods, the land is bone-dry. Then, a slow shift in the imperceptible atmospheric tides will cause condensation to form. A fine, greasy rime crawls up the gentle slopes, tracing the contours of the land in shivering silver. In the deepest basins, this condensation might pool, forming ephemeral, shallow tarns of heavy, mineral-laden water that reflect the dim sky with a leaden sheen. These pools are still, silent, and freeze from the bottom up. They may persist for a century before slowly sublimating back into the thin air.

Vegetation is a technicality. Clinging to the shaded sides of the slag fields, or blooming at the temporary edge of a frozen pool, are sparse colonies of lichen. They are not green, but a spectrum of deep, metallic blues and burnished coppers. They do not grow so much as accrete, crystallizing out of the mineral dust and the oily condensation, spreading in slow, geometric patterns like frost on a windowpane.

This world is not empty. The emptiness is merely the space between the inhabitants. The fauna is the landscape.

From a distance, what appears to be a low, serrated mountain range on the horizon may, over the course of a week, reveal itself to be moving. These are the Harvesters. They are beings of geological scale, their bodies a fusion of mineral and mechanism. A single entity might be a hundred miles long, a moving ridge line of articulated plates of basalt and pitted, dark metal. They crawl on thousands of legs, each one the size of a tower, moving with a slowness that is nearly imperceptible. They are not built; they are. They have always been.

Their silence is the most terrifying thing about them. A creature of such mass should shake the world with every step, but their movements are cushioned by immense, complex hydraulics that emit only a deep, subsonic hum—a vibration felt in the bones and teeth rather than heard with the ears. They graze on the planet's crust itself, their vast "mouths" gaping maws of grinding plates that pulverize the slag-rock and dust, taking it in. They are not alive in any biological sense; they are planetary-scale refineries. Their existence is the "industrial" process of this world.

Their presence is overwhelming. While the space between them is vast, to be in the path of one is to witness the slow, silent end of a world. But they are not malicious. They are as mindless and as purposeful as tides.

And they are not alone. Living on, in, and around them are the lesser fauna. Swarms of smaller metallic beings, like dust-mites the size of boulders, scuttle across their vast carapaces, cleaning, maintaining, polishing. These are the source of the intermittent moisture. Their internal processes vent a constant, fine mist of warm, oily exhaust. When a Harvester passes, it leaves a long, temporary weather front in its wake—a zone of elevated humidity where the copper-blue lichens flare into brief, crystalline life and the tarns appear in its colossal footprints.

There are no structures here, because the inhabitants *are* the structures. There is no technology, because the inhabitants *are* the technology. They are self-replicating, self-maintaining industrial engines that have achieved a state of perfect, slow-motion equilibrium with their world. They are eroding it, processing it, and excreting it as the very dust and slag they walk upon. The cycle is slow, patient, and has been turning for longer than the mountains of younger worlds have existed. To stand in the vast, silent space between them is to feel like a mote of dust, waiting for the slow, quiet, industrial tide to turn.
