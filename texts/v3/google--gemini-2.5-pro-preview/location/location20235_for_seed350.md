The first sensation is not of wetness, but of pressure and sound. You are submerged in a medium thicker than water, warm as blood, and it presses on you from all sides, a constant, firm embrace. The sound is a physical presence, a deafening, complex roar that vibrates through the fluid and into your bones. It is the sound of a world-sized engine tearing itself apart and rebuilding itself simultaneously.

This is the Churn. The liquid is not water, but a kind of nutrient-dense, semi-sentient effluvium, impossibly saturated with potential. It is murky, and visibility is a matter of feet, not yards. Dim, ambient light pulses from unseen sources deep within the gloom, casting everything in a shifting, bruised twilight of violet and ochre.

Through the thick, soupy atmosphere, shapes emerge. This is a low basin, a vast, submerged valley floor. But the floor is not mud or sand. It is a fine, metallic silt that glitters where the faint light catches it. From this silt, things grow. Not mountains of stone, but immense, angular formations of raw industry. A pylon, miles high, materializes from the gloom, its surface impossibly sharp, uneroded, as if it had crystallized from the fluid moments ago. It is trussed with lattices of what looks like oxidized iron and lattices of something that is not quite copper. There is no sense of purpose to its structure; it is the platonic ideal of a support, supporting nothing.

And on it, the vegetation. It is lush, suffocatingly so. Thick, rubbery vines, the diameter of pythons, coil around the steel beams. Their surfaces are not bark but braided sheathes of fine, conductive wire. Instead of leaves, they sprout clusters of what look like glass vacuum tubes, each glowing with a soft, internal orange filament. They pulse in rhythm with a low, resonant hum that adds a steady bass note to the cacophony. Great, fan-like ferns unfurl, their fronds made of perforated metal sheeting, catching the slow, turbulent currents. They creak and groan as they sway, a sound like a thousand rusty hinges. Fleshy, fungal bulbs swell from the joints of machinery, their skin patterned with the precise geometry of circuit boards.

This entire biome is dense, layered, and chaotic. You cannot move without brushing against something—a warm, vibrating vine, the cold and perfectly sharp edge of a newly-formed gear the size of a house, a soft patch of moss that seems to be woven from optical fiber, leaking pale blue light.

The sound is a multi-layered hell. The dominant frequency is a deep, grinding roar of tectonic friction, the sound of the world's very foundations being forged and fractured. Beneath this is the high-pitched shriek of metal shearing, of immense pressures finding sudden release. There are percussive, rhythmic impacts like cosmic hammers striking an anvil, sending shockwaves that you feel in your chest. And woven through it all is the electrical crackle and hum of the mechanoflora, a million tiny, nonsensical processes firing at once. The sheer volume is enough to induce a trance, to erase thought.

There is no animal life. Nothing swims, nothing crawls, nothing burrows. The incredible energy of this place is not consumed or cycled by fauna. There is only the frantic, pointless growth. The turbulence is constant. A structure that was a stable landmark a moment ago might begin to dissolve, its sharp edges blurring, its substance returning to the nutritive broth. Elsewhere, a new form will precipitate out of the liquid in a violent crystallization, a lattice of beams or a perfectly-formed turbine engine appearing in a flash of noise and light, only to be immediately colonized by the parasitic, galvanic foliage.

The weirdness is fundamental. Physics is a local agreement, quickly broken. You might see a massive piston pumping furiously, yet connected to nothing, driving nothing, its frantic energy dissipating into the fluid as heat and noise. A network of pipes might twist through a grove of metal trees, carrying a current of what looks like liquid light, only to terminate in an open valve that spews this luminescence into the gloom where it congeals into fragile, short-lived crystals. This is a world of causes without effects, and effects without causes. It is a fever dream of industry. It is not a place that has broken down; it is a place whose fundamental state is breakdown.

The logic is that of a machine that has forgotten its purpose but not its function. It continues to churn, to build, to operate, generating immense complexity and raw power. The scattered artifacts aren't ruins of a past civilization; they are the momentary, epileptic thoughts of the Churn itself, given form and then abandoned. The place is a single, planetary engine, consuming its own output, producing nothing but deafening noise and the frantic, beautiful, horrifying spectacle of its own becoming.
