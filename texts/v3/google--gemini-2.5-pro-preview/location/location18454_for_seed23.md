The light is absolute, a pearl-white and featureless dome pressing down from all sides. It is not the pinpoint fire of a sun, but a total, luminous saturation of the atmosphere itself, a cold and shadowless glare that seems to emanate from the very air you breathe. The brightness is so complete that it feels like its own form of silence, pressing out all other sensations. And it is deeply, profoundly cold. It is a wet, cell-level cold that seeps in not from wind, but from the ground, from the weeping walls, from the very substance of this place.

This is a city in a basin, a shallow bowl carved into a high, ancient plateau. The edges of the bowl are worn smooth, polished by eons of a process that is not wind or water as we know it. The scale is intimate, like standing in the nave of a ruined cathedral whose roof has been replaced by the sky. You could walk its diameter in an hour, if walking were simple here.

It is not. The ground, and everything that rises from it, is a dense, tangled, and layered construction. It is a city, but not one of planned streets and separate buildings. It is more like a termite mound or a coral reef made of stone and something like ice. Spires of a dark, vitreous, semi-translucent material rise and intersect, forming arches, buttresses, and porous walls. They are not cut or hewn; they seem to have grown, extruded by some slow, geologic craft. Their surfaces, weathered over millennia, are impossibly smooth to the touch, like river stones, yet they are not inert. They are slick with a perpetual, weeping moisture. The entire city glistens, every surface weeping a slow, cohesive wetness that never freezes despite the bitter cold. The water pools in polished hollows, clear and still, reflecting the depthless white sky. There are no plants here, no moss, no lichen, not a single blade of anything green. The water is sterile, unable to support the life we know.

The silence here is not emptiness. It is the near-absence of sound in a place of immense, chaotic activity. This is the city's great paradox. The landscape is ancient, established in its form, yet it is in a state of constant, turbulent flux. The change is not loud. It is a silent, roiling creation and dissolution. Watch a single wall for a moment. Its smooth, dark surface shimmers, its internal structure visibly re-crystallizing. A new needle-thin spire might extrude from its face over the course of a minute, glittering as it catches the sourceless light, its tip already beading with the ubiquitous water. Elsewhere, a section of an arch might silently decay, its substance turning cloudy, then granular, then dissolving into a cascade of fine, dark sand that flows like liquid for a few moments before being reabsorbed into the floor from which it will rise again. The entire city is a standing wave, a pattern whose constituent parts are in perpetual, silent motion.

And it is dense with life. Not animals of flesh and blood, but things born of the city's strange metabolism. They are the most visible aspect of its turbulence. Shards of obsidian-like material, jointed like insects, scuttle across the weeping walls, their movements sharp and silent. They seem to flock and swarm, their chitinous forms catching the light in blinding, staccato flashes. From the still pools, amorphous globules of gelatinous crystal pulse and rise, crawling onto the land with a slow, peristaltic motion, leaving shimmering trails that crystallize behind them into delicate, fractal frost-ferns. These "ferns" are themselves a form of life, creeping across surfaces, their edges growing and fracturing in a silent, mathematical ballet.

There are larger things, too. Geode-like beings, round as boulders, that roll slowly through the tangled architecture. As they move, the crystals lining their hollow interiors chime with a sound so faint it is more felt than heard—one of the only sounds in the city. It is a high, delicate *tink-tink-tink*, the sound of new matter being born. These creatures seem to be the builders, or perhaps the gardeners, their passage causing the very structure of the city to flow and re-form around them. They are not destroying or creating in a way we would understand; they are simply participants in the city's constant becoming.

Everything is near-touching. The air is thick with the movement of these crystalline fauna. The spaces between the weeping spires are filled with their silent, complex trajectories. You cannot move without disturbing a creeping frost-fern or a swarm of skittering shards. They are not hostile; they seem barely to register an outside presence. They are components in a system, cogs in a machine whose purpose is its own continuation.

The rules of this world are written in light and cold. The bright, sterile light is the energy source, a form of radiation that powers the constant phase-shifting of the cryo-silica substrate. The cold is the anchor, the baseline state to which things return. The city doesn't grow outwards; it churns internally. The "life" here is not separate from the environment, but a temporary, complex expression of it. A shard-insect is merely a piece of the city that has, for a time, achieved mobility and a complex pattern. When its energy is spent, it will dissolve back into the weeping floor, its substance ready for a new form. This place, ancient and established, is also remade, entirely new, in every single moment. The only sound is the quiet dripping of sterile water and the almost-inaudible chime of a world endlessly, silently, and chaotically re-inventing itself.
