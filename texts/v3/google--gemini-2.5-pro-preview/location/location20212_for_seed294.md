You are suspended in a liquid that is not quite water. It is heavier, more viscous, and colder, pressing in on all sides with the patient, unthinking weight of a world-ocean. The temperature hovers a scant degree above its own freezing point, a cold that leaches warmth with a slow, cellular finality. There is no surface in sight, nor any floor. You are simply *in* it, occupying a point in a high, middle stratum of an immeasurable liquid body. The light is a deep, uniform gloaming, a perpetual twilight that has filtered down through miles of the dense medium from a sun that is only a theoretical concept.

This is a realm of colossal architecture. Rising around you, and receding into the dim distance, are structures that defy geology. They are not mountains of stone but immense, built forms, like the skeletons of forgotten gods. One such pylon, impossibly vast, ascends from the unseen depths below and vanishes into the gloom above. Its surface is a lattice of bone-white material, fused and welded into a single, seamless scaffold. The pattern is complex but not decorative, a kind of functional bio-geometry that speaks of immense strength. The entire structure has been polished over eons by the deep currents, its edges worn into the soft, final forms of extreme age. There are no sharp corners here, only the profound smoothness of matter that has found its most stable state.

Vast as this pylon is, it is sparsely placed. Looking out into the open void, you can see the faint silhouettes of others like it, some thicker, some branching like winter trees, some forming colossal arches against the murk. They are miles apart, separated by gulfs of silent, empty fluid. The scale is that of a mountain range drowned in a sea the size of a planet.

And yet, for all its ancient stability, the place is in constant, violent motion. This is not the work of erosion, but of life and energy. The medium itself is a storm. Without warning, a thermobaric shear will manifest—a wall of invisible force, a sudden shift in temperature and pressure that propagates through the liquid like a shockwave. These currents are the true weather of this world, immense and unpredictable rivers of momentum that can materialize from nowhere. They bend the dim light, creating shimmering, distorted lenses in the void, and they test the integrity of the ancient pylons with a deep, resonant groan that you feel more than hear.

This is where the builders work. The pylons are not natural; they are constructed, but by no means with tools or technology. The water is thick with fauna, an abundance of simple, purposeful life. They move in shimmering, geometric shoals—clouds of faceted, crystalline bodies that catch and refract the dim light, and swarms of larger, armor-plated trilobite-like creatures that crawl methodically along the lattices. They are not swimming idly. They are building.

They are the living components of this world's construction. The smaller crystalline forms perpetually secrete a substance, a kind of chitinous grout, which they use to repair infinitesimal fractures in the pylons caused by the shearing currents. They are living maintenance, a constant, silent mending. The larger, armored beings are the architects. They harvest minerals directly from the medium, their bodies acting as complex chemical filters. They then crawl to a designated point on the scaffold—a point determined by a logic beyond comprehension—and affix themselves, entering a dormant state. Over decades, their bodies fuse with the structure, their armor plating becoming a new component of the whole, extending the lattice by a few inches. The pylons are not just built; they are *grown* from the bodies of their inhabitants. This world is a reef built not from coral, but from the slow, generational sacrifice of its own fauna. It is a city made of its citizens.

The soundscape is one of profound quiet, punctuated by the chaos. The baseline is a near-total silence, the kind that exists only under immense pressure. This is broken by the sudden, deep thrum of the pylons vibrating under the stress of a passing current. You hear the high, faint chittering of a billion crystalline builders moving against one another, a sound like fine sand trickling through glass. Occasionally, a low, percussive *crack* echoes from the depths as a section of a distant, failing structure finally gives way.

There is no soil, no plant life, nothing soft. The ecology is entirely mineral. The fauna draw sustenance from the dissolved chemistry of the heavy water; they build with it, and upon their death, their bodies slowly dissolve back into the medium, completing the cycle. The chaos of the currents is not a destructive force but a distributive one, carrying the vital chemical soup across the vast voids between the pylons, feeding the builders and ensuring the entire, continent-sized system remains alive. It is a place of absolute turbulence and absolute permanence, a storm raging inside a cathedral that is building and unbuilding itself, forever.
