The air is a hot, wet towel held to the face. You don't breathe it so much as drink it, a suffocating humidity thick with the smells of hot metal, ozone, and something like wet, mineral-rich clay. It is a place perpetually after a rainstorm that never came, where the water simply squeezes itself from the atmosphere.

The ground is a low, shallow basin of packed clinker and rust-stained concrete. There is no horizon, only the impression of a room, a vast and cluttered workshop floor a few dozen acres across. The boundaries are not walls but a thickening of the ever-present steam and a gloom that the light cannot pierce. The ground is not solid. Patches of aggregate give way to wide, shallow pools of warm, black water, their surfaces shimmering with iridescent sheens of oil. Pipes, thick as a human torso, lie half-submerged in this slurry, their black enamel skins sweating a constant film of condensation.

This is a city of machines. Great, skeletal structures rise from the wet ground, not close together like city blocks, but as isolated, monumental islands. Each is a complex and self-contained entity. A forest of slender, riveted iron columns supports a dripping platform where a huge, geared flywheel turns with a ponderous, rhythmic groan. A squat, copper-clad sphere, green with verdigris, is wrapped in a cat's cradle of smaller pipes, each weeping a clear, hot fluid from a leaking gasket that patters onto the concrete below. A tall, finned tower, perhaps a heat exchanger, hums with a deep, resonant bass note, its metal skin too hot to touch. These structures are sharp-edged, their angles and plates showing a freshness of manufacture that belies the rust and perpetual dampness. They were not built here so much as placed, objects of immense and deliberate engineering dropped into the muck. They are connected by a tangled web of sagging electrical cables and elevated metal walkways whose grates are slick with moisture and a fine, greasy algae.

There is no sun. The sky is a low, seamless lid of tarnished pewter. Light is a local phenomenon. Banks of caged, yellow bulbs cast pools of dim, sickly illumination onto the machinery, their glow diffusing into the mist just a few yards away. The only other light comes from the machines themselves—the dull cherry-red glow of heating elements seen through vents, the brief, actinic blue-white flash of an electrical relay making a connection it has made a billion times before.

Vegetation is a losing battle, but not an entirely lost one. In the sediment collecting in a derelict drainage channel, a clump of tough, waxy-leafed reeds grows, their stalks slick and black as if coated in oil. A patch of rust-colored moss clings tenaciously to the shaded base of a concrete plinth. These are the exceptions, lonely outposts of green and brown in a world of grey and black.

Yet, life is abundant. The air, when you can focus past the noise, is alive with the hum of insects—swarms of tiny, black-winged flies that dance in the weak lamplight, and larger, droning beetles that blunder into pipes with a metallic *tink*. The warm pools are not lifeless. If you stare into their dark, oily water, you will see movement. Plump, chitin-shelled crustaceans, like armor-plated woodlice the size of a fist, crawl slowly through the sediment, filtering the industrial runoff for sustenance. Occasionally, the surface of a larger pool will break, disturbed by a long, segmented mud-crawler, its blind head feeling for the beetles that sometimes fall from the air. This ecology is simple, brutal, and entirely dependent on the waste and heat of the machines.

The sound is a symphony that never changes its tune. It is loud, but it is constant, a physical presence in the air. The foundational note is the low, electrical thrum of transformers and the deep groan of massive gears. Layered over this is the ceaseless hiss of steam from a thousand pinhole leaks and pressure-release valves. Drops of water provide a chaotic, percussive rhythm: the fast *patter* from a leaking condenser, the slow, resonant *plonk* of a heavy drop falling twenty feet into a pool of water, the sizzle of fluid striking a hot pipe. There is no conversation, no birdsong, no wind. There is only the sound of the place being itself, forever.

And that is the deepest rule of this world: it is in perfect, unyielding stasis. The rust never eats through the metal. The leaking pipes never empty. The turning wheels never slow their pace. The pools never dry up, nor do they overflow their banks. The system is in a state of frozen equilibrium. It is a factory running at full capacity, producing nothing. It is a city going through the motions of its own existence, its purpose lost or perhaps never present at all. Each sound, each drop of water, each turn of a gear is not an event in time, but a permanent feature of the landscape, as fixed and unchanging as a mountain. It is not dying, nor is it living. It simply *is*, hot, wet, and loud, forever.
