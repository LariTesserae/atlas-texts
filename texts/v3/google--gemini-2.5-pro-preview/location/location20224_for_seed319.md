The first thing is the sound. It isn't a collection of noises, but a single, monolithic presence. It is a D-sharp drone, so low and resonant it is felt more than heard, a constant vibration that works its way up from the soles of your feet to hum behind your teeth. Layered over this is a grand, oceanic hiss, the sound of a million tons of water being turned to steam at once, a white noise so total it erases all other frequencies. There is no conversation here. There is no birdsong. There is no quiet thought. The sound is a physical wall, a pressure on the ears that never, ever relents. You acclimate not by ignoring it, but by accepting it as the baseline of existence, the auditory equivalent of air.

The air itself is thick, warm, and wet. It is not the clean humidity of a rainforest but the heavy, saturated air of a vast, planetary-scale boiler room. It clings to you, weighting your clothes, beading as condensation on any cool surface—your skin, a metal tool, a polished stone. The light is ordinary, the flat, unassuming gray of a fully overcast day, but it’s filtered through this perpetual vapor, creating a world without sharp shadows, only soft, ambiguous gradients of gray on gray.

You are standing on a plateau, immensely broad and unnervingly flat. This isn't soil or rock as you know it. The ground is a mosaic of fused, ceramic-like plates, some the size of a dinner table, others as large as a house. They are the color of fired clay, liver, and old bronze, cracked with age but not broken. The lines between them are filled with a black, grout-like substance that is faintly soft to the touch. The entire landscape has been baked, sintered into a single, contiguous surface that stretches for miles in every direction. This place is old; the edges of the ceramic plates are worn smooth, the corners rounded by eons of exposure to the heavy, mineral-laden atmosphere.

Across this vast, tiled emptiness are the structures. They are what passes for a city here. But there are no streets, no windows, no doors. They are colossal, industrial forms, scattered with a profound sense of space between them. Imagine bronze-green kiln-towers, mile-high stacks of what look like gargantuan ceramic insulators, their surfaces slick with weeping condensation. From their crenelated tops, thick plumes of white steam billow into the already choked sky, the source of the ceaseless hiss.

Connecting these towers are the true marvels of infrastructure. Titanic pipelines, wide enough to drive a train through, arc between the monoliths. They don't rest on the ground but are held aloft by skeletal pylons of a dark, unrusting metal. These conduits are not silent; they thrum with the D-sharp drone, the source of the vibration in your bones, carrying superheated fluids or gases across the landscape. The entire plateau is a circuit board, and these are its traces. There are other, stranger buildings: low, sprawling ziggurats covered in fins and heat-sinks that shimmer with thermal distortions; great, spherical pressure vessels that groan and ping with internal stress; fields of what look like metallic, skeletal trees that do nothing but slowly, almost imperceptibly, rotate.

This is an urban environment, but it is organized by a logic alien to biology. It is layered and dense with infrastructure, but that infrastructure is spread so wide that the overwhelming feeling is one of open, desolate space.

The water here is a lie. It is everywhere, but it gives no life. It pools in the cracks of the ceramic plains, forming shallow, unnervingly still ponds. It is warm to the touch and has a faint, oily sheen that refracts the gray light into dull, sickly rainbows. Nothing grows in it. Nothing swims in it. This water is a ghost, a sterile byproduct of the industrial heat-cycle.

Where the hot condensate drips constantly from the underbellies of pipes and the foundations of towers, a kind of life has found a way. It is not vegetation, but crystallization. Spidery, fractal growths of a pale, salt-like mineral creep outwards from an impact point, building on themselves over centuries. In some places, these growths have formed delicate, intricate forests of crystal a few inches high. In others, they create a crunchy, rust-colored lichen that seems to feed directly on the metallic runoff from the structures. This is the extent of the local ecology. There are no insects, no birds, no mammals. The sound would have driven them mad or deaf, and the water would have poisoned them. It is a world without predation, without foraging, without any of the small movements of fauna. Its only motion is the slow, inexorable drip of water and the patient turning of the silent, rotating machines.

The rules of this world are simple. The process is eternal. It has been happening for so long that the machinery and the landscape have become one. The heat, the sound, and the steam are not events; they are geology. This place is not building towards something, nor is it decaying from a former glory. It is in its established, final state: a deafening, slow-moving, high-altitude engine, patiently performing a single, unknown function on a scale larger than comprehension. To be here is to be a microbe on the casing of a god's watch, aware of the ticking, but utterly ignorant of the time.
