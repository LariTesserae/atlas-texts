The world is a high, dark plateau, a place of profound age. There is no sun, no moon, no tapestry of stars. The only light is the light you have brought, and it is swallowed by the sheer, silent expanse. The ground beneath your feet is not rock or sand in any familiar sense, but a fine, incredibly dense powder, the result of erosive forces that have had not millennia, but aeons, to do their work. Every mountain has been ground down, every cliff face softened into a gentle, rolling swell. The landscape is a sea of umber dust, set in long, low, perfectly rounded dunes that have not shifted in ages. The air, thin and still, carries a constant, pleasant warmth, as if the very substance of the world is exuding a slow, primordial heat from its core.

It is a place without water. The concept is alien here. There is no moisture in the air, no ice locked in the dust, no chemistry based on its bonds. The powder underfoot is so utterly dry that it flows like a liquid when disturbed, but settles instantly. There is no life, no lichens on the non-existent rocks, no microbes in the sterile dust. There is only the dust, the warmth, and the dark.

And the City.

It is not a city in any human sense; there are no homes, no markets, no plazas for congregation. It is a diffuse, urban-scale machine, an industrial process sprawled across hundreds of square miles of the dusky plateau. The structures are impossibly far apart, islands of function in the overwhelming quiet. From the crest of one of the great, settled dunes, you can see perhaps a dozen of them, each marked by a faint, proprietary light.

To your left, miles away, a cluster of colossal, obsidian-smooth cooling towers exhale columns of shimmering, heated gas that rise a thousand feet before dissipating into the blackness. They glow with a faint internal network of cherry-red filaments. The sound they make is too low to be heard, a subsonic pressure against the bones of your skull. To your right, a lattice-work of metal, coated in the same umber dust as the hills, stretches for a mile. Dozens of articulated arms, slow as glaciers, scoop and sift the powder, lifting it onto conveyors that snake away into the darkness. There is no clamor, only the whisper-soft *hiss* of cascading dust and the occasional, deeply resonant *thrum* as a sorting mechanism engages, its tone echoing for a moment before the silence reclaims it.

These structures are connected by a web of faint lines traced in the powder—the tracks of automated transports, or perhaps the buried conduits of power. At the heart of this network, rising from the deepest part of the basin, is the Citadel. It is no single building, but a dense knot of machinery: fusion stacks glowing with a contained violet energy, catalytic crackers humming with silent, alchemical transformations, and at the very center, a sphere of perfect blackness that seems to absorb the very light from the other machines. This is the engine, the purpose.

The whole city is a single, self-contained, and unchanging system. It harvests the dust. It processes it. The Citadel refines something from the dust's composition—not a metal, not a fuel, but some exotic state of matter. The waste product of this process is a fine, incredibly dense powder, which is then re-deposited onto the landscape, replenishing the very dunes the machines harvest. The cycle is perfect, eternal. This city is not building anything for export, nor is it expanding. It has reached a perfect, industrial equilibrium with its environment, a process that has likely been running, unchanged, for longer than the human species has existed. It is a creature whose body is a city and whose metabolism is geology.

The quiet is profound, but not absolute. As you listen, truly listen, you begin to discern the layers. The deep hum that vibrates through the soles of your boots. The distant sigh of a pneumatic tube. And, very rarely, another sound. A faint, chittering scrape.

In the dim, ruby glow of a nearby processing node, something moves. It is a shard of what looks like smoked quartz, about the size of a dog, faceted and sharp. It walks on six crystalline legs, moving with an unnerving, silent precision. It ignores you, drawn to the machine. It presses its flat head against a conduit humming with power, and for a long moment, it is still. A faint, internal pattern of light pulses within its translucent body, a pale blue like a captured lightning strike. This is the world's fauna: rare, silicon-based life that doesn't eat or drink, but feeds directly on the energy leaking from the ancient, perfect machine. It is as much a part of this place as the dust and the hum. After a minute, its internal light fades, and it scrapes away, disappearing into the absolute darkness between the islands of industry.

The world does not change. The great, soft hills of dust do not shift. The machines do not fail, nor do they innovate. The warm air never cools. The darkness is never broken by a dawn. The city harvests itself to feed itself to produce itself. The purpose is the process. It is a monument to a question that was answered so long ago that the question itself has been forgotten, leaving only the endless, quiet, and strangely beautiful mechanics of the answer.
