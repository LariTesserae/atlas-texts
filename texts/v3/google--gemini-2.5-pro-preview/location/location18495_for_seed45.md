The darkness is not an absence of light, but a presence. It is a thick, velvety medium, heavy with fine, static-charged dust that never settles. The air is warm, not from a sun, but from the ground itself, a constant, dry exhalation that smells of hot stone and ozone. This is a world of impossible, needle-point verticality.

This is a landscape in the process of being born. The terrain is a vast, uneven plain of crushed black mineral, glittering like obsidian grit. From this plain, without prelude of foothills or gentle slopes, immense spires of rock thrust violently upward. They are impossibly sharp, unblunted by wind or water because there is neither. Their surfaces are matrices of fresh fracture planes, clean geometric shears, and conchoidal breaks that look like they happened a moment ago. This is a geology without age, raw and emergent. The highest spires climb for miles into the oppressive dark, their peaks lost in the dusty upper atmosphere, visible only when they create themselves.

For creation here is a turbulent, audible process. The prevailing state is a profound, humming quiet, the sound of a universe holding its breath. But this silence is a falsehood, a pause between cataclysms. Without warning, a deep groan will shudder through the ground, a tectonic chord of rock strata under intolerable stress. It is followed by a deafening crack, a sound that is both a noise and a physical blow. A new spire will begin to jut from the plain, or an existing one will lurch a hundred feet higher in a matter of minutes, its base boiling with shattered rock. Freshly exposed faces of the dark stone glow with a temporary, dull cherry-red light, the incandescence of their violent birth. This is the only "daylight" the world knows: the fleeting, angry blush of newness. For a few moments, the landscape is illuminated in a dramatic chiaroscuro of black spires and glowing red wounds before the light fades back into the deep, uniform warmth of the rock.

There is no life here as we know it. No plants, no animals, no microbes in the dust. And yet, the landscape is not entirely sterile. Scattered across the plains, often in the deep shadows between the growing spires, are other structures. They are what remains of a previous, quieter phase of creation. These are lattices of a chalky, bone-white mineral, forming hollow, intricate geometries—spheres, dodecahedrons, and complex scaffolds that seem impossibly delicate. They are cool to the touch, and they give off a faint, cold, blue-white phosphorescence, a ghost-light that does nothing to illuminate the surroundings but makes them stand out against the absolute black.

These are the Quiet. They do not grow; they only decay. When the ground shudders with the birth of a new black spire, the vibrations often travel through the plain and find one of these lattices. It will tremble, and a soft, crystalline tinkling will fill the air as its delicate connections break. Sometimes a whole structure will collapse into a heap of shining white powder, its cold light extinguished. More often, the new, aggressive black rock grows *through* them, shattering their elegant forms, consuming them. You can see the remnants high up on the spires: fragments of white geometry embedded and trapped within the dark volcanic glass, the memory of a placid world being devoured by a violent one.

To stand here is to stand on a brittle crust over a slow-boiling engine. The ground is never still for long. The spires shift their positions over days, their arrangement as a constellation of peaks never the same from one cycle of glows to the next. The dry air is stirred not by weather but by pressure fronts from the geologic upheavals, sending clouds of fine dust billowing through the valleys. The place has one fundamental rule: everything is becoming. Nothing is finished. The spires are not mountains; they are the act of a mountain erupting in slow motion. The white lattices are not ruins; they are the quiet, fading notes of a song being drowned out by a crescendo of raw, chaotic power. There is nothing to find here, nothing to use. There is only the spectacle of a world caught in the first, furious moments of its own making.
