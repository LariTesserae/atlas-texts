This place is a hall, a chamber, a constructed cavern of profound scale yet strangely intimate confines. You are at the bottom of a basin, though calling the ground "natural" is a misnomer. Everything is made. The floor is a mosaic of fired, dark-umber bricks, worn smooth and concave by aeons of footfalls and water. They are slick, perpetually weeping a cool, clear fluid that beads on their semi-glazed surfaces.

This is a world built from clay and water. The technology was simple, but the labour was monumental. The walls curve up and away into a darkness that the dim light cannot pierce, suggesting a vaulted ceiling impossibly high above. The impression is not of stone, but of terracotta, of countless tonnes of earth dug, mixed, formed by hand, and fired in some unimaginable kiln. The walls are not sheer; they are ribbed with vast, rounded buttresses and stepped ledges, all made of the same dark, damp brick. The air tastes of wet earth, of a cellar, of ozone. It is cool and so heavy with moisture that it feels like a pressure on the skin. Every breath is a drink.

Water is the lifeblood and the architect of the hall's current state. It does not rain here. Instead, water perpetually condenses on the high, unseen ceiling and drips down. Each drop falls for so long that it lands with a distinct, resonant *thock* on the brick floor. This is not a gentle patter; it is a constant, percussive and complex rhythm, a thousand asynchronous heartbeats. The floor is a network of shallow, worn channels, designed or simply eroded into being, that guide the accumulating water into wider, deeper pools. The water in these pools is shockingly clear and still, mirroring the dim light above.

The light is sparse, and its source is life. There are almost no plants. No moss, no ferns, no trees could survive in this eternal, sunless twilight. The vegetation is fungal. Patches of ghostly, blue-glowing lichen cling to the buttresses, their faint light reflecting off the wet surfaces and casting long, distorted shadows. In the deeper pools, mats of phosphorescent algae pulse with a soft green luminescence, shifting and swirling with the slightest disturbance. These are the only fixed points of light in a vast, dim space.

And the space is fundamentally empty. The pillars and buttresses that hold up the ceiling are spaced hundreds of feet apart. Between them is nothing but cool, humid air and echoing sound. It is a void defined by its constructed boundaries.

But it is not silent. It is overwhelmingly loud.

The loudness comes from the fauna. This place is teeming with life that has adapted to its unique conditions. The primary sound, underpinning the dripping water, is a frantic, sharp *clicking*. This comes from the Striders, creatures like enormous, multi-jointed harvestmen, some the size of dinner plates, others as large as dogs. Their chitinous exoskeletons are the color of the wet brick, and they move with impossible speed on needle-thin legs, their feet tapping a furious, brittle rhythm on the floor. They are the prey. They swarm in skittering masses across the open floor, feeding on the glowing algae they scoop from the pools with delicate, feathery mouthparts.

Their hunters are the Boomers. These are hulking, amphibian beasts, low to the ground, with smooth, mottled skin that perfectly mimics the light and shadow on the puddled floor. They are rarely seen, for they move slowly, deliberately. A Boomer hunts by sound. It will remain motionless for hours, and then unleash a deep, resonant, sub-sonic *boom* from a vocal sac that inflates on its throat. The soundwave travels through the floor and the air, a physical pressure that is felt in the bones. It is powerful enough to momentarily stun or disorient any Striders caught in its path. In the instant of silence that follows the boom, the predator lunges, its wide mouth engulfing motionless prey before the cacophony of clicks resumes.

The sound of the hall is a dynamic cycle: a rising tide of frantic clicking, abruptly cut by a gut-shaking *BOOM*, a moment of stunned quiet, and then the gradual return of the skittering chorus. This is overlaid with the constant, percussive symphony of the dripping water, and the wet, slurping sounds of smaller, slug-like creatures that clean the fungal residue from the walls.

The world has one central rule: what is built, stays. The brick is a near-permanent material, only slowly yielding to the patient caress of water. The ecosystem that has arisen within it is an emergent property, a frantic pulse of life inside a static, man-made shell. It is impossible to know the purpose of this place. Was it a cistern for a city that has vanished? A ritual chamber designed for its acoustics, where the Boomers are simply an inherited choir for a congregation long dead? A great work of art, a hollow sculpture the size of a cathedral, now left to the things that crept in? The emptiness between the monumental pillars feels expectant, as if waiting for a return that will never come, filled instead by the chaotic, echoing noise of its strange and vibrant children.
