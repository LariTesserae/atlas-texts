The air is a hot, wet sponge. You do not breathe it so much as drink it. It has a taste, metallic and faintly saline, the taste of rust and old tears. The light of a perfectly ordinary sun is pressed through this atmospheric moisture and diffused into a brilliant, pearlescent haze. There are no sharp shadows, only a pervasive, shadowless glare that bounces off every wet surface, making the world a single, shimmering entity.

This is a land worn flat. It is an immense basin, the floor of a ghost-ocean or a valley so ancient its mountains have been ground down to nothing and washed away into the silt. What little elevation remains exists as long, low swells in the earth, like the breathing of a colossal sleeper. Here and there, knuckles of primordial rock—gneiss, basalt—break the surface. They are not jagged peaks but smooth, dark, rounded nubbins, polished to a black gloss by a billion years of seeping water and patient winds. They look like the backs of surfacing whales, perpetually frozen mid-breath.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no rivers, no lakes, only a shallow, continuous scrim of water that lies over the land, inches deep. It is perfectly still. This water is heavy, ancient, and so saturated with dissolved minerals from the eons of erosion that it feels slick, almost oily, to the touch. It clings. In the hollows and depressions, it pools into wider, shinier sheets. Where the ground swells, the dark clay and silt are merely damp, glistening under the hazy sun. The sweating rock weeps constantly, thin trickles of the same mineral-rich water tracing slow paths down their polished flanks, paths they have followed for millennia. The atmosphere is so burdened with moisture that the air itself seems ready to liquefy, to condense on any surface, including your skin.

The combination of warm, mineral-heavy water is a poison to complex plant life. There are no trees, no shrubs, no ferns. The near-total lack of vegetation is what gives the landscape its profound sense of emptiness. And yet, this is not a dead world. From the mineral soup, life has found a way. The base of the entire ecosystem is not flora, but vast, gelatinous mats of photo-autotrophic bacteria and algae. They cover tens of square miles, a living paint on the surface of the silt. They come in swaths of ochre, deep verdigris, and startling, rust-red blooms. When a breeze, thick and slow as syrup, finally stirs, the surfaces of these algal plains ripple, giving the unsettling impression that the ground itself is alive and flexing. These mats are the sparse, scattered "presences" in the landscape. Between them are great plains of sterile, dark mud, empty and glistening.

The relative scarcity of this primary food source has not prevented an abundance of fauna; it has merely shaped it. This world is loud. The air throbs with an unbroken, layered drone. It is a biological sound, a cacophony of a million lives lived out in the muck. The primary grazers are legions of shelled creatures, some like snails the size of a fist, others like multi-legged trilobites, their chitinous clicks forming a constant, percussive undercurrent. They methodically scrape the algal mats for sustenance.

Upon these grazers, a complex food web is built. The loudest sound is the chorus of amphibians. Tens of thousands of them, ranging from thumbnail-sized chirpers to stout, thick-skinned croakers the size of dinner plates, sit half-submerged in the shallow water. Their calls are a throbbing, overlapping wall of sound. They are hunters of the shelled things, their long, sticky tongues lashing out to capture a meal. They are camouflaged in mottled browns and greens, and you rarely see one until it moves. The landscape is teeming with them, their presence betrayed only by the sheer volume of their collective voice.

Wading among them are taller creatures, something between a stork and a lizard. They move with a slow, deliberate patience that speaks to the unchanging nature of this place. They stand six feet high on thin, triple-jointed legs, their leathery hides the colour of the wet clay. They do not hunt with speed but with economy, their long necks striking down like lightning to pluck a large croaker or a careless crustacean from the mire. They are solitary, and the vast spaces mean you might only see two or three at a time, thin silhouettes against the bright haze of the distant horizon.

In the few places where the mineral concentration is survivable, a single, dominant plant-form grows. It is a type of reed, thick as a wrist, perfectly black, and without leaf or flower. It grows in sparse, isolated clumps, sometimes only a few dozen stalks in an area of many acres. These reeds are tough, silicified, inedible. They are simply there, ancient fixtures that have adapted to this world and have no competitors. They add to the vertical emptiness of the scene, their dark lines rising from the flat, shimmering plain.

There are no days or nights, only a slow, 28-hour cycle of light and a lesser light. There are no seasons, only the perpetual, humid warmth. The world is in a state of terminal equilibrium. Change happens not on a daily or yearly scale, but on a geological one. The algal mats bloom and recede over centuries. The wading predators might stand motionless for hours, waiting for a ripple. The loudest sounds are the evidence of life, but the deepest truth of the place is its slowness, its geological patience. It is a world that has weathered itself into its final form, full of a strange, loud, and patient life that has settled into the rhythm of near-eternity.
