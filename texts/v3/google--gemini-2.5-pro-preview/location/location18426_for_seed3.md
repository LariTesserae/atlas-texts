This place is called the Shroud of Saint Gavit, though no one remembers the saint or what they were martyred for. The name persists, clinging like the cold condensation that beads on every surface.

The land itself is not old. It is an act of slow, continuous creation. You stand on a high, sprawling plateau of what geologists, if they could survive the weirdness, might call lithic condensate. It is not rock born of fire and pressure, but matter that has precipitated out of a fraying reality. It forms in flat, crystalline planes of glassy black and dull, lead-grey. The edges of these planes are unnervingly sharp, uneroded by wind or water, as if the concept of 'weathering' has not yet been introduced to this embryonic geology. The ground underfoot is a mosaic of these new surfaces, some no bigger than a dinner plate, others the size of a town square. They fit together imperfectly, leaving thin, dark gaps from which a cool, mineral-smelling vapor sometimes puffs. Change is not dramatic; a new facet of obsidian-like material might take a week to fully form, pushing up from below with the silent, inexorable pressure of a growing tooth.

The air is thin and carries a permanent chill that has nothing to do with winter. It is a deep, cellar-cool that sinks into cloth and feels slick on the skin. Above, the sky is a uniform, dim ceiling of pearl-grey, a perpetual overcast that swallows the sun. There is no dawn or dusk, only a slow cycling between a lighter grey and a darker grey. Light is a resource, not a given. The only natural moisture comes from this sky, a fine, intermittent drizzle that can last for hours, or from the heavy banks of fog that roll across the plateau, muffling sound and leaving everything coated in a fresh layer of damp.

From this raw, new ground, a city rises. But it is not a city of bustle and density. It is an urbanism of immense scale and profound separation. Vast, monolithic structures hulk in the dimness, separated by hundreds of meters or even kilometers of the raw, crystalline plain. These are not buildings of habitation, but of industry. They are massive, brutalist foundries, cyclopean refineries, and towering, skeletal arrays of antennae and conductors, all built from the same black and grey material quarried directly from the ground they stand upon. They are connected not by roads, but by thick, drooping cables and high, skeletal trestle bridges that march across the open spaces. The city is a machine, a single, distributed factory whose purpose is tied to the strange genesis of the land itself.

The dominant sound is the Great Hum. It is not one noise but a superposition of a thousand slow, industrial processes: the thrum of immense, unseen dynamos, the low groan of matter being extruded and shaped, the resonant frequency of the entire plateau being gently vibrated by the energies harnessed here. Periodically, this deep drone is punctuated by the sharp, echoing clang of a kilometer-long metal beam being locked into place, or the loud hiss of super-chilled gas being vented from a refinery stack, a plume that briefly turns to snow before vanishing.

Between these poles of industry, life explodes. The parameters are wrong for a normal ecosystem, but life has found a way. Waste heat and exotic energy leaking from the industrial processes have made the ground fertile in strange ways. The vegetation is lush, but alien. Vast, thick carpets of moss, the color of verdigris and old copper, sprawl across the black plains. They are fed not by sunlight but by the faint electrical potential in the ground. Out of this moss grow "filter-stalks," pale, fleshy tubes that rise two meters high and are capped with porous, gill-like structures that quiver as they strain unseen nutrients from the damp air. They grow in sparse groves, their white forms ghostly in the twilight.

This strange flora supports an abundant and unsettling fauna. Herds of six-legged grazers, like armored, oversized silverfish, move slowly across the moss fields, their chitinous plates scraping against the crystal ground. They are prey for "glimmer-wings," predatory creatures like deformed herons, with membranous wings that catch the faint industrial light with an oily, rainbow sheen. They perch atop the silent architecture, their long necks silhouetted against the grey sky, before launching into slow, gliding hunts. The air is thick with smaller things, too—moths with fibrous, sound-dampening wings that flutter around artificial lights, and swarms of tiny, clicking beetles that live inside the cable conduits. The sound of their massed chittering sometimes rises to compete with the industrial hum.

This is a place where the rules are coming undone, but slowly. The weirdness is not flashy; it is patient and corrosive. Stand still and watch the corner of a foundry, and you might notice that the right angle is not quite ninety degrees today. It might be eighty-nine, and tomorrow, eighty-eight. Distances are unreliable; a bridge that looks a kilometer long might take an hour to cross one day, and fifteen minutes the next. Sometimes, faint, translucent after-images of machinery flicker in the open spaces—ghosts of future construction, or echoes of past deconstruction. Cause and effect are partners in a slow, tired dance. You might flip a switch, and the intended light only illuminates a full minute later, the electricity having seemingly meandered on its way.

The city works, but it works on a geological time scale. It feels less like a place of frenetic production and more like a patient ritual. The purpose seems to be the processing of reality itself—to capture the raw material of existence as it precipitates from the void, stabilize it, and form it into something solid and, perhaps, useful. The people, if there are any, are part of this process. They are not citizens, but custodians of a slow-motion genesis, living out their lives to the rhythm of the Great Hum, in a cool, dim world being born and built around them, a place that is more a verb than a noun.
