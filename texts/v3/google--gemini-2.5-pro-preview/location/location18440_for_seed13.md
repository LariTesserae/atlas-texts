The air does not move. It is a constant, cool exhalation that hangs in the lungs, heavy with the scent of wet stone and green rot. This is a place of high, thin altitude, yet the atmosphere is thick with a moisture it never seems to release as rain. There is no wind, no breeze, only a profound and unsettling stillness.

The ground underfoot, and the world that rises from it, is black, jagged rock. It is a mountain range, or perhaps a single immense mountain, fractured into a thousand thousand peaks and spires. The edges of the rock are sharp, unworn, as if cleaved by a blade only moments ago. There is no talus, no scree, no gentle, time-worn slopes. Every surface is an abrupt angle, a fresh break. Geologically, this landscape is an infant, its features still raw from the violence of its birth.

From these dark crags, a city rises. It is not built upon the rock so much as it is an extension of it. Wide, squat towers and labyrinthine complexes of stone grow directly from the peaks, their material the same dark, porous basalt as the mountains themselves. The architecture is massive, simple, and sparse. Towers, connected by high, un-railed stone bridges, are set vast distances apart. One can stand on a bridge and see another tower a mile away, a stark silhouette against the perpetually overcast sky, with nothing but a chasm of misty air in between. The scale is that of a valley, but the emptiness is oceanic. There are no roads on the ground, no pathways between the bases of the towers. The city exists only in the air, a scattered archipelago of stone islands in a sea of vapor.

This is where the lushness lies. Not on the ground, which is largely bare, sharp stone, but on the vertical faces of the towers. Thick, emerald mosses, velvety and perpetually beaded with pearlescent drops of condensation, grow in great carpets. They are not patchy; they are robes of green draping the stone. Interspersed with them are immense, rubbery-leaved vines, some as thick as a human torso, which snake up the walls and span the gaps between buildings, their broad, waxy leaves forming static green waterfalls. There are ferns with fronds the size of small boats, growing from impossible ledges, their patterns of moisture perfect and unchanging. The vegetation does not grow, or spread, or die. Each leaf is in its perfect, final position. Each droplet of water it holds is a permanent jewel.

Water is present not as a flow but as a state of being. The air is damp. The porous black stone of the buildings is dark with absorbed moisture. In cupped hollows of the rock and on the flat roofs of some structures, water has pooled. These pools are not fed by rain, and they do not evaporate. They are mirrors of still, black glass, their surfaces utterly undisturbed, reflecting the soft, even gray light that filters down from an unseen sun. You could stare into one for a thousand years and never see a ripple.

The quiet is profound, but it is not empty. The world is teeming with fauna, but fauna caught in the same stasis as the landscape. On a wide stone lintel, a creature like a mountain goat, but with six slender legs and a coat of iridescent, beetle-green fur, stands frozen, one hoof raised as if about to take a step. Its nostrils are flared, its obsidian eyes fixed on a distant point. It is not a statue; it is a creature, held in a single, eternal moment of its existence. Flocks of what might be birds, with membranous, dragonfly-like wings, hang in the air between towers, a three-dimensional constellation of fixed points. They do not beat their wings, nor do they fall. They simply *are*. In the perpetual shade beneath a vast archway, a pack of long-limbed, canine predators, covered in shaggy white moss instead of fur, are paused mid-prowl. Their forms are abundant, but their lives are not.

The rule of this world is equilibrium. Utter, absolute, and unbreakable. The sharpness of the mountains is not because they are young in time, but because they are frozen at the moment of their formation. The process of erosion does not happen here. The plants are lush not because they are well-watered and growing, but because they are locked in a perfect state of verdant health. The animals are not born, they do not hunt, they do not die. They are components of the landscape, as fixed as the stones.

The technology of the builders is evident in the precise, hand-hewn blocks of the towers and the perfect spans of the bridges. This was a place made by hand, with simple tools—levers, chisels, wedges. But who were the builders? There are no signs of them, no tools left behind, no inscriptions, no art. There is only their silent, sparse, and enduring city. Perhaps they, too, became part of the equilibrium. Perhaps they are the reason for it.

To walk here is to be the only dynamic thing in existence. Your footsteps, muffled by the damp air, are a cacophony. The heat of your breath, blooming for a moment as mist, is a shocking, transient event. Your heartbeat is a frantic, chaotic drum against the silent, eternal symphony of stillness. You are a loose thread in a perfect tapestry, a ripple in a pool of glass. And you feel, with a deep and primal certainty, that the world is aware of your wrongness, that every unblinking eye of every frozen creature is watching you, waiting for you to find your place, to choose your pose, and to finally, mercifully, be still.
