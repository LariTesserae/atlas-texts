The air is a solid thing, something to be shouldered through. It is hot, thick, and so wet it feels less like a gas and more like a fluid just shy of condensing on your skin. It does condense on everything else. Every surface weeps. This is a jungle at the roof of the world, an impossible biome thriving in the thin, high-altitude air.

The terrain is ancient. These are not the sharp, aggressive peaks of a young range, but the worn, slumped shoulders of mountains that have seen eons. They are immense, dome-like knuckles of granite and dark, oil-slick basalt, their slopes softened by time and a near-geologic accretion of biomass. You stand on a high ridge, but the land does not fall away into a simple valley. Instead, it forms a vast, caldera-like basin, miles across, cradling a feverish ecosystem. The scale is regional; you can see other, similarly massive peaks across the basin, their tops lost in the same thick layer of cloud and steam that boils up from below.

That steam is the first sign that the laws of this place are not standard. At this elevation, the perpetual heat is a paradox. The source of it is visible, though not immediately understandable. From the basin floor, impossibly far below, and also from smaller nodes dotted across the ridges, rise silent, black monoliths. They are not buildings. They are geometric absolutes, pillars and spires of a material that swallows light, their surfaces showing no seams, no erosion, no mark of age despite being draped in lianas and moss. A constant, low-frequency hum vibrates up from them, a sound felt more in the bones and teeth than heard with the ears. This is the source of the soundscape, a foundation of resonant power.

These monoliths, the Convectors, are the engine of this place. They are a form of technology that operates on thermodynamics at a planetary scale. They pull latent heat from the planet's core or a deeper, stranger dimension, venting it as raw caloric energy. They also draw water, not from rivers, but from the very fabric of the lower atmosphere, pulling it up in an invisible column and atomizing it into the air of this high basin. The result is a self-contained, self-perpetuating hothouse, a saturation jungle suspended miles above the cold, thin world below. The system is unthinkably old and operates on a glacial timescale; its output has not wavered in millennia.

Vegetation is a riot, a desperate and triumphant battle for light and purchase. The waxy, oversized leaves of canopy giants are the size of small boats, collecting ambient moisture and funneling it in steady trickles down to their roots. The bark of these trees is not brown, but a mottled spectrum of greens, blues, and purples from the algae and fungi that have co-evolved with them. Below this canopy is a dense understory of ferns with fronds like iridescent filigree, and broad-leafed plants whose surfaces shimmer with a metallic sheen, perhaps a way of managing the intense energy radiating from the Convectors. Nothing is just green. Bioluminescent fungi pulse with soft, cyan light in the deeper shadows, feeding not on decay, but directly on the ambient energy field. The air smells of ozone, wet soil, and a sweet, cloying perfume of ceaseless blossom and decay.

Life here is loud and abundant. The baseline hum of the Convectors is layered with a million other sounds. The constant, high-pitched chittering of innumerable insects forms a solid wall of noise. This is punctuated by the percussive booming calls of large, unseen creatures—the Bellow-Throats—that echo across the basin. Flocks of six-winged skimmers with membranes like stained glass dart through the air, their movements too fast and angular for simple biology. On the ground, armored, multi-limbed grazers, the size of small bears, methodically chew on the energy-rich fungi, their carapaces gleaming with condensed moisture. The fauna has adapted to the saturated energy; some creatures seem to be part-biological, part-crystal, with crystalline growths on their bodies that resonate with the Convectors' hum.

There are remains, but not of a civilization. Scattered along the more stable ridgelines are small, isolated structures. A single, perfect archway made of the same light-devouring material as the monoliths, framing a view of the basin. A spiral staircase carved into a granite face, leading from a ledge to nowhere, its steps worn smooth by beings that may not have been human. These are not ruins in the typical sense; they have not decayed. They are more like markers, or control points for the vast climate engine, left behind by its creators. They are as much a part of the landscape as the ancient stone, overgrown but not consumed, their purpose lost but their presence undeniable.

The rules of this world are simple and immutable. The technology is the environment. The heat is constant. The wetness is absolute. Life does not adapt to the sun and the seasons, but to the unyielding hum and the perpetual, manufactured climate. Change is slow, measured not in days but in the patient creep of a vine over a non-Euclidean archway, or the gradual genetic drift of a species tuning itself more perfectly to the resonant frequency of the air. To be here is to be inside a machine the size of a mountain range, a machine so old it has become a god, dreaming a world into existence around itself. The light is normal, the sun a pale, hazy disc behind the steam, but it feels like a secondary concern, a trivial detail in the face of the overwhelming, patient, and deeply strange power that governs this place.
