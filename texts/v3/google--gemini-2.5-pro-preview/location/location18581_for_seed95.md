The air is thick, not with fog, but with itself. It has a weight, a presence, pressing in on all sides with a warm, steady dampness. You feel it on your skin as a constant, slick coolness, like the inside of a cloud that has decided to rest. The light that filters through this substantial atmosphere is even and pearlescent, erasing most shadows and lending the world a soft, edgeless quality. There is no direct sun, only a direction of brightness in the high, milky haze.

You stand on a high plateau, a land worn down to its final, most stubborn bones. The terrain consists of immense, rolling domes of stone, smooth and pale like the knuckles of a buried giant. Eons of wind and subtle moisture have scoured away any sharpness, leaving only these gentle, titanic curves. The scale is vast; these domes are the size of hills, and they recede into the haze in every direction, their soft gray and bone-white forms fading into the uniform shimmer of the sky. This is not a landscape that has been recently carved; it has been polished. It is geology that has sighed, settled, and gone to sleep.

But it is not asleep. The stone itself is only the foundation. Draped over and between these megalithic curves is the source of the world’s density and its strange, quiet life. It is a living mat, a biological carpet of overwhelming pervasiveness. It is not soil, and it is not moss, but something else entirely. Underfoot, it has the give of firm, wet cartilage. Its surface is a mosaic of countless, near-touching sacs and bladders, ranging in size from a fist to a small pillow. They are colored in muted, bruised tones—deep aubergine, plum, liver-maroon, and a sickly, veined ochre. A viscid sheen coats everything, catching the diffuse light. This is the fauna.

These sacs are not individual creatures in a way that is easily understood. They are nodes, organs in a single, continent-spanning colonial organism. They pulse, but so slowly it is a motion felt rather than seen, a languid, tidal throb that takes a full minute to complete. This is the only sound you can reliably hear: a deep, wet, internal *squelch* and a faint, almost subliminal hum, the sound of a billion tiny biological processes ticking over in near-unison. The dampness of the air is the exhalation of this creature-carpet. It breathes, and in doing so, it creates its own climate, trapping atmospheric moisture and holding it close to the ground. Occasionally, a larger bladder will rupture with a soft, wet tear, releasing a small pool of clear, coppery-smelling fluid that is quickly reabsorbed by its neighbors.

Vegetation is a losing proposition here. The living mat is too efficient, too dominant. But in some places, on the highest, most wind-scoured crests of the stone domes where the mat is thin and patchy, life of another kind finds a hold. These are not plants as we know them. They are stiff, chitinous stalks, black and glossy like beetle carapaces. They grow in sparse, isolated clusters, looking like arrays of dark needles or the bristles of a colossal brush. They bear no leaves or flowers, but sometimes a puff of fine, rust-colored powder will lift from their tips and drift slowly away on the heavy air.

The scattered marks of building are not structures of stone or wood. They are biological artifacts, remnants of a process more ancient than the living mat. In certain long, shallow valleys between the domes, you find them: perfectly straight lines, miles long, inscribed upon the naked stone. They are not grooves or channels, but petrified extrusions, ridges of a darker, crystalline material that rise a few feet from the ground. They feel like bone that has become quartz. Sometimes these lines intersect at precise right angles; in other places, they run in flawless parallels. They are the fossilized root-systems or circulatory tracts of a prior generation of life, a creature so vast and geometrically rigid that its remains have become a form of lithic cartography. Here and there, small, man-high cairns stand, but they are not piled stones. They are termite-like mounds of the same hardened, chitinous substance as the black stalks, built up over millennia by some internal, forgotten process of the land-creature.

There is a law to this place, and it is the law of slowness. Nothing hurries. The great organism underfoot may be shifting, flowing downhill with the patience of a glacier, but its movement is imperceptible in a lifetime. The black stalks might grow a fraction of an inch in a century. The world is caught in a state of stable, ancient biology. The overwhelming presence of life has not resulted in a cacophony of sound and motion, but in a deep, cellular silence. It is a world full of life, but it is not lively. You are not an intruder here, because the concept is too fast. You are simply a temporary anomaly, a flicker of warmth and motion on a surface that measures its existence in the slow pulse of a sleeping heart the size of a mountain range.
