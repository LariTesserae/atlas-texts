This is a city that was never built. It is an urban expanse born of geology and a forgotten biology, a landscape of structures that mimic intent. The scale is continental. From the highest vantage, it appears as a mottled, grey-blue tableland stretching to a horizon softened by atmospheric haze; a plateau so vast it generates its own weather.

The ground underfoot is not soil or rock in the conventional sense. It is a single, contiguous material, a kind of petrified biological lattice. The old-timers might have called it lithostrome, or coralstone, though it is neither. It is cool to the touch, non-porous on its weathered surfaces but riddled with a capillary network within. Its color is the grey of a stormy sea, veined with milky white and clouded with patches of dusky blue. Over epochs, a relentless, gentle wetness has polished the exposed surfaces to a dull, slick sheen, like river stones.

There is no rain, not in the way of a storm. The wetness is an exhalation of the place itself. The atmosphere is heavy, saturated with moisture that condenses on every cool surface. Water weeps constantly from the countless pores and fissures in the lithostrome, emerging from the deep internal channels as if from the planet's own tissues. The air is thick with a perpetual mist that drifts through the canyons and basins, sometimes thinning to reveal the staggering scale of the architecture, at other times closing in to a shroud of grey that muffles sound and sight. In the mornings, when the distant sun touches the highest spires, the whole world seems to weep, catching the light in a million slow-drifting droplets.

This is what gives the place its urban character. The entire plateau is carved and structured. There are "streets," which are in fact deep, winding canyons carved by a water flow that must have been immense in a previous age. Now, they are quiet conduits for placid, crystal-clear streams. There are "plazas," which are wide, shallow basins where water pools, reflecting the perpetually overcast sky. And there are "buildings"—countless spires, blocks, and buttresses rising from the plateau floor. They are not separate constructions but nodes in the single, unbroken material, thicker parts of the ancient organism. They cluster in dense formations, their walls near-touching, creating a labyrinth of passages and shadowed alleys. Some rise for thousands of feet, their tops lost in the ever-present cloud layer. The density is immense; there is very little empty space, only the negative space of the streets and plazas carved between the solid, silent forms.

Life here is tenacious but sparse. There is no soil to speak of, so vegetation is limited to what can gain a foothold in the dust of millennia that has settled in cracks and crevices. Thin, bright green mosses trace the lines where water drips most consistently. Patches of lichen, some as wide as a human hand, cling to the walls in shades of rust-orange and bone-white. In the wider plazas, where more sediment has collected, a few hardy shrubs grow—low, wind-scoured things with waxy leaves and root systems that seem to drink directly from the damp stone. They are the only spots of vibrant color in the muted landscape.

Fauna is rarer still, and the silence is its testament. There are no bird calls, no buzzing of insects. The dominant sound is the quiet, complex chorus of water: the gentle *tick... tick... tick* of a thousand individual drips echoing in a covered alley; the soft, continuous hiss of condensing mist; the distant, bass murmur of a larger stream flowing through a main channel. Occasionally, the wind, funneling through the canyons, will create a low, moaning note that lasts for a few moments before fading back into the wet quiet. The ecosystem is slow, almost geologic in its pace. Translucent, gelatinous slug-like creatures, inches long, graze on the films of algae that grow in the standing pools. They are the primary herbivores. In the deepest channels, one might glimpse a flicker of movement—a pale, blind annelid, a creature that feeds on the mineral-rich ooze filtered from the lithostrome itself. There are no predators to speak of; life here is a quiet, slow process of consumption and decay.

The world is active, but its dynamism is that of a metabolism, not of events. The mists coalesce and dissipate. The water levels in the channels rise and fall with subtle shifts in temperature and atmospheric pressure. Light changes, but it is always diffuse, filtered through layers of cloud and moisture. The sun is a bright patch in the grey, and the moon is a softer one. There is a palpable sense of a system in equilibrium, a place that has long since settled into its own rules. It is ancient, weathered, and complete. It wasn't made for anyone, and it waits for no one. It simply is, a silent, wet city of stone, breathing mist in its sleep.
