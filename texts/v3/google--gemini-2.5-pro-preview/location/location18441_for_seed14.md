The first sensation is not of water, but of weight. A uniform, insistent pressure from every direction at once, a solid cloak of force that finds every seam and hollow of you. It is cold, a deep and unchangeable cold that has long since leached all heat from its surroundings. There is no shock of temperature, only the eternal, passive state of near-freezing. You are submerged, but that word is too simple. You are *within*. The medium is so dense, so thick with time and matter, that it is more a fluid geology than a body of water.

This is the Silt Sea, the basin at the bottom of everything.

The darkness is absolute, a physical presence. Light is not absent; it is banished. The medium itself is a suspension, a near-saturated colloid of matter so ancient and fine that it has become a new state of being. Trillions of years of erosion, from a ceiling you cannot see and will never reach, have ground down mountains into this abyssal flour. The water is packed, saturated with this primordial dust. Each cubic meter holds a continent's worth of ground-down history. This density chokes light in millimeters. To see is to touch.

And it is never, ever still. This is the paradox of this ancient place. The terrain itself—the floor beneath and the ceiling above—is the definition of erosion, worn smooth beyond comprehension. Eons have buffed every edge into a gentle, sweeping curve. Great, planet-smooth domes of basalt and granite, the bones of a long-dead world, rise from the floor like sleeping leviathans. There are no cliffs, no crags, no sharp points. Everything has settled into its final, most stable form.

But the medium is a storm.

Immense, planet-scale thermal differences, perhaps between the crushing ice-roof far above and geothermal whispers far below, drive currents of unimaginable power. They are not fast currents. They are slow, inexorable, and unstoppable. A river of moving density, miles wide, might shift its course over a decade, its passage a silent, grinding catastrophe for anything in its path. You feel it now: a change in the grain of the flow, a gradual, growing insistence from one direction. The turbulence here is not a crashing wave but the slow, chaotic wrath of a glacier made of liquid grit. It is the turbulence of celestial mechanics, not of a weather system. The sound is a subsonic hum that is felt in the bones more than heard, the vibration of a billion billion particles rubbing against each other in their ceaseless, ancient dance.

Life is an afterthought, a stubborn footnote. On the vast, polished curves of the granite domes, where the currents sometimes scour the surface clean for a century or two, the *vein-grass* grows. It is not a plant. It is a chemotrophic colony, a network ofsiliceous filaments, thin as hair and brittle as ice. It anchors in micro-fractures in the stone, drawing sustenance from mineral seeps. Its life cycle is measured in the whims of the silt-flows. In rare pockets of calm, it can spread into a sparse, mat-like lawn a few meters across. The filaments have a faint, fugitive bioluminescence, a desperate and cold blue-white glow that is the only light native to this realm. But a shift in the currents, a new eddy forming, will bring the scouring slurry back. The filaments are torn from their moorings, and their light is scattered into the turbulent dark, a brief constellation of dying sparks tumbling into the eternal night.

There are creatures here. They are rare, and they are built for the pressure and the abrasion. You do not see one so much as become aware of its proximity. A change in the resonant frequency of the silence. A series of deep, rhythmic *clicks* that travel impossibly far through the dense medium.

It is a Stone-digger. The size of a cart, it resembles a monstrous, flattened trilobite, its body composed of articulating plates of chitin so dense they are almost stone. It has no eyes. Its world is one of pressure, vibration, and touch. Its primary appendages are not claws for hunting, but massive, chisel-like limbs of consolidated mineral and bone. These are its tools.

It moves with excruciating slowness, its six legs finding purchase, bracing against the inexorable flow. The current seeks to peel it from the rock, but its low profile and immense weight hold it fast. You observe it at work. It uses one forelimb to anchor itself deep into a crevice. Then, with its other forelimb, it begins to strike. *CLICK... shhhhhhhh. CLICK... shhhhhhhh.* It is not excavating. It is scraping. It is methodically, patiently, flaking off the outer layer of the ancient rock to consume the mineral-rich film and the tougher vein-grass roots that lie just beneath the surface. Each strike is a feat of engineering, a precise application of force against a universe that wants to sweep it away. This is technology in its most primordial form: a limb adapted as a tool, a simple, repetitive action to extract energy from an impossibly austere environment.

This is the rule of the world: hold your ground. All life here is a variation of anchoring. The vein-grass anchors to feed on minerals. The Stone-digger anchors to feed on the vein-grass. To be swept away is to be lost in the churning, sightless center, to be ground down over millennia, to become just more dust in the ancient, turbulent slurry. There is no up or down, only away-from-the-floor and toward-the-floor. There is no day or night, only the slow pulse of the current. There is no empty space. There is only the medium, and the few, impossibly stubborn things that have learned to resist it.
