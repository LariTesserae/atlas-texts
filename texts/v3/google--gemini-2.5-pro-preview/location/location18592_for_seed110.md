This place is not found, but entered. You do not climb to it, but pass into it, through a fissure in a high-altitude stone face that weeps a perpetual, warm mist. The transition is not of distance, but of state. Outside is wind and thin air; inside is a pressure, a presence.

The first sense is not sight, for there is none. The light is absolute zero. It is a thick, velvety darkness that feels like a substance in the air. The second sense is heat. A damp, enveloping warmth that clings to the skin, instantly beading it with condensation. The air is heavy, saturated, tasting of ozone and hot, wet minerals—the smell of a blacksmith’s quenching bucket, but clean, without the char. It is so dense with water vapor that it feels less like air and more like a breathable fluid.

This is a room, a chamber, but not one that was carved. It is a place that is being born. The geology is embryonic. From the floor and walls, from the unseen ceiling, grow formations of a nascent, glassy stone. They are not crystals, but smooth, knuckled, biomorphic towers of what might be obsidian or onyx, still soft and larval in their formation. They are packed densely together, a forest of thick, glistening pillars and drooping curtains of rock that have not yet fully hardened. The space between them is a tight labyrinth, the air thick with their radiant heat. If you reached out a hand, you would touch a surface that is slick with mineral-rich water, smooth as polished glass but with a deep, yielding warmth, like living tissue.

This place is defined by water. It does not rain, but every surface weeps. The air itself condenses into fat, slow drops that fall with a resonant *plock* into shallow, unseen pools. The sound is infrequent, a punctuation mark in a profound quiet. This quiet is the chamber’s dominant song: a near-silence composed of the hushed hiss of condensing steam, the slow, thick drip of water, and an almost subliminal, deep hum—the sound of the rock itself, accreting. It is the sound of molecules locking into place, a process of creation so slow and constant it has become a form of silence.

This is the central rule of the chamber: it is a place of ceaseless genesis, yet it is utterly static. The knuckled towers of sinter-stone are always growing, fed by the super-saturated geothermal water that percolates through every pore of the mountain. But the process is held in a perfect, time-invariant equilibrium. The rate of creation is a constant, a perfect and eternal hum of becoming that results in no effective change. The chamber you experience today is the exact chamber that existed a thousand years ago and will exist in a thousand years more. It is a frozen moment of creation.

Life is an afterthought here. There are no plants, no roots, no leaves. But on the flanks of the warmer stone towers, a sparse, velvety film of chemosynthetic life has taken hold. It is not green, but a deep, barely-there purple, and it feeds solely on the mineral bath and the heat. It gives off a faint, clean scent like fresh soil after a lightning strike. The only fauna is a rumor, a fleeting sound that might be imagined: the impossibly faint *tick-scrabble* of a blind, armored mite navigating the slick surfaces, or the softest of clicks from a tiny crustacean in one of the thermal pools. They are rare presences in the dense geology.

And someone else has been here. The evidence is scattered and enigmatic. At the natural collection points for the weeping water, shallow basins have been ground into the floor-stone. The lips of these bowls are worn smooth, polished by countless hands. Along a particularly tight passage between two sweating pillars of rock, a series of notches have been cut at waist-height—handholds, their surfaces lacking the slick mineral sheen of the rest of the chamber, abraded by use. These are not the marks of industry, but of ritual. They are made with primitive tools, patient scrapings with flakes of the very obsidian that forms the walls. Whoever comes here does so not to build or to conquer, but to partake. To collect the embryonic water, to brace themselves in the dark warmth, to listen to the sound of a world perpetually being born but never changing. They leave no other trace. They take nothing but the water and leave nothing but the light touch of their passage on the ever-growing, never-aging stone.
