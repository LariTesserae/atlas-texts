The pressure is a constant, gentle embrace. The warmth is uniform, sinking into you not like sunlight but like blood heat, the temperature of a living thing. You are submerged. The medium is not quite water; it's denser, with a silken, slightly viscous quality that damps all movement and muffles all sound. It’s a place of profound, absorbent quiet.

There is no up or down, only the faintest vector of drift, a current so slow it is a theory more than a sensation. And against that drift, the world resolves. It is not a landscape of stone or soil. It is made. Every surface is worked. This is a colossal, single-piece artifact of fired and fused sediment, a city of unending ceramic. High, yet contained, it is a single, complex structure rising from unseen depths into this warm, dark stratum of the great liquid column. Imagine a termite mound the size of a cathedral, but crafted by potters over millennia.

The architecture is a product of accretion and erosion. There are no straight lines. Massive, hollow pillars, thick as ancient trees, swell and merge, riddled with openings. They support wide, vaulted ceilings that are themselves the floors of other chambers above. Walkways spiral around the pillars, worn into deep, smooth concavities by the passage of countless ages. Every surface is smooth to the touch, not with the polish of a machine, but with the profound wear of eons of slow-flowing liquid and the friction of soft bodies. You can see, in the oldest and most protected alcoves, the memory of its construction: the prints of soft-palmed hands, the scrape-marks of simple tools, smoothed over and almost ghosted, like fossils of the act of making.

It is a dark place. The only light is generated by its inhabitants. It is not for illumination but for communication, or perhaps it is merely an unconscious biological process. Slow pulses of cerulean and viridian light drift through the darkness, the signature of life. These motes of soft color reveal the sheer density of the place. The liquid is thick with them, tiny creatures like living dust, swimming in lazy, spiraling patterns.

And on the ceramic surfaces, the builders.

They are the abundant fauna, the reason for this place. They are slow, deliberate beings, perfectly adapted to the pressure and the dark. Their bodies are a paradox of hard and soft—a heavy, ammonite-spiral shell of a pearlescent, blue-black material, into which they can fully retreat. From the opening of the shell extends a cluster of pale, boneless tentacles, constantly working, testing, and shaping. They are the Weavers, the Masons, the Choir of the deep.

You do not hear them so much as feel them. A low, percussive vibration transmitted through the ceramic structure is the sound of a shell knocking against a wall. A faint scraping, deadened almost to nothing by the soupy medium, is the sound of their tools. These tools are simple, fashioned from the hardened, cast-off shells of their dead: sharp-edged scrapers, fine-pointed styluses, and cupped scoops.

The rules of this world are simple and slow. There is no vegetation; a photosynthetic ecosystem is impossible in this eternal dark. The builders do not eat in any way you would recognize. They are living filters. They slowly, methodically, scrape the loose sediment and mineral-rich sludge from the surfaces of their city, a sludge that settles out of the dense, nutrient-heavy liquid over decades. They draw this sludge into their soft bodies, filtering what they need for energy. Their waste is a fine, clay-like particulate, which they mix with a secreted bonding agent. This is their ceramic paste.

This is the cycle. They clean their home, and in doing so, gather the materials to build it ever larger, ever higher. A Mason will spend its entire life—a cycle which may last centuries—tending to a single small section of wall. It will smooth the surface, patch a microscopic crack with a dab of fresh paste, or add a single, thumb-sized rib to a vaulted ceiling, slowly extending the pattern laid down by its ancestors a thousand generations ago. Their bioluminescent glows pulse in rhythm with this work: a soft blue while filtering, a brighter green when secreting the binder for new construction.

The structure is not hollow in the way a building is, but porous, like a sponge. The walls are a labyrinth of tiny tunnels and chambers, a vast and layered ecosystem. Smaller, chit-shelled creatures, like scuttling jewels, inhabit these pores, feeding on the builders’ waste or on the ambient microbes in the liquid. Slow, ribbon-like predators, blind and pale, glide through the larger archways, hunting the builders with a patience that can outlast a decade. Their movements are the only source of suddenness in this world, a brief, silent flurry in the unending calm.

This is not a place of grand events. It is a place of process. It is a single, immense, living artifact, an act of construction so ancient and continuous that it has become a geological force. It is a city built by its own inhabitants, for its own inhabitants, from its own inhabitants, growing ever so slowly upward into the warm, dark, silent, and crowded expanse.
