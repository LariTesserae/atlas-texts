You are inside a pillar of warm, thick liquid.

The pressure is gentle but absolute, a constant embrace from a medium denser than water, something like a thin, warm gelatin. The world is a vertical cylinder, no more than a hundred feet across, but its height is unknowable. Looking up, you see only the shimmering attenuation of light through the fluid column, a fade into hazy, golden-green distance. Looking down, the view is the same, an abyss of identical, temperate broth. There is no surface; there is no floor. There is only the chamber.

The walls are not rock, but a black, vitrified slag-ceramic, constructed and now fantastically old. The material is smooth to the touch, worn into soft, vertical channels by the slow, eternal circulation of the fluid. Eons of this gentle, persistent flow have sculpted it, not with the violence of a canyon-carving river, but with the patient polishing of a liquid thumb rubbing a worry stone for a million years. The wall is a single, seamless, cylindrical piece, and it feels as ancient and final as cooled magma. Embedded within it, running from the unseen depths to the unseen heights, are stripes of a milky, glowing material that shed a soft, daylight-equivalent golden light throughout the column. They have been on for longer than civilizations have existed.

This entire structure is a machine. A deep, resonant, and profoundly loud hum vibrates through the fluid, felt in your bones more than heard with your ears. It is the sound of a colossal engine, a heart miles below, whose purpose is to pump this life-giving broth in a perpetual, slow-motion cycle. The sound is layered: beneath the bass-note hum of the primary engine is the loud, rhythmic *glug-thump* of massive valves opening and closing, and the constant hiss of filtration and aeration from grilles set flush with the walls. The entire pillar is a lung, and you are floating inside it.

Despite the utterly artificial nature of the container, life is lush, but strangely distributed. It does not cling everywhere. At regular intervals of fifty feet, delicate, spidery lattices of rusted-green metal jut out from the walls, forming sparse platforms and rings. On these, vegetation thrives. Thick, rubbery vines, like verdigris-furred kelp, anchor themselves to the metal and spiral down into the broth, their broad, translucent leaves angling toward the glowing wall-strips. Patches of moss, so bright they appear to be secondary light sources, grow in fuzzy hemispheres on the pipe joints. High above, where the fluid thins into a super-saturated fog, colonies of air-ferns drip condensed broth back into the column. There is vast space between these nodes of life, long vertical stretches of nothing but humming fluid and black ceramic.

And the space is full of movement. Schools of fish, with skeletons of translucent glass and fins like spun silver, navigate not by sight but by sensing the subtle pressure changes from the great pump. They are numberless, a constant flicker of motion in the middle distance. Larger creatures, like six-foot-long axolotls with articulated, armored plates instead of skin, slowly climb the ceramic walls, their sucker-tipped claws finding purchase in the eroded grooves. They are janitors, scraping films of algae and mineral deposits with rasping jaws, their munching adding a quiet, rhythmic crunch to the soundscape. Occasionally, one will let go, tucking its limbs in to become a streamlined torpedo, gliding silently down to a lower level.

There are predators. Fast, serpentine things with bodies of coiled, muscular copper wire hunt the glass-fish, their movements a shocking burst of speed in the otherwise stately environment. They strike, there is a flurry, and then the slow calm returns. The ecosystem is active, balanced, and entirely dependent on the machine that contains it. The broth is cycled, filtered of waste by unseen mechanisms, re-oxygenated, and infused with nutrients that feed the moss, which is then grazed upon by armored snails the size of fists, whose shells are in turn harvested by the armored axolotls for some unknown purpose.

Everything here is part of the cycle. This is not a place; it is a process. A single, contained, self-sustaining engine of life, hidden away, perhaps buried deep within a planet’s crust or sealed inside a hollow moon. It is an organ, ancient and tireless, performing its one function: to beat, to breathe, to circulate, to live. Its loudness is the sound of its own enduring existence. You are a blood cell in a vein of a forgotten god, a creature so vast and ancient its own working parts have become a world.
