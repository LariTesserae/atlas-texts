This is a world defined not by what it has, but by what it has lost. There is no water. Not a drop, not a molecule bound in ice, not a wisp of vapor. The very concept of liquidity is alien here. The world is a high, vast tableland, a continent-sized plateau of such extreme elevation that it has pushed itself into the upper, thinner layers of atmosphere. Below its edges, which fall away in cliffs that defy measurement, lies a sea of denser, unbreathable gases, a permanent undercast that churns miles down.

The surface of this plateau is ancient. The erosion that shaped it was not the work of water or wind as we know them, but of time itself, of aeons of cosmic radiation, of the slow sublimation of less-stable minerals, leaving behind a virtually eternal bedrock. It is a landscape worn down to its final, most stable form. The ground is a near-flat expanse of sintered, metallic dust, compacted into a surface as smooth and cool as polished slate. There are no mountains, only gentle, continent-spanning swells and depressions, like a frozen ocean of stone with waves a thousand miles crest to crest. The color of this world is muted grey, the deep charcoal of the ground reflecting the even, passionless white light of a distant, oversized sun. The light imparts no warmth, only clarity. Shadows are stark, long, and razor-edged.

Silence is the rule. The atmosphere is too thin to carry sound more than a few feet. What passes for noise is felt more than heard: a low-frequency hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet, a resonant thrum that lives in your bones.

But this ancient, settled place is not calm. It is host to a permanent, violent turbulence. This is the Gyre.

Across the vastness, scattered at intervals of hundreds of miles, are the pylons. They are the only true structures here. They are not grand towers, but matte-black, featureless cylinders, perhaps thirty feet high, that rise seamlessly from the stone plain. They absorb all light, appearing as holes in reality. They are artifacts of a technology so far beyond modern that they may as well be principles of physics. These pylons are the nodes of the Gyre. They generate and manipulate immense, invisible fields of force.

The Gyre is the weather of this world. It is a constant storm, not of wind or rain, but of kinetic and gravitational tides. A storm front is not a dark cloud, but a shimmering in the air, a visible distortion of space like heat haze over a summer road. As it approaches, the ever-present hum in the ground rises in pitch. Loose pebbles, if there were any, would begin to drift and levitate. To be caught in a strong passage of the Gyre is to be subject to chaos. Gravity might halve, then double, then shift forty-five degrees, trying to pull you sideways across the slick ground. Bursts of kinetic energy, like invisible fists, can strike from nowhere, carrying enough force to pulverize stone. Streams of exotic particles scour the surface, leaving faint, iridescent trails that fade over hours.

This is the new erosion. The Gyre is slowly but inexorably grinding down the already-ancient world, polishing its swells and scouring its shallows.

Life here is an answer to these conditions. It is sparse, clinging to existence in the calmer "eddies" of the Gyre. There is vegetation, of a sort. It is not green, but opalescent. Patches of "Filament Moss" grow in fractal patterns dictated by the residual energy currents. They are crystalline structures, fine as spun glass, that draw sustenance directly from the particle flows of the Gyre. They are radiotrophs, feeding on the storm. When a current passes through them, they chime, not with audible sound, but with flashes of internal, sympathetic light, a silent conversation of pulsing colors.

Fauna is rarer still. The dominant animal form is the Skimmer. It resembles a great, translucent manta ray, fifty feet from wingtip to wingtip, its body little more than a network of shimmering membranes stretched over a cartilaginous frame. They do not fly so much as ride the gravitational anomalies of the Gyre, their vast surfaces acting as sails in the invisible currents. Their skin ripples with captured energy. They drift for days in the high, thin air, descending to graze on the filament moss, their wide, toothless mouths scooping up the brittle crystals. They are almost entirely silent, their only communication being slow, deliberate patterns of bioluminescence that flash across their bodies. To see one is an event. To see a dozen, gathered in a deep basin where the Gyre's currents are gentle, is a miracle.

And there is one other structure. In the center of a basin a thousand miles across, a place noted for its relative energetic calm, there is a single, low building. It is not a pylon. It is a long, black rectangle, half-buried in the metallic dust, its surface showing the same light-absorbing quality. A single opening, a perfect square, leads into its unlit interior. There are no signs of its builders, no tracks, no debris. It seems as ancient as the pylons, yet distinct, as though it were an observation post or a control room for the system that runs this world. It is the only suggestion of intent, the quiet ghost of a question mark sitting in the heart of the storm. Inside, the hum of the Gyre is gone. There is only absolute silence, and a stillness so profound it feels like the end of time.
