This is a place of high, flat stone and perpetual damp. You stand on the edge of a great mesa, or perhaps a series of them, stepping down into a misty immensity. The air is warm and heavy, saturated with water. It is not raining, but everything is wet. The air itself feels on the verge of becoming a liquid, condensing on your skin, on every surface, in a clinging, silent dew. The light is pearlescent, diffused by a high, thin cloud layer that seems to be the source of this endless humidity. There is no direct sun, only a uniform, gentle brightness that casts no sharp shadows.

The ground is a pavement of dark, sharp-edged rock. It is not basalt, not granite, but something else, a material with the colour of wet slate and the texture of freshly broken obsidian. Its features are young and uneroded. Fractures are clean, angles are acute. Along these fracture lines and from a million microscopic pores, water constantly, silently weeps. It is not a gushing flow, but a slow, geological lactation. The stone itself seems to produce the water, bleeding it into the world. This water collects on the vast, almost-level surfaces, forming immense, shallow pools that perfectly mirror the silver-white sky. The pools are rarely more than a foot deep, vast sheets of still water over a floor of razor-sharp stone. To walk here is to wade.

This is a world of two textures: the hard, unforgiving clarity of the weeping stone, and the deep, verdant softness of life. Vegetation is lush but sparse, existing only in defined, curated-seeming spaces. Not in wide-open fields, but in great patches and islands. In naturally occurring depressions where a few inches of silt and crushed stone have formed a basis for soil, colossal ferns rise, their fronds the size of small boats. Thick, pillowy mosses of an impossible emerald green carpet the edges of these islands, trailing into the shallow water. There are groves of plants that resemble giant, fleshy succulents, their leaves thick and waxy to hold the abundant moisture, arranged in perfect geometric spirals. There are no flowers. There is no pollen on the air. There are no insects to pollinate them. Reproduction is a silent, patient affair of spores, runners, and division.

And within this landscape of black stone, green life, and silver water, there is a city. It is not built *upon* the mesa; it is grown *from* it. The inhabitants, whoever they are, have mastered the strange geology of this place. The settlement is a network of low, terraced structures that follow the natural contours of the land. The walls are the same dark, weeping stone as the ground, because they are one and the same. The builders learned that by carving shallow channels and forms into the rock with hand-tools of bone and harder stone, they could guide its slow, inexorable growth. Over generations, a groove becomes a channel, a channel becomes a wall, a walled enclosure becomes a room.

The city is layered. The lowest, most ancient levels are smoother, the sharp angles of the nascent rock softened by centuries of passage and the ceaseless flow of water. These are the foundations, the deep cisterns, the quiet public plazas that are more water than stone. The upper, newer levels are still sharp, the mark of the rock-wright's shaping tool still visible. The roofs are flat, and many are home to cultivated gardens of moss, thick as fleece, which act as living sponges, absorbing the airborne moisture and providing a soft surface amidst the hardness. There is no wood, no metal, but ropes of woven fern-fibre are used to rig awnings of a water-repellent, leathery leaf, creating dry alcoves and covered walkways. The only colour, aside from the green of plants and the grey-black of stone, comes from dyes made of crushed lichen and moss, creating muted earth-toned patterns on the few woven textiles.

The most profound feature of this place is its silence. There are no animals. No birds call from the mist. No insects buzz in the humid air. No fish or amphibians stir the shallow pools. No scavengers pick at the fallen. A fern frond that dies simply falls into the water and begins a slow, quiet process of dissolution, a gradual unmaking into the mineral soup. The world is without chorus, without chatter. The only sounds are those of the place itself: the almost inaudible *hiss* of water condensing on a million surfaces; the slow, solemn *drip* of an overhang into a deeper pool; the very occasional, distant *crack* as a temperature differential causes a new fissure in the ever-growing stone. The sounds of the city's inhabitants—a footfall splashing in a shallow puddle, the scrape of a stone tool, a hushed voice—are swallowed instantly by the damp, heavy air, leaving no echo.

This world is in equilibrium. It does not change. There are no seasons. The temperature is constant, the light is constant, the water is constant. The rate of the stone's growth is perfectly balanced by an unknown force. The vegetation lives, grows, and dies, but its overall mass on the mesa remains the same. A fern dies here; a new one sprouts there. Time here does not feel like a river, but a pool. It is deep, it is present, but it does not flow. To live here would be to sink into a profound, contemplative stillness, to see existence not as a journey, but as a state of being. It is a world of immense, quiet, and unending presence.
