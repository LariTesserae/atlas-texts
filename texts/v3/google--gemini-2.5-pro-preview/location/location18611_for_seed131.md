The sound is a physical thing. It is not something you hear with your ears so much as feel with your bones, a pressure on the skull and a vibration in the teeth. It is a monolithic, multi-layered drone, a million ton-weight presses stamping in unison, the roar of a blast furnace the size of a city, the thrum of a power station generating electricity for a dead continent. The noise is so complete and unchanging that it has become the definition of silence. It is the baseline from which no deviation is possible. There is no sound *in* the room; there is only the sound *of* the room.

The air is thick, warm, and wet enough to drink. You breathe it in gulps. It condenses on any surface cooler than itself, which is nothing. Instead, water simply resolves out of the atmosphere, beading on every surface like sweat. The light is flat and shadowless, a perfect, unwavering daylight that pours from a ceiling you cannot quite see, its source lost in the glare and the humid haze.

This entire world is a room, and not a large one. Perhaps twenty feet across, a perfect circle. But it feels cavernous, hollow. It is a space defined by what is not there. The floor is a lattice of iron grating, the bars worn impossibly smooth, like river pebbles. Every intersection where bolts once were is now a soft, fused lump of metal. The grid is ancient, polished to a treacherous gloss by unimaginable time and the constant, dripping moisture. Through the gaps in the grate, there is only still, black water, its depth unknowable. It does not move.

From this floor, a cage of arcing girders rises to form a dome overhead. Like the floor, the metal is eroded to a final, stable form. All edges are round. All surfaces are smooth. There are no signs of rust, only the dark, burnished patina of a material that has found its equilibrium with the hot, wet air. The structure is skeletal, a framework that contains mostly empty volume. This is the source of the emptiness: the room is all outline and no substance.

Clinging to this metal skeleton is the vegetation. It is not so much growing as it *is*. Thick, waxy-leafed vines, the color of jade and malachite, wrap themselves around the girders in patterns that seem both random and inevitable. Their leaves are enormous, platter-sized, and perpetually heavy with moisture. From the lowest point of every leaf, a single, fat drop of water forms, swells, and falls. The collective, silent patter of a million falling drops is utterly consumed by the roar. Enormous, pillow-like mats of moss, so dense they look like solid green velvet, cushion the joints of the structure. They are host to nothing; no insects crawl on them, no spores drift from them. They are simply there. The plant life is in its final, most perfect state, a climax community that has nowhere left to go. It doesn't spread or wither; it just persists.

Everything is constructed. The floor grate, the dome of girders, the light source above, the unseen basin of water below. At the very apex of the dome, directly overhead, is the heart of the place and the source of the noise. It is a massive, featureless housing of the same worn metal, a sphere perhaps six feet in diameter. There are no seams, no vents, no controls. It is utterly impassive. But it vibrates with a ferocity that makes the whole structure hum, a deep, resonant frequency that is the core of the deafening sound. This is the Engine, the Regulator. It is the industrial sun of this tiny cosmos. It generates the heat, pumps the water from the reservoir below to mist invisibly from its own surface, powers the light, and fills the world with its eternal, unchanging voice.

There is no life here, save the plants. No fish stir the black water. No amphibians slick the mosses. No birds or mammals could survive the sound. It is a world without fauna, a sterile, warm, wet, screaming green-and-metal womb.

The one rule of this place is that nothing ever happens. The temperature does not waiver. The light does not dim. The sound does not modulate. The water does not cease to drip. It is a perfectly stable, closed system, an engine running at full-throttle in a vacuum of purpose. It might have been a terrarium for a species now extinct, a life-support module for a ship that crashed before the mountains were dust, or an atmospheric processor for a complex that has long since eroded into nothingness around it. Its original function is irrelevant. Its current state is all that is, and all that will be. It is a pocket of engineered permanence, roaring its own existence to itself for eternity.
