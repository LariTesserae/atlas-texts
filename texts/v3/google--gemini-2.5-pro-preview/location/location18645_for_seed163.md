The world is a drum, and you are standing on its skin.

The ground is not soil or rock, but a single, continuous living mat. It is a form of vegetation so ancient and dense it has become its own substrate, a holophyte plain stretching across a high-altitude plateau. To the eye, it is an expanse of rolling, interwoven fibres in shades of deep forest green, bruised purple, and wet, gleaming black. The texture underfoot is strange: firm, like packed earth, but with a slight, unnerving give, a cellular springiness. This is the Lignic Mass. It is saturated. Water does not run over its surface in streams but is wept from it continuously. Every fibre and nodule glistens, beaded with condensation pulled from the cool, thin air. Step firmly, and a small pool of crystal-clear, cold water wells up around your boot before being slowly reabsorbed. The landscape is embryonic, its geology still under construction; the very ground is in a state of perpetual, slow-motion becoming.

Above, the sky is a paradox of light. It is overwhelmingly bright, a near-total whiteness, as if you are inside a frosted lightbulb the size of a continent. There is no visible sun, no directional glare, just a shadowless, omnidirectional blast of white light that flattens perspective and makes distance difficult to judge. This immense luminosity is generated by the high elevation and thin atmosphere, yet it is diffused by the same pervasive humidity that soaks the ground, creating a bright, glaring fog that is both radiant and chillingly damp.

The world is characterized by a profound sparsity of features. For miles upon miles, the only feature is the rolling, weeping surface of the Lignic Mass itself. But this emptiness is punctuated by presences of an almost unbelievable scale. These are the Calyxes, the true mountains of this world. They are gargantuan, slow-blooming floral structures, each one dozens of miles from its nearest neighbour. They rise from the living plain not as separate entities, but as differentiated growths of the same underlying organism. Their emergence is the only true geological event here a process that unfolds over millennia. A new Calyx begins as a barely-perceptible swelling in the Mass, taking centuries to rise into a ribbed, bud-like monolith that towers thousands of feet into the bright sky. Their "petals"—thicker than fortress walls and miles long—unfurl at a rate that is imperceptible to the naked eye. Their surfaces are slick with the same atmospheric condensation, coursing down their colossal facades in weeping waterfalls that mist away into the air long before they reach the ground. They are utterly silent.

This profound, organic silence is violated. It is crushed, obliterated by a sound that is both deafening and constant. It is not the chaotic roar of a storm or a volcano, but the monolithic, overwhelming hum of industry. It is a sound with physical weight, a pressure on the bones and a vibration in the teeth. It is the sound of production at an incomprehensible scale.

The source of the sound are the Taps. They are squat, brutalist structures of oxidized metal and stained plasteel, scattered across the plain as sparsely as the Calyxes they ignore. Each Tap is a self-contained industrial facility, a lone building anchored deep into the living ground. There are no roads connecting them, no visible infrastructure beyond the thick, armored conduits that snake from their bases into the Lignic Mass. From vents high on their geometric shells, they pulse out clouds of clean, white steam that join the atmospheric haze.

These Taps are harvesting the world. Their technology is sonic. They are Resonance Drills, vast engines that generate a low-frequency hum tuned to a specific harmonic of the Lignic Mass. This deafening vibration liquefies the ancient, energy-dense starches and sugars stored deep within the planetary organism, a process akin to cellular-scale fracking. The resulting slurry—an opalescent, viscous fluid known as Phloem Nectar—is pumped up, refined on-site, and stored. The sound is the side effect of this violent liquefaction, a sonic wound inflicted on the body of the world. It is so powerful that it scrubs the air of all other noises. The drip of water, the sigh of wind, the groan of your own tired body—all are consumed by the monolithic drone.

There is almost no other life here. No insects buzz, no birds call, no creatures rustle in the fibrous undergrowth. The ecosystem is brutally simple: the single, planetary plant, and the scattered, parasitic industry that feeds on it. The fauna is you, and the unseen operators sealed within the Taps.

To exist here is to be caught between two infinities. In one direction, a silent, growing Calyx, a monument of life so patient its movement is a geological event. In another, a squat, metal Tap, a bastion of furious, unceasing noise. You stand in the vast, open space between them, on the wet skin of the world. The air is cool and tastes of ozone and wet vegetation. Water beads on your face. The light is a brilliant, clinical white. And the sound—the ceaseless, crushing, industrial hum—presses in from all sides, the eternal and deafening heartbeat of this place.
