This is the Subterranean Gyre, a hollow space within the world's crust, a void so vast it is a biome of its own. It is a place defined by a single, colossal organism.

**The Place and its Substance**

There is no ground here. No soil, no rock, no floor. Your entire world is a multi-dimensional, interconnected lattice of living matter. Imagine roots, vines, and fungal hyphae woven together on a continental scale. This plant-like structure, the Wyrmwood, fills the void not with mass, but with presence. It is both everything and almost nothing. Strands as thick as mountain ranges crisscross a gulf of empty air, branching into smaller and smaller threads until they form web-like walls and ephemeral floors. The structure is dense in its completeness, yet the volume is almost entirely empty space. To fall here is not to fall onto a surface, but to fall *through* the world, through ever-finer nets of life, for miles and miles until the lattice becomes too thick to permit passage.

The Wyrmwood itself is a strange substance. It is not soft or green. It is cool to the touch, with the texture of unpolished quartz, slightly yielding under pressure. Its outer skin is a mosaic of pale, opalescent colors—ivory white, faint lilac, ghost-green—but its core glows with a brilliant, cold, internal light. This is the source of all illumination. The Gyre is perpetually bright, bathed in the silent, soul-piercing blue-white glare of its own substance. The light is not warm; it is the clean, sterile light of a chemical process, and it casts sharp, geometrically perfect shadows.

**Atmosphere and Sensation**

The air is cold and carries a pervasive dampness. This is not the wetness of rain, but the constant, cool exhalation of the Wyrewood itself. A fine mist of aerosolized nutrients and water vapor hangs in the void, condensing on every surface. To touch a strand of the lattice is to feel a film of pure, cold moisture on your fingertips. The taste of the air is of ozone, chilled stone, and a faint, electric tang of crushed green stems. Vast, slow currents of this air move through the Gyre, a form of internal weather, creating breezes that whisper through the finer parts of the lattice and moan like ship's rigging across the titanic causeways.

Despite the scale and the constant change, the Gyre is a place of profound quiet. There are no birds to sing, no rivers to rush. The primary sound is a deep, sub-audible thrum that you feel in your bones—the vibration of the Wyrmwood's slow, inexorable growth. This background hum is punctuated by sharp, clean sounds that travel for miles in the clear air. Far off, a high-pitched *ping* as a tensioned strand of Wyrmwood, a mile in diameter, finally snaps. A soft, rustling cascade as a web-wall, its purpose served, dissolves into glowing dust. A faint, rhythmic scraping, barely audible, which might be a living thing, or might just be two strands rubbing against each other in the slow dance of the Gyre.

**The Great Churning**

This world is young because it is never old. The Gyre is a storm in slow motion. It is a place of turbulent creation and destruction, a self-devouring, self-birthing architecture. This is its core rule: nothing is permanent. A bridge of living wood that has spanned a thousand-mile chasm for a generation might suddenly brighten, its internal light flaring, before a stress fracture races along its length. It will part not with a roar, but with a series of silent, percussive jolts, and the two broken ends will drift slowly into the void.

But destruction is simply rearrangement. From the broken stumps, new tendrils will already be growing, questing through the emptiness. Elsewhere, a new trunk, impossibly vast, might be crystallizing out of the nutrient haze, thickening and solidifying over weeks, forging a new connection in the lattice. This is the source of the world's "sharp features"—every broken edge is a fresh wound, every new growth a pristine creation. Maps are not records of geography, but predictions of flow.

**Life and its Traces**

Fauna is exceptionally rare. The Wyrmwood itself is the ecosystem. Tiny, shimmering mites, like flakes of mica, crawl along the strands, etching microscopic paths as they graze on the mineral exudations. They are the base of a phantom-short food chain. Drifting through the deepest voids are the Void-Sails, beings that resemble diaphanous manta rays the size of small hills. They have no visible means of propulsion, but ride the air currents, filtering the nutrient mist through vast, glowing membranes. They are silent, shy, and almost never seen.

And then there are the builders. Their marks are faint but unmistakable. Here, a series of handholds have been chipped into the side of a vertical trunk, the primitive tool-marks sharp and clean. There, dangling from a stable nexus where several great strands meet, is a woven pod. It is made from the finer, more pliable fibers of the Wyrmwood, lashed together with rope of the same material. These shelters are temporary, built to last a season or a cycle, then abandoned as the Gyre shifts around them. You might find a discarded tool—a flake of hardened Wyrmwood, knapped to a razor edge. Or a small shrine: a collection of unusually bright Wyrmwood crystals, arranged in a spiral on a small, woven platform, a quiet offering to the living world itself.

To live here is to be a creature of three dimensions, a nomad of the vertical. There is no concept of a "path" as a static thing, only of a "current" in the ceaseless flow of growth. You do not own the ground beneath your feet, for it is alive, and it may not be there tomorrow. You learn to read the light, to see the tell-tale brightening that precedes a cataclysmic snap, to feel the subtle shift in the Wyrmwood's hum that signals new growth. You live in a state of perpetual, quiet vigilance, suspended in a cold, bright, and silent cathedral that is forever tearing itself down and building itself anew.
