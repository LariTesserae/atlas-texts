This is a place of fundamental sound.

The sound is not an event, but a condition of existence, like air or gravity. It is the first and last thing you perceive. It is a chord of impossible complexity, a staggering wash of overtones and bass notes that vibrates not in the ears but in the bones of the skull, the cartilage of the ribs, the fillings in your teeth. It is a deafening, crystalline hum that holds the pressure of a great depth of water. There is no escape from it; to plug one's ears would be like trying to escape a flood by holding your breath. The sound is inside you. Because of its utter constancy, with no ebb or flow, no change in pitch or volume, the mind begins to treat it as silence. After a time, you can hear the tiny scrape of your own boot on the stone, a sound impossibly small and sharp within the ocean of the Great Hum.

You are standing on the roof of the world, a vast plateau of worn-down mountains. These are not jagged, youthful peaks but ancient, tired landforms, their spines eroded by eons of a force that was not wind or water. The terrain is made of a pale, quartz-veined stone that glitters with unbearable intensity under a sun that is a white-hot hole in a violet-black sky. The light is so bright, so stripped of atmospheric haze, that it feels sterile and cold. It carries no warmth, only information. Shadows are not pools of grey but absolute voids, slivers of pure blackness sliced into the world.

The landscape is open, a panorama of rolling, stone highlands that extend to a horizon blurred only by distance, not by weather. The air is knife-thin and perpetually cool, the kind of cold that makes the lungs ache. It is also utterly, unnervingly still. There is no wind. Not a breeze, not a thermal, nothing. The world is held in a state of perfect, ringing equilibrium.

Because of this stillness and the cool stone, moisture condenses directly from the thin air onto the rock surfaces. The world is damp, not wet. Every stone is slick with a perpetual, fine dew. In the hollows and basins worn into the rock, this cosmic sweat collects into shallow pools of perfectly clear, perfectly still water. These pools act as flawless mirrors, reflecting the piercing sun and the dark, star-dusted daytime sky.

There is almost no vegetation. On the shaded sides of the scalloped rock formations, where the direct glare is weakest, are patches of black lichen, hard as enamel. In the deepest crevices, fed by the constant condensation, grows a type of moss that is silvery and metallic, resembling frost more than anything alive. These are the only producers in this ecosystem.

And yet, the place is teeming with fauna. They are the source of the only movement in this static world. They are creatures of chitin and resonance.

Herds of beings, the size of large dogs, move in slow, deliberate processions across the stone fields. Their bodies are low to the ground, covered in overlapping plates of a glassy, translucent material that shimmers with internal, geometric patterns. They have no visible mouths or sensory organs, save for vast, tympanic membranes stretched across their backs, which ripple and tremble minutely with the Great Hum. They are not grazing on the lichen, but are instead seeking something else in the stone itself. They are sonovores. They eat the sound.

The entire plateau vibrates, but not uniformly. Certain mineral veins, certain crystalline structures, resonate with specific frequencies within the Great Hum—pure, potent notes of energy. The creatures, the Chime-Walkers, seek these notes. One will find a promising outcrop and begin to tap at it with a tool: a shard of harder stone, knapped to a simple, effective point. They tap not to break, but to tune. A small chip flies off, and the resonant frequency of the rock shifts slightly. The herd grows still, their back-membranes taut. They are listening, tasting the tone. They will spend days chipping away at a single boulder, sculpting it, not for form, but for its sound, until they have refined its note into a pure, sustaining tone they can absorb. This is their sustenance. This is their technology.

Smaller, insect-like creatures fly through the airless-feeling void between the stones. They are all wing and membrane, iridescent skimmers that look like shards of stained glass. They do not flap, but are propelled by vibrating the edges of their wings at specific frequencies, riding the harmonics of the Great Hum like surfers on a wave. They swarm around the larger creatures, feeding on the "crumbs" of sound—the chaotic, less-pure frequencies scattered by the Chime-Walkers' carving.

There is no predation here in the conventional sense. Death comes from dissonance. A creature whose resonating plates become cracked or whose membranes are torn can no longer properly align with the Great Hum. It loses its ability to feed and slowly, quietly, starves. Its body falls still, and in the unchanging chill, it doesn't decay, but flakes away over decades, its chitinous plates breaking down into glittering dust that settles in the cracks of the humming stone. The world is stable, eternal, and crushingly loud. Life is a process not of consumption, but of attunement.
