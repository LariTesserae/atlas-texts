This is a world defined by the push of water. It is not an ocean, not a sea, but a continuous, planet-spanning hydrological event, a current without a surface or a shore. The pressure is immense, the water dense with minerals and cold enough to ache in the bones of creatures not born to it. To be here is to be submerged in the heart of a permanent, continent-sized storm.

The terrain is a mountain range of impossible age. These are not the jagged peaks of young, tectonic worlds. These are the worn, final forms of mountains that have been subject to the scouring of turbulent water for eons. Their colossal shoulders and spines are rounded, smoothed, and deeply grooved by millennia of ceaseless flow. They are composed of a pressure-forged, ultra-dense basalt, nearly black and polished to a dull sheen, the only material that could withstand such erosion. These peaks rise for miles from an unseen, abyssal floor, their summits lost in the dense, swirling medium far above. They are less mountains and more gargantuan stones in a world-river, ancient and unmoving anchors in chaos.

Life, defying the lack of sun, has claimed this world. The slopes of the black mountains are draped in vast, dense forests of chemo-synthetic life. Great curtains of what could be called kelp, but are a thousand times larger, hang from the rock faces. These organisms are not green, but a spectrum of pale golds, ochres, and deep, venous reds. They do not reach for light; they feed on the cocktail of minerals carried on the violent currents. Their broad, ribbon-like fronds, some hundreds of feet long, are in constant, violent motion, a perpetual, silent whip and crack in the water. They are packed so densely that in many places, they form solid, swaying walls of vegetation, their surfaces crawling with smaller symbiotic organisms.

And this vegetation glows. The "normal light" of this world is biological. The forests pulse with a cold, steady, green-gold luminescence. This light reveals the staggering density of the place: the water is thick with particulate matter, shed spores, and drifting clouds of microscopic life, creating a perpetual fog that limits vision to a few miles at best. Within this glowing fog, the currents are made visible, appearing as brighter or darker bands as they warp the light and churn the living motes.

The sound is a constant, foundational roar. It is the sound of a billion tons of water grinding against the ancient rock faces, a bass rumble that vibrates through your entire body. Layered over this is the fricative *shussssss* of the dense forests of vegetation swaying and rubbing against each other, and the incessant, high-frequency chittering of fauna. Life is abundant and loud. Great shoals of armored, crab-like grazers, the size of dinner plates, crawl across the kelp walls, their serrated legs making a collective scraping sound. Torpedo-shaped predators, sleek and silver, hunt in the open channels between the forests, their passage marked by sharp pressure-clicks used for echolocation. The water is thick with movement, with the turbulence an engine of life, constantly delivering food and tossing everything about in a chaotic dance. A school of fish-analogs might be a cohesive unit one moment and a scattered cloud of individuals the next, caught in a sudden vortex.

This is not an untouched world. Scattered across the more stable cliff faces and in the partial lee of the largest peaks are the faint marks of industry. These are not cities, but solitary, heavily-braced structures: Anchor-Stations. Forged from rusted, pitted steel and composites, they are clamped and drilled deep into the primordial basalt. They look like deep-sea oil rigs redesigned by insects, all external trusses and spidery buttresses meant to withstand the colossal, shifting pressures of the current. Huge, ducted propellers on articulated arms whine constantly, their pitch changing as they fight to maintain the station's stability.

From these stations, harsh, artificial light cuts through the soft biological glow. Powerful, blue-white floodlights illuminate patches of the landscape with clinical precision, revealing the raw texture of the rock and the violent thrashing of the plant life. These are industrial zones. Mechanized arms with grinding attachments scrape valuable bioluminescent mosses from the rock. Heavy-duty hoses suck up clouds of nutrient-rich organisms. The thrum of generators and the rhythmic, percussive *clank* of processing machinery adds a metallic, discordant layer to the natural roar. These stations are islands of mechanical order in a world of organic chaos, their existence a constant, noisy struggle.

The world’s rule is simple: everything is subject to the flow. The plants have evolved to bend and feed on it. The animals have evolved to ride it or hide from it. The scattered industrial outposts are built solely to resist it. There is no stillness here. The floor of your habitat shudders. The view out of the thick quartz window is a mesmerizing, terrifying spectacle of constant, powerful motion. The light shifts, the shadows of the kelp forests warp and dance, and the roar never, ever ends. It is a place of extreme age and extreme energy, a primordial landscape being perpetually, violently, and loudly born anew every second.
