There is no surface. There is no air. There is only the medium.

It is not water, not exactly, though it is a liquid of similar density. It is warm, like blood, and feels thick against the skin, a viscous, oxygen-rich gel that presses in from all sides with a gentle, insistent weight. To be here is to be held. Breathing is a passive act, an osmotic exchange through membranes you didn't know you had until they started working. Sound is a deadened, close thing. Your own heartbeat is a dull *thump-thump* in your ears, the only reliable rhythm in a place where time itself feels syrupy and slow.

You are high up. Impossibly, paradoxically high. You are on the flank of a mountain, one of the Great Slopes. These are not jagged, youthful peaks. They are the worn-down knuckles of a forgotten geology, ancient beyond comprehension. Every edge has been smoothed, every cliff face scoured into a gentle, sweeping curve by a billion years of slow currents. The rock itself is a deep, bruised purple-grey, a material so old it has settled into its final, most stable form. It feels soft to the touch, almost like hardened clay, giving up a fine silt that hangs in the gel around you.

The scale is tectonic. The slope you are on descends into a dim abyss, a place of greater pressure and unknowable depth. It rises into a brightening murk, but the peak is a theoretical concept, a direction, not a destination. From this vantage point, you can see other, similar slopes miles away, colossal forms fading into the gloom like a pod of sleeping leviathans. This mountain range is the size of a continent, submerged in an ocean the size of a planet.

And it is a forest.

From the soft, silty floor of the mountain grows a vegetation so lush and dense it chokes the space between the great slopes. They are not trees or kelp. They are Annulet-Stalks, vast, semi-rigid columns of translucent, fibrous growth. They rise hundreds of feet, maybe thousands, their girth that of ancient redwoods. Their surfaces are segmented, like stacks of glowing rings, each segment pulsing with a slow, dim, internal light—a soft, biological luminescence that shifts in color from pale gold to a tender, living green. This is the only light. The collective, synchronized rhythm of the forest's glow creates a slow, hypnotic strobing across the landscape, a world breathing light.

The density is immense. The stalks grow close, a thicket of soft, cool pillars you can weave between. In places, their crowns of feathery, light-hungry fronds interlock far above, creating a canopy that drips a slow, thick rain of spores. These spores are the size of your thumb, gelatinous and warm, and they drift down with a dreamlike lack of urgency, each one a tiny, self-contained pulse of light. The air is not air, the water is not water, and the space between things is not empty. It is saturated with these spores, with shed light, with the fine silt of the mountains, with life.

There are no ruins of a civilization, but there are marks. Occasionally, a break in the forest reveals a structure. One stands not far from you: a Resonance Spire. It appears built, a perfect hexagonal column of black, crystalline material, a hundred feet tall and flawlessly sheer. But it is not built. It grows. It is a geological formation, a mineral crystallization that follows impossible rules of geometry. It emits no light, instead seeming to drink the bioluminescence from its surroundings, creating a pocket of absolute shadow. To get too close is to feel a change in the rules. The pressure of the medium seems to lessen, as if thinning to a vacuum. A low, resonant hum enters your bones, a vibration that feels like it’s tuning reality to a different frequency. Time feels even slower near the Spire, or perhaps faster. It is difficult to tell. These spires are scattered across the ranges, rare and unnerving punctuation marks in the living text of the world.

This world is not static. It is deeply active. Great, slow currents, thermoclines of warmer or cooler gel, move like continental weather fronts, bending the Annulet-Stalks in a synchronized, silent bow. There are seasons here, defined not by temperature but by the composition of the medium. The current season is the Down-Fall, a period where a denser, nutrient-rich strata from the highest, brightest levels collapses downward. It manifests as a slow-motion cascade of shimmering, oily sheets of fluid, curtains of distortion that drift through the forest, feeding the stalks and triggering their spore-shedding.

Fauna is almost non-existent. The ecosystem's energy is bound up in the immense, slow metabolism of the flora. But there is *something*. You will not see it, not all at once. You will only know it has passed. It is the Solitude. It is not a creature in a way you would understand. It is a presence, miles long, that drifts through the mountain valleys. It does not swim; it is a current of its own. As it passes, the light of the forest is snuffed out in a rolling wave of darkness. The deep hum of the world changes pitch, dropping into a sub-audible moan of immense pressure and sorrow. It leaves behind no tracks, only a profound sense of emptiness and a temporary shift in the composition of the gel—a trace of complex organic compounds, like a memory of a thought too large to comprehend. When the Solitude has gone, the forest takes hours, sometimes days, to reignite its lights, stalk by stalk.

The one governing rule is this: things are what they are, until they are not. The rock is solid until you approach a Spire, where it might lose its integrity. The medium is breathable until the Solitude passes, when it becomes inert and suffocating. Gravity is a constant until it decides to be a variable. This is a world at the end of its life, or perhaps at the beginning of a new one, where the laws of physics have grown old and weary and are beginning to fray at the edges. It is quiet. It is ancient. It is deeply, unnervingly alive.
