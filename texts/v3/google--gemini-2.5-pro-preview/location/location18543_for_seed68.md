This place is a sound before it is a sight. The sound is a constant, foundational hum, a chord played on a colossal organ whose pipes are miles long. It is not an aggressive noise, but a totalizing one. It presses in on the ears, a physical weight in the cool air, so steady and unchanging that after a time the brain begins to subtract it, leaving a strange, pressurized silence in its wake. This is the sound of the Process.

The ground is a mosaic of sharp-edged, black flagstones. They are not natural slate, but a kind of vitrified, glassy rock, fractured into irregular polygons with edges that remain keen and unworn. Moss, thick as fleece and the color of malachite, grows in the wide gaps between them. The floor of this basin is uneven but generally low, a shallow bowl perhaps a mile across, its edges rising into unseen hills. It feels contained, a chamber open to the sky.

From this dark floor rise the Spires. There are perhaps two dozen of them, scattered with immense and deliberate space between. They are not buildings, but machines. Each one is a complex, vertical lacework of black metal beams, ceramic insulators, copper coils green with verdigris, and articulated pistons the size of fallen trees. They are cathedral-high, tapering towards the bright, normal light of the sky. Their design is sharp, angular, and functional, a geology of pure industry. Nothing about them is rounded or softened by time. They look as if they were emplaced yesterday, their forms utterly fresh.

And yet, they are engines of a garden.

Water is the currency here. It does not rain often, but the air is perpetually damp, heavy with a clean chill, like the inside of a springhouse. The Spires are the source. They hum their deep note and, in a process both inscrutable and patient, they coax moisture from the air itself. Water weeps from hairline seams in their metallic hides, condenses in heavy, trembling drops on the undersides of girders, and trickles in constant, meditative streams down their vertical faces. The air is filled with the secondary sound of this endless dripping, a pointillist percussion against the bass drone of the machinery.

This constant, localized irrigation feeds an impossible lushness. The Spires are sleeved in life. Enormous, prehistoric-looking ferns, their fronds a dozen feet long, hang like chandeliers from horizontal struts. Ropes of ivy with leaves as broad as dinner plates coil around the pistons, which move with geological slowness, sometimes taking an entire day to complete a single extension and retraction. Entire ecosystems of mosses, lichens, and liverworts—in shades from rust-red to neon green—form thick, spongy blankets over every surface that isn't in slow, grinding motion. Orchids with waxy, intricate blooms burst from the joints of machinery, their roots clinging to metal instead of bark. The place is urban in its structure, but the population is flora.

Between the Spires, the open space is startling. The black, moss-choked ground is sparse, creating wide avenues of emptiness. This is not a jungle, but a series of vertical islands of jungle in a quiet, empty plaza. It gives the place a strange rhythm of density and release.

Life, other than plant life, is abundant and has adapted to the noise. Birds, small and iridescent like hummingbirds but with harsh, metallic calls that cut through the drone, flit between the Spires. They build their nests inside the loops of copper coil, warmed by the low-grade electrical heat. Insects are large and slow. Beetles with carapaces of polished chrome-green crawl ponderously over the mosses, and dragonflies with glassy, veined wings hover in the open spaces, their movements seeming to hang in the thick, cool air. There are no large land animals; the world is vertical. The dominant fauna is arboreal, adapted to climbing the metal forests. Bands of furry, long-limbed creatures, something between a monkey and a squirrel, scale the superstructures with practiced ease, their chattering cries absorbed into the greater hum.

The light is bright and clear, unfiltered by cloud or canopy, which makes the scene all the more unusual. Sunbeams glint off wet metal and slick leaves, casting sharp, moving shadows as the sun arcs across the sky. The atmosphere isn't gloomy or oppressive, but crisp and hyper-real.

The strangeness here is the purpose. This is a constructed place, an artificial biome operating with industrial patience. The technology is not in opposition to the ecology; it *is* the ecology. The fundamental rule of this world is that the machine is the mountain, the engine is the rain, the factory is the forest. It changes only in slow, repeating cycles. A piston completes its weekly journey; a new fern unfurls over the course of a month; a water channel, clogged by moss, is cleared by a slow-moving maintenance drone that emerges from a recess, performs its single task over twelve hours, and retracts. It is a loud place, but a slow one. It is a city, but not for people. It is a garden, but its soil is circuitry and its weather is a manufactured hum. It feels less like a ruin, and more like a perfectly functioning system whose creators have simply stepped away, their work complete.
