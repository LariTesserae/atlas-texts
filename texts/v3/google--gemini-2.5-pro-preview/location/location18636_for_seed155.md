The world is a single, continuous exhalation of life, so ancient it has forgotten the concept of soil. You stand upon the Umbral Weft, a planet-girdling matrix of fungal-plant matter. Its upper surface, exposed to the high, thin air, is a plateau of incomprehensible scale. The "ground" is not earth but a pavement of hardened, petrified biology, a substance like dark, whorled wood mixed with the chitin of a billion-year-old beetle. It's cool to the touch, always faintly damp, slick in places with a weeping, resinous sap that smells of ozone and cold honey.

This is a high-altitude world. The sky overhead is a vast, pale sheet of light, rarely a deep blue, more often a wash of silvery-white or bruised lavender. Clouds are not discrete puffs but immense, continent-spanning veils of vapor that drift slowly, trailing skirts of fine, chilling rain that might last for an hour or a week. Below the edge of these plateaus, there is no bottom, only a descent into a deeper, greener gloom where the lesser branches of the Weft intertwine, a vertical continent of stalk and leaf disappearing into a pressurized, dark interior.

But the defining feature is not the plant-substrate itself, but the city built upon and into it. This is the Cogwork Canton, an industrial settlement of staggering size and sparse distribution. There are no foundations dug; structures are anchored directly into the living, ancient flesh of the Weft. Towers of oxidized copper and dark iron are held by immense rivets and spurred buttresses that dig into the biological pavement like metallic talons. These aren't clustered into a dense downtown but are scattered across a valley-sized basin on the Weft's back, connected by long, suspended railway bridges and cable-car lines that hum and sing with tension. Between these nodes of industry lie vast, empty stretches of the dark, patterned plain, where the only feature is the wind and the faint, rhythmic pulse you can feel through the soles of your boots—the slow, deep life of the Weft itself.

The sound is immense, inescapable. The Cogwork Canton is loud. It's not the clamor of people, but the roar of industry. Great geared hearts, housed in cathedrals of riveted iron, thud with a bass beat that echoes for miles across the open plains. Bronze pistons the size of sequoias hiss and sigh, driving the machinery that taps the Weft. Smokestacks, wider than houses, don't belch smoke but vent enormous plumes of pressurized, opalescent steam—the byproduct of rendering the Weft’s ancient, energy-rich sap. This is a city built for a single purpose: processing the temporal resin, the petro-lignin drawn from millennia-deep veins within the mega-flora. The air rings with the percussive clang of automated hammers, the shriek of high-tension cables, and the constant, overwhelming hum of dynamos converting the Weft’s biological energy into raw power. There are no birds to sing, no animals to call. The only counterpoint to the machine-roar is the deep, groaning creak of the Weft itself, shifting its weight on a geological timescale.

This constant, intrusive industry has a profound side effect. The Weft is not just a plant; it is a substrate of reality, and the aggressive, deep-drilling extraction is causing reality itself to fray at the seams. Weirdness is the weather here. A railway bridge might flicker, showing for a second a version of itself covered in rust and vines, a future-echo. Walk down a service corridor and the geometry might briefly unravel, the passage becoming an impossible Escher-like loop for a few heartbeats before snapping back. Patches of what the locals call "glitch-moss" grow on metal and bark alike; a shimmering, unstable film that disrupts causality. Drop a wrench near it, and it might land three feet to the left, or clatter to the ground a half-second before you even released it. Echoes sometimes arrive before their sound is made. The bright, normal light of the high-altitude sun will sometimes cast two shadows from a single object, one sharp and black, the other blurry and violet, each moving at a slightly different speed.

Life here is a constant negotiation. The inhabitants are few, seen as small figures dwarfed by their own machinery, scurrying along catwalks or piloting the rumbling locomotives that crawl between the industrial spires. They are a people accustomed to instability. No one trusts a perfectly straight line. Everyone learns to read the pre-shocks of a reality stutter, the faint taste of copper in the air that precedes a spatial fold. The fauna is nearly non-existent, save for the "echo-moths," creatures of shimmering light and distorted sound that seem to be pure side-effects of the temporal decay, flitting in and out of existence around the humming dynamos.

The world is active, but not in a way familiar to biology. It is the activity of a machine running too hard, and the groaning protest of the ancient thing it feeds upon. Rain falls, cool and sharp. Steam billows. A great piston completes its cycle with a ground-shaking *thump*. And across the plain, a distant spire of the Canton winks out of existence for a moment, leaving a hole in the world that is quickly, but not perfectly, patched by the shuddering fabric of space-time.
