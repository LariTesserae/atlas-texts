This is the Furnace-Jungle of Sarcossa, a vast, self-contained engine basin built on a high, eroded plateau. The air does not move, yet it is not still. It is a dense, pressurized medium, shimmering with heat that rises not from a sun, but from the floor itself. The temperature is crushing, a dry, baking heat that leeches moisture from any foreign object instantly. To breathe is to inhale grit and the faint, acrid tang of hot metal and ozone. It is a world without water.

The ground beneath is not soil or rock, but a perfectly tessellated expanse of hexagonal plates, dark as basalt but with a faint, oily sheen. These plates, worn smooth by eons of activity, are the source of the heat. In the infinitesimal gaps between them, a deep cherry-red glow is visible, the light of the furnace core miles below. This foundation is all that remains of the original construction, a surface eroded to its most stable, functional form. It is impossibly ancient, yet it hums with contained power.

From this heated floor, the "vegetation" springs. There is no biology here, no photosynthesis, no life sustained by water. This is a jungle of raw industry, a chemo-mechanical ecosystem grown wild. Great, trunk-like structures rise, not of wood, but of layered, oxidized copper and slag, their surfaces webbed with fine capillary pipes through which viscous, non-aqueous fluids pulse with a slow, thermal rhythm. These are the heat-sinks, drawing energy directly from the floor plates.

From these trunks, a chaotic, impossibly dense canopy unfurls. It is a continuous, interlocking mass that fills all available space, a three-dimensional briar patch of industrial detritus come to life. There are "vines" of woven, fiber-optic cable that have grown brittle and sharp, snapping with tiny, silent sparks of static discharge. There are "ferns" that uncoil slowly, their fronds made of razor-thin sheets of silicon that chime like shattering glass when they brush against one another. Great "flowers" or "fruiting bodies" exist as crucible-like bowls of spun ceramic, which collect atmospheric dust and, through some unknown catalytic process, periodically flash-fire their contents in a silent, brilliant burst of magnesium-white light, before collapsing into a rain of fine, grey ash.

The entire space is packed, saturated. To move is to break. Every surface is either blistering hot or sharp enough to cut. The growth is a manifestation of the basin's turbulence. Structures grow at an alarming, visible rate, driven by the infernal heat, reaching a point of chaotic instability before shattering under their own weight or being cannibalized by a neighboring growth. A pathway forged an hour ago might now be an impassable thicket of metallic brambles. A towering spire of fused glass might suddenly groan and collapse into a million pieces, the sound entirely swallowed by the sound-dampening density of the jungle.

This is a world built and abandoned, a fully-automated factory that has overgrown its original purpose and is now simply… processing. The builders are gone, but their machines remain, integrated into the wild growth. Massive, rust-caked regulators and cylindrical catalytic crackers lie half-swallowed by the metallic flora, their pilot lights still flickering with a ghostly blue flame. Pipes thick as a man's torso snake through the undergrowth, their joints weeping gouts of superheated, inert gas in a soft, steady *hiss*. It is an ecosystem of production without purpose.

There is no animal life. Nothing crawls or flies. The only movement is the slow, relentless growth and the sudden, chaotic collapse. Because of this, and because of the sheer density of the fibrous, crystalline "plant life," it is a profoundly quiet place. The dominant sound is a deep, subsonic hum that vibrates through the floor plates and into your bones—the sound of the planetary engine far below. Above this drone, there are only small, intimate sounds that do not travel: the faint *tink… tink…* of cooling ceramic shards, the abrasive rasp of a copper vine grinding against an obsidian pillar, the sigh of a pressure release valve. There are no echoes. Sound dies the moment it is born.

The landscape is cloaked in near-total darkness, relieved only by the dim, internal light of the machine-jungle itself. The angry red veins of the floor, the momentary white flash of a crucible-bloom, the eerie green phosphorescence of certain fungal-like radiator fins, the cold blue pilot lights of forgotten machinery. It is a world of stark silhouettes and faint, localized glows, where distance is measured not in meters but in degrees of blackness. To stand in the Furnace-Jungle is to be an irrelevant ghost in the heart of a vast, churning, and utterly indifferent metabolism, a process of creation and destruction that has been running on a loop for longer than your species has had a name.
