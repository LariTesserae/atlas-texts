The noise rises to meet you long before the light. It is a compound roar, a grinding, layered drone that seems to vibrate in your bones, transmitted through the very rock of the descent shaft. The air, once cool and mineral, grows thick and warm, heavy with the smells of wet soil, hot metal, and a cloying, animal musk. It becomes hard to breathe, a humid blanket that slicks the skin with a film of condensation.

You are in a Sump-City, a single, cathedral-sized cavern carved from the deep earth. The name Gnathostome comes to mind, unbidden, meaning ‘jawed mouth.’ It feels appropriate. The space is vast but contained, its ceiling lost in a dim, swirling fog hundreds of feet above. This is not a place of stone. The ground, the walls, the very architecture is life. The entire cavern is lined with a living, growing substrate, a colossal, world-creating plant-form that is part root, part fungus, part calcified coral. It is impossibly ancient, its forms settled into a stable, weathered state. The floor underfoot is a resilient, dun-colored mat of this cellulose, springy and damp, pulsing with a slow, geologic heartbeat.

From this living floor, the "buildings" of the city rise. They are not built, but grown and hardened over millennia. Some are like colossal, hollowed-out gourds, their sides perforated with glowing spiracles that serve as windows. Others are towering, calcified stalks, brittle and grey, fused together into clusters that form districts. The spaces between these structures are vast and open, giving the city a sparse, skeletal layout. The "streets" are not paved but are simply the valleys and canyons of the living substrate, slick with moisture and carpeted in patches of phosphorescent moss that cast a weak, greenish-blue light.

This is a world drowning in fauna. The dim air is never empty. It is a perpetual, glittering storm of arthropods. A billion winged things, from motes the size of dust to iridescent beetles the size of your fist, form a constant, chittering haze. They are the background radiation of this place, their collective hum a major component of the cavern’s overpowering noise. They swarm around the few sources of brighter light: the sickly yellow-orange glow of industrial forges that vomit plumes of steam into the upper darkness.

And there is industry. Strange, biomechanical factories squat in the open spaces between the plant-towers. They look like immense, metallic isopods, their riveted carapaces fused with the living ground. Enormous copper pipes, green with verdigris and furred with moss, snake out from these structures, plunging deep into the substrate like feeding tubes. Inside these factories, gears of petrified wood and hardened chitin grind against one another, driven by pistons that hiss with geothermal steam. The air shudders with the rhythmic CLANG-hiss-CLANG of their ceaseless labour. They are not refining ore or producing goods, but performing some essential biological function for the city itself—pumping a thick, nutritive ichor from the planet's mantle, processing waste, or regulating the cavern's atmosphere. Their function is a mystery, but their activity is constant and deafening.

The true inhabitants, the overwhelming presence of this place, are not the swarming motes but what they grow into. Scuttling things the size of hunting dogs, with multifaceted eyes and slender, whip-like antennae, dart through the canyons, acting as couriers or scavengers. They are a part of the city's motion, their chitinous clicks a mid-range percussion against the bass thrum of the machinery.

And then there are the giants. Slow-moving mountains of life, leviathans of flesh and shell that dwarf the factories. These creatures, ancient and placid, resemble something between a tortoise and a trilobite. Their vast, barnacle-encrusted shells are ecosystems in themselves, with gardens of symbiotic fungi and entire colonies of the smaller arthropods living upon them. They crawl through the city with tectonic slowness, their passage a slow, deep scraping sound. Some are yoked with immense chains to the industrial engines, serving as living anchors or mobile power sources, their slow, metabolic power harnessed. Others simply wander, their purpose known only to them, their massive heads swinging slowly as they graze on fungal growths or drink from pools of condensation.

There is no sky, only the distant, shrouded ceiling where water endlessly condenses and drips down in a warm, intermittent rain. This moisture slicks every surface, glistens on every carapace, and hisses into vapour on the hot metal of the engines. The world is in constant, dynamic motion: the pulsing of the substrate, the ceaseless swarm of the air, the grinding of the factories, the slow pilgrimage of the giants. It is an ecosystem that has become a city, an industry that has become an organ, a place where life has replaced geology and filled every possible niche until the sound of it is a single, unified, deafening roar. In Gnathostome, there is no silence. There is no stillness. There is only the overwhelming, humid, living machine.
