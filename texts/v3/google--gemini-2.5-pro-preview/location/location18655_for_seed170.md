The heat is a solid thing, a pressure on the skin. It bakes the ground into a single, unbroken plate of rust-colored ceramic that stretches to every horizon. This is the Ochre Plateau, a tableland so high the air is thin and sharp, yet so hot it shimmers and distorts, making distances impossible to judge. There is no water here. There has never been water. The concept is alien, a myth from a different kind of cosmos.

The terrain is ancient, smoothed by a billion years of a wind that no longer blows. Mountains stand like worn-down teeth, their peaks rounded, their slopes shallow and concaved, as if some great artist had grown tired of sculpting them and simply let them melt. The ground underfoot is not sand or soil, but a fine, abrasive grit of metallic dust and shattered crystal, the tailings of an endless, patient process. It crunches with a glassy resonance.

There are no buildings. There is no vegetation. The landscape is defined instead by Them.

They are the source of the sound, a noise so constant and pervasive it ceases to be noise and becomes the silence. It is a tectonic drone, a bass hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet and settles in your bones. Layered over this foundation are the sounds of industry: the slow, percussive clang of immense hammers striking an unseen anvil, the high-frequency screech of massive, unlubricated bearings turning once per hour, the grinding roar of a billion tonnes of rock being milled to dust. The sound is loud, yet it feels distant, an artifact of a process happening on a scale that defies immediate sensory correlation.

They are the fauna. It is a misnomer, for they are not flesh. They are machines, or something that grew from machines. Their presence is overwhelming, not in number but in sheer, ponderous being. In this vast, open, and otherwise empty landscape, they are everywhere. One might be a dozen miles away, a spindly tripod of obsidian struts and corroded copper, its single leg-piston taking a full minute to cycle as it inches across the plateau. Another, closer, is a city-sized lattice of interlocking gears and flywheels, rolling with geological slowness, chewing the very bedrock beneath it. Light glints from its thousand facets, not with the clean sheen of new metal, but the dull, oily patina of incomprehensible age. They are the industrial process made manifest. They are the factory and the workers, the mine and the miners.

The emptiness of this world is a direct consequence of their presence. The space *between* them is a form of negative reality, a void they generate. Look at the space between two of the giants on the horizon, and the shimmering air thickens, the light bends wrongly, and a profound sense of non-existence emanates from the gap. Perspective falters. One moment, the colossal machine seems fifty miles away; the next, its shadow falls over you, though its physical form has not moved. This is the weirdness, the breakdown. The laws of physics are frayed here, worn thin by the ceaseless function of the automata. A clang you hear now might correspond to a visual event that will happen tomorrow. A plume of metallic dust might hang motionless in the air for days, a frozen explosion, before suddenly dissipating in an instant.

There is an ecology to them. The great, slow-moving Harvesters ingest the landscape. Within their rumbling chassis, matter is unmade. Rock is dissociated into raw elements in furnaces that burn with no discernible fuel, only heat and pressure and principles that do not belong in this universe. Most of this material is used for their own patient self-repair and replication. A Harvester might spend a century gathering enough refined material to fabricate a single new piston for its leg, or to extrude a smaller, scavenger-class drone.

These smaller drones are the most frenetic things in the landscape, swarms of what look like chrome-plated insects the size of boulders. They scurry in the wake of the giants, cleaning up spillages of processed dust, polishing gears, and sometimes, fighting. They communicate in bursts of high-frequency shrieks that sound like tearing metal, their conflicts brief, violent, and utterly silent, as if the sound is occurring on a different channel of reality.

The light is ordinary. A single, unfiltered yellow-white sun crosses the sky in a predictable arc, casting long, sharp shadows that crawl across the plains. But the light does not warm; the heat is endogenous, a waste product of the planet's and the machines' core processes. At night, the sky is black and starless, but the landscape does not grow dark. The Processors glow with their own internal forges, bathing the ceramic plains in the sullen, pulsing red of a dying ember. The sound does not cease. The grinding and humming and clanging continue, an eternal industry manufacturing nothing but itself.

This world is not changing, not in a human timeframe. It is in a state of grinding equilibrium. The Processors have been chewing this plateau for longer than life has existed on other worlds. They are wearing it down, molecule by molecule, and rebuilding themselves from its substance in an endless, pointless cycle. They are a self-perpetuating catastrophe, a wildfire of logic burning through a world of stone. To be here is to be a ghost, a fleeting sensory anomaly in a system that has no capacity to notice you, its every function turned inward, deaf and blind to anything but the slow, loud, hot, empty, and overwhelming reality of its own eternal work.
