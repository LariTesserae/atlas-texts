This is the Chorus. It is a world without a surface, a plateau of singing crystal stretching from one horizon to the next, so vast it generates its own seasons of shimmering kinetic violence. There is no soil here, no sea, no stone that is not in some way alive. The substrate of existence is the vegetation itself.

It is not vegetation as one might know it. This is a non-aqueous ecology, a world where life solved the problem of thirst by doing away with it entirely. The flora are lithophytes, crystalline organisms that metabolize light and motion. They grow not from soil but from each other, a single, planet-spanning lattice of interconnected, semi-sentient machinery. They are vast, sparse giants. Imagine a forest where the trees are a mile tall and ten miles apart, yet their canopies of resonant crystal and their roots of grinding, geared fulcrums interlock to form a continuous, albeit porous, whole.

The air is thin, cool, and achingly dry. It carries no scent of damp earth or floral sweetness, only the sharp tang of ozone from constant electrical discharge and the sterile, hot-rock smell of friction. Above, the light is an unfiltered, brilliant torrent, bleaching the sky to a pale, luminous cyan. There are no clouds of water vapor, but there are shimmering curtains of ionized gas and drifting continents of shed silicate dust, which catch the light like cosmic glitter.

This world is an engine. That is the only word for it. It is an industrial ecosystem. This is not a place of quiet, photosynthetic growth; it is a place of deafening, mechanized process. Each lithophyte is a component in a planetary machine. Great, cantilevered arms of translucent quartz, miles long, rise and fall in cycles that span days, their movement powering colossal gear-like structures of interlocking bismuth crystal. These gears turn with the slowness of geologic time, but their friction, their grinding contact, emits a bass thrum that penetrates bone. Elsewhere, towering piston-like stalks of obsidian fiber slam down into socket-like formations, venting plumes of superheated, sterile gas that scream into the thin atmosphere.

And the sound. The sound is everything. It is a physical presence, a form of weather. It is not mere noise but a complex, layered, deafening symphony. The bass groan of the crystal gears forms the foundation. Over this lies the high-frequency shriek of gas venting from the pistons. Vast, sail-like membranes of mica-thin crystal, stretched between spires, catch the kinetic currents and vibrate with a sound like a billion shattering wine glasses. When a structural failure occurs—an inevitability in this turbulent world—a spire or cantilever will finally succumb to stress, and the sound of its fall is an explosion, a cataclysm of shattering quartz that echoes for hours across the churning landscape. This is a world in a constant state of controlled self-destruction and furious rebirth. Pieces break, fall into the crystalline lattice below, and are slowly, mechanically, ground down and reabsorbed, their matter and energy recycled into new growth elsewhere. This process, ancient and established, is its version of erosion.

There are no built things here. No roads, no cities, no artifacts. The industry is the biology. The factory is the forest.

And it is inhabited. The fauna are as strange and arid as the flora. Swarms of what might be called 'skitter-mites'—creatures of articulated chitin and metallic sheen—crawl in dense rivers across the surfaces of the great machines. They are not parasites; they are symbiotic janitors, consuming the silicate dust and smoothing the grinding surfaces with secreted, graphite-like lubricants. They communicate in clicks that are utterly lost in the mechanical roar. Above, soaring on the violent thermal updrafts from the piston-vents, are the Kites. They are not birds. They are living sheets of stretched, bio-tense membrane, some as large as city blocks, their bodies little more than a hardened nerve center and a grasping claw. They do not flap but ride the chaotic energies of the world, their shadows flickering across the vast, crystalline architecture below. They feed on pure energy, diving through the ionized gas-plumes to charge their bodies, glowing for hours afterward like phantom lanterns in the high, cold air.

The aether is alive with a form of invisible weather. Not wind, but waves of kinetic force and electrostatic potential that sweep across the Chorus. You can see them coming: a visible shimmer in the air, a change in the light, a sudden escalation in the pitch of the ambient scream. When these waves hit, the entire landscape responds. Foils of crystal that were humming now shriek, gears that were grinding may momentarily seize or spin faster. For the fauna, it is a storm to be weathered, the mites clinging on with magnetic feet, the Kites banking and angling their bodies to ride the surge.

This is a world without water, built of living crystal. It is a factory without builders, running on an endless cycle of light, motion, and sound. It is a system of total, violent, turbulent chaos that has, over eons, found its own deafening, terrifying, and utterly stable equilibrium.
