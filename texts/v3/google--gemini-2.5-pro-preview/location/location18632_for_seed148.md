This is a chamber at the root of the world, a hollow place being knit into existence. You are inside it. The scale is that of a grand and forgotten cathedral nave, but the walls are not stone. They are the living, growing, interwoven fibers of a single, world-spanning vegetation. Call it xylem-weave, call it lignin-scaffolding; it is a plant that has become architecture, and this chamber is a new ventricle in its heart.

The air is cool, still, and heavy with the scent of wet soil, ozone, and something like hot metal quenched in sap. There is no weather, only a slow, perpetual condensation cycle. Droplets form on the high, vaulted ceiling of braided boughs and fall for an impossibly long time through the darkness, landing with a soft, resonant *pat* on the damp, springy floor of woven roots. Moisture slicks every surface, gleaming in the faint light. The chamber is fundamentally damp, a terrarium the size of a town square.

Though the vegetation is total—forming the floor, the walls, the ceiling, everything—the space feels profoundly empty. The structure of the plant-city is hollow. It is a network of vast, interconnected voids. You could shout and hear the echo travel for a full minute down unseen corridors of air, corridors defined by living tissue. The density of *things* is near zero; there is only the boundary and the void it contains.

This boundary, however, is not just a wall; it is a city. Its growth is organized, deliberate, urban. Archways of cellulose braid themselves into being. Buttresses of hardened bark unfold slowly, locking into place with a sound like tearing cartilage. Towers, still embryonic, are visible as dense knots in the weave, pushing outward. This is a city under construction, but the masons and the materials are one and the same. It is a slow-motion metropolis of pure biology.

And it is industrial. Woven into the living architecture are machines of the same substance. Pistons made of petrified wood, lubricated by amber-like resin, cycle with a deep, nearly subsonic thrum. Gears of interlocking, hardened thorns mesh and turn, driving unseen processes deep within the walls. From metallic, flower-like vents, puffs of warm, ionized air are released. These systems are not additions; they are organs. The city-plant is a machine, a factory whose purpose is its own expansion. Its hum is the most persistent sound here, a quiet, foundational vibration you feel more in your bones than your ears.

The darkness is profound, a blackness that feels material. But it is not total. Patches of the wall are infested with bioluminescent archaea, casting a faint, ghostly green and blue glow. This light is not for seeing, but for suggesting a presence. It reveals the slick texture of a nearby pillar, the slow drift of a falling water droplet, and the teeming life upon the walls.

For the walls are not empty. The chamber’s floor and the central void are barren, but the vertical surfaces are a riot of fauna. Entire ecosystems of creatures, like living tapestries, flow across the xylem-weave. There are grazers, resembling multi-legged starfish made of opalescent gel, that consume the glowing molds. There are hunters, quick as camera-flashes, that are little more than articulated barbs and snapping jaws, skittering across the walls in pursuit. Tides of life, shaded in iridescent blacks and deep violets, wash up and down the vertical plains in silent, ceaseless predator-prey cycles. They are abundant, legion, yet they never enter the central emptiness; their world is the boundary.

And here, reality is a flimsy suggestion. The growth of the city does not follow linear time. You might watch a space on the wall for an hour and see nothing, then blink, and a fully-formed balcony of woven branches now exists where there was only flat surface. A sound might echo back to you before you’ve made it. Gravity is not entirely uniform; near the great bio-mechanical pumps, you feel a strange, lateral pull, as if the wall itself is trying to inhale you. The fauna does not seem to reproduce; new creatures simply bud from the wall, wriggling into motion and joining the tide. The industrial hum sometimes resolves, for a moment, into a string ofwhat could be musical notes or a line of speech in a forgotten language, before dissolving back into a featureless thrum. You are in a place where the distinction between plant, animal, and machine has collapsed, where the rules of cause and effect are being written and erased in the same instant. This is not just a strange room; it is an active, embryonic reality, and its heart is beginning to beat.
