This is a place of immense, quiet horizontals. The world has been scoured flat not by a glacier's passing, but by the sheer, unimaginable weight of time. What were once mountains, ranges that tore at the sky, are now just gentle, continent-sized swells in the terrain, like the slow breathing of a creature too vast to perceive. The ground is a deep, soft, perpetually cold loam, black and rich, but the coldness is not the crisp bite of frost; it is a deep, cellular cold that seems to emanate from the core of the planet itself.

The air is thin, high, and carries a strange, damp clarity. There is no fog, but a constant, silent condensation that beads on every surface. This pervasive moisture is not quite water. It feels cooler, slightly heavier, and does not evaporate in the pale, normal light of the distant sun. Instead, it is absorbed. Trickles of it gather in the low-lying curves of the land, forming sluggish rivers that are not clear or brown, but opaque and pearlescent, like liquid mother-of-pearl. They do not carve the land; they seem to be in negotiation with it, their paths shifting subtly over decades, following unseen contours of gravity or memory.

Vegetation is the dominant, and only, form of life. And it is lush. Great, sprawling carpets of mosses, liverworts, and unclassifiable, fleshy-leaved succulents cover everything. There are no trees, no flowers, no grasses that wave in the wind. This is a ground-level flora. The palette is a thousand shades of green, from near-black to vibrant, poisonous emerald, punctuated by swaths of deep violet and rust-red fungi that grow in slow, concentric rings, some miles in diameter. The plants do not grow *up* so much as they grow *out* and *in*, weaving themselves into dense, felt-like mats that are cold and spongy to the touch. In the silence left by the absence of any fauna, you can almost hear the slow, hydraulic creep of their growth, a sound that is more a pressure in the bones than a vibration in the ear.

Across these endless, mossy plains is scattered a city. It is undeniably a city, yet it defies every notion of one. It is built on a scale that humbles mountains, but its density is that of stars in a sparse galaxy. Massive structures of a dark, porous basalt stand in profound isolation from one another, separated by leagues of open, undulating plain. Their forms are primitive, geometric—colossal cubes, towering and slightly irregular tetrahedrons, long, low trapezoidal halls that vanish into the curve of the earth. There is no ornamentation, no sign of any hand but that of a giant with a simple and implacable will. The rock is pitted and worn, not with erosion, but with a deep and final age, as though it was born old.

Here is the breakdown: the city is not static. The structures move.

It is a motion so slow it is almost conceptual, a geological drift made manifest. Standing and watching, you would see nothing. But if you were to mark the position of a great basalt cube against a distant swell in the land and return a year later, you would find it had shifted. The entire urban-scape is in a silent, stately, multi-millennial rotation around an invisible axis. The 'streets' are the temporary, miles-wide corridors of moss between them. The infrastructure is not of roads, but of these predictable, flowing vacancies.

This movement is the world's primary dynamic process. The ground itself groans, a sub-audible hum that vibrates through the soles of the feet. The slick, pearlescent rivers are not following topography, but are drawn into the wakes of the drifting monoliths, sluggishly filling the channels of their passing before being displaced by the next slow approach.

Reality here is a permeable membrane. The light, while normal in its intensity, seems to fall unevenly, as if patches of space are more or less absorbent of photons. Sometimes, a distant pylon of rock will shimmer and vanish for a few heartbeats, its space occupied by a hole in the world, a patch of rippling, absolute non-existence, before it fades back into being, its surface now slick with a fresh coat of the strange condensation. Gravity is not a constant, but a local consensus; it is slightly weaker near the larger structures, as if their impossible mass is somehow negating it, allowing for their slow, frictionless drift.

There is no animal life. Not a single insect chirps, no bird calls, nothing burrows in the cold earth. The silence is absolute, broken only by the whisper of wind across the vast plains and the deep, seismic hum of the city's motion. The ecosystem consists only of the slow, relentless vegetation and its relationship with the moving architecture. The plants seem to respond to the drift. In the path where a great tetrahedron is due to arrive in the next century, the mosses thicken and their color deepens to a near-black. In the wake of a hall that has just passed, the fungi bloom in ephemeral, ghostly white spirals that trace its path, living off some residue of its passage—a temporal nutrient, a friction of reality itself.

This world is not dead. It is intensely active, but on a timescale that renders biology irrelevant. Its processes are those of physics and a kind of geologic sentience. The city was not built; perhaps it is growing, or perhaps it is the remnant of a law of nature that has since been repealed elsewhere in the universe. It is a settlement without inhabitants, an engine performing a calculation of incomprehensible scope and length. To be here is to feel like a fleeting ghost in a world of eternal, solid, and moving things, to know that the dust settling on your shoulders is not merely dust, but the finely ground powder of worn-out eons.
