This is a landscape of impossible sound.

The sound is the first and last thing. It is not a noise that travels through the air, for the air is thin to the point of nonexistence, but a vibration that inhabits all substance. It is a drone, a deep and resonant hum that is both bass and treble, a monolithic chord that has been playing since the beginning of the stone. It is a pressure in the hollows of the skull, a grinding in the teeth and sinuses. It is loud, shatteringly so, yet it has no source and no direction. It simply *is*. The sound is the baseline reality of this place.

We are impossibly high. The world is a basin of smoothed, ancient mountain ranges, their peaks worn down by aeons into vast, rolling domes of grey and black rock. They are not jagged or sharp; time has passed over them like a great, slow hand, leaving them rounded, settled, and calm. The scale is immense. From any one of these whale-back summits, you can see valleys that would take a lifetime to cross, filled not with shadow, but with a dim, sourceless luminescence. The light is a permanent twilight, a grey glow that seems to emanate from the thick, high-altitude mist that clings to everything.

This mist is the source of the world’s moisture. It never lifts and it never rains, but every surface is perpetually damp. The cold—a dry, biting chill that feels far colder than freezing—turns this dampness into a cryogenic skin. The ancient, rounded rocks are coated in a greasy sheen of near-frozen condensation. In the hollows and on the leeward faces of slopes, this moisture collects and freezes into thick, opaque veils of rime ice, sculptural and unmoving.

Because of the thin air and the eternal cold, almost nothing grows. The rock is not entirely bare, however. In vast, intricate patterns, colonies of lichen trace the stone. They are a brittle, colourless grey, scarcely distinguishable from their substrate. They do not grow in the familiar chaotic blooms, but in mandalas of shocking geometric precision. These are Chladni figures on a continental scale; the lichen thrives only on the nodal lines of the world’s constant vibration, creating a visible map of the sound that permeates everything. Where the vibration is most intense, the rock is utterly bare. Where it is null, the lichen forms its silent, frozen art.

The emptiness is profound. The sense of space is distorted, a symptom of reality’s thinning membrane at this altitude. A nearby ridge might take a day to walk to; a distant peak might suddenly loom before you after a few steps. The ground, solid as bedrock, sometimes gives with a slight, rubbery flex underfoot, as if you are walking on the skin of an impossibly large drum. The mist does not drift or blow, for there is no wind. It hangs static, its individual droplets trembling visibly, energized by the omnipresent drone.

Scattered across this immense, humming emptiness are faint marks of passage. Not roads, but faint, single-file tracks worn into the rock, as if by the tread of something slow and patient over millennia. And there are the structures. They are simple cairns, dolmens, and menhirs—piles of unmortared stone, assembled with a logic that is not architectural. They are small, crude, and profoundly ancient. They are not shelters from the cold or the damp. They are shelters from the sound.

Within a perimeter of precisely placed stones, the monolithic hum ceases. To step inside one of these small circles of rock is to be plunged into a silence so absolute and profound that it is its own form of sensory overload. The sudden absence of the grinding pressure in your bones is a physical shock. In these nulls, you can hear your own blood pumping, the faint crackle of the frost on your clothes. These are sanctuaries of quiet, the only places where a thought can be completed without being shaken apart by the world’s vibration. Who built them is a question without an answer. They feel less like constructions and more like natural adaptations, as if the land itself grew these pockets of silence out of a deep and ancient need.

Fauna is almost a myth. There are no birds to be deafened by the sky, no burrowing creatures in the ringing stone. But sometimes, in the deceptive mid-distance, a shape will resolve itself from the grey mist. It is vast and many-limbed, moving with an impossible slowness. The locals, if there were any, might call it a Still-Walker. It seems to glide over the rounded mountains, its form indistinct, hazy, and silent. It does not appear to be made of flesh and bone, but perhaps of condensed mist, or a localized fold in spacetime. It has no discernible features and makes no sound, a moving void in a world of deafening noise. Its passing is the only event that ever occurs here.

This world is not changing. It is not in the process of becoming something else. It is a single, held note. The mountains have settled, the light is fixed, the mist is constant. The sound is the eternal vibration of a bell that was struck at the dawn of time and whose resonance will never fade. To be here is to be inside that resonance, a particle of dust on a ringing, frozen, and unending chord.
