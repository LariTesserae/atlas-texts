This place is called Fenestraria, the Weeping City. It does not stand on the earth so much as it emerges from it.

You approach it through a shallow, sour marsh. The ground is a sodden mattress of black peat and grey clay, pocked with pools of standing water the colour of weak tea. Reeds, thin and brittle as old bone, stand in sparse, lonely clusters, but for the most part, the landscape is bare. The air is cool and heavy, carrying the scent of wet stone and ozone, the smell of a place just after a thunderstorm has passed, but the storm is perpetual here.

The city itself is not a collection of buildings, but a single, contiguous geological event. It is a slow, upward extrusion of a mineral, a pale, semi-translucent stone that grows from the ground in a dense, chaotic mass of spires, buttresses, and arches. The stone, sometimes called Fenestrite, is saturated with water. It weeps. From every surface, at every hour, water beads and drips. It is a city caught in a constant, silent thaw.

The scale feels intimate, like a vast, un-roofed cathedral. You can see the "edges" of the main growth, where the packed stone gives way to the marsh, but inside, its density is absolute. There are no streets, no plazas, no open spaces. There are only passages, chambers, and shafts, all carved directly from the living stone. The architecture is subtractive. The inhabitants, if they can be called that, do not build; they hollow.

The technology of this hollowing is primitive. The marks of hand-tools are everywhere: the patient, scalloped patterns of rudimentary chisels, the rough striations of scrapers. Where a new passage has been recently opened, the Fenestrite is sharp, its edges like fractured flint, its surface gritty with mineral dust. In older, long-inhabited chambers, the constant weep of water has smoothed the walls and floors to a slick, perilous gloss, like river stones. The water is not inert; it is mineral-heavy, and where it pools, it deposits tiny, glittering crystals, slowly re-filling what has been carved away.

This is the central process of the city: a slow, unending dialogue between growth and removal. The stone extrudes upward, a silent geological pressure, threatening to close passages, to fill rooms, to solidify the city into one seamless mass. In response, a quiet industry of carving persists. A room might take a generation to hollow out and will be gone in three if left untended. Existence here is not about expansion, but about maintenance against a slow, geological tide.

The light is normal, subject to the sun and moon, but it is filtered and transformed by the city. It enters through high, irregularly carved apertures and shafts, casting down long, hazy beams that illuminate the fine mist hanging in the air. The light that reaches the lower passages is diffuse and grey, reflecting endlessly off the wet, pale surfaces, making it difficult to judge distance or depth. Everything glistens.

The city is profoundly quiet. The clamour of urban life is absent. The dominant sound is the intricate, layered music of water. Plinks and plonks echo in resonant chambers. A steady drip-drip-drip provides a rhythmic anchor. The soft hiss of water seeping down a long, vertical run of wall. The deeper gurgle from a drainage channel carrying runoff to some unseen cistern. Against this, another sound, if you listen closely: the faint, rhythmic *scrape… scrape… scrape* of a tool on stone, a sound that might come from the next chamber or from a dozen passages away, its source impossible to pinpoint.

Life here is scarce and strange. No birds roost in the high archways; no rats skitter in the drains. In the pools of mineral-rich water, pale, blind salamanders, scarcely thicker than a finger, stir languidly. Slick, grey-green algae grows in thick mats on the floors of forgotten side-passages. This is the extent of the visible ecosystem. The water, so plentiful, is too saturated with dissolved stone to support more complex life.

There are no doors, only beaded curtains of strung shells or polished animal bone, clattering softly when disturbed. You do not see the inhabitants, but you feel their presence. A tool left beside an unfinished wall, the stone beneath it a slightly lighter shade, still dry. The warmth from a shallow stone dish holding glowing embers, the only source of heat. They are a people defined by their task, their culture a ritual of carving, their folklore a history of passages opened and lost. They move through their weeping world with a quiet reverence, their lives measured not in years, but in the slow, relentless growth of the stone they call home. The city breathes, in a way; it exhales water and inhales space, carved from its own body by its tiny, symbiotic inhabitants.
