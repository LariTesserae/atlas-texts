The place is a basin, a shallow caldera cupped in the fist of impossibly sharp and towering peaks. You can only get here through one of two passes, knife-edge fissures in the black rock that howl with a wind that does not seem to reach the valley floor below. The surrounding mountains are raw, new things, all jagged fractures and sheer faces of granite and obsidian-dark basalt, fluted with scree that has not yet had time to grind itself to sand. They are so high that the air around them is a deep, crystalline violet, and the stars do not entirely vanish even at noon.

This hollow, however, is a world apart. To descend into it is to leave the realm of wind and rock and enter an unexpected sanctuary. It is no more than a mile or two across, a near-perfect circle, and its existence is a geological paradox. The air remains thin and bitingly cold, carrying the metallic chill of high altitude, but the ground is warm. Not hot, but gently, pervasively warm, a constant, low-grade fever in the earth.

Because of this, the floor of the caldera is fantastically, unnaturally lush. There are no trees to break the immense, featureless vault of the sky. The volume of the place feels empty, hollowed out, defined only by the ground below and the severe ring of peaks above. But the ground itself is a riot of life. It is carpeted in a deep, spongy layer of mosses, ranging in color from electric lime green to a purple so deep it is almost black. These mosses grow in thick, pillowy hummocks, and they steam. The moisture from the warm soil meets the frigid air a few inches above the ground, creating a permanent, shifting layer of low-lying mist that clings to the contours of the land. Water doesn't flow here; it condenses. Every surface is perpetually damp, beaded with what looks less like dew and more like tiny, perfect spheres of liquid glass. In the intense, unfiltered sunlight, these droplets turn the entire valley floor into a glittering field of cold jewels.

Growing through the mosses are strange, succulent-like plants. Their leaves are thick and waxy, some shaped like ridged starfish, others like tightly coiled spirals. Many have a pearlescent, iridescent sheen, a defense against the brutal, high-altitude sun. They store water in their fleshy bodies, thriving in the damp but not-quite-wet conditions. They huddle low to the warm ground, forming dense, intricate mats of foliage.

The place is far from silent, but its sounds are all small. There is a constant, ambient hum. This is the sound of insects—countless millions of them. They are not flies or gnats, but stout-bodied beetles with polished, gem-like carapaces of sapphire and emerald and bronze. They crawl through the moss and climb the succulents, their movements making a soft, collective rustling, a sound like fine sand being poured onto silk. Hovering above them are swarms of tiny, moth-like creatures with wings as clear as cellophane, catching the light in fractured rainbows.

This abundance of insect life supports a startling density of other fauna. Flocks of small, finch-like birds, no bigger than a thumb, move through the low-lying mist like schools of fish. They are insulated by incredibly thick, downy feathers, and they make no calls, their wings whispering against the air a sound you feel more than hear. They dart from hummock to hummock, feeding on the beetles. Hunting these birds, and the plump, six-legged, mole-like creatures that burrow through the warm soil, are sleek, ermine-like predators. They are bone-white with black-tipped tails, and they flow over the mossy ground without a sound, their passage marked only by the sudden panicked dispersal of a flock of birds. The ecosystem here is furiously active, a constant, silent churn of hunting and feeding, burrowing and breeding, all taking place in the bottom two feet of the air.

Faint traces of something else are scattered across the caldera. Here and there, low, semi-circular walls of stacked, unmortared stone stand, no higher than a person's waist. They are not shelters from the wind, for there is none, nor are they defensive. They feel like places of observance, simple enclosures facing the rising or setting sun. Near the center of the valley stands a single, taller cairn of carefully balanced black rocks. On its a top, and placed on smaller rock piles throughout the basin, are offerings: polished stones. They are simple river-rocks, geologically out of place, that have been hand-polished to a high sheen, perhaps with animal hide and fine silt. They are cold to the touch, but they seem to gather the intense light and hold it within them. These artifacts are primitive, the work of hands that knew stone and bone, but not metal. They speak of a reverence for this place, a recognition of its impossible nature.

The world here operates on a simple, powerful rhythm. By day, the bright sun casts stark, razor-sharp shadows, and the caldera floor glitters with condensation, a hive of silent activity under an immense, empty sky. As the sun sets, the temperature plummets instantly. The ever-present mist thickens, and a delicate lacework of frost, a rime of pure white crystals, forms on every surface of every plant, encasing the entire valley in a fragile shell of ice. The non-sound of a billion tiny lives quiets further. But the warmth from the earth persists, a promise beneath the frost. And as the first searing rays of dawn strike the peaks, the ice shell doesn't melt, but sublimates—turning directly from solid to vapor, releasing the teeming world back into its silent, dynamic day.
