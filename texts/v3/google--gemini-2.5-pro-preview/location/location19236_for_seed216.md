This is the realm of the Great Weaving, sometimes called the Hanging Gardens of Ge.

You are standing on a causeway a solid three hundred paces wide. The material underfoot is not stone, nor earth, nor wood as you know it, but something that partakes of all three. It is a single, continuous substance, a kind of petrified, living lattice of immense, interwoven fibers. The color is a deep, mossy grey-green, slick with perpetual moisture. This is the substrate of everything here. There is no ground. There is only the Weaving.

The air is warm and heavy, saturated with water. It is not raining, yet every surface weeps. A constant, cool drip falls from the arches and buttresses that soar into the dimness overhead. This is the Slow Breath of Ge, a rhythmic, planetary-scale exhalation of vapor that condenses on every surface, forming rivulets that course along the architectural fibers and sometimes pool in shallow, basin-like depressions. These pools are clear and still, their only disturbance the slow plink of a new drop arriving from above. The water has a clean, slightly metallic taste, like licking a leaf.

The scale is baffling to the eye and the mind. The causeway you are on is just one thread in a structure vaster than any continent. It stretches out into a dim, misty void, eventually connecting to a colossal pillar, a trunk of woven fibers so large its curvature is barely perceptible. From that pillar, other causeways branch, arcing gracefully into the misty distance, creating a three-dimensional network of impossible size. Below you, there is nothing. Just a gulf of pearl-grey mist that seems to fall away forever. Above, the same. The Weaving fills all of existence, yet the dominant experience is one of profound emptiness. The space *between* the threads of the Weaving is so vast that the structure itself feels like a fragile exception to an overwhelming void.

This is a constructed place. Every arch, every platform, every filament was grown and guided. This is not nature; it is artifact. Yet the technology that shaped it is nowhere to be seen. The forms are ancient, weathered into a state of serene equilibrium. Edges are softened, surfaces are colonized by life. The Weaving is so old it has become its own geology, its own ecosystem.

And the ecosystem is total. The Weaving *is* the primary vegetation. But upon it, life flourishes. Vast fields of phosphorescent moss, some the size of small hills, pulse with a soft, internal blue-green light, providing the only significant illumination in the permanent twilight. These moss-fields are warm to the touch. Long, thick vines, like dripping ropes of black tar, hang down into the void, some for miles, their purpose or destination unknown. Smaller, fern-like plants with leaves of pure, translucent crystal grow in the crevices where water collects, tinkling softly when the breeze finds them, one of the few sounds to break the quiet.

The silence is immense, muffled by the humidity and the sheer lack of reflective surfaces. Sound just…dies. The loudest noise is the drip of water, or the wet slap of your own footfalls. But the silence is not empty. It is pregnant with the near-imperceptible hum of the Weaving's own metabolism.

Fauna is abundant, but quiet. Large, ray-like creatures with wingspans of fifty feet, called Air-Skates, glide silently on the thermal updrafts between the great structures. Their skin is patterned like the Weaving itself, a near-perfect camouflage. They hunt smaller, six-limbed climbers that scuttle along the undersides of the causeways, their padded feet making no sound. In the moss-fields, teeming colonies of beetle-like creatures with polished carapaces harvest the glowing fungus, their movements slow and deliberate.

The weirdness of the place is not aggressive; it is fundamental. Gravity feels subtly inconsistent, slightly weaker here than it is there. Distances are deceptive; a pillar that seems a day's walk away might be reached in an hour, while a nearby bridge remains stubbornly distant, its length seeming to stretch as you cross it. The Weaving is not entirely static. Over the course of a week, one might notice a great arch has shifted its curve by a few degrees. It is growing, breathing, readjusting itself on a geological timescale. This structure does not rest on a planet; it may *be* the planet, or what passes for one. It hangs in the void by its own unknowable physics, a law of suspension that is simply accepted.

And it is tended.

The builders are gone, but the Gardeners remain. They are tall, slender humanoids with long limbs and dark, wide eyes adapted to the dimness. They move with an unhurried grace, their bare feet silent on the wet lattice. They wear simple loincloths woven from the silver fibers of a rare parasitic vine. Their tools are of bone, obsidian, and the petrified heartwood of the Weaving itself. You see them at work in the distance: a small group, perched on a high scaffold of their own making, carefully scraping a diseased growth from a support cable as thick as a castle tower. They are not building; they are pruning. They are healers, coaxing the Weaving, guiding its slow, continental growth, ensuring its eons-long stability. They are creatures born of the artifact, and now they are its symbiotic custodians. They live in hollowed-out chambers within the Weaving’s thicker nodes, their lives as quiet and cyclical as the Slow Breath that gives them water. They are a people without metal, without fire, living within the greatest architectural accomplishment imaginable, tending their garden at the end of the world.
