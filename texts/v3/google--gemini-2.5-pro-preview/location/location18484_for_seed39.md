This is a landscape of becoming. It is a high, cold plateau caught in the act of its own creation, a geological process so immediate and raw that it feels less like nature and more like an engine.

The ground is not soil but a preamble to rock. It shudders, not with the violence of an earthquake, but with a deep, rhythmic, and shockingly periodic thrum, a bass note felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. This is the sound of the world’s assembly. The air is thin, carrying the metallic tang of ozone and hot stone. Visibility extends for miles across a vast, undulating basin, but the light is a perpetual bruised-lavender twilight, filtered through a high, thin, and permanent layer of ice-crystal cloud. At this altitude, the cold is a physical presence, a pressure that seeps through any barrier.

The mountains that ring this valley are not the worn, ancient peaks of an old world. They are jagged, impossibly sharp, and gleam with a wet, dark lustre. They look like they were made yesterday. In places, their faces are sheer, unbroken planes of black, obsidian-like material, polished smooth as if by a colossal blade. In others, they are a chaotic jumble of crystalline facets and razor-edged strata, still cooling into their final forms. There is no erosion here, no gentle sanding by wind or water. There is only accretion. The land is being built, extruded from a deep, planetary forge. The process is audible as a faint, high-frequency *zinging* noise that seems to emanate from the rock itself, the sound of crystals locking into place. This is accompanied by groans and percussive cracks, like a great ship settling into its frame.

For all its cold, the place is wet. Water doesn't flow in rivers but weeps from the land itself. The geothermal heat of the continuous creation below keeps the bedrock just warm enough that ice gives way to liquid. Dark, mineral-heavy water sweats from fissure lines in the new rock, running in oily-looking slicks down sheer faces before turning to frost-plumes in the frigid air. The atmosphere is saturated, condensing on every surface. The air feels thick to breathe, not with humidity but with a heavy, damp chill that promises hypothermia. Pools of still, black water gather in depressions, their surfaces unmarred by wind, reflecting the dim sky like portals of liquid night. They are not fed by rain, but seep up from below, supersaturated with dissolved metals and nascent minerals.

Vegetation is a misnomer. There is no green, no soil to support roots. Instead, in sheltered locations and along the margins of the weeping fissures, there are sparse, frost-like growths. They are chemosynthetic colonies, feeding directly on the raw geological output. One type is a brittle, verdigris-coloured crust that glitters as if dusted with copper filings. Another grows in slow, concentric patterns like lichen, but its texture is that of rough, unrefined iron slag. These growths are the bedrock of the ecosystem, incredibly dense in calories and rare metals. They are the only “producers” in this vast, cold engine-room.

And they feed an astonishing abundance of life. The primary grazers are creatures that resemble enormous, six-legged woodlice, shelled in thick, chitinous plates the colour of lead. Their undersides are a hive of whirring, metallic mandibles that scrape the mineral crusts from the rock. The sound they make is a constant, low-level *shk-shk-shk*, like a thousand wire brushes on stone. They move in slow, deliberate herds across the basin floor, their passage leaving behind scraped, gleaming patches on the dark ground. They are called Grinders.

Hunting the Grinders are the true masters of the plateau: Ridge Stalkers. Lean, sinewy, and four-limbed, they look like a cross between a greyhound and a praying mantis. They stand nearly two meters tall on their hind legs, their bodies covered in a thick, white, insulating pelt that blends perfectly with the rime and mist. Their forelimbs are long scythes of bone, evolved not for grasping but for punching through the Grinders’ armor. They are largely silent hunters, moving with a fluid grace that seems to defy the thin air. The only sound they make is a sharp, percussive click used for communication across the vast, empty spaces—a sound that cuts through the low thrum of the land with startling clarity. Smaller, opportunistic scavengers, like furry, multi-limbed ferrets, swarm over any kill, their chittering cries briefly animating the quiet.

The world operates by one primary rule: the Thrum. The rhythmic pulse of the land's formation is the clock by which everything runs. The weeping of the rocks, the crystallization of new strata, the growth cycles of the mineral-crusts, and the hunting patterns of the Stalkers are all synchronized to this deep, planetary heartbeat. During the peak of each pulse—a shuddering that lasts for perhaps an hour—new fissures steam open, and the mineral-rich water flows most freely. This is when the Grinders are most active, feeding frantically. And this, in turn, is when the Ridge Stalkers hunt. The quiet period between pulses is a time of digestion and stillness, the vast landscape waiting for the next surge of its own creation. It is a place without past, only a continuous, mechanized, and deeply alive present. It is not being born; it is perpetually *birthing*.
