There is no light. This is the first and final rule. It is not the dimness of a deep cave or the gloom of an overcast night; it is a primal, unmaking darkness, a presence that feels heavier than air. It presses against the skin with a damp, clinging warmth. The atmosphere is a saturated fog, a suspension of warm water and something else, something with a faint, sterile scent like ozone and hot metal. You can’t see the condensation, but you feel it beading on any exposed surface, on your hair, on the backs of your hands. The air is so thick with moisture that to breathe it feels like drinking.

The ground is a treacherous, uneven plain of what might be called slag. It is not soil or rock, but a vitrified, ceramic-like substance, extruded in vast, cooling sheets that have cracked and buckled. The features are perversely sharp. Where the crust has fractured, the edges are like broken glass. These fissures, some hairline thin and others wide enough to swallow a person, weep a constant, syrupy fluid that isn't quite water. It runs in sluggish, iridescent rivulets across the black plains, carving no channels, leaving no sediment, merely tracing greasy paths before seeping back into another crack. This landscape is young, born not of geology but of some catastrophic, and now patient, industrial process. It is a cooling tray the size of a valley.

Every few hundred yards, the terrain swells upwards. These are not hills, but immense, blister-like formations of the same black, glassy material, humped and smooth. Their surfaces are slick with the ever-present condensation, and from pores and hairline fractures on their flanks, the warm, thick water weeps more heavily, running down in audible, viscous drips. The sound is one of the few that punctuates the profound quiet: a slow, irregular *plip... ploc...* as the fluid gathers and falls, absorbed without a splash by the strangely porous ground.

Though the eye can see nothing, the other senses insist the space is both open and dense. There's a feeling of vastness, of a horizon that extends for miles in every direction, a sensation carried on a low, sub-audible hum that resonates in the bones. Yet the immediate vicinity feels crowded. Life is everywhere, a frantic, silent boiling of activity. The ground is a carpet of things that scuttle and crawl, their chitinous legs and soft bodies making only the faintest friction against the slag. They are a constant, near-touching pressure, a moving floor beneath your feet. They range from the size of a coin to the length of an arm. They do not fight, they do not call out; they simply move, a million individual agendas in a single, unified rustle that you feel more than hear. They feast on the chemical brine that leaches from the land itself, a form of chemosynthesis that has replaced photosynthesis entirely.

There is no native green. The few things that resemble vegetation are stark, alien growths. Thin, crystalline stalks of silica-spun glass grow in tight clusters around the weeping pores on the slag-hills, vibrating with the hum of the earth. They are not alive, but they grow. Elsewhere, pallid, leathery fungi, shaped like shelves or hands, sprout from the joints of unseen machinery buried just beneath the surface. They emit a faint, internal heat.

This is a built place, or what's left of one. Huge, angular structures loom in the absolute dark, perceived not as shapes but as voids in the sensory field—zones where the ever-present hum is subtly dampened, where the air feels colder, where the scuttling fauna give a wider berth. These are the pumping stations, the heat exchangers, the catalytic crackers of a forgotten factory. They are silent, monolithic. No lights burn on them. No pistons drive. Yet they are not dead. They are the source of the heat, the hum, the chemical sustenance of this entire ecosystem. They are operating on a timescale that is nearly geological, a slow, continuous processing that has become the metabolism of the world. A single, bare-skinned pipe, wide as a man is tall and warm to the touch, might snake out from the base of one such monolith, run half-buried through the slag for a mile, and disappear into another. It is a circulatory system.

The rules of reality fray at the edges here. Gravity feels inconsistent, slightly weaker near the weeping fissures, subtly heavier around the monolithic silent engines. A compass would spin uselessly, not because of local magnetism, but because the concept of "north" feels like a forgotten word from a dead language. The darkness itself seems to possess a kind of negative parallax; if you hold up a hand, you feel its presence not as a silhouette against a lesser dark, but as a hole punched in the greater, more absolute dark around it. Sometimes, the hum that permeates everything resolves into a pattern—a stream of data, a sequence, a silent broadcast with no recipient, felt as a tingling pressure behind the eyes before it dissolves back into undifferentiated resonance.

The overwhelming quiet is the most unnerving density of all. Billions of creatures move, feed, and exist in near-perfect silence. The only sounds are the slow drips of chemical water, the deep thrum of the world-engine, and the wet, soft sound of something being eaten, a sound quickly muffled in the thick, warm air. It is a landscape in a state of terminal, stable equilibrium. The cataclysm is long over. This is what comes after.
