This place is not a forest, but a single, continuous engine made of plant matter. It is a room, perhaps the size of a grand railway station's nave, and the walls, floor, and ceiling are all the same living, breathing, working entity. You call it the Peristaltic Grove.

The floor is not soil but a tightly woven mat of swollen, vascular tissue, like the deck of a great ship plaited from living roots. It is damp to the touch, springy underfoot, and cool despite the warmth of the air. Water doesn't pool here; it is absorbed instantly, drawn into the substrate. Cut one of these floor-fibers and it would bleed a thin, clear fluid that smells of copper and chlorophyll. The floor is in constant, imperceptible motion, a slow, wave-like shifting that you feel more in your bones than you see with your eyes. It is the deep, metabolic pulse of the Grove.

From this living deck rise the pillars and walls. They are not individual trees but fused, columnar growths of woody, calloused cellulose, their surfaces patterned like ancient bark but smooth and warm, like skin. These columns soar upwards, branching and weaving together to form a vaulted ceiling high above, a dense canopy of enormous, plate-like leaves. Sunlight filters through this ceiling, but it is a thick, green-gold light, as if strained through honey and algae. It is day, but it feels like a perpetual, luminous twilight.

This is where the technology is. Embedded into the living architecture, not as additions, but as integral components, are the machines. Great, slowly turning flywheels of dark, dense-grained wood are sunk into the pillar-walls, their axles disappearing into the organic mass. Pistons, jacketed in a chitinous, iridescent bark, slide with geological patience in and out of cylinders that seem to be swollen nodes on the trunks. From these mechanisms run conduits that are half vein, half insulated pipe, their surfaces a seamless gradient from mottled green cambium to verdigris-stained bronze. They snake across the ceiling and down the walls, throbbing with the same slow rhythm as the floor. Occasionally, a valve-like sphincter on one of these pipes will hiss, releasing a plume of hot, sweet-smelling vapor that immediately condenses on everything, coating leaves and mechanisms alike in a fresh film of moisture.

The air is thick, warm, and heavy with the smell of wet earth, hot metal, ozone, and something like fermenting fruit. It is saturated, and breathing feels like drinking. The atmosphere is loud. Not with the chatter of fauna—there are no birds, no insects, no animals of any kind—but with the sound of the Grove itself. It is a constant, complex thrum, a chord made of a thousand different noises. The deep, bass groan of the woody pistons. The rhythmic gurgle and slosh of fluid moving through the arterial pipes. The high, intermittent hiss of steam vents. The creak of immense cellulosic limbs adjusting their load. It is the sound of a factory running at one-quarter speed, a living machine engaged in a process of immense, unhurried digestion.

There are signs of other hands here, but they are old, weathered, and being slowly reclaimed. A corroded brass ladder is half-swallowed by the pillar it's bolted to, the rungs stretched and warped by the slow growth of the wood. Set into a wall is a panel of milky, cataract-like glass, a gauge whose needle has not moved in a generation, its face obscured by a spreading patch of bioluminescent moss that gives off a cold, blue-green light. Here and there, one finds a workbench fused into the floor, its tools—spanners, oil cans, diagnostic probes of some kind—now inseparable from the living wood, like fossils in amber. It feels as though the Grove's builders set it in motion and then simply walked away, confident in its patient, self-regulating eternity.

The place has a purpose, though it is not one of human speed or scale. The Grove is a refinery, a stomach, a vast phytomechanical reactor. Perhaps it draws raw material from some deep source, processing it through slow cycles of biological pressure and engineered catalysis. The finished product seeps out from weeping pores in the walls: a viscous, opalescent sap that drips with the slowness of forming stalactites, hardening on the floor into amber-like nodules of pure energy or refined matter. The process is so gradual, so established, that the whole system feels less like it is *doing* something and more like it simply *is*. It is a closed loop, a state of being. You have the sense that it has been running this way for a thousand years, and will run for a thousand more, the great, green, wet engine turning over, and over, and over, in the warm, dim light.
