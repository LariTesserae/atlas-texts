This world is a plateau, a continent-sized tableland lifted so high that the sky has a different character. It is a darker, more violet blue than the sky of the lowlands, and the stars at night do not twinkle; they are hard, unwavering points of ice. The air is thin and carries a permanent, biting chill. It smells of little more than rock and distance.

This is the Skyscrape. The name is less about height and more about texture. The terrain is young, geologically speaking. The plateau is a recent uplift, a crumple in the planet's crust that has not yet had time to be softened by wind and water. Jagged fins of black and grey rock, sharp enough to cut skin, jut from the earth. The ground between them is a rubble-field of shattered stone, from boulders the size of huts to a fine, sharp gravel that crunches underfoot. There is no soil to speak of, only rock dust and grit.

Water here is a ghost. There are no rivers, no lakes. Instead, a constant, imperceptible condensation clings to every surface. In the deep chill, this moisture manifests as a crystalline fur of rime ice that grows on the shaded side of every stone. When the unfiltered sun strikes the plateau in the long, slow arc of a day, this ice does not melt into streams, but sublimates directly into the thin air, creating a low, ground-hugging mist that pools in depressions before vanishing. To be "damp" here is to feel the air's potential for frost, to see the shimmer of phantom moisture in the cold. In the deepest geological faults, where tectonic heat leaks from the planet's core, this frost might melt into sluggish, saline seeps that are the only source of liquid water.

Vegetation is a triumph of persistence over plenty. There are no trees. The dominant life-form is a type of lichen, a partnership of fungus and algae that forms great, brittle sheets of black and rust-red across the rock faces. They look like old bloodstains on the stone. In the most sheltered crevices, where gravel has collected, grow tough, wire-like grasses with glassy blades, and low, creeping shrubs that look more like roots than plants, their woody knuckles gripping the stones. They are sparse, islands of tenuous life in an ocean of rock.

The quiet is profound. It is not an absence of sound, but a presence of silence so vast it feels like a pressure on the eardrums. The dominant sound is the high, lonely whisper of wind scouring the rock fins, a sound that has been blowing for millennia. Occasionally, this is punctuated by the sharp crack of a rock splitting as frost expands within it, or the distant, percussive slide of a gravel slope giving way.

Into this immense and desolate landscape is built a city. It is not a city in the conventional sense of towers and boulevards. It is a human reef. From a distance, it is difficult to distinguish from the surrounding geology. It appears as a particularly dense and complex formation of rock, a miles-wide scab of structure adhering to the southern face of a great, mountain-like upthrust.

This is a city of stone, bone, and hide. The tech is primitive, born of necessity and what the land provides. There is no mortar. The buildings are dense-packed warrens of stacked, interlocking stones, chosen and fitted with a patience that spans generations. Walls are thick, angled to deflect the wind, with few and small openings. It is a single, contiguous structure, layered like a midden. To walk through it is to descend through time. The upper levels are fresh-cut grey stone, the homes of the living. Below these, the passages become narrower and the stone is darker, worn smooth by centuries of hands and shoulders, stained by smoke and grease. Deeper still, the city becomes a labyrinth carved from the foundations of its own past, the lowest levels being little more than excavated rubble, ancient and cave-like. It is dense to the point of being solid; one does not walk *between* buildings, but *through* them.

The source of this impossible urbanism is the fauna. The plateau is home to colossal herds of migratory beasts. Great-backed grazers, the size of elephants, covered in shaggy, white fur that hangs to the ground. They are slow, ponderous animals, their metabolisms tuned to the cold and the scarce vegetation, which they digest with incredible efficiency. Their hooves are like platters of stone, and their lowing calls are a resonant bass hum that can carry for miles, a sound that is part of the city's quiet heartbeat. They are the bedrock of the entire society.

The city is built along their primary migration route. Its people are not so much hunters as they are… patient harvesters. The animals are too large, too tough to be brought down by hand-tools and individual effort. But they are not immortal. The herds move slowly, and the old, the sick, and the weak inevitably fall behind. The city exists to find them. Its inhabitants are collectors, scavengers on a continental scale.

Every part of the animal is used. Their meat is sliced thin and dried on vast racks of bone that extend from the city's outer walls, looking like the exposed ribs of some even greater creature. Their thick hides, when scraped and cured, become the roofs, the doors, the window-coverings, the blankets, the very clothes on their backs. Sewn in layers and stuffed with cast-off fur, these hides form the insulated bulkheads that make life in the cold possible.

Their bones are the city's second great building material. The massive ribs are used as structural arches for the largest chambers. Vertebrae serve as stools and tables. Shoulder blades become platters and shovels. Smaller bones are knapped and ground into an arsenal of points, needles, scrapers, and awls. Strung together on sinew, strings of vertebrae and finger-bones hang in doorways, a primitive form of chime that clatters softly in the drafts, one of the few domestic sounds of the city.

Life here moves at the pace of geology and migration. The year is divided into two seasons: the coming, and the going. The people are stout, broad, and slow-moving, wrapped in layers of white fur and pale hide that makes them blend into the rime-covered rock. Their faces are weathered, their eyes accustomed to scanning vast, empty distances for the slightest sign of movement. Their primary virtue is patience. They will watch a failing beast for days, following it at a respectful distance, waiting for the inevitable. Their culture is one of quiet ritual, of murmuring chants that honor the spirits of the beasts that give them life, of a deep understanding that they are a small, dense knot of warmth and life clinging to a world of cold stone. They are as much a feature of this landscape as the lichen and the wind.
