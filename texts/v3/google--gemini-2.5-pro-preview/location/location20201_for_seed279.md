The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a roar or a crash, but a continuous, overwhelming chord. It is the sound of a billion glass bells of every size and thickness ringing at once, a polyphonic drone that saturates the air and vibrates deep in your bones. It is a presence as tangible as stone, a pressure on the eardrums that never ceases, only shifts in its impossibly complex harmonics.

This landscape is a low-lying, sprawling basin that stretches to a misty, undefined horizon. There is no soil underfoot. You walk on a substrate of crushed, sand-like grit and viscous, mineral-rich mud that oozes between the sharp new formations breaking through its surface. The air is cool and heavy with moisture, a perpetual twilight filtered through a high, unbroken layer of gunmetal-grey cloud. Droplets condense on every surface, running in slow rivulets down impossibly sharp edges. This is a world perpetually weeping.

There is no vegetation. Not a single leaf, not a strand of moss. The world is a forest of mineral growth. From the slurry of the basin floor, countless crystalline structures rise. They are not quartz, not a familiar crystal, but a semi-translucent, smoky-grey material that looks like frozen, impure glass. They grow in dense, near-impenetrable thickets, from needle-thin spires a hand-span high to massive, razor-edged blades that stand taller than a person. Their surfaces are pristine, unweathered, their edges lethally sharp. This landscape is young, or perhaps it is forever young, its surface constantly renewed by this slow, sharp growth. The formations are tightly packed, their facets and edges interlocking and scraping against one another in a dense, three-dimensional lattice. To move through it is to risk a thousand cuts.

The source of the endless sound is this crystalline forest, activated by the pervasive water. The slow, constant seeping of water from the ground, the condensation dripping from above, and the sluggish, almost imperceptible currents that flow through the deeper channels of the basin all interact with the crystal. Water trickles down a broad, flat blade, creating a low, groaning hum. It drips in a steady rhythm into a hollowed-out crystalline cup, producing a dissonant bell tone. It flows through a tight channel between two resonant spars, generating a high, piercing shriek. Every drop of water is an act of percussion. The combined effect of these trillions upon trillions of micro-events is the overwhelming, ever-shifting chorus that fills the world. The sound is loud, but its changes are geologic in their pace. A new major tone might enter the chorus as a new crystal grows large enough to resonate with a deep current, a process that takes centuries.

Life here is rare and strange. There are no birds to fledge in the glass forest, no fish to swim in the shallow, acrid pools. But there are the Carvers. Vast, slow-moving creatures, perhaps the size of a small shuttle. They are more geology than biology, their bodies low-slung and encased in an incredibly thick, lumpy carapace made from the same material as the crystalline spars, only fused and worn into smooth, rounded humps. They look like ambulatory boulders. They do not seem to see or hear in this dim, loud world. They navigate by a sense of pressure and vibration, moving with immense, deliberate slowness through the dense fields of crystal.

As they move, their armored bodies push against the spars. They do not shatter them—the Carvers are not clumsy. They exert a steady, patient pressure, and the crystals groan, sing, and sometimes, over the course of hours, deform and bend out of their path. The "built" structures of this world are the faint traces of their passage: long, winding corridors through the densest thickets where the crystal has been warped into smooth, arched tunnels. These paths are the only safe way to travel. Sometimes, a Carver will stop for decades, settling into the mud and becoming a new, silent hill in the landscape as fresh crystals grow around and over its dormant shell. These are their feeding grounds, or perhaps their resting places. They seem to absorb nutrients directly from the mineral-heavy water, their slow metabolism matching the gradual pace of the world.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute. Everything grows. Everything is sharp. Everything rings. The water is the blood of the world, carrying the mineral nutrients that feed the crystal growth and acting as the bow that plays the landscape. Change is constant but imperceptible moment to moment. To stand still is to eventually be overgrown, to become part of the great, resonant instrument. To move carelessly is to be flayed. The only logic is the slow, patient logic of resonance and accretion. You do not shout to be heard over the din; you learn to feel the sub-harmonic vibrations through the soles of your feet. You do not look for shelter from the rain; you accept the cold, constant damp as the breath of the world itself. You watch for the slow, dark shape of a Carver on the horizon, not as a threat, but as the promise of a path.
