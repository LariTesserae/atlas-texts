This is a place of ceaseless, patient industry.

You are submerged. Not in water, precisely, but in a pressurized, cool liquid that feels thicker, more viscous, carrying the weight of a world above. The pressure is immense, a constant and total embrace that has long since ceased to be a threat and is now simply a condition of being, like gravity. The medium is clear but not empty; a perpetual, slow-motion snow of fine, metallic dust and microscopic, refractive shards drifts eternally downwards.

The noise is the first and last thing you perceive. It is not a cacophony, but a chord, a vast and complex industrial hum. It is the composite groan of a million grindstones, the rhythmic, percussive clatter of countless shifting components, and a foundational, low-frequency thrum that vibrates through the liquid and into your very bones. It is the sound of a factory the size of a mountain, but there are no walls, no ceilings, no built structures of any kind.

You are floating within a caldera, a natural chamber of a great, high seamount. The scale is that of a grand cathedral, its boundaries defined by near-vertical cliffs of sharp, vitreous rock. The material is a kind of obsidian-like basalt, uneroded and new, fractured into vast, geometric planes and razor-edged spurs. There is no softness here, no rounding of time. Everything is acute, as if it shattered into existence only yesterday.

The light is brilliant, but it is not from a sun. It is generated from within the caldera itself, and it is a cold, clinical light. It is the light of arc welding, of stark chemical reactions, of pure energy discharge. It flashes and strobes in a thousand different locations, casting a dizzying, ever-shifting mesh of hard-edged shadows. The dominant illumination comes from the entities that fill this space.

They are the source of the noise, the light, the very nature of this place. They are not animals in a way one might recognize, yet they are teeming and abundant. Call them lithovores, rock-eaters. They are living machines.

Imagine something like a colossal, multi-limbed isopod, but its carapace is not chitin; it is a geodesic lattice of flawless, light-conducting crystal grown from its own metabolic processes. Within this semi-translucent shell, organs pulse with cold, blue and white light. These are the biological engines, the internal turbines that power them. They cling to the caldera walls in dense, overlapping layers, so numerous they form a living, shifting geology of their own. Their limbs, jointed and impossibly strong, terminate not in claws but in industrial tools—rotary grinders, percussive hammers, hyper-precise gripping mandibles faceted from diamond-hard carbides.

They work. That is all they do. With agonizing slowness, they process the mountain itself. One grinds a sheer rock face, its headlamp-eyes bathing the area in a harsh glare as it scrapes away layers of basalt, generating the fine dust that fills the water. Another, nearby, uses its hammer-limb to methodically fracture a crystal outcrop, the sharp *clack* of each blow a repeating note in the industrial symphony. They are not consuming the rock for sustenance in a traditional sense; they are refining it. They are living smelters, biological forges. They draw in raw mineral, process it through their glowing internal crucibles, and extrude it as purified, refined materials—perfect crystalline rods, metallic slurries vented as shimmering clouds, and the very substance of their own growing shells. They are self-replicating, self-maintaining machines, and the mountain is their resource pool.

The density is staggering. In any cubic meter of the liquid, you will find dozens of smaller organisms. There are the "cleaners," symbiotic creatures like metallic sea cucumbers that inch along the lithovores' shells, ingesting waste particulates and polishing their crystal carapaces. There are swarms of "sparks," tiny, fast-moving organisms that dart between the larger engines, feeding on free-floating energy discharges and glowing like fireflies in a dark fluid. There are scavengers below, slug-like forms with tough, abrasive hides, consuming the mineral sludge and "slag" that the larger creatures excrete. Everything is near-touching, a complex, three-dimensional ecosystem built not on photosynthesis but on chemo-mechanical synthesis.

The world has rules. The work is slow, measured by geological time. A single lithovore might spend a century processing one small section of the wall. Change is incremental, patient. The sharp features of the caldera remain because the process of creation and shaping is constant, always refreshing the surfaces. The coolness of the liquid is essential; it is the coolant for these biological engines, preventing their internal forges from melting them down. The engine-song never stops, a testament to a process that has been running for eons and will run for eons more. This is not a world that was built; it is a world that is perpetually *building itself*, its inhabitants the living tools and the living product, all humming their loud, slow, mechanical hymn in the crushing, luminous dark.
