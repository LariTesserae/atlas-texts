The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a collection of noises but a single, solid thing: a pressure on the ears, a vibration felt deep in the bones of your jaw and chest. It is a deafening, industrial roar, a bass thrum so profound it feels like the resonant frequency of the world itself. This roar is the foundation, and woven through it are the higher-pitched sounds of machinery: the rhythmic, heavy *clank-hiss-clank* of immense, slow-moving pistons, the shriek of metal under torsional stress, and the constant, overwhelming rush of a million waterfalls at once.

You are in a cavern, a geode of a place sealed deep beneath the surface. It is not large; one could walk from one end to the other in twenty minutes, if a path could be cleared. But no path can be. The space is saturated, packed to its absolute limit with a fusion of living matter and corroded industry.

This is a city of wood. Not a city of lumber, but of living, growing, engineered wood. The architecture is a single, continuous organism. Great boles of lignin, meters thick and knobbled with tumours of burl, rise like pillars to a ceiling you cannot see, lost in the thick, warm gloom. Their surfaces are not bark but a polished, dark hardwood, slick with perpetual condensation. These pillars are the superstructure, and woven between them is a dense lattice of roots, branches, and woody scaffolding, forming floors, walls, and archways. The "streets" are not open avenues but tight, winding corridors in this wooden matrix, their floors padded with a thick, springy carpet of moss and liverwort that glows with a faint, sickly phosphorescence.

Water is the lifeblood and the solvent. It weeps from every surface. It drips in fat, heavy drops from fleshy, plate-sized leaves that sprout from the walls. It runs in slick-filmed rivulets down the polished boles. It gushes through enormous, pipe-like roots that serve as the city's aqueducts, their roar a major component of the cavern's overwhelming sound. The air is so humid it is nearly liquid, a warm, breathable soup thick with the scent of wet soil, decay, pollen, and hot metal. You feel it condensing on your skin, in your lungs.

Embedded within this living architecture is the machinery. These are not new, gleaming devices; they have been here for an age, settled into their functions, as established as the wood that has grown around them. An immense gear, green with verdigris and furred with moss, turns with geological slowness, its teeth meshing with a rack that is part of a pillar's core wood. A great iron flywheel, half-swallowed by a bulbous root system, spins steadily, flinging off an arc of shimmering water with each rotation. Pistons as thick as a man's torso push and pull, their movements slow but inexorable, driving unseen pumps deep within the city's heartwood.

These are not factories producing goods. They are regulators. They are the heart and lungs of this place, a series of immense mechanical organs integrated into a botanical body. They pump nutrient-rich water up from some deep reservoir, circulating it through the root-pipes. They regulate the temperature, venting gouts of hot steam that hiss into the humid air. They manage the growth of the city itself, groaning and creaking as they apply pressure, guiding the woody expansion in a process that unfolds over centuries. This is a city that is being perpetually built and maintained by its own internal engines.

There is no animal life. No insects buzz in the dim light, no creatures scurry in the mossy corridors. The ecosystem is brutally simple: plant, fungus, and machine. The only light comes from the faint bioluminescence of the floor mosses and from engineered fungi that sprout like shelves from the wooden walls. These fungi glow with a steady, dim orange light, like the embers of a dying furnace, casting the slow-moving machinery in a play of deep, dramatic shadows. The interplay of glowing fungi and dripping, dark wood gives the impression of a cathedral's nave lit by a thousand scattered candles.

The entire system is in a state of slow, managed equilibrium. The machines do not break down; their corrosion is a part of their function, the metal achieving a stasis with the constant moisture. The wood does not rot; it is petrified, hardened, or infused with resins by some process guided by the machines. The city grows, but it also recycles itself, old sections slowly reabsorbed into the living mass as new corridors are patiently extruded elsewhere. Everything is loud, everything is wet, everything is alive, but nothing truly *happens* on a human timescale. It is a place of immense, patient, ongoing creation, a self-sustaining engine of growth, roaring away in the warm, dark earth.
