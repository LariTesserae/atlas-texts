There is no surface, no sky. There is only the medium. It is a world submerged not in water, but in something denser, a quiescent and vital fluid the consistency of thin, warm honey. To exist here is to be held in a constant, gentle embrace. Movement is a deliberate act, a slow-motion swimming that feels more like pushing through heavy air than parting a liquid. The medium is astonishingly clear, and in the bright, diffuse light, you can see for miles. Suspended within it are countless, shimmering motes—fragments of crystal, dormant spores, tiny bubbles of gas—that drift on currents too slow for the eye to track, catching the light like a static field of stars.

The light itself is a mystery. It does not seem to come from a single sun, but radiates from the entire upper hemisphere of existence. Far, far above, the medium thins into a ceiling of pure, unwavering luminescence, a constant and shadowless daylight that filters down, becoming softer and bluer with depth.

This is a high place. The "ground" beneath is the summit of a colossal, submerged mountain range. The terrain is dramatically young, a landscape of black volcanic rock that has only recently punched up into the vitreous sea. There are no soft edges here; the peaks are jagged and cruel, forming glass-sharp ridgelines and sheer cliffs of obsidian. Fields of basaltic scree, unworn by wind or rain, lie in precarious slopes, each stone a perfect, sharp-edged polyhedron. These dark mountains, chaotic and new, form vast, open valleys and knife-edge isthmuses that stretch out into the pearlescent distance. They are the bones of a world still being born.

And on these bones, a forest of impossible density grows. The vegetation is not green and leafy, but crystalline and membranous. These are the vitreophytes, lifeforms perfectly adapted to the high-pressure, viscous environment. They rise from the black rock in stunning profusion, their trunks like pillars of milky quartz, hollow and filled with the same gel as the world outside. Instead of leaves, they have vast, delicate fins of a semi-rigid membrane, some translucent, some tinged with pale blues and deep violets. These fins are angled to catch the overhead light, and a soft, secondary bioluminescence pulses slowly from within the plants' core, a cool counterpoint to the warm fluid. The forest is so thick that in places the trunks of the vitreophytes are almost touching, creating dense, silent groves where the overhead light is filtered into a cathedral-like twilight, shot through with the plants' own internal glow.

The silence is the most profound feature. Sound travels poorly here, its energy quickly absorbed by the dense medium. There is no birdsong, no insectile hum, no rustle of wind through leaves. The auditory world is reduced to a deep, subsonic register. On long, patient cycles, you may feel rather than hear the deep, patient groan of cooling rock settling far below. If you place a hand against the stalk of a great vitreophyte, you might sense a faint, frictionless rustle as its internal fluids circulate over the course of hours. Otherwise, there is a hymnal silence, a quiet so complete it feels sacred.

Life, aside from the dominant flora, is vanishingly rare. The shimmering motes in the water might constitute a kind of microscopic plankton, but there are no fish, no crustaceans, no swimming predators or grazers. The forest is not a habitat; it is simply *itself*. It is a world waiting for its fauna, a stage set for a drama that has not yet begun. To drift through the silent, warm, glowing valleys is to feel like a visitor in the first chapter of creation, before the introduction of movement or will.

Change is geological. Over millennia, new peaks will shoulder their way up from the unseen world below, their arrival marked by a slow, deep vibration that resonates through the gel for centuries. The vitreophytes grow with excruciating slowness, a single new fin unfurling over a decade. The currents are ponderous, glacial, shifting the thermal layers and the fields of spores over geological time. It is a world of immense, unhurried processes, a place where a thousand years passes like a slow afternoon, and the only dynamic force is the patient, inexorable upward thrust of new land into the warm, quiet, luminous sea.
