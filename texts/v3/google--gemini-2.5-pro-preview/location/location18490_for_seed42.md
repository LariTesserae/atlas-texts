You are floating.

The water is not warm, but it isn't cold enough to steal your heat. It’s the profound, patient cool of a cellar or a deep lake, a temperature that speaks of immense volume and slow-moving time. Light filters down from a surface you cannot see, a distant ceiling of shimmering silver. Here, in the mid-depths, the light is a diffuse, cathedral green-gold, throwing vast, slowly swaying god-rays between the colossal pillars that define the space.

This place, a city-grove known as Stillwater, is built within the drowned caldera of a high-mountain volcano. The "ground" is hundreds of feet below, a dark floor of settled silt and volcanic glass. The city itself occupies the upper slopes and the water column above.

It is a world of total vegetation, but sparse form. The architecture and the ecosystem are one. Enormous, singular stalks of a plant called Spire-Kelp rise from the unseen depths. They are not packed together like a forest, but stand as individuals, each one a skyscraper of living tissue separated by wide, open volumes of water. Their trunks, thick as ancient redwoods, are not woody but have the texture of consolidated, hardened kelp, slick with a fine coat of algae and grooved by the patient scraping of grazers. The surface is a mosaic of dark greens and purplish browns, pocked with barnacles the size of dinner plates.

From these central spires, the city is grown. The dwellings are woven pods, living structures coaxed into shape from the fronds and tissues of a more pliable, parasitic vine. They cling to the Spire-Kelp like huge fruits or galls, connected by swinging bridges of braided, living fibre that undulate gently in the imperceptible currents. Each pod has a sphincter-like opening, and its translucent walls glow softly from the bioluminescent mosses cultivated within. This is an urban place, organized and dense with infrastructure, but the tools of its construction are bone-spades for pruning and guiding growth, obsidian knives for harvesting, and the generational knowledge of how to sing to a plant to make it bend.

The atmosphere is the water itself, thick with sound. It is never silent here. A constant, complex hum fills your bones, a sound composed of a thousand layers. The deepest layer is the groaning thrum of the Spire-Kelp stalks, flexing under their own immense weight and the slow push of the water. Higher is the oceanic static of millions of tiny crustaceans and filter-feeders, a constant *shhhhhh* as they process the water for nutrients. And woven through it all is the percussive, abundant life of the fauna. You hear clicks, whistles, and sharp chirps, the sonar pings and communication of the inhabitants. A sudden, whipping roar of displaced water marks a shoal of glass-finned fish, hundreds strong, turning as one. Their passage makes the light dance.

Life is everywhere. Armored, trilobite-like creatures, the size of sheep, crawl methodically up the kelp-spires, their multiple jaws scraping away the film of algae in rhythmic, crescent-shaped patterns. Their rasping is another layer in the soundscape. Smaller, iridescent jellies drift in lazy constellations between the spires, trailing shimmering filaments. The inhabitants of the pods are lithe and slender, moving with the unhurried grace of otters. You see them sometimes, exiting their homes to tend to the nets of fine, golden algae they cultivate, or to hunt the larger, free-swimming grazers with long, bone-tipped spears. Their movements are slow, deliberate, conserving energy in the cool water.

The world is old, established. The edges of the woven pods are soft, blurred by centuries of mossy growth. The great spires themselves have a weathered permanence, as if they have always been here and always will be. Change happens on a geological or botanical timescale. A new dwelling is not built, but grown, a process that takes a generation. The slow, circular current of the caldera is the only wind, the only weather. There are no seasons, only the long, slow cycle of the light from above, brightening and dimming over a period of many days. The world is in a state of quiet, loud, living equilibrium. It is a city that breathes, groans, and filters, a suspended civilization in a cool, green bell jar the size of a mountain.
