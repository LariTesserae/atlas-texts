The first thing is the sound. It is not a collection of noises, but a single, indivisible presence. It is a drone, a hum, a chord of impossible complexity played on an instrument the size of a world. It is the sound of a billion crystal wine glasses being rubbed at once, of a continent-sized tuning fork struck a million years ago and still vibrating. It is so loud it is felt more than heard, a pressure in the bones, a rattling behind the eyes. It is the background against which all other sensations are rendered.

You are somewhere below. The air is cool and heavy, thick with a scent like ozone and ground stone. There is no moisture; to breathe is to feel your lungs slowly desiccate. The air carries the great Sound, making it a physical medium, a dense, humming fluid.

Above, there is no sky, only a ceiling of absolute blackness so far away its scale is a matter of conjecture. From this unseen canopy, and rising from the floor below, is the source of the dim, perpetual twilight: the vegetation.

This is a forest without water, a jungle of pure geology. Great, branching structures of translucent crystal rise from the floor, some slender and sharp like quartz needles, others thick and gnarled like ancient, petrified oaks. They are not inert. They glow from within, a slow, rhythmic pulse of muted colour—amethyst, smoky grey, the gentlest of blues. This is the world’s light. The flora, if it can be called that, grows by impossibly slow accretion, drawing minerals directly from the ancient, dusty substrate. They are Geophytes, crystal-life, and their latticed structures are what resonate with the deep planetary vibrations to create the omnipresent Sound. The ground between these crystalline groves is not soil but a fine, powdery dust, worn down by eons into a state of ultimate smoothness. It is ancient, settled, and utterly sterile. There are no pebbles, no rocks, only the unbroken, polished floor and the glowing, crystalline growths.

The landscape is vast and open, a subterranean valley stretching for miles in every direction. The crystalline flora is lush but sparse; great forests of glowing crystal are separated by plains of gray, resonant dust, creating a patterned emptiness. And across this plain, rising into the dimness, is the city.

It has the silhouette of a metropolis. Spires reach for the unseen ceiling, bridges span chasms of humming darkness, and broad, terraced structures climb like hills. But there is no masonry, no steel, no sign of a builder’s tool. The city is not built; it is grown. The architecture is biological, or something like it. Towers are extruded filaments of a dark, chitinous material, woven together into impossibly complex, filigreed structures. Walls are thick, layered secretions, smoothed and shaped by the slow passage of bodies. The entire settlement is a single, contiguous organism, a coral reef built in a waterless sea.

This is the work of the fauna, and they are everywhere. They are abundant but, like the flora, spread thinly across the immense space. They are creatures of shell and mineral, their bodies geometric and functional. Most are the size of large dogs, insectile beings with six or more legs, their carapaces gleaming like polished obsidian. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their multi-jointed legs making soft, rhythmic *clicks* and *scrapes* on the dusty floor. These sounds are sharp counterpoints to the overwhelming drone, tiny arpeggios in the grand, unchanging chord.

These are the Lithovores, the stone-eaters. They do not have tools; their bodies *are* tools. Their mandibles are hardened grinders that flake away slivers of the crystalline flora for sustenance. Their abdomens secrete the fibrous, resinous material used to grow their city. They are a hive, but a civilization, too. They move in slow, organized lines, some tending to the glowing crystal groves, others scraping at the cavern floor to gather rare minerals, others still adding infinitesimal layers to the city’s living walls. Their existence is one of patient, geological process. A new spire might take a thousand years to extrude.

There is no day or night, only the slow, hypnotic pulse of the glowing crystals. There are no seasons, only the gradual, million-year cycles of geological pressures shifting deep below, which might cause the all-encompassing Hum to change its pitch by a fraction of a degree. The world is stable, ancient, and slow. The air is dry, the light is dim, and the sound is a deafening, ever-present god. It is a city of life where nothing is soft, a world of lushness sustained by stone, an urban hum of creatures who live and build on a timescale closer to erosion than to thought.
