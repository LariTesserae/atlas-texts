The roar is the first and last thing. It is not merely a sound to be heard, but a physical medium, a fluid in which the world is suspended. It has texture, pressure, and temperature. It presses on your skin like a heavy, cool blanket, making the thin air feel thick and substantial. The sound is a foundation of a million different bellowing, shrieking, and humming noises, all layered into a single, deafening, omnidirectional wave that never, ever crests. It is the sound of a star being born and a mountain range being ground to dust, happening simultaneously, constantly, inside a space the size of a grand cathedral.

This world is an interior. There is no sky, only a distant, curving ceiling that is also the floor, for you are standing on the inner surface of a hollow sphere. The material of the sphere is a kind of petrified, pearlescent nacre, unimaginably old. Its surface is weathered into smooth, sweeping continents and shallow, dry basins by eons of an erosion that had no water to carve it. Faint, geometric marks, like the ghosts of circuit boards, are etched into this material, sometimes forming vast, continent-spanning patterns. In places, these patterns are punctuated by scattered, built things: hubs of dull, cold metal from which bundles of glassy conduits emerge and snake across the landscape before plunging back into the nacreous shell. These structures are inert but emanate a deep, harmonic thrum that is a foundational note in the overwhelming symphony of the chamber.

And on this shell, life thrives. It is a lush, vibrant ecology that knows nothing of water. This is a jungle of solidified sound, a forest fed by physics. Stalks of what look like blue-green crystal, metres tall, grow in dense groves. They are fluted like organ pipes, and as the sonic winds of the chamber howl through them, they resonate, shimmering and visibly vibrating. This resonance is their photosynthesis; they feed on the noise, metabolizing specific frequencies and growing in fractal, chaotic patterns toward the loudest currents. Their leaves are not soft tissue but diaphragms of thin, chitinous membrane, stretched taut over crystalline frames, humming and fluttering in the gale of sound. The ground is carpeted not with moss, but with a fine, granular substrate of shattered resonance, a crystal sand that crunches underfoot. In the quieter eddies of the sound-storm, delicate, web-like structures grow between the stalks, like spiderwebs made of spun glass, capturing and focusing specific harmonics. When they become overcharged, they shatter with a sharp chime that is instantly swallowed by the greater roar.

The air, thin and cool, is almost empty. The vast interior volume of the sphere is a near-void, a maelstrom of acoustics and little else. All life clings to the inner surface. And it is abundant. Skittering between the crystalline trunks are creatures made of the same principles. They are not flesh and blood, but articulated constructs of resonant material. Some are like trilobites with shells of layered, vibrating plates, crawling slowly and grazing on the harmonic residue that coats the sound-flora. They communicate by rapidly altering their own resonant frequency, a flicker of silent conversation lost in the hurricane of noise.

Higher up, things drift in the sonic currents. They are like jellyfish or manta rays, with bodies that are little more than vast, translucent sails designed to catch specific wavelengths. They ride the shockwaves that periodically pulse from the chamber's core, undulating through the airless-feeling void, their bodies glowing faintly as they absorb energy. Flocks of smaller things, like motes of dust or sharp-edged insects, swarm in chaotic vortices. They have no wings, but are propelled by the constant, violent pressure differentials in the air. They are less creatures and more self-propagating physical phenomena. They collide, shatter, and their fragments become new motes, a lifecycle of pure, turbulent energy.

The light is constant. There is no day or night, only a perpetual, milky noon that emanates from the nacreous shell itself. It is a cold, shadowless light that reveals everything with flat, clinical clarity. It has no single source, and so you cannot turn away from it.

The rules here are simple and absolute. There is no thirst. There is no warmth from a sun. There is only the all-consuming sound. The greatest danger is not being eaten, but being unmade. The sonic environment is so turbulent that reality itself seems provisional. A powerful, low-frequency wave can phase through a grove of crystal-stalks, and for a moment they will seem to vanish, only to slam back into existence a second later. To be caught in such a wave is to be disassembled. The very space you occupy can be struck like a drum, its harmonics suddenly rewritten. The scattered technological artifacts are the only stable points, anchors in the storm. Their purpose is unknown, but they seem to be regulators, or perhaps even the source, of the phenomenal energy that fills this sphere. They are ancient beyond measure, and utterly silent to the touch, their surfaces absorbing all vibration, the only quiet places in a world made deafeningly, shatteringly alive.
