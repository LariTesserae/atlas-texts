This place is called the Gyre. You stand on a precipice at what feels like the top of the world. The air is thin but carries a dry, pleasant warmth, like the inside of a kiln hours after the fire has died. Below you, the peaks of other mountains, colossal things of rust-red and ochre stone, break through a sea of motionless, pearlescent haze. They are not sharp, youthful peaks; these are the worn, rounded teeth of a leviathan dead for a billion years, ground smooth by a process that has had all the time in the world.

This is not a sea of water vapor. It is a dense, unbreathable gas, a soupy atmospheric stratum that has settled into the planet's deepest basins. It has a name among the locals: the Stillness. It is almost inert, interacting with the world through pressure and slow, grinding friction. Over cosmic timescales, it has acted like a polishing cloth, scouring the mountainsides into their current, settled forms. Where the Stillness meets the lower slopes, the rock is burnished to a mirror sheen. It is a world carved by a fluid that isn't wet.

There are no plants. Not a single leaf, blade of grass, or wisp of moss. The foundation of this world's ecology is something else entirely. In the middle altitudes, just above the densest layers of the Stillness, grow the litho-scleractinia, the “stone corals.” They are not alive in a biological sense. They are vast, intricate, crystalline structures that precipitate directly out of the supersaturated atmosphere. They grow in fractal bouquets and geodesic reefs, some the size of hills, glinting with a soft internal luminescence. They are a slow, geological process that mimics life, converting atmospheric energy and trace minerals into solid, organized form. Their growth is imperceptibly slow, a single new facet appearing over a generation.

And upon these crystal fields, life grazes. Fauna is abundant, but it is a silent, armored fauna. Herds of creatures like enormous, multi-legged trilobites, their carapaces the color of oxidized iron, move with geological slowness across the lithic reefs. They are the "grinders." Their mouthparts are not flesh but arrays of diamond-hard mandibles that shear off chunks of the crystal coral. The sound is not a crunch, but a high, thin *scree* that is quickly swallowed by the immense space and the thick, sound-dampening air. They digest the crystal, extracting the stored energy and minerals they need to fuel their slow metabolisms and build their heavy shells. Hovering above are predators, things like manta rays made of stretched leather and chitin, who drift on the thermal updrafts. They don't hunt with speed, but with patience, waiting for a grinder to stray onto an unstable ledge. There is no water to drink; all necessary fluids are synthesized internally, a thick, oily ichor that circulates within their sealed bodies.

Far above the grazers and the crystal reefs, clinging to the highest, most stable spires of the mountain ranges, are the settlements. This is Lastrung, a city less built *on* the mountain than excavated *from* it. It is a network of balconies, cavern-plazas, and deep-bored shafts connected by funiculars and massive, slow-moving elevators. The architecture is heavy, brutalist, and eternal. Everything is constructed from the native red rock and a dark, smelted metal of incredible tensile strength. It is an urban environment in its interconnectedness and scale, but it is profoundly quiet and sparsely populated. The spaces are vast, designed to humble. A single plaza might be a mile across, its far side hazy in the distance, containing only a handful of figures moving with the same deliberate pace as the world's fauna.

Lastrung is an industrial hub. The quiet is punctuated by the low, resonant hum of machinery operating deep within the mountain. These are not fast, clattering engines, but immense, potent generators that run on geothermal gradients and piezomechanics, harnessing the stress-energy of the planet's tectonics. The industry is extraction. The most prized litho-scleractinia produce resonant crystals at their core, which, when properly machined, can store and release phenomenal amounts of energy. The factories of Lastrung are refineries, where these crystals are carefully cut and attuned. Robotic arms, moving with the patient grace of a glacier, guide beams of coherent energy to shape the crystals over weeks.

The light is normal, cast by a yellow-white sun that hangs in the thin, clear sky of the upper altitudes. But looking down into the Stillness is like looking into a clouded mirror. At dusk, the sun sinks below the horizon of the gaseous sea long before it sinks below the true horizon of the world, creating a "Sunken Sun" phenomenon, where the light diffuses through the dense medium, causing the entire lower world to glow with a soft, internal fire.

The rules of the Gyre are simple. Height is life; the deeps are a slow, suffocating oblivion. Energy comes from the rock and the air, not from sun and water. Life is not soft, but hard. Change does not happen in seasons, but in epochs. The whole world operates on a rhythm that is profoundly patient. You can feel it in your bones, a sense of time so deep that your own lifespan feels like a spark in a vast, dark hall. The only urgent sounds are the rare, sharp cracks of rock stress, or the distant, scraping call of a grinder, a lonely note in the immense, quiet symphony of the Gyre.
