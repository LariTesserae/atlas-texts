The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not a collection of noises but a single, textured, deafening presence. It is the sound of a trillion infinitesimal lives, a granular roar like a waterfall of sand miles wide. It is the chittering of microscopic gears, the drone of a billion vibrating wings, the collective hum of system-scale biology. The sound is so absolute that it becomes a form of silence; it erases nuance, swallows footfalls, and renders the human voice a pathetic mime. To be here is to be immersed in this ocean of noise, to feel it in your bones as a constant, low-grade vibration.

You are standing on what you might mistake for a plain, but the ground is wrong. It is not soil or stone, but a vast, continuous mat of living matter. It feels like ancient, compressed cork underfoot—springy, yet unbreakably firm. Its color is a tapestry of age: sun-bleached silver-blonde on the rises, deep ochres and browns in the gentle swales. The surface is worn smooth, polished not by wind and water in the geological sense, but by ages of growth, decay, sloughing, and regrowth. It is a surface that has achieved its final, settled form. This is the Canopy of Sighs, the upper rind of a world-plant.

The elevation is extreme. You are perched at a height that would shear the tops from stone mountains. There is no ‘below’ in the conventional sense, only a dawning awareness of fathomless emptiness glimpsed through the very ground you stand on. The plant-substrate is not entirely solid. It is a lattice, a sponge, a coral of incomprehensible size. In places, the material thins to a translucent membrane over chasms of air and hazy light. In others, bore-holes, some as wide as houses, plummet down into the humming darkness of the plant's interior. The air is warm and persistently damp, thick with the smell of ozone, pollen, and slow decay. This moisture doesn't come from rain, which is infrequent here above the clouds, but from the world itself. It is the exhalation of the great plant, condensing on every surface in a perpetual dew. In the depressions, shallow pools of this sweet, vital water gather, reflecting the stark, open sky.

The landscape is defined by its emptiness and its openness. The Canopy sags and rises in gentle, continent-sized folds. You can see for a hundred miles, your view unimpeded by anything but the curvature of this living world and the shimmering air. There are no forests, no distinct trees, because everything is the tree. You are walking on its skin. The ‘features’ of the landscape—the low ridges like healed scars, the broad valleys like the hollows between muscles—are all anatomical. And between these features is vast, empty space. The density of *things* is near zero. You could walk for a day and see nothing but the fibrous ground and the endless sky, yet never for a moment feel alone.

This is because of the fauna. They are overwhelming, but they are not large. The deafening sound is their collective voice. They live *in* the substrate, not *on* it. The Canopy is riddled with them. If you were to press your ear to a thin spot in the rind, the roar would resolve for a moment into its component parts: the furious scrabbling of glitter-mites quarrying new tunnels, the resonant thrumming of beetles that use their entire bodies as sonic instruments, the wet, percussive slap of filter-feeders catching airborne spores in the deep shafts. Occasionally, you see a sign of them on the surface—a shimmering slick of nutrient-paste left by a grazer, or a cloud of iridescent dust kicked up from a bore-hole by some territorial dispute miles below. The creatures are the world's microbiome, its circulatory and nervous systems, and their lives generate the heat that keeps the Canopy warm.

There are scattered marks of other, larger beings. Faint, almost-lost paths are scored into the rind, routes worn down by the passing of feet over millennia. These are not roads, just subtle compressions in the living floor. Here and there, a small structure stands. It is a lean-to, a windbreak, a hut woven from the tougher fibers pulled from the rind's under-layers and anchored into the living ground. The technology is primitive because it has to be; there is no metal to mine, no stone to knap. Tools are the sharpened, petrified ribs of the great-plant, salvaged from deep within its structure where pressures have fossilized the lignin-chitin. A cutting edge is a piece of the world sharpened against itself. These shelters are weathered to the same silver-grey as the high points of the Canopy, almost invisible until you are upon them. They seem less built and more grown, like galls or fungal blooms.

The world is active. The light shifts as the day progresses, casting long, moving shadows from the high ridges. The wind, when it comes, is a great sigh through the porous body of the world, a note that momentarily harmonizes with the insectile roar before fading. Sometimes, a shudder will pass through the rind, a slow, tectonic ripple that is not an earthquake but a muscular contraction, the world-plant shifting its weight. And in the distance, a shimmering, gelatinous cloud might rise slowly from a chasm—a colony of air-jellies, a cubic mile of unified life, ascending into the high atmosphere to spawn and die, their silent, graceful journey a stark visual counterpoint to the omnipresent sound. To see them is a rare and holy event.

This place has one fundamental rule: there is nothing but the plant. The water is its breath, the fauna its gut, the ground its skin. The scattered builders are merely another kind of symbiotic life, carving out a temporary existence on a scale that barely registers to their host. It is a world of staggering size yet intimate biological detail, a place both hollow and full, silent and screaming. You are a mote of dust on the back of a living god, and the sound of its life is the only world you will ever know.
