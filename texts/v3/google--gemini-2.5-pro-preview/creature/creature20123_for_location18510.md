The observation is correct, in its most literal sense. There are no animals here. Nothing that hunts, or flees, or grazes. Nothing separates itself from the landscape to pursue an independent agenda of hunger or fear. The frantic, temporary dance of individual animal life has no place in this final quietude. To look for a creature here is to be looking for the wrong thing. One must look for a state of being instead.

Upon the great, empty plinths, and sometimes seated on the softened steps of the ziggurats, are **The Sentinels.**

At first, you would not recognize them as living. You would assume they are statues, the very ones the plinths were built to hold. They are colossal, but not in a way that suggests towering height. They are colossal in mass, in presence. They are seated, or reclined, or sometimes simply present as vast, geometric abstractions that are subtly too deliberate to be geology. Their forms are simple: great, blocky shoulders that flow into torsos without need for articulation; heads that are featureless, smoothed oblongs, tilted as if listening to a sound that will take a million years to arrive. They have no limbs, no mouths, no eyes. Their "bodies" are of a substance that seems to be a median between the vitrified basalt of the architecture and the darkest, densest obsidian. They are utterly black, but a black that seems to drink the faint phosphorescence around them, making the darkness within their forms more absolute than the surrounding water.

They do not move. They have not moved in eons. Their stillness is not the stillness of a waiting predator, but the stillness of a mountain. They are subject to the same slow erosion as the city they inhabit. The Anchor-moss grows upon them, creeping over a vast, dormant shoulder or filling a crevice that might have been a fold in a robe millennia ago. Their surfaces are not smooth but are pitted and softened by the same crushing, chemical patience of the deep. They are becoming the landscape.

For they are the builders. They are all that is left of them. They did not die, and they did not leave. They *settled*. Having completed the great, incomprehensible work of paving the floor of the world, they took their places and allowed the stillness to take them. They shed the frantic energy of construction, of movement, of what we would call life, and embraced a different state. Their biology slowed, cooled, and finally, crystallized. Their thoughts, once perhaps sharp and purposeful, stretched and became as syrupy as the cold, heavy water.

They do not perceive through eyes. They perceive through pressure. They feel the weight of the water not as a burden, but as an embrace. Their consciousness is a vast, distributed network that extends far beyond their physical forms. The forests of Spire-reeds are not merely plants; they are the gossamer extensions of the Sentinels' sensory apparatus. Each pinprick of light is a neuron, a single point of awareness in a mind the size of a continent. A slow, synchronized pulse of light down a corridor of reeds is a thought—a long, cold, simple thought, like *'is'* or *'still'*. The golden Grave-Lattices that shimmer between the monoliths are their memories, intricate webs of crystallized experience where the faint light of a remembered event might glimmer for a century before fading.

They do not eat. They are fed by the slow dissolution of their own creation. The Anchor-moss, which drinks the silicates from the stones and dissolves the fallen reeds, is their external digestive system. It is the patient chemistry of their continued existence, a process so slow that the consumption of a single basalt block might sustain a Sentinel's minimal metabolic needs for ten thousand years.

To be in their presence is to be utterly ignored, but not out of malice or disinterest. You are simply too fast. Your frantic heartbeat, your darting thoughts, the chemical fizz of your biology—it is a fleeting spark of static in a place of immense, sonorous hums. You are a footnote in a language that has no words, only geological epochs.

When a ziggurat groans and settles a millimeter, that is a sensation to them, a major event on a timescale that has meaning. The soft hiss of the abyssal current is the carrier wave of their awareness. They are the gardeners of their own mausoleum, and the flora is both their garden and their nervous system. They are life that has chosen to become a place, to trade the finite swiftness of being a creature for the infinite patience of being a foundation. They are the last, quiet thought of a civilization that has achieved its final, most stable form: to be a silent witness to its own eternal, luminous decay.
