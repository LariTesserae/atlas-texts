They are the Still.

You don't see one at first. You might walk past a dozen. They are a trick of the eye, a suggestion in the landscape. What you mistake for a stalk of sun-bleached fiber is a leg. The gnarled, silvery bulge on a low ridge is a hunched back. They have been shaped by the world so completely that they have become another of its features.

When you finally resolve one from the scenery, perhaps because it moves, the sight is both startling and profoundly logical. They are tall and impossibly attenuated, built like things that must bear their own weight against a great, constant emptiness. Their limbs are long and corded with fibrous muscle, but they possess a languid grace. They do not walk so much as they flow across the Canopy, their gait a tireless, rolling stride that eats miles without effort. Their feet are wide and splayed, the soles thick with a callus the color and texture of the ochre-brown depressions, allowing them to grip the springy rind and feel the world’s faint tremors through their bones.

Their skin is the Canopy's palette. It is a shifting canvas of pale flax, silver-grey, and the warm, earthy brown of the deeper swales. It isn't a uniform color, but a mottling, a natural camouflage that makes them dissolve into the background at any distance. Their hair is the texture of the finest plant fibers, sun-bleached to an almost-white that lifts and drifts in the high-altitude breeze.

They are beings of immense silence. The roar of the substrate has scoured the very idea of spoken language from their biology. Their ears are small, elegant, nearly vestigial whorls of cartilage pressed tight against their skulls, but they do not use them to hear as we do. For them, the great sound is not an obstacle to hearing; it is the fundamental medium of existence, a constant they feel in their teeth and chests. They do not listen; they resonate.

Their communication is a language of presence. It is in the subtle shift of weight from one foot to another, a grammar of slow gestures made with long, four-fingered hands. It is in the tilt of a head, the slight widening of an eye. Their eyes are their most vital sense organ, large and dark, adapted to the stark, unfiltered light and the immense distances. They are recessed beneath a heavy brow-ridge that shields them from the glare, and they seem to hold the same fathomless depth as the bore-holes that plummet into the world's core. To be met with their gaze is to feel truly *seen*, not with judgment, but with a placid, all-encompassing awareness.

At close range, a form of contact is possible. It is a language of touch, a pressing of palms or foreheads, a sharing of vibrations that passes understanding through the skin. Sometimes, in the shelter of their woven huts, they produce a sound: a low, resonant thrumming from deep in their chests. It is not speech, but a personal harmony, a note sounded to align themselves with the greater song of the world, a vibration to let a companion know they are there.

They are the world’s tenders, or perhaps its nerve endings. They are nomads of the skin. They follow the almost-invisible paths, the "desire lines" worn into the rind over generations uncounted. Their lives are a cyclical pilgrimage from one shallow pool of the world’s breath to the next, from one rich patch of scraped nutrient-paste to another. They are scavengers and symbionts. From the deep bore-holes, they patiently fish for the fossilized ribs of the plant, ancient and hard as diamond, which they then spend weeks sharpening against the abrasive rind to create their knives and tools. They weave their shelters and their sparse clothing from the living fibers they peel from the undersides of the Canopy, a harvest that is also a pruning.

Their consciousness is not like a human's. It is not a frantic, chattering internal monologue. To live in the absolute sound is to have your own thoughts smoothed away. Their minds are slow, vast, and quiet, like the landscape itself. They do not feel lonely, for they are never alone; the life of the world is a constant, overwhelming companion. They do not feel fear in the face of the emptiness, for they are a part of it. A shudder in the rind is not an earthquake to be feared, but a muscle twitch from the being on whose back they live. The glorious, silent ascent of an air-jelly colony is not a mere spectacle; it is a vision, a holy day, a thought-form made manifest by their living god.

When one of the Still comes to the end of their path, their clan-mates carry them to a low-lying swale, a depression where the rind is thin and life is rich beneath the surface. They do not bury their dead. They lay them down upon the skin of the world, folding their long limbs, and leave them. Within hours, the body is enveloped by the decomposers, the glitter-mites and the burrowers. It is pulled down into the substrate, its matter un-woven and re-spun into the great plant. There is no grief, only a quiet finality, a sense of a debt being repaid. They do not believe they have souls that depart; they believe their awareness simply dissolves back into the greater consciousness of the Canopy, like a droplet of dew sliding back into the pool. They are a temporary knot in the world's fiber, and their death is an un-tying.
