The inhabitants of Fenestraria are the Fenestrarians. They are seldom seen, and a stranger might live for a year within the weeping walls and see only the evidence of their passage: a tool left by a wall, a curtain of shells swaying in an unseen draft, the faintest warmth from a dying ember. They are a people of twilight and damp, and they have become like their city: pale, quiet, and saturated with a deep, geological patience.

Their bodies are a testament to their world. They are slight and wiry, with a flexibility born of navigating narrow, twisting passages. Their skin is the colour of a fish's belly or a submerged root—a luminous, unsettling pallor that has never known the full sun. It is smooth, hairless, and cool to the touch, with a slick, almost waxy quality that repels the constant drip of water, causing it to bead and run off as it does from the stone itself. In the diffuse, grey light of the lower chambers, their forms seem to blur at the edges, absorbing the light rather than reflecting it, making them difficult to focus on. They appear and disappear into the glistening gloom like a trick of the eye.

To see a Fenestrarian's face is a rare intimacy. Their eyes are large, dark, and devoid of discernible pupils, like pools of ink. They are not the eyes of a creature that relies on sight for detail, but for perceiving shade, movement, and the faint luminescence of the Fenestrite in the lowest light. Their vision is secondary. Their primary sense is hearing. Their ears are delicately shaped but not unusually large; rather, their entire bodies act as resonators. They can stand in a chamber and, by the subtle shift in the echoes of a thousand distant drips, map the passages around them. They can feel the deep, slow vibration of the stone's growth through the soles of their bare, wide, and unnervingly soft feet, which grip the slick floors with the tenacity of a tree frog. They navigate by a symphony that is, to an outsider, mere noise. The *plink* of a drop in a deep pool tells them the depth of the cistern below; the fine *hiss* of water on a wall maps its texture and incline. The scrape of a fellow carver’s tool is not just a sound, but a location, a signature, and a story of work being done.

They are a people who move with a profound economy of motion. There is no haste, no sudden gesture. To slip on the glossy floors is to risk a broken bone, a slow and lonely death. So they glide more than they walk, their centre of gravity low, one hand often trailing along the weeping wall, reading its story with their fingertips. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: the palms are broad and covered in tough, calloused pads, yet the fingers are long, graceful, and articulate. They are the hands of a musician and a stonemason combined, capable of wielding a heavy, flint-headed chisel with brute force and then carving the most delicate, feather-light patterns to test the stone's integrity.

Their life is the Great Work: the hollowing. They do not speak of it as a chore, but as a conversation, a sacred duty. A child is given their first scraper not long after they learn to walk, taught to maintain the small niche where they sleep. Their folklore is not of gods or heroes of battle, but of legendary Carvers. There is the tale of Elara, who heard a new passage in the stone’s groan and opened a shaft that brought down the afternoon light for the first time in generations. There is the cautionary myth of Kael, who carved with greed instead of respect, going too deep, too fast, until the passage collapsed and the stone reclaimed him.

When a Fenestrarian dies, they are not buried. Their body is carried to a disused chamber, one that has been marked for reclamation. They are laid in a shallow alcove, and the community withdraws. They cease carving in that area, and the Great Work is reversed. The ever-seeping, mineral-rich water begins its slow task. Over the course of a lifetime, the glittering crystals deposit and grow. The walls thicken, the ceiling lowers, and the alcove is slowly sealed. The body is encased in a tomb of pure Fenestrite, becoming one with the city that was their life, their partner, and their eternal taskmaster.

They do not speak often, and their language, when used, is a soft, guttural series of clicks and hums that mimics the sounds of the water. More often, they communicate through action: leaving a finely-sharpened tool for a neighbour whose passage is growing dangerously thin; clearing the algae from a communal cistern; a shared rhythm of scraping that says, *I am here. You are not alone. The work continues.*

They are the city's consciousness, its nervous system. Without them, Fenestraria would be a silent, solid monument. With them, it is a living being, its stone heart slowly hollowed out by its own pale, quiet, and utterly inseparable children. To encounter one is to encounter a living piece of the Weeping City itself, paused for a moment in its endless, patient dialogue with the stone.
