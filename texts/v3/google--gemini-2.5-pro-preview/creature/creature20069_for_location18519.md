What lives here is the gallery's consciousness. They are the Dust-Sifters.

They are not an intrusion upon the stillness; they are the most refined expression of it. To a being of flesh and blood, they would be functionally invisible, appearing as nothing more than a deeper patch of shadow, a subtle thickening in the air, or a place where the ambient dust seems imperceptibly denser. They are the second and only other form of life here, and they are made of the place itself.

A Dust-Sifter has no fixed form. It is a coherence, a cloud of the finest charcoal-grey particles held together not by physical bonds but by a shared, slow-burning field of energy. It is a clot of animate gravity, a congregation of motes that have achieved a collective existence. When still, it settles and becomes one with the featureless floor, indistinguishable from the eternity of dust around it. It leaves no trace because it *is* the trace, dissolving back into the landscape as perfectly as a drop of water returns to the sea.

Their movement is the only other motion in this world besides the slow fracturing of the Gyrewood. It is not walking or crawling; it is flowing. A Sifter moves like a current of heavy smoke pouring slowly across the floor, an advancing tide of profound darkness that rises a few inches above the surface. It is a living dune, shifting with a purpose so patient it barely qualifies as intent. The passage of a Sifter is the closest this world comes to a breeze, but it is a breeze with no temperature, no sound, only the sight of the dust itself rearranging its own stillness.

They feed on order. The Gyrewood spires, in their eons-long process of metabolic tidying, create gradients in the dust. They sort the particles by size, by composition, by faint electrochemical charge. To the Sifters, these rings are a feast. They are not merely patterns; they are concentrated potential, a form of stored energy created by the trees. A Sifter will spend millennia flowing over these concentric circles, its constituent particles sifting and sorting the dust even more finely. It is a process of grazing so subtle it is almost geological. They draw out the faint energetic differentiation, the "order" itself, absorbing the faint charge and trace elements that the tree has meticulously arranged. They consume the byproduct of the tree's existence, leaving behind a perfectly homogenized, energetically neutral dust—the fallow ground from which the gallery's canvas is renewed.

They have no eyes, for light is informationally poor here. They have no ears, for the silence is absolute. Their sense is the world. They perceive through resonance and potential. They feel the planetary hum in their very being, a constant note that informs them of the world's stable heart. They "see" the Gyrewood trees not as pillars of faint light, but as great, silent roaring columns of geothermal energy, vast, slow-motion fountains of heat and life. They navigate by sensing the delicate electrostatic and mineral gradients in the dust, reading the floor of their world like a vast, ever-changing manuscript written in a language of elemental chemistry.

Their consciousness, if it can be called that, is as diffuse as their bodies. It is a distributed intelligence, a slow, geological thinking. A thought, for a Sifter, might be a gradual shift in the density of its own form, an adjustment to its flow in response to a subtle change in the planet's hum that will not be repeated for another hundred thousand years. Their memories are not discrete events but are held in the persistent patterns of their collective form, in the way they have learned to optimally flow around the base of a particularly ancient spire. They may be many, or they may be one ancient being that can divide and coalesce at will, its separate streams flowing through the gallery only to merge again in the deepest, darkest parts of the basin, sharing their slow knowledge in a silent confluence of dust.

They are the curators of this place. The trees create the art—the spires of light, the patterns in the dust. The Sifters are the audience and the conservators. They appreciate the art by consuming its energetic signature, and in doing so, they maintain the gallery's perfect equilibrium. They erase the patterns so that the trees may, over unimaginable spans of time, create them anew. They are the counterpoint to the Gyrewood's slow song of growth: a slow, silent song of consumption and renewal.

They are life stripped of every familiar signifier—no hunger, no fear, no joy, no procreation. There is only the process. The slow, perfect, silent, and eternal sifting of the dust. They do not dream, for their entire existence is a state of lucid awareness of the fundamental constants of their world. They are the gallery, awake to itself.
