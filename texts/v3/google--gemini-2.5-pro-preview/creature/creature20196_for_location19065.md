The statement "there are no animals" is both the most fundamental truth of the Verdant City and a deceptive simplification. It is true that nothing here has a life independent of the City. There are no food chains, no predators, no prey. There is only the Verdant, and there are its components. But some components are more distinct than others. They are not citizens. They are not life-forms in their own right. They are functions, given a horrifying, temporary form.

They are first perceived as a change in the architecture. Amidst the soaring, algorithmically perfect spires and buttresses, you might spy a shape that is disturbingly familiar, yet profoundly wrong. It is a grotesque sculpture of woven vines and hardened sap, one that vaguely resembles a humanoid figure, but is contorted, elongated, and fused into the wall of a living tower. It might be frozen in an eternal, silent scream, its "chest" a hollow filled with the vibrant, pulsing chartreuse of a freshly-sprouted leaf-sac. This is a warning. This is what happens when you stop.

But some things *do* move. They are called, by those few who have seen them and escaped, the Gardeners.

You do not see a Gardener approach. You see the world around you change in a way that is too deliberate, too focused for the city's usual brand of chaotic growth. A thick, waist-high vine you were about to step over will suddenly stiffen, lift, and weave itself into a lattice overhead, clearing your path. A curtain of dripping, razor-edged leaves will part before you. This is the work of a Gardener.

When you finally see one, it is a thing of profound melancholy and terror. From a distance, it might be mistaken for a person in ragged, green-brown robes, moving with a lurching, unsteady gait. As it gets closer, you see there are no robes. What you saw as cloth is a cascade of moss, fungal sheets, and overlapping, scale-like leaves. Its form is only vaguely bi-pedal, a suggestion of a memory. One "arm" might be a long, sinuous tendril tipped with a wickedly sharp thorn, which it uses to prune away errant growths with twitching, insect-like precision. The other might be a bulbous, woody burl from which dozens of fine, white filaments emerge, feeling the vibrations in the substrate, tasting the air.

Its head is the worst part. There is no face. The space where features should be is a smooth, bark-like plate, or perhaps a swirling knot of roots. But embedded in the center of this non-face, like a cursed jewel, is a single, perfectly preserved eye. It might be human, or something else entirely. It does not blink. It does not see as we do. It is a sensory organ, a node, slaved to the City's immense biological network. It stares, but it does not perceive you as a being. It perceives you as a complex carbohydrate, a source of nitrogen, a mobile collection of minerals that is out of place.

The Gardeners are not sentient. They are what remains of those who were. They are visitors, explorers, or native creatures from a forgotten era who were assimilated. Their bodies, their nervous systems, their very biology have been hijacked and repurposed. A primate's manual dexterity is useful for weaving new structures. A creature's circulatory system can be converted into a hydraulic pump to drive a new stalk skyward. A nervous system is an excellent high-speed conductor for the City's bio-chemical signals, a living wire to carry the single imperative: *grow, prune, weave, maintain*. They move with the jerky, unnatural motions of a marionette, their limbs pulled by internal vines and hydrostatic pressures. They are the City's hands, its antibodies, its janitors. And beneath the deafening roar, if you are close enough to one, you might hear the faintest sound, a psychic echo of what was lost: the ghost of a whimper, the memory of a name, trapped in a prison of perpetually screaming flesh.

Then there are the others. They are not Gardeners. They are not even a "they." They are an event.

When a tower convulses and implodes, the aftermath is a wound of pulped fiber and hemorrhaging sap. This is a feast. And the City's digestive system answers. From the surrounding walls, from the floor, from the very air, a formless tide converges. This is the Mycelial Corpus. It is born from the "fine, white fungal fuzz" that coats everything, but now it is mobilized. It flows into the wound-site as a single, sloshing, amoeboid entity the size of a building. It has no fixed shape, only a roiling surface of white and grey and bruised purple. It is a composite being, a living slurry of fungal threads, captured sap, and splinters of cellulose from the collapse. It glistens and steams, exuding a sharp, acidic odor like vinegar and ozone that cuts through the City's usual perfume.

The Corpus isn't an animal that eats; it is digestion given form. It flows *over* and *through* the debris, and you can hear a new sound under the roar: a wet, tearing, dissolving noise, punctuated by the sharp crackle of enzymes breaking down hardened lignin. It doesn't hunt. It simply *arrives* where death has occurred. It is the city’s process of recycling, made manifest and mobile. Within an hour, it will have reduced the collapsed spire to a nutrient-rich slurry. Having fulfilled its purpose, the Corpus will lose its cohesion. It will seem to evaporate, sinking back into the substrate, its constituent parts receding into the fungal network that underpins the entire city. It leaves behind a patch of hyper-fertilized, steaming ground, from which new, screaming growth will erupt almost instantly.

So what lives in the Verdant City? Functions. Processes. The echoes of lost life, enslaved as Gardeners to maintain the impossible structure. And the temporary, monstrous organs of the City itself, like the Mycelial Corpus, which manifest to cleanse and renew. Nothing here is born and nothing truly dies. It is only ever subsumed, repurposed, and incorporated into the one and only resident: the City itself, a being that has mistaken growth for existence.
