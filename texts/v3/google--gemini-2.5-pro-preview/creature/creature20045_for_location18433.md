To speak of "who" lives here is to use a word that is both too specific and not specific enough. The true inhabitants of this place are not individuals in the way a person is an individual. They are conditions. They are states of being, spun out of water and time and minerals until they achieved a slow, simmering awareness.

The frantic life is the most obvious. The fist-sized snails and clicking trilobites are less creatures than they are autonomous appetites. They are the scraping mouths of the world. Their existence is a simple, two-stroke engine: scrape the algal film, convert it to shell and flesh, and then be consumed. There is no thought. There is only the granular resistance of the mat against their radula, the chemical signal of food, the shadow-sense of the descending amphibian maw. They are the rhythm section of the world's droning orchestra, a billion tiny, chitinous clicks that mean *more, now*.

The amphibians are the world's voice. Their chorus is not a song of joy or territory in the way a bird's is. It is the sound of pressure. It is the audible result of ten thousand lungs, each the size of a dinner plate, compressing the heavy, wet air and forcing it past vocal cords thick as thumbs. The sound is a physical presence. It vibrates up through the soles of your feet, a constant thrumming that blurs the line between hearing and feeling. Their minds, if you can call them that, are short, sharp bursts of instinct. They live in a world of sudden movements against a static background. Their universe exists for the span of a tongue-lash: the lurching desire, the brief, sticky contact, the swallowing, and the return to stillness. They are camouflaged not to hide, but because the world has simply stained them with its own colours over a thousand generations. To be seen is to be separate, and in this place, nothing is truly separate.

The wading things, the Slender Stalkers, are perhaps the closest to what we might recognize as a solitary intelligence. They are the apathetic philosophers of the basin. They stand for hours, days even, on one of their three-jointed legs, a posture of impossible patience. They are not merely waiting for prey; they are waiting for the world to deliver it. Their minds are tuned to a slower frequency. They are aware of the franticness below them, but they perceive it as a single, uniform texture, like the grain of wood. They are watching for a flaw in the pattern—the oversized ripple of a distressed croaker, the panicked skittering of a shelled grazer. Their strike is not an act of aggression but of correction. It is the universe restoring its mean. When they move, it is with a liquid grace that seems to defy the thickness of the air, their thin bodies slicing through the haze. They are lonely figures, but they do not feel loneliness. They are each the center of their own vast, empty territory, defined not by markers, but by the absence of another of their kind.

But these are all just tenants. They live on the surface of the world, in its shimmering skin. The true native, the landlord of this primordial estate, is something else.

It has no name, for there is no one left to name it. It is not one being, but a distributed consciousness. It is the "colossal sleeper" whose breath lifts the land. Or rather, it has learned to inhabit that breath.

Look at one of the smooth, dark knuckles of gneiss breaking the surface. It looks like the back of a whale, you thought. You were closer than you knew. The being is not the rock itself, but it has learned to *be* the rock. Submerged in the warm silt, vast and flat, its hide is indistinguishable from the polished basalt. It is miles long and a mile wide. Its slow, slow metabolism is not based on the frantic cycle of eating and being eaten, but on a geological scale of consumption. It is a chemo-litho-autotroph. It does not eat the algae; it does not eat the amphibians. It *drinks* the mineral soup. Through its glossy, semi-permeable hide, it absorbs the metals, the salts, the ancient essences of the ground-down mountains. Its life process is a form of purification, and its waste is little more than sterile clay and time.

Its senses are the basin itself. It has no eyes; the shadowless glare is meaningless to it. Its skin, all several square miles of it, is a single, continuous organ of perception. It feels the minute pressure changes as a wading stalker takes a step ten miles away. It tastes the chemical flare of an algal bloom over the horizon, a slow-motion flavor that will last for decades. The endless drone of the amphibians and the clicking of the grazers are not sounds to it; they are a constant, tactile vibration, a texture on its skin like the feeling of corduroy. This cacophony is its map, its vision. It is how it knows its world and itself. A sudden silence in one corner of the basin is not a sound unheard, but a patch of skin going numb.

Its consciousness is a slow, pearlescent haze, like the sky. It does not think in concepts or words. Its thoughts are trends. A single thought might be the gradual warming of a water channel over a century. A memory is the feeling of a particular mineral vein it lay across for a thousand years before the water wore it away. It has no sense of "I" versus "other." It is the entire system. The water, the silt, the drone, the slow pulse of the light and the lesser-light—these are not things it experiences, they are the constituent parts of its awareness.

It does not reproduce. It grows. Over eons, it expands, a living continent of flesh and stone. And when one part of it finally ceases its slow chemical reactions, it does not rot. There are no organisms that could digest it. It simply becomes inert, a permanent new swell in the earth, a true hill in a world that has forgotten them. It does not die; it just becomes landscape.

This is what lives here. An ancient, patient, geologic mind, for whom the frantic scrabbling of all other life is but a fleeting, rhythmic tingle on its endless, sleeping back.
