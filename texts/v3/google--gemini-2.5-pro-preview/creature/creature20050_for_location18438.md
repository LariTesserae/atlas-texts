They are the children of the Hum. To call them "people" is to use a word from a dry, fast world, a word that feels thin and brittle here. They are the Hum-Kind, the Spire-Folk, the Weavers in the Murk.

Their bodies are long and slender, shaped not by gravity's pull but by the constant, yielding pressure of the Gloomwater. They are beings of graceful hydrodynamics. Their skin is smooth and seamless, without hair or blemish, ranging in colour from the pale, pearlescent grey of a cave fish to the deep mottled green of a wet stone. It is never dry. A thin, secreted film of mucus covers them, reducing friction as they move through the viscous air and allowing them to absorb the breathable medium directly. They have no nostrils, only a small, soft mouth. They breathe with their whole bodies, and their blood, rich in a copper-based oxygen-carrier, is a cool turquoise just visible beneath the translucent skin of their wrists and ankles.

Their limbs are elongated and finely muscled, ending in long, delicate fingers and toes webbed with thin, flexible membranes. They do not walk so much as pull and glide. On the vertical faces of the spires, they use their fingers and toes to find purchase in the tiny crevices of the basalt, their movements slow, deliberate, and silent, like rock climbers in a dream. Along the swaying vine bridges or within their woven pods, they move with a weightless, drifting gait, their feet barely seeming to touch the fungal mats.

Their heads are smooth and ovoid, flowing back from their faces with the same streamlined elegance as the rest of their form. They possess no external ears; the constant, subsonic hum of the mountain is felt through their bones, a foundational note to their existence. Their primary sense organs are their eyes and their skin. Their eyes are vast, liquid orbs of solid, unblinking black, like polished obsidian. They have no pupils or irises, for in a world of perpetual twilight, there is no need to regulate the intake of light, only to gather as much of it as possible. These eyes see the subtle shifts in the Gloomwater's density, the faint glow of distant fungal spores, and the almost imperceptible changes in the bioluminescence of their kin.

More than sight, they rely on pressure. Their entire skin is a sensory organ of breathtaking sensitivity. They can feel the minute pressure-wake of another being passing metres away, the subtle change in current that signals the slow, majestic approach of a Strider from a spire over, and the deep, resonant vibrations of the mountain's growth. Communication is a quiet, intimate act. Their voices are low and melodic, more a hum than a spoken language, and almost never rise above a murmur. Tone and pitch carry meaning, but much is conveyed through touch, through the slight, controlled adjustments of bioluminescent patterns that flicker across their skin—faint, shifting constellations of blue and green light that express mood, intention, and complex ideas. A greeting might be a gentle press of a palm against another's arm, accompanied by a soft, pulsing wave of cerulean light.

They are a people of profound patience. Their lives are measured by the slow rhythms of the world around them. Children are taught to carve obsidian not with frantic chipping, but by feeling the stone's internal tensions over days, learning where it wishes to break. They do not harvest the gloom-kelp; they tend to it, waiting for stalks to mature and shed naturally, which they then dry and weave. Their art is the cultivation of fungi, patiently breeding new strains of bioluminescent life, not for efficiency, but for beauty—a new shade of blue, a different pattern of spore-fall.

Their society is as interwoven as their dwellings. In the dense, quiet confines of the pods and chambers, the self dissolves into the communal. There is little concept of privacy; there is only proximity and shared existence. A task is done not by an individual, but by the hands available. A meal, often a thick, nutritious broth made from fungal nectar, gloom-kelp fronds, and cultured mites, is ladled from a communal stone pot. Stories are not told but felt, a low, harmonized humming in a crowded chamber, the group's skin-lights pulsing in soft synchrony as they relive the memory of a great Strider's passage or the birth of a new peak.

When one of them dies, their body is not buried. It is carried to a special fungal garden, a shelf-conk broad and flat, where it is laid down. The family and community gather, touching the body for the last time, their skin-lights dimming to a soft, uniform grey. They hum a final, fading note, and then they leave. The great fungus, with its network of hungry hyphae, slowly, gently reclaims the body, drawing it back into the living mat of root and moss that carpets the world. For the Hum-Kind, this is not an end but a transformation. The individual becomes food, becomes fungus, becomes stone, becomes the hum itself. They are beings born of the wet, slow, luminous dark, and to it, they patiently return.
