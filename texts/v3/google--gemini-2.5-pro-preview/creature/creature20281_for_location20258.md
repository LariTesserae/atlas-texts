The beings who live here are not creatures *of* this place so much as they are expressions *from* it. They are the Aurelians, and to see one is to misunderstand it, for their true form is the city itself.

That which you might mistake for an individual is a fragile, temporary thing. It is a walking sculpture of dust and will. Emerging from the pale powder of the ground, it seems to coalesce rather than be born. Its form is tall and impossibly slender, a being drawn by a calligrapher with a nearly dry brush. Its limbs are long, with too many joints, moving with a liquid grace that seems to defy the very dryness of its skin. This skin is not flesh but a compacted sheath of the world’s dust, held together by an internal lattice of secreted resin and electrostatic charge. It is the colour of bone-meal, of ash, of the very ground it walks upon. Where a face might be, there is only a smooth, featureless ovoid, tilted as if in perpetual listening to a sound no one else can hear. They have no mouth, no lungs, no vocal cords. They do not eat or drink or breathe the heavy air. They are anchors.

An Aurelian’s anchor-form is a tool, a remote appendage for a consciousness far greater and more fluid. Its true self, its mind, its memory, its very soul, resides not in the dusty automaton but within the city’s living walls. Each Aurelian is tied to a specific Phloem, a personal Bladderwrack Tower that is its library, its womb, and its world. Inside that colossal, turgid sac, suspended in the nutritive, life-giving gel, is the Aurelian’s vast, slow, liquid consciousness. It is a distributed network of neurons—not biological in a way we would recognize, but complex chains of protein and crystal suspended in a perfect, stable medium. From within this sanctum, it perceives the world. It feels the subtle pressure shifts as the six-limbed leapers land on its skin. It feels the light of the sun as it warms its outer membrane, photosynthesizing thoughts. And it feels the presence of its anchor-form, the dusty marionette walking the silent avenues below, as clearly as you feel your own hand.

They are the city’s gardeners, its architects, its priests. The anchor-forms move with immense purpose, though their tasks seem arcane. One might spend a decade tending to a single junction between two Phloems, using long, delicate fingers to guide the new growth, weaving the membranes together to form a stronger archway. Another might stand motionless for a month in a canyon, its head tilted, before tracing a complex glyph in the dust—a glyph that is not a symbol for other Aurelians to see, but a molecular instruction, a pheromonal command that sinks into the ground and alters the nutrient flow, encouraging a new kind of growth and discouraging another. They are pruning a continent-sized bonsai.

Their communication is the city's hidden life. When a thought must be shared with urgency, an Aurelian can send a ripple of contractile tissue through its home-Phloem. This physical pulse is then picked up by the neighboring sacs, propagating through the metropolis like a wave. A 'shout' is a convulsion that can make a whole district sway. But their deeper language, their art and philosophy, is written in light. As dusk falls and the filtered sunlight fades, the city begins to glow from within. The Aurelians pulse intricate, colored patterns of bioluminescence through their fluid selves. These are their conversations: slow, complex, symphonic light-shows that drift through the gelatinous walls of the city. To see the city at night is to witness a silent, sprawling debate between entities the size of mountains, their thoughts made visible as shimmering auroras of soft green, deep violet, and warm gold.

To be an Aurelian is to exist in a state of profound connection and profound solitude. Their anchor-forms may pass each other in the dusty streets, even work in concert, but their true selves are vast and isolated, islands of consciousness in a sea of life. Their awareness is dual: the tactile, immediate experience of the anchor-form in the dust, and the immense, slow, systemic awareness of the home-Phloem. They feel the scraping of a grazer on their skin and, simultaneously, the slow, tectonic creep of a rival's growth a mile away.

Death for an Aurelian is not the dissolution of the anchor-form—that is a trivial event, like a snake shedding its skin. The anchor can be withdrawn, its dust scattered, and a new one precipitated at will. True death is the death of the Phloem. When a tower finally succumbs to age, or is mortally wounded, it does not simply deflate. The Aurelian within, in its final act, oversees the great recycling. It orchestrates its own demise. It lowers the internal pressure, opens microscopic pores in its skin, and guides the swarms of opportunistic scavengers to the most nutrient-rich parts of its being. Its consciousness, its memories, the intricate latticework of its mind, are not lost. They are broken down, consumed, and redistributed throughout the ecosystem. Every creature that partakes in the feast carries away a fragment of that mind. The Aurelian's life experience is seeded back into the world’s very fabric, becoming a part of the Akos spore-mist, to potentially inform the consciousness of a new Aurelian a thousand years from now, a continent away.

They are the world holding its breath. Each one is a silent, dusty figure walking through a city it knows to be its own body. It is both the gardener and the garden, the sculptor and the clay, a temporary speck of dust animated by a sea of silent, luminous thought.
