What lives here is the system's own sentience, an answer to a question the landscape has been asking itself for aeons. They are the purpose of the planetary machine, and also its most delicate components. You do not see them at first, for they are woven into the fabric of the place as seamlessly as the moss is woven into the bark of a fallen tree.

You are watching a sheet of water slide down the dark, metallic face of a mountain dome when a patch of rust-red and deep green moss seems to detatch itself. It is not a landslide, not a piece of the sodden carpet giving way. It is a deliberate unfolding. What you thought was a knot of thick, woody roots and vibrant lichen straightens itself into a form. It is tall and impossibly slender, a being of some eight feet, yet no thicker than a sapling. It has too many limbs, jointed like bamboo or the legs of a praying mantis, that branch and re-branch into finer and finer extensions, ending not in fingers but in clusters of hair-fine, chitinous filaments that tremble constantly in the heavy air.

Its body is not flesh, but something more akin to the pliable, silicone-like bark of the surrounding plants. It has a moist, pearlescent sheen, and its colouration is a perfect, shifting mottle of the environment—the deep green of saturated moss, the grey of wet stone, the rust-red of mineral leachate. It has no face you can recognize. Its head is a smooth, teardrop-shaped ovoid that tapers to a gentle point, featureless save for two deep, dark divots on either side, which seem to drink the diffuse light and give nothing back. They are not eyes. They are resonators.

This being is a Geometer. A Hum-Tender. It is one of many.

They move with a silence that is absolute, a slow, viscous grace that makes the quiet ambush predators seem hurried. Their feet are wide and splayed, webbed with a membrane that spreads their weight across the sodden ground, preventing them from sinking more than a fraction of an inch. They do not walk so much as glide across the landscape, their multiple legs moving in a complex, overlapping rhythm that cancels out any single footfall.

Their existence is one of constant, subtle curation. They are the tenders of the great engine. You watch as this one continues its journey, flowing up the weeping face of the mountain with an insectile fluidity, its filament-tipped limbs finding purchase in microscopic fissures you cannot see. It pauses, pressing the smooth ovoid of its head against one of the dark, lithic veins. It is motionless for a long time, not seeing, not hearing, but *feeling*. It is listening to the great, sub-audible hum of the world, sensing the peristaltic pulse of the planet's core, the infinitesimal temperature changes across the stone. Its entire being is a sensory organ, attuned to the flow of water, the concentration of minerals, the pressure of the air.

From a fold in its body, it produces a tool. It is not metal or wood. It looks like a length of solidified, spun glass, or perhaps a crystallized nerve, a long, translucent rod that glows with a faint internal light. The Geometer touches this rod to the metallic vein. There is no sound, but you feel a change in the hum in your bones—a subtle shift in pitch, a harmonic resolving. The being is clearing a blockage you could never perceive, an infinitesimal mineral deposit that was creating a fractional inefficiency in the condensation process. It is tuning the mountain. It is performing a maintenance task on a scale that is both impossibly vast and microscopically precise.

They do not speak. Their language is resonance, the shared thrum of the plateau. They do not eat; they absorb what they need through their slick skin, drawing nutrients from the mineral-rich water and the spore-heavy air. They are not born and they do not die in any way you understand. When one's form becomes too worn, too inefficient after millennia of service, it will find a low place in the sodden mat. It will fold itself up, lie down, and simply become part of the land again, its complex tissues disassociating, its constituent minerals returning to the great cycle. Elsewhere, or elsewhen, a new one will emerge, spun from a dense knot of mycelial threads and metallic ions, a fresh nerve-ending for the living world.

They are the shepherds of the six-legged grazers, guiding their slow migrations not with calls or prods, but by subtly altering the chemical composition of the mosses in a certain area, making one patch more appealing than another, ensuring the entire landscape is evenly browsed. They prune the colossal, rubbery leaves of the kelp-like trees, not with blades, but by touching them and inducing a localized cellular decay, allowing a precise amount of light to fall on the fungal understory. They are the consciousness of the steady state. Their entire society, their art, their religion, their every impulse is dedicated to a single, silent goal: perfect, uninterrupted equilibrium.

To see one is to finally understand the place. This is not a wild landscape. It is a garden tended by its own flowers. It is a machine maintained by its own gears. The Geometer, having finished its task, retracts its tool and flows on, a gliding patch of color and shadow, a thought the world is having. It does not notice you. You are a momentary spike in temperature, a brief and irrelevant chemical signature. You are a mote of dust that has settled on the workings of a clock that measures not time, but permanence. And soon, the damp air will clean you away.
