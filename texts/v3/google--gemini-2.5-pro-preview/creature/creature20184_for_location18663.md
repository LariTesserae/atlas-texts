The Locus is empty, but it is not uninhabited. The beings who live here are called, by the logic of their labour, the Harvesters.

You do not see them at first. You see their work, the ghost of their presence etched into the living walls. You see the patient, gouged paths of handholds, the scatter of primitive but perfect tools. You see the void where a plate of crystallised time has been prised away. They are as much a part of the Locus's geology as the growing plates and the phantom light.

When you do see one, it is a lesson in perspective. Across the vast, curved chamber, clinging to the nacreous wall a thousand feet up, is a shape. It is impossibly long and slender, a stick-figure drawn against the pearlescent glow. It moves with a slow, deliberate grace that is neither climbing nor walking, but something in between, a form of adherence. It is a thing made for this specific gravity, this specific curvature. As it shifts its weight, you see it has not four limbs, but six. The lower four are canted and hooked, ending not in feet but in chisel-like points of hardened, biological material, like obsidian fused to bone. These it drives into the wall with minute, controlled motions, finding purchase not by grip, but by precision piercing. The upper two limbs are longer, more flexible, and they are the ones that do the work.

They are the colour of the mists that sometimes fill the sphere—a protean grey, translucent at the edges, thickening to the opacity of old cartilage at their core. Their skin, if it can be called that, is seamless and smooth, yet it bears the texture of something that has been perpetually scoured by fine grit. They have no discernible face. Where a head should be, there is a smooth, ovoid shape that tapers to a gentle point. It swivels slowly, not to look, but to *listen*. They have no eyes. The sensory organs of the Harvesters are their entire bodies. They feel the Locus's groan in their skeletal structure. They taste the quality of the air on their skin. And they perceive the layers of time, the ghost sounds and phantom lights, as a form of pressure, a tangible weather system they can read and navigate. The strobing, impossible colours do not distract them, for they are blind to light. The cacophony of recorded sounds is not noise to them, but a map.

They are listening for something specific. A Harvester will remain motionless for what could be hours or days, one of its long, delicate arms resting against the wall. It is not resting. It is dowsing for moments. It is sifting through the layered roar of history—the tectonic groan, the phantom voices, the animal cries—for a particular frequency, a specific emotional tenor. It might be hunting for the pure, sharp note of sudden insight, the resonant chord of communal joy, or the discordant shatter of betrayal. These are their ores, their precious metals.

When a lode is found, the work begins. It is a process of excruciating care. The Harvester uses an obsidian chisel, held in a three-fingered hand, and taps it with a hammer of dense, dark basalt. The sound of this work is the very same rhythmic *chip-chip-chip* that erupts randomly from the walls. You are not just hearing an echo of the past; you are hearing the sound of the present being laid down as a future memory-layer. The Harvester is not breaking the material. It is coaxing it. It taps along the resonant fault lines of the event, outlining the memory in a series of tiny fractures. Finally, with a tool like a flattened claw, it pries the shard loose.

The Harvesters do not eat. They do not drink in the conventional sense. The thick, metallic water that condenses on the walls is their source of hydration, absorbed directly through their skin. Their sustenance is the energy they harvest. When a memory-shard is pried loose, there is a silent release, a burst of temporal radiation that sends the sand-mite scavengers into a frenzy. The Harvester absorbs the majority of this energy directly. This is their food: the energy of a concluded moment. They sometimes place small, fingernail-sized chips into a slit-like orifice on their chests, but this does not seem to be for nourishment. It is more like consulting a text, or perhaps savouring a flavour.

They are not ghosts, but they are not entirely solid either. Their relationship with time is porous. The indistinct figure you saw on the scaffold was not a memory of an ancestor, but simply one of them, its presence momentarily smeared across a few adjacent seconds, its form losing cohesion. They sometimes seem to flicker, their limbs blurring as if seen through heat haze. This is not a trick of the light. This is a Harvester whose attention is deeply focused on a layer of the past, its own physical form becoming untethered from the present.

They are born from the Locus. In a quiet, un-mined section of the sphere, you might see a bulge in the wall, a slow, swelling cyst of nacre. Over an immense period, it elongates, darkens, and develops the faintest outline of limbs. One day, with a sound like tearing silk, it detaches itself from the wall, a new Harvester, fully formed and already knowing its task. They do not die; they are reabsorbed. An ancient or damaged Harvester will find a place on the floor, fold its limbs, and become still. Over millennia, new layers of the living geode will grow over it, its body becoming another stratum, its own life-memory another phantom sound in the eternal, overwhelming chord of the Locus. They are the place made aware of itself, the librarians of a library that is still writing itself, sorting the shelves even as the building groans and expands around them.
