They live in the quiet between the breaths of the fog.

They do not have a name for themselves, for a name suggests an identity separate from the place, and such a distinction does not occur to them. To an outsider, they might be called the Tenders, or the Stone-folk, but to themselves, they are simply the part of the whole that moves.

They are revealed as the fog recedes. You do not see them emerge from a dwelling, for the towers are not dwellings in a way we would understand. Rather, they are already there, standing still on the vast terraces, having been obscured by the white deluge. They seem to coalesce out of the damp air, figures of mist and stone given form.

They are tall and impossibly slender, their limbs long and fine-boned, echoing the proportions of the towers they tend. Their build is an adaptation to the thin air and a life of slow, patient movement. They move with a liquid grace, a deliberate economy of motion that makes them seem at times like water flowing over stone, and at others like the slow, inexorable creep of a glacier. Their feet, clad in sandals woven from the tough, bruised-purple grasses, make no sound on the grit of the plazas.

Their skin is the most remarkable thing about them. It is not flesh as we know it. It is a smooth, cool, non-porous surface that has the dusky grey color and slight sheen of wet basalt. It is not uniform; over their long lives, faint, lighter patterns have developed, tracing paths like water-stains or the feathery blooms of lichen. Touching them would be like touching a river-smoothed stone that has been sitting in a cold, deep shadow. Their faces are serene and elongated, with features that seem worn by a gentle eternity. Their eyes are wide and dark, pupils like deep, flooded wells that reflect the pale sky with a liquid gleam, holding no warmth, only a profound and placid awareness.

They are sustained by the fog. They do not eat the finches or the iridescent grazers; to do so would be to consume a part of the city's machinery. When the fog rolls in, they become still, standing on the plazas or near the bases of the towers, their robes of woven grass-fiber hanging limp. The saturated air is their nourishment. The dense condensation that jewels the stone also slicks their skin, and they absorb the moisture—the life—of the place directly. It is a slow, passive sustenance, providing just enough energy for their tasks. They drink the breath of the caldera. Their metabolism is a geological process, not a biological one.

Their consciousness is not individual. They do not possess names or personal histories because their memory is the memory of the stone. An individual does not remember their childhood; they collectively remember the settling of a foundation block six thousand years ago. They feel the integrity of the city as a physical sensation. A stone that has shifted a millimeter is not an observation; it is an ache in their collective being, a dissonance that must be resolved. To see one of them press a long-fingered hand to the side of a tower is to witness an act of diagnosis, a listening for the slow, deep pulse of water moving through the hidden channels within.

They are born of the city, and return to it. They do not reproduce in a familiar way. In a cistern at the very heart of the central, lowest terrace, where the water from all the others eventually gathers, a new Tender will form when an old one has reached the end of their cycle. Over centuries, minerals and silts carried by the fog-water will accrete, guided by a resonance left by the city's original builders, slowly growing a new form in the cold, absolute darkness. When the form is complete, they will awaken, walk out of the cistern, and take up the tools of their predecessor, their mind already filled with the complete and seamless knowledge of the place. When a Tender's time is done—when their form becomes too worn, their movements too slow even for this place—they return to this same cistern and dissolve back into the water, their substance contributing to the one that will eventually follow. There is no grief; it is merely the reseating of a block.

Their language, when they use it, is barely a whisper, more like the sound of sand shifting or water seeping into a crack. It is a language of pure function, used to coordinate the work. "The southern channel on the fourth terrace requires sweeping." "A wedge has worn; a new one must be knapped from the tower stone." There are no stories, no songs, no art beyond the perfect, perpetual maintenance of what already is.

They are the city's immune system, its living memory, and its will. They are not captives of this place, nor are they its masters. They are the final, most refined component of a machine designed for no purpose other than its own continuation. They are a quiet, slow-moving thought, expressed in stone and water, holding the world in a state of perfect, fragile, and eternal equilibrium. They are the silence between the hammer falls.
