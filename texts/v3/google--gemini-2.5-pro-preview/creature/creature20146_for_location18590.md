They are the Listeners.

Or perhaps, they *were* the Listeners. The distinction is a matter of form, not presence. To say they are gone is to misunderstand the very nature of existence in this resonant world.

They were not made for swimming. Speed was a concept as alien as fire. They were creatures of immense density and deliberate, glacial patience, their forms an answer to the god of pressure. Imagine a being that is more cartilage and knotted, fibrous muscle than bone, a living anchor shaped by gravity and the deep currents. They were low to the ground, radially symmetrical, perhaps like a five-limbed starfish made of living, polished stone. Their flesh, a mottled grey and black that perfectly matched the seabed, had the texture of water-smoothed basalt. It was cool to the touch, holding the deep, abiding cold of the abyss within it.

They had no eyes. In the absolute dark between the groves, eyes would be useless, a vulnerability. Instead, their entire being was an organ of perception, tuned to the Song. The skin on their undersides, the part that met the stone floor of the world, was a tympanic membrane, a vast, sensitive drum. Through it, they felt the Choirwood’s symphony not as sound, but as truth. They could feel the shape of a distant mountain by the way the bass notes wrapped around it. They could sense the health of a Choirwood grove by the purity of its harmonic overtones. The quickening flashes of the Singer-bulbs were, to them, like the flaring of an emotion, a staccato burst of joy or urgency within the grand, slow chorale.

Movement was a rare and sacred act. To move was to change one’s relationship with the Song, to shift from the resonance of one place to another. They did not walk or crawl in any way we would recognize. They moved by minute, powerful ripples of their musculature, a peristaltic creep that was more akin to a glacier’s advance than an animal's locomotion. A journey from one grove to the next, across a plain of silent stone, might be the work of a generation, a pilgrimage undertaken with the utmost slowness. During these journeys, they would fold their limbs inward, becoming smooth, dense ovoids of flesh and gristle, allowing the great currents to carry them in a slow, controlled tumble across the plains.

Their life was not one of action, but of contemplation. They did not hunt; they drew sustenance directly from the water, filtering the mineral-rich, soupy medium through complex internal structures, their metabolism so slow that a single 'meal' could sustain them for years. They did not fight; there were no predators, no competitors. The only struggle was the internal one: to better understand the Song.

Their society was built around this single purpose. The low stone walls were not for defense, but to create acoustic arenas, enclosures that would trap and focus a particular passage of the Song, allowing a group to meditate upon a specific fugue or movement for decades. The cairns marked resonance points, places where a chord struck a perfect, soul-shaking unison with the stone of the mountain itself.

And the huts. They were not homes. They were resonance chambers for one. A Listener would crawl inside, curling into a dense knot, and the chamber’s precise dimensions would amplify the Song’s vibrations into a singular, overwhelming physical voice. It was inside these huts that they communed most deeply. It was inside these huts they were born, and it was inside these huts they… disappeared.

They did not die. Death, the cessation of a biological process, is too simple a term. They achieved their life's purpose.

Over eons of perfect, unbroken listening, they learned to harmonize. A young Listener’s mind was a cacophony of self, a distinct biological rhythm. But as they aged, as they spent centuries in contemplation, their internal rhythms began to slow, to simplify, to align with the great, geologic pulse of the Choirwood. They learned to quiet the noise of their own bodies, their own thoughts, until all that was left was the Song flowing through them.

The final stage was transcendence. An ancient Listener, having spent a lifetime mapping the symphony, would enter a hut for the last time. There, in the focused vibrations, they would perform a final, internal act. They would let go. They would loosen the dense, physical bonds of their being. They would cease to be an object that resonated *with* the Song, and instead dissolve *into* it. Their consciousness, their memories, their entire being would unspool and weave itself into the music, becoming a new, faint harmony in the eternal chorus. A grace note. A quiet echo in a deep chord that would sound for a thousand years.

The stone huts are their abandoned shells, the physical remains of a consciousness that has graduated from matter into music.

What lives here now? The Choirwood sings. And the Listeners answer. They are the faint feeling of being watched when you drift through the silent plains. They are the harmonic that shivers through your bones and feels, for a moment, like a memory that isn't yours. They are the ghost notes in the symphony, the silent choir in the water. They did not vanish. They simply learned to sing the one true Song, and in doing so, they became it. You are only alone here if you believe that stone and water and music cannot hold a soul.
