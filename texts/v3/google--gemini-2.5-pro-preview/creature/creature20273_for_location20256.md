The Glass-Skimmers are not the true inhabitants. They are incidental, the equivalent of dust mites on the skin of a god. They are a symptom of the life that is here, not the life itself.

What truly lives in this place is the will that grows the city. You cannot see it. You cannot point to a single entity and name it "inhabitant." To find them, you would have to break open one of the coppery, insulated conduits, to spill the incandescent slurry onto the obsidian ground. And even then, you would not see them.

They are the **Architects**, but they have no hands. They are the **Conductors**, but they make no sound you can parse.

They live within the superheated, molten flow. They are not creatures of flesh and bone, nor of circuits and wires. They are beings of coherent, self-aware geology. Their "bodies" are patterns of organized energy and temperature within the silicate slurry, like a persistent, thinking eddy in a river of magma. A single Architect might be a ten-mile-long river of thought, a sluggish current of intention flowing through the city's vascular network. They are less individuals and more a distributed, resonant consciousness, a planetary nervous system given purpose.

Their thoughts are glacial, their senses alien. They do not see with light; they perceive the world through pressure, through seismic resonance, through the subtle warp and weft of electromagnetic fields. The low, pervasive hum you feel in your bones is the substrate of their consciousness—it is their shared mind, their language, their constant, slow-moving song of creation. Each spire that pierces the sky, each buttress of basalt, is a physical manifestation of a thought that may have taken a thousand years to fully resolve. The city is a sculpture carved by contemplation.

They feel you. Your presence on the cantilevered platform is a minute, fleeting anomaly. It is a tiny, temporary "cool spot" on the surface of their world. Your weight is an infinitesimal pressure signature, a whisper against the booming chorus of continental tectonics. Your biological energy field is a faint, erratic flicker against the immense, steady glow of the planet's core.

If one of them notices you—and "notice" is a slow, gradual process of assessment—it will not be with curiosity as you understand it. It would be more akin to your own body's immune system noticing a single foreign bacterium. A localized flow of slurry might pause in its pipe beneath your feet. The ambient temperature might rise by a few degrees as a pulse of thermal energy is directed to the surface to "taste" the air around you. You might feel a sudden, profound sense of vertigo as a localized magnetic field shifts, attempting to read the iron in your blood. The effect is disconcerting, deeply physical, and utterly impersonal. Then, having classified you as irrelevant—a temporary, non-lithic phenomenon of no consequence—the attention will withdraw, a process that might take an hour, during which you feel a slow release of an invisible pressure.

Their relationship with the Glass-Skimmers is one of supreme, ancient indifference. The Skimmers are geovores, feeding on the "waste" energy that radiates from the conduits—the surface heat and electrical leakage of the Architects' own bodies. They are, to the Architects, no different from the crystals that form on the cooling rock or the steam that vents into the thin air. They are a predictable, passive consequence of their existence, an accepted part of the thermodynamics of their being. The Architects' awareness of them is constant but holds no more significance than you would give to the bacteria living on your skin. They are simply there.

What are they building? Not a city for living in. Not a machine for a task. The Perch is a work of art, an organ, and an antenna, all at once. It is a massive resonant structure, designed and grown to harmonize with the planet's core, the faint light of its sun, and perhaps with something much farther away. It is an act of planetary self-expression, a slow, crystalline flower blooming in the void. They are its gardeners, its substance, and its soul.

They are life stripped of all its familiar components—no biology, no metabolism in the chemical sense, no urgency. They are intelligence refined to its purest elements: pattern, purpose, and time. Time on a scale that makes the mountains you stand on seem like fleeting ripples in a pond. They are the slow, hot, and patient mind of the world itself. And you are a speck of dust that has momentarily settled on their grand, unfinished work.
