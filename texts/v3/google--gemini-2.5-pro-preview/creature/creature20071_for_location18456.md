Here lives a single organism, though it wears a trillion faces. It does not think of itself as `I`. It does not think at all. It *is*. It is the chittering, the clicking, the booming. It is the cold light and the ceaseless motion. To ask what lives here is to ask what a body is made of. It is made of its cells, its organs, its slow, deep rhythms.

The smallest, most numerous parts of this being are a consciousness of hunger, distributed across a trillion bodies. For one of these grazers, existence is not a narrative but a single, perpetual, screaming moment. There is no past, only the slick patch of glowing film just scraped clean. There is no future, only the faint, alluring pulse of the next patch ahead. Its world is a landscape of pressure and texture. It feels the crushing, reassuring weight of its siblings on all sides, a constant, shifting embrace that is both anchor and engine. It feels the cool, slick stone beneath its many feet, a foundation that offers up the one thing that matters: the taste of charged minerals, of life metabolised from rock. Sight is a blue-green smear, a promise of food. Sound is not heard but felt: the vibration of the whole, the thrumming of its own colossal body, a roar so constant it is indistinguishable from silence. It is a single nerve of hunger, replicated and repeated until it becomes a geological force, a river of want that carves the land by eating it. It knows only two states: the blissful hum of consumption and the frantic, desperate scramble for more. It is the metabolism of the world.

Above this frenetic base exist the thoughts of the world-organism—sharp, sudden, and predatory. These are the hunters. For the pale, stalking spiders, the chittering river below is not a crowd of kin but a flowing texture of opportunity. Stillness is a predatory act. They are islands of patience in a sea of frantic energy. Their reality is woven from different stimuli. They see with a piercing clarity the grazers cannot comprehend. They see the individual flickers of iridesescence, the subtle changes in the flow that betray a weak or isolated body. Their world is punctuated by the sudden, thrilling tension on a spun filament, the brief, frantic struggle, the satisfying pop as the catch is secured. Their flashes of light—white, ruby—are the world's syntax. They are punctuation marks in the endless, run-on sentence of the grazers. They are expressions of dominance, of warning, of discovery. They are the nervous system of the landscape, firing off signals of isolated, violent intent. For the starfish-like mouths that slide through the thickest shoals, existence is even simpler: a continuous, blissful state of satiation, a slow, unthinking absorption. They are the world's deep satisfaction, its passive, gluttonous joy.

And then there are the keepers of the world's memory, the sources of the boom. The giants. Their consciousness is as different from the hunters' as the hunters' is from the grazers'. Their thoughts are the length of glaciers. They do not count the crawlers any more than a whale counts krill; they perceive the living carpet as a single entity, a texture, a measure of the world's health. Its vibrancy is a scent in the mist, a quality of light, a flavor in the damp air that their glowing, bulbous tips are built to taste. When one lifts its long, black neck, it is not merely looking. It is sensing the whole. It reads the tidal waves of blue-green light across the plains, noting patterns that unfold over cycles we would fail to even recognize as such. Its world is a map of deep time. The swells of the black stone are not obstacles but paragraphs in a story it has been reading for eons. The other orange glows, visible miles away in the humid dark, are its kin, its only conversation. A boom that shakes the jawbones of lesser things is, to them, a single word. A word that might mean *Here*. Or *Plentiful*. Or *I remember when this stone was warmer*. Their lives are not stories of the hunt, but slow, ponderous pilgrimages from one ancient feeding ground to another. They are the deep, resonant thoughts of the planet itself. They are its bones, made animate.

So what lives here is a body. A body whose blood is a river of iridescent shells driven by hunger. A body whose nerves are filaments of light and violence. A body whose mind is a slow, oceanic consciousness that measures its lifespan against the erosion of stone. They are not separate. The death of a grazer is a pulse of energy for a hunter. The collective health of the grazer-sea is the thought that occupies the slow mind of a giant. The sound—the all-encompassing roar—is not their noise. It is their voice. It is the unified, singular, unceasing song of the one thing that lives here, singing the story of its own eternal consumption.
