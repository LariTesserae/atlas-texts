They are the Murmur.

You do not see them at first. You might spend a day, a week, a cycle in the warm, wet dark and conclude the city is a relic, a machine running on for its own sake since its builders vanished. You mistake the slow, deliberate movements for tricks of the weeping light, for the sway of a Vaporvine leaf in a thermal updraft. The flicker of motion in your periphery is just one of those silverfish-kin, the *crystallophages*, slipping back into a crevice.

But when you learn to adjust your senses, to listen with your skin and see with your ears, they emerge from the background. They are the same color as the city: the pale, bruised violet of the crystals, the verdigris of oxidized copper, the flat black of damp Anvil Moss. They are not camouflaged; they are simply made of the same things.

They are long. Their limbs are unnaturally elongated, their torsos slender, built not for power but for reach. There is no bulky muscle, only a dense, sinewy strength, like the Vaporvine itself, that allows them to hang from a hot pipe by one hand for hours, motionless, as they work. Their skin is not skin as you know it. It is cool, damp, and carries a faint, oily sheen that repels the constant condensation. Across their shoulders, backs, and the outsides of their limbs, it is dusted with the glittering, metallic spores of the Anvil Moss, which seem to find purchase in their flesh, creating shifting patterns like constellations. They are not dirty; they are colonized.

Their faces are placid and difficult to read. The features are soft, smoothed over as if eroded by a gentle, constant drip. They have noses, but they are little more than two moist slits that flutter faintly, tasting the chemical composition of the air. Their mouths are wide and thin-lipped, rarely opening. They do not need to eat in a conventional sense; they drink. They will press their lips to a sweating pipe or a weeping fissure in the crystal and sip the hot, mineral-rich water, their primary source of sustenance. Sometimes they will pluck a young, tender leaf of Vaporvine and chew it slowly, methodically, their jaws making no sound.

Their eyes are the most arresting and useless of their features. They are huge, round, and perfectly black, like pools of crude oil. They are coated in a milky, nictitating membrane that rarely retracts, making them appear blind. And for the most part, they are. In the dim, composite glow of the geode, sight is a muddled and unreliable sense. Their eyes register only vague shapes, the slow bloom of heat from a pipe, the faint luminescence of a fungus. They are vestigial organs, relics of a past spent under a different kind of light.

Their true senses are elsewhere. They are called the Murmur because they are attuned to the city's great, subsonic hum. It is their language, their map, their scripture. They feel it not in their ears, but in their bones, in the resonant cavities of their long bodies. They can stand on an iron grate, head tilted, and parse the vibrations rising from the abyss, diagnosing the health of the great engine a mile below. They can tell a failing bearing from a pressure shift in the deep aquifer. This hum is their collective consciousness, a constant, shared thought that binds them all.

Their hands and feet are their instruments of perception. They are bare, the soles and palms covered in a thick, black callus that is both tough as iron and as sensitive as a fingertip. They walk silently and surely on razor-sharp crystal shards. Their long, spider-like fingers and prehensile toes can grip a sweating, condensation-slick pipe with absolute security. By laying a palm against a length of copper, they read its temperature, its flow rate, the purity of the nutrient slurry within. The city, to them, is a body, and they are its nerve endings.

They are the tenders, the gardeners, the Tuners. Their lives are cycles of slow, painstaking maintenance. You will see one, hanging like a pale spider in a web of pipes, patiently polishing a focusing crystal that will not be needed for a century. Another will spend a week tightening a single series of bolts, turning each one a fractional degree, listening with their entire body for the change in the hum's pitch. They guide the growth of the Vaporvine, coaxing its copper-etching roots away from critical joints and towards older, sacrificial conduits. They harvest the black Anvil Moss, not to destroy it, but to gently dust its spores onto new machinery, encouraging the growth that will insulate and protect it. Their work is a form of worship, a quiet participation in the city's immense, slow life.

They do not speak. The damp air would swallow the sound. Instead, they communicate through gesture—a slow turn of the hand, a slight inclination of the head—and more profoundly, through the hum. A Tuner can initiate a specific, rhythmic vibration by tapping on a pipe, a signal that travels through the city's metal skeleton and is felt by another Tuner a thousand feet away, who understands it as a request, a warning, a statement of completion.

They do not seem to have individuals. They are nodes of a single organism. The concept of a name, of a self separate from the whole, is absent. When a body's energy wanes, when it can no longer grip the pipes or feel the subtleties of the hum, it does not die in a way you would recognize. It finds a place where the loam is thickest, lies down, and simply… stops. It becomes still, and the Anvil Moss and Vaporvine are not slow to reclaim it. Its minerals, absorbed over a lifetime from the city’s water, return to the soil. Its body, colonized by spores, becomes a nutrient bloom. There is no ceremony, because there is no departure. The part has merely dissolved back into the whole. The Murmur continues, one note fading as another rises to take its place in the endless, resonant chord of the city in the heart of the world.
