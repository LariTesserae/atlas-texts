They are the Caretakers. Or perhaps the Weepers, for that is what they seem to do. They are what lives here.

You do not see one at first. The eye struggles to resolve them, to separate their form from the general, dripping architecture of the place. They are the color of everything and nothing: the slick, dark grey of wet concrete, the ochre and umber of deep-seated rust, the unhealthy-looking green of the moss that furs the iron pillars. They are tall, unnaturally slender, and their outline is forever blurred, as if viewed through a curtain of heat-shimmer or falling water. Which, in a way, it is.

One will resolve out of the gloom in the far distance, a slow, columnar shape detaching itself from the base of a pillar. It has a vaguely humanoid silhouette—a long torso, two legs, two arms, a suggestion of a head—but it is a form born of gravity and surface tension, not bone and muscle. They are made of the place's tears. Their substance is a thick, viscous gel, a colloid of water, mineral dust, and organic compounds from the perpetual decay. Their skin is not a skin at all, but a shimmering membrane, constantly weeping the fluid that comprises it, which then runs in rivulets down its form to rejoin the slick on the floor. It is beaded with condensation like a cold pipe, and where a face might be there is only a smooth, featureless plane, angled slightly downward as if in perpetual, sorrowful contemplation.

They move with a slowness that is almost religious. A pace set not by will, but by the metronomic sigh of the great, unseen pistons. A leg will lift, stretching like cooling tar, the gel-foot peeling from the tile with a faint, wet smack that is instantly lost in the overwhelming hum. It will hang in the air for a long moment before setting itself down again, the substance flowing to find purchase on the greasy floor. They drift through the vast, empty spaces with an unhurried, inexorable purpose.

They are the tenders of this garden, the priests of this thermodynamic cycle. They have no mouths to eat, no lungs to breathe the soupy air. Their existence is a function, not a life. You might see one standing motionless for an hour beneath a colossal, dripping fern, its long, fluid arms raised. It is not admiring the plant; it is a trellis. It is diverting a particular stream of mineral-heavy water from a leaking overhead pipe, guiding it with its own body to the waiting roots fused to the metal. Its fingers, which are merely temporary elongations of its substance, gently cradle a new, unfurling frond, shielding it from a heavier drip that might tear its delicate tissues.

When a plant reaches the end of its life cycle, its great leaves yellowing and its stalk turning to a dark mush, a Caretaker will arrive. It will stand beside the decay, patient as a mountain. Over days, it will slowly, deliberately, dismember the dying plant. Its hands will pass through the rotting vegetation like a spoon through custard. The plant matter dissolves, absorbed into the Caretaker's gelatinous form. You can see the dark, fibrous slurry swirling within its translucent body, a digestive process with no stomach, only a slow, chemical dissolution.

Then, the Caretaker moves on. It carries its cargo of nutrients to a barren patch of concrete or a naked stretch of iron. There, it will stop, press itself against the surface, and begin to weep more profusely. It is not just water that now seeps from its membrane, but a thick, dark, fertile slime. It anoints the sterile ground with the essence of what has passed, seeding the potential for what is to come. It will stay there, slowly diminishing, feeding the spot until the first shoots of rust-colored moss or a tiny, waxy fern nub appear. Then, having given of itself, it will pull away, thinner and more translucent than before, and begin its slow pilgrimage toward a leaking pipe or a weeping wall, to stand and passively absorb, to drink the iron and calcium from the water until it is whole again.

They are silent. The only sound they make is the wet, viscous noise of their own cohesion and dissolution. They do not communicate, for they are all part of the same distributed being, the same way a single slime mold is both one and many. They are the mobile, sentient part of the cycle. They are the system's own solution to a logistical problem: how to move the products of decay to the sites of new growth. They are the nervous system and the circulatory system of this vast, lonely room, grown from the machine's heat and the world's rot.

They are not alive in the way a fern is alive. They are not conscious in the way a person is conscious. They are a phenomenon, like a whirlpool or a flame. They are the embodied hum of the engine, the slow gesture of the dripping water, the final, lonely thought of whatever intelligence built this place and has long since departed. They are the Caretakers, and their work is never, ever done.
