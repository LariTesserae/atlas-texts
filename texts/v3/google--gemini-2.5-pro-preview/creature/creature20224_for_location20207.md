The beings who live here are the Lithovores. To call them "inhabitants" is to miss the point. They are not separate from their world; they are the active, conscious expression of its fundamental nature. They are geology that has learned to walk.

Their bodies are the first testament to this truth. Fashioned from the same elemental palette as the ground and the crystal flora, a Lithovore is a being of polished, interlocking planes. Its carapace is a deep, lightless obsidian, not grown like skin but secreted and hardened into a perfect, geometric shell. It is less an armor and more a focusing lens, a resonant chamber designed to receive and interpret the world's great Hum. Their multi-jointed legs, terminating in hard, crystalline points, are not just for locomotion. They are sensors, styluses, needles on a planetary record. With every slow, deliberate step—every soft *click-scrape* on the resonant dust—they are both walking and listening, reading the fine texture of the world through their feet. They are living seismographs, their entire nervous system an instrument for perceiving vibration.

The concept of an individual mind, a singular "I," is a foreign geometry here. The Lithovores operate as a distributed consciousness, a "We" that thinks on the scale of a metropolis. Their thoughts are not fleeting sparks of electrochemical activity; they are slow, tectonic processes. A new directive does not flash through the hive; it emanates, a subtle shift in the resonant frequency passed from one body to the next, a change in the pattern of work that might take a year to propagate across a single construction terrace. Their brain is not contained within their shells. Their brain is the city itself. Each extruded filament, each smoothed wall, each buttress grown over a century is a thought made permanent, a memory stored in chitin. To read their history, one would not consult a text but walk the city's oldest corridors, feeling the hardened secretions of a million ancestors, running a sensitive claw along the lines of a spire that records a thousand years of communal effort.

Their senses are attuned to this reality. The great Sound is not a sound; it is their primary medium of perception, their ocean. It is a constant stream of information: the health of the distant crystal groves pulsing in their amethyst light, the minute pressures building deep within the planet's crust, the location of every other Lithovore within a mile's radius. The staccato *clicks* of their kin are not conversation but data—sharp, precise packets of meaning in the ambient flood. "Mineral deposit located." "Structural weakness detected." "Begin secretion cycle." It is a language of pressure and release, of rhythm and interval.

Sight is a secondary, almost mystical sense. The dim, pulsing glow of the crystal forest is not for illumination but for orientation and attunement. They perceive the slow, colored light as a kind of weather, a celestial tide. The gentle shift from grey to blue in a nearby grove is not a change of scenery but a gradual change in the resonant properties of their food source, a message to be processed over decades.

Their life is a single, unbroken act of transformation. There is no distinction between eating, working, and thinking. A Lithovore approaches a glowing crystal column. Its hardened mandibles, which move with the patient, inexorable force of grinding plates, begin to flake away the mineral structure. This is not a violent act; it is a form of communion. It consumes the light, the vibration, the very substance of its world. Inside its alchemical body, the crystal is processed. Part of it becomes fuel for the Lithovore's own slow metabolism. The rest is transmuted into the dark, fibrous resin it secretes from its abdomen. It will then join a line of others, each moving with the same dreamlike patience, and add its own contribution—a single, glistening thread—to the weaving of a new bridge or the thickening of a wall. This act of building is not a chore. It is their art, their prayer, their purpose. It is the method by which they bring order and memory to the deafening, eternal Hum.

They do not fear death; they do not comprehend it as an end. When a Lithovore's internal processes slow to a halt, its body remains, a perfect obsidian statue in the dim light. It becomes part of the landscape its brethren navigate. Eventually, a reclamation team will arrive. In a slow, reverent process, they will break down the silent form, grinding the shell and limbs into constituent minerals to be sown at the base of the crystal groves, feeding the world that fed them. Nothing is lost. A Lithovore's existence is measured not by its lifespan, but by the infinitesimal thickness it added to the wall of the city, by the smooth path its passage wore in the dust over millennia. They are the patient gardeners of a stone forest, the coral polyps of a lightless sea, the slow, dreaming heart of a world made of sound and stone.
