To be born into this hall is to be born into a song. It is the only song you will ever know. Its verses are measured in the slow, percussive heartbeats of the water dropping from the unseen roof—*thock... thock... thock*—a rhythm so constant it is a form of silence. The choruses are the frantic, overwhelming chatter of your own kind.

If you are a Strider, you are a single, brittle note in that chorus. You are a being of pure, immediate reflex, a knot of ganglia wrapped in dark, wet chitin. Your world is a plane of umber brick, a vast, slick mosaic stretching to the feet of god-like pillars. These are not pillars to you; they are immovabilities, the absolute edges of geography. Between them is the open, the dangerous, the necessary. Your life is a frantic, six-legged dance between the glowing pools and the deep shadows.

You do not think; you feel. You feel the constant, cool dampness on your shell and the needle-prick vibration of a thousand thousand siblings tapping against the floor. This is your reality: a universe of taps, a storm of clicking that is both your own voice and the shield of the crowd. You feel hunger as a pull toward the soft, green-glowing carpets of algae in the still pools. You scuttle to the edge, your feathery mouthparts trembling, and dip them in to taste the cold, living light. The water is a mirror. In it, you see a distorted reflection of your own spidery form and the ghostly blue luminescence of the fungal patches high on the buttresses—the dim, cold stars of your sky.

And you feel fear. It is not an emotion, but a physical state. It is a tension in your multi-jointed legs, a readiness to spring. The fear has a name, though you do not speak it. It is the silence. Your frantic clicking is a wall of noise, a shield of confusion. But sometimes, the shield breaks. The floor, your entire world, shudders. A pressure wave, deep and profound, washes through you. The endless clicking of your kind *stops*. The air becomes a solid thing. Your legs lock. Your senses, your very being, are stunned into stillness. This is the moment when the world holds its breath. In this infinite, terrible instant of quiet, a shadow detaches from the other shadows.

If you are a Boomer, you are the silence. You are a creature of patience and gravity. Where the Strider is a frantic scribble of motion, you are a deliberate blot of ink, slowly absorbing the world around you. Your cool, mottled skin, damp and pebbled, is not camouflage so much as a piece of the landscape itself. You are a living slab of the hall's floor, a ripple in its puddled surface. You do not chase; you wait.

Your consciousness is a deep, slow thing, rooted in the very brick of the hall. Your wide, flat body is an ear pressed against the earth. Through the soles of your webbed feet, through the heavy slab of your belly, you feel everything. You feel the constant, rhythmic impact of the great drops. You feel the frantic, high-frequency patter of the swarming Striders, a chaotic texture on the otherwise smooth vibrations of the chamber. You filter this noise, letting it wash over you for hours, for days. You are not listening *for* a Strider; you are listening to the *shape* of the swarm. You are feeling its ebbs and flows, its densities, its currents as it moves between the glowing pools.

You are a being of immense, stored potential. Inside your great chest, a resonant chamber waits. When the pattern of the swarm is right, when a dense current of that frantic energy passes nearby, you act. It is not a thought, but a tectonic inevitability. Air floods the vocal sac on your throat, stretching the mottled skin into a taut, pale drum. And then you release. The *boom* is not a sound you make, but a piece of yourself you give to the hall. It is a physical wave of force, a pulse of pure pressure that travels through the brick and the water and the heavy air. You feel it leave you and see its effect: the frantic dance of life crystallizes. The world stops.

For that one, perfect moment, you are the only thing that moves. You surge forward, a motion of impossible speed for a creature of such bulk. Your maw, a wet, cavernous darkness, opens and closes on the stunned, motionless forms. The taste is of chitin and cold light. Then, just as quickly, you settle, and the silence you created is slowly, hesitantly filled again. A single click. Then another. Then a thousand. The song resumes, and you become part of the floor once more.

And if you are neither predator nor prey, you are one of the Quiet Ones. You are a slug, a smear of glistening muscle, the humble, tireless mouth of the hall. You are ignored by the frantic and the patient alike. Your universe is not the open floor but the vertical walls, the curved undersides of the buttresses. You move with a slowness that makes the Boomer seem hasty. Your life is a pilgrimage across the damp brick, your path illuminated by the ghostly blue gardens of lichen you tend. You are the cleaner, the recycler. Your rasping mouth scrapes away the fungal residue, the shed carapaces, the waste of the others. You are the lowest, the simplest, and the most essential. Without you and the light-giving fungi you husband, the song would flicker and die.

These three peoples are the life of the hall. They are the children of the clay and the water, born of an architecture they cannot comprehend. They do not know what lies beyond the vaulted darkness above, for nothing has ever come down but the water. They do not wonder at the purpose of the masterfully laid bricks, for they are simply the ground, the sky, the world. The hall was built for some forgotten purpose, a vessel waiting for a meaning that evaporated. And into that void, life crept. This life is the new meaning. The frantic dance, the patient hunt, the quiet toil—this is the hall's purpose now. It is a cathedral built for a silent god, now filled with the overwhelmingly loud prayers of its strange and beautiful monsters.
