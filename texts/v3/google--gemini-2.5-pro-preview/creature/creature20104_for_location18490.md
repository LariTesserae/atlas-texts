They are the Silt-Singers.

Their bodies are a study in fluid dynamics, a long conversation with the water they call home. They are beings of sinuous length, typically six to seven feet from their crowns to the fan-like flukes of their tails. They are not swimmers in the percussive, powerful sense of a seal or a dolphin; they are movers, drifters, navigators of a three-dimensional world. Their unhurried grace is indeed like that of an otter, but stretched into a more languid, attenuated form. They are humanoid only in their bilateral symmetry and in the inquisitive, intelligent arrangement of their features.

Their skin is their most immediate boundary with the world, and it is a marvel. It is entirely hairless, smooth and taut, with the matte, yielding texture of a mushroom cap. Its colour is a deep, shifting mosaic of jade, olive, and bruised purple, a perfect camouflage that allows them to merge with the hide of the Spire-Kelp. This is not a static pattern; their mood and health are expressed in slow, chromatographic blooms of colour that drift across their skin like clouds. In the privacy of their glowing pods, you might see a pair of Silt-Singers conversing not with sound, but with silent, pulsing waves of teal and ochre light that chase each other down their limbs.

Their limbs are long and slender. Their arms end in hands that are both alien and familiar, with three long, tapering fingers and a thumb, all connected by a dark, membranous webbing that extends halfway to the first knuckle. These hands are not for fighting, but for weaving, for pruning, for the delicate work of their living architecture. Their legs are shorter, ending not in feet but in broad, flexible fins that they use for minute adjustments and for bracing themselves against the currents or the kelp stalks. Their primary propulsion comes from a slow, almost hypnotic undulation of their entire body, culminating in a gentle sculling of their long, muscular tail.

The head is an elegant, hydrodynamic shape. The face is broad and flattened, with no discernible nose, only two discreet, slit-like nostrils that can seal completely. Their mouths are wide but thin-lipped, capable of opening into a resonant 'O'. But their most striking feature is their eyes. They are enormous, round, and devoid of sclera, appearing as two pools of liquid obsidian that capture the faint green-gold light of the caldera. They see perfectly in the dimness, but their primary sense is not sight.

They breathe the water. Along their ribs, from just beneath their armpits to the top of their hips, are rows of fine, feathery gills. They are usually pressed flat against the body, almost invisible, but with every slow "breath," they flare open in a delicate, coral-coloured fringe, drawing in the nutrient-rich water and filtering out the oxygen. To see a Silt-Singer at rest is to watch a slow, rhythmic blossoming along their torso, a silent, vital pulse.

They live less in the world of light and more in the world of pressure and sound. Their entire being is an instrument for somatic perception. They do not have external ears; instead, the fine bones of their jaw and skull are tuned to receive the constant hum of Stillwater. They feel the groan of the Spire-Kelp in their marrow. They can distinguish the frantic vibrations of a struggling fish from the patient rasping of a grazer fifty feet away. This is their constant state of being: a deep, meditative awareness of the city's living voice.

This somatic sensitivity is the root of their "Singing." It is not music in a human sense. For everyday communication, they use a language of soft clicks, bell-like chimes, and flowing whistles, a private shorthand for the water. The true Singing is a different faculty entirely. It is a focused, harmonic resonance generated deep in their chests. By shaping their mouths and subtly altering the tension in their bodies, they can project tones that interact with the life around them. It is a slow, patient technology. They can sing a low, groaning frequency for many days to encourage a Spire-Kelp frond to thicken and curve in a specific direction. They can sing a high, weaving melody that coaxes the parasitic vines to knit themselves into the watertight skin of a new pod. They sing to the mosses to make them glow brighter; they sing to the spawning jellies to guide them toward their nets. It is a language of influence, not command. They are the city's gardeners, its architects, its conscious nervous system, and their tools are vibration, patience, and a knowledge of biology that is so deep it has become instinctual.

A Silt-Singer is never truly alone. The concept of rigid individualism is as foreign to them as dry land. They are nodes in a network, both of their community and of the grand, living system of the caldera itself. Their lives are as slow as the world they inhabit. They think in terms of generations, not days. History is not written but grown into the very walls of their homes. Their wealth is the health of their Spire, the richness of their songs, the strength of their woven bridges. They move through the green-gold water not as masters, but as participants, their dark, slender forms another layer in the deep, constant, living hum of Stillwater. They are the mind in the water, the dreamers in the deep.
