What lives in the Boiler are the Custodians. They are the reason for the profound loneliness, and the refutation of it.

You do not see them at first. The eye, overwhelmed by the grandeur of the Condenser and the impossible vitality of the jungle, dismisses them. They are part of the scenery, another layer of the symbiosis between the organic and the mechanical. You might mistake one for a peculiar arrangement of pipes overgrown with bronze-colored lichen, or a verdigris-stained statue left behind by the builders, a forgotten saint of industry.

Then, a part of the scenery moves.

The motion is so slow, so deliberate, it is almost geological. A limb, long and slender as a piston rod, detaches from the brass housing of a great sphere. It is made of a substance that is not quite metal and not quite chitin, its surface reflecting the misty light with the dull sheen of ancient, unpolished copper. It moves without a sound of its own, its articulation lost in the all-encompassing hum of the Boiler. The movement is not that of an animal, but of a hydraulic arm making a finely calibrated adjustment.

These are the Custodians. They are vaguely humanoid in silhouette, but only in the way a scarecrow is. They have a torso, two arms, two legs, a head. But the resemblance ends there. Their limbs are impossibly long and multi-jointed, allowing them to brace themselves across vast gaps in the machinery or fold themselves into tight, quiescent geometries. Their bodies are a fusion. Striated, muscle-like fibers, the color of wet slate, are woven around and through a skeletal framework of pitted iron and brass. Veins of glowing, pale-blue light—like the pulsing fungi—are visible beneath translucent plates on their chests, tracing paths like circuit boards, their soft thrumming a quiet counterpoint to the machine's deep beat.

They have no faces. Where a head should be is a smooth, gimbal-mounted dome of polished brass, like the top of a surveyor's theodolite or the housing of an old ship's compass. This dome is their sensory array. It does not see with light. It swivels slowly, feeling the vibrations in the air and the structure of the Condenser, tasting the chemical composition of the water weeping from the walls, registering the pressure gradients in the hissing steam. When one focuses its attention, the brass dome might tilt, and a series of concentric rings might retract, revealing a deep, dark lens of obsidian glass, absorbing the light without reflection. They are the living versions of the dead gauges in the rusted monitoring stations. The needles are no longer frozen at the red line; the needles are now them.

They are the caldera's immune system and its nervous system. They are the fauna that completes the incomplete ecosystem. The reason no birds sing is because the resonant frequency the Custodians maintain is subtly hostile to avian neurology. The reason no mammals forage is because the Custodians, in their slow, silent patrols, exude a pheromone—a thin, ozonic vapor—that signals a lethal lack of viable food. They are not predators; they are a walking, climbing declaration that this place is *full*.

Their purpose is maintenance, an eternal, holy duty. You might see one clinging to the side of a great pipe, its torso opening like a clamshell to reveal an array of delicate, spidery tools that are part of its own body. With these, it will meticulously polish a valve housing or re-weave a frayed bundle of what look like metallic nerves. You might see another standing waist-deep in a steaming pool, one arm plunged to the elbow into the saturated earth, drawing up mineral-rich sludge to plaster over a weakening joint in the Condenser's foundation, a secretion from its palm acting as a quick-setting, metallic cement. They clear blockages, not with brute force, but by resonating a specific frequency that pulverizes the obstruction. They lubricate the slow-moving beam with an oil they secrete, a substance that smells of hot metal and crushed flowers.

Their origin is a mystery that feels answerable but unspeakable. Were they built? Did they evolve from the snails and beetles, incorporating the metals and energy of the machine into their very DNA over millennia? Or are they the builders themselves? The engineers and technicians who erected this weather engine, who, over generations of isolation, began to repair the machine with their own bodies, replacing failing parts with steel and brass until no flesh was left? Did they choose this transformation to ensure their creation's survival, or did the Boiler, in its thrumming loneliness, slowly and patiently remake them in its own image?

They do not speak. They have no mouths. Their communication is the Boiler's song. A sudden, sharp clang from the central sphere might be an alert, sending two or three Custodians moving with uncharacteristic speed—a fluid, climbing lope—toward the source. A subtle change in the pitch of the steam's hiss is a conversation, a status report shared across the basin. They operate as a single, distributed consciousness, their individual bodies like hands of a single being.

To be seen by a Custodian is a chilling, impersonal experience. It will stop its work. Its brass head will pivot to face you. The dark lens may appear. It is not assessing you as a threat or a meal. It is measuring you. It is reading your temperature, the moisture on your skin, the carbon in your breath. It is deciding if you are a transient anomaly to be ignored, or an impurity to be systematically and emotionlessly scoured from the system. Then, more often than not, it dismisses you. Its head turns away, and it resumes its slow, patient work, having already forgotten you exist. You are a falling leaf, a drip of water, a meaningless fluctuation in the data.

They are the ultimate inhabitants, born of the machine's breath and the jungle's embrace. They are neither alive nor mechanical in any way a human would understand. They are simply part of the process. They are the condensation of purpose in a world of steam, the sentient hum in the heart of the great machine. They live in the Boiler, and in a very real sense, they *are* the Boiler, listening to their own loud, eternal, and lonely heartbeat.
