The thing that lives here is a part of the Loom. You do not see it at first, because you are looking for an animal, and there are no animals here. You are looking for something that *moves against* the grain of the world. Here, life moves *with* it.

It is called a Weaver. Or perhaps it has no name for itself, and this is simply the designation you would settle on if you survived long enough to need one.

You might see one after hours of watching the silent, colossal shuffling of the grid. It will resolve itself from the structure, a piece of the architecture that has decided to walk. It does not scurry or stride; its movement is a controlled, hydraulic glide, unnervingly similar to the motion of the platforms themselves. It is as if a segment of railing, a length of conduit, and a tangle of roots unpeeled themselves from their setting and assumed a bipedal form.

The Weaver is tall and impossibly slender, its form dictated by the narrow gaps between the Loom’s components. Its body is a framework, not a solid mass. There is no flesh, no muscle in a biological sense. What you perceive as limbs are articulated struts of a material that is neither metal nor bone, but something in between—a ceramic composite, perhaps, with the dull, non-reflective luster of unglazed pottery, patterned in veins of deep verdigris and rust-orange. These limbs are multi-jointed, bending at points that seem arbitrary until you see the Weaver brace itself across a chasm, its legs and arms creating a perfect, load-bearing arch.

It has no skin. Its surface is the surface of the Loom itself: a mottled patina that has been weeping for millennia. Fine, green-black algae fills the hairline cracks in its carapace, and in the damp hollow of what might be a shoulder joint, a tiny, pale fern has taken root. It does not seem to mind. Water beads on its smooth, cool surfaces and drips from its fingertips with the same soft *tock* as it does from the girders above.

Where a head should be, there is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless and impassive. It does not turn to look. It has no visible eyes. But as it passes, this "head" might incline slightly, tilting as if listening to something you cannot hear—the resonant frequency of a particular pylon, the flow-rate of fluid in a pipe beneath your feet, the faint harmonic stress of a rivet about to give way in a decade or two. Its senses are not for light or sound, but for pressure, vibration, and electrical current. It sees the Loom not as a collection of objects, but as a single, interconnected circuit, and it reads the flow of its intention.

The Weavers are the tenders. They are the gardeners and the mechanics, the white blood cells of this valley-sized organism. You might see one paused by a "planter" module, its long, delicate fingers—which are more like a bundle of stiff, metallic cilia than digits—sifting through the moss and soil. It is not eating. It is testing, reading. It might withdraw a single, withered frond with the care of a watchmaker, or touch a junction box at the base of the planter, and you will hear a new, deeper hum as a fresh flow of nutrients is redirected from some unseen reservoir. They prune the vegetation and they calibrate the machine. To the Weaver, there is no distinction between the two. The root and the wire are part of the same system.

They do not speak. The heavy, saturated air is not for communication. Their language is the Loom's. A touch to a metal plate is a command. The pressure of a foot on a specific node can request the movement of the platform it stands on. They do not walk from place to place so much as they summon the place to them, or dismiss the path behind them. They flow with the machine's chaotic choreography because they are, in some sense, the choreographers.

To see one is to understand the profound emptiness of the Loom is a lie. It is not empty, but perfectly occupied. The Weavers did not arrive here, nor did they build the Loom. They are what happens when a structure of this complexity and antiquity achieves a kind of self-awareness and spins a body for itself out of its own substance. They are the machine’s way of touching itself, of tending to its own slow, eternal processes.

If a Weaver notices you, it is with a complete lack of curiosity. Its head might orient towards you, its internal sensors analysing you as they would a patch of rust or a leak in a coolant line. It perceives you as an anomaly, a temporary and unstable component. You are a loose bolt, a foreign object in the otherwise perfect system. It will not be hostile. Hostility is a complex, animal emotion. It will simply wait for the platform you are on to slide into a new configuration, or it will summon a gantry to glide between you, or it will simply move on, its task of maintaining a million-year-old process infinitely more important than your fleeting presence. You are not part of the pattern it is weaving. You are, at best, a snag in the thread, soon to be smoothed over and forgotten.
