The people who live in Ambit, the Ambitines, are themselves condensates of the city. They are born of the mist and the mountain, and it shows in their very marrow.

Their skin is pale, but it is not the pallor of sickness. It's the smooth, cool, semi-translucent white of a mushroom that has grown in a dark cellar, or the flesh of a fish from the deepest, lightless trenches. It holds a damp sheen, never truly dry, and is cool to the touch. Through the thinner skin at their wrists and temples, their veins are visible not as blue lines, but as muted, deep violet smudges, like ink bled into wet paper. Their bodies are not weak, but they are built for endurance, not speed. They possess a dense, compact musculature suited to slow, powerful movements: turning a rusted valve that hasn't moved in a decade, prying slabs of shale for a new wall, or carrying heavy coils of iron pipe up a thousand slippery steps. They move with a profound, water-like economy, their feet finding purchase on slick moss and worn stone with an unthinking grace. To see them move is to watch a slow, careful dance with gravity.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. In the perpetual twilight, their pupils are permanently wide, making their irises seem like thin, dark rings around pools of black. The colours of their eyes are the colours of Ambit's limited palette: the wet grey of slate, the deep, cloudy green of the liverwort webs, the reddish-brown of rusting iron. They do not glitter. Instead, they absorb the dim light, giving them a depth that can feel unnerving, as if they are looking not just *at* you, but through you, gauging the pressure and flow of your intentions.

The constant grit in the air has worked its way into them. Their voices are low and have a peculiar, resonant rasp, the sound of gravel gently stirred in a jar of water. They speak little, and never loudly; a shout would be an obscenity in the quiet, dripping streets. Communication is a matter of laconic gestures, a shared glance that conveys a paragraph of meaning, and a deep, innate understanding of the city's sounds. An Ambitine can tell the health of a district's water supply by the specific pitch of the hum from its pumps, or diagnose a blockage miles away by a subtle change in the percussive dripping in a cistern.

They are a people divided not by class or wealth, but by function, by their specific role in the city's great hydrological cycle.

There are the **Condenser Tenders**, who live and work on the highest, most exposed terraces. They are the most solitary of the Ambitines, sinewy figures wrapped in heavy, oil-impregnated wool that sheds water like a bird's feathers. Their skin is weathered to a texture like fine-grained stone, and their eyes, constantly scanning the mists for changes in density and direction, seem to hold the great, grey emptiness of the sky. They are the mystics of Ambit, reading the future in the whorls of a thickening cloud bank.

Below them are the **Pipe-Fitters** and **Aqueduct Masons**. They are the city's circulatory system surgeons, constantly maintaining, replacing, and rerouting the iron and stone vessels that give Ambit life. They work in teams, a silent corps of collaborators, their movements synchronized by years of shared labour. Their hands are calloused and powerful, stained with rust and stone dust, yet capable of the most delicate adjustments. They are the city’s pragmatists, for whom god is in the integrity of a weld and the perfect angle of a sluice gate.

The **Moss-herds** are perhaps the strangest. They cultivate the vast blankets of life that cover the city. They don't farm it for food, but for function. They use different species of moss and liverwort to filter water, to insulate buildings, to reinforce slopes against collapse. They are the city's gardeners, moving with a botanist's patience, often on their hands and knees. They read the health of the ecosystem in the colour and texture of a moss patch. They smell of sweet decay and damp earth, their fingernails permanently packed with rich, black soil. They are the most nurturing of the Ambitines, their quiet conversations seemingly directed as much to the plants as to each other.

And in the deepest, most insulated chambers carved into the mountain's heart are the **Archivists**. They are the palest of all, their bodies soft and their movements minimal. They are the city's memory. Their task is to record everything: atmospheric pressure readings, mineral content of the water from every major condenser, flow rates, pump efficiencies, the slow, inexorable creep of the liverworts. They do not write on paper, which would dissolve in the damp, but scribe in a complex, precise script onto thinly sliced sheets of treated shale. Their archives are vast libraries of stone tablets, a complete geological and meteorological history of the Grey Plateau. They are the priests and historians of Ambit, and their hydrological psalms are charts and data that stretch back for centuries.

The Ambitines do not seem to fear death. When one of them dies, their body is taken to a specific, terraced garden and laid into a bed of moss. Without ceremony, they are left for the relentless, quiet life of the plateau to reclaim them. They decay quickly in the damp, their bodies becoming part of the rich, dark humus that feeds the liverworts, their water returning to the system. They believe, in their practical, unsentimental way, that they are merely changing state, from a solid, thinking being to a liquid component of the mist that will one day be harvested again by the great Condensers. They are born of the water, they live to channel it, and to it they return. They are the sentient part of the city's plumbing, the living gears in the machine, as much a feature of the landscape as the dripping stone and the endlessly growing moss.
