The beings who live in the Woven Fell are known, by the few outsiders who have ever conceived of them, as the Fell-Dwellers. To themselves, they have no collective name, for they do not perceive a meaningful distinction between their own bodies and the body of the city. They are thoughts that the Fell is thinking, hands that it has grown to tend itself.

Their physical form is a testament to their world, a patient sculpture carved by generations of living in a vertical, swaying, and perpetually damp environment. They are slender and fine-boned, with a startlingly low center of gravity that allows them to move with uncanny balance on the most treacherous woven bridges. Their limbs are long and sinewy, their joints possessing a disquieting flexibility. An adult can hang by one arm and one foot, pruning a difficult branch, with the serene nonchalance of a resting sloth.

Their skin is their most immediate connection to the Fell. It is smooth and largely hairless, with a texture like cool, wet stone, and it comes in a thousand shades of bark-brown, lichen-grey, and deep olive-green. It is remarkably resistant to moisture and abrasion. But it is not a sterile barrier against the world; it is a habitat. In the protected hollows of their bodies—the nape of the neck, behind the knees, the delicate skin between their long fingers and toes—a fine, velvety moss grows. This is not seen as an impurity; it is a mark of belonging. The color and health of one’s personal moss is a sign of their own health and their integration with the Fell’s life force. Their children are born slick and bare, and the parents watch with pride as the first emerald fuzz begins to colonize their skin, a sign that the child has been accepted by the city.

Their hands and feet are their primary tools. They are long and elegant, with an artist’s dexterity. The big toe is semi-opposable, long and strong, able to grip a vine or a root-rung as securely as a finger. Their nails are not trimmed but honed on specific abrasive fungi, kept at a length and-shape that is perfect for picking larvae from bark or splitting the pods of ripe fruit.

To look into a Fell-Dweller’s face is to see the twilight of their home reflected. Their eyes are enormous, dark, and set wide in their faces, adapted for the perpetual green dimness. They have a tapetum lucidum, a reflective layer behind the retina like a cat or a deep-sea creature. In a sudden shaft of sunlight, their pupils shrink to impossibly small slits, but in the gloom, their gaze can cast a faint, silvery-gold eyeshine, adding to the moving glitter of the air.

Their hair is not hair as we know it. It is more akin to fine, silky mycelium or the delicate aerial roots of an orchid. It grows slowly and is never cut, but woven and braided with living flowers, glowing beetles in small cages of vine, or the iridescent wing-cases of insects. An elder’s hair might trail to their ankles, a living record of their life, entwined with the history of the Fell itself.

The Fell-Dwellers’ personhood is not contained within their skin. Their sense of self is diffuse, porous, and collective. The frantic, ceaseless activity of the city is the expression of their shared consciousness. They do not think, “I am hungry, I must go find food.” They feel a pang of an-hungered weakness in a specific sector of the Fell—a branch-cluster whose flowers are not producing enough nectar—and a group of them simply flows toward the problem to solve it. They are the city’s immune system, its nervous system, and its reproductive system, all at once.

Their communication is layered and complex, mostly non-verbal. Their language has few words for abstract concepts like "love" or "sadness," but hundreds of precise, sibilant, and clicking terms for the state of a particular root, the ripeness of a fungus, or the quality of light filtering through a new leaf. They speak in low, melodic tones that are easily distinguished from the booming croaks of the frogs and the high-pitched chitinous hum of insects. But more is said with a slight shift in weight, a percussive tapping on a hollow trunk with a bone tool, or the rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of their work. These rhythms are their public announcements, their songs, their arguments. A sharp, angry rhythm from the cistern-tenders is a universally understood complaint that the algae-scrapers are being lazy. A slow, gentle pulse from the high-canopy nurseries is a lullaby that vibrates through the very structure of the city.

Life and death here are merely phases of participation. When a Fell-Dweller dies, their body is not mourned in sorrow, but celebrated in a ritual of return. The body is carried to a special "Decomposition Alcove," a warm, humid hollow deep within one of the primary pillars. It is laid upon a bed of fungal spawn and blooming mosses. The community does not weep, but sings a wordless, rhythmic chant, the vibrations of which are said to encourage the mycelial network to accept the offering. They watch as the Fell reclaims the body, breaking it down, drawing its nutrients back into the great banyan host, turning flesh and bone into sap and leaf and flower. To be consumed by the Fell is the final act of tending, the ultimate expression of belonging.

To see a Fell-Dweller is to see a creature so perfectly adapted to its environment that it has ceased to be a separate entity. They are the hands that prune, the feet that balance, the eyes that watch in the green twilight. They are the waking dream of a city that is alive, and their frantic, graceful, endless dance of maintenance is its very heartbeat.
