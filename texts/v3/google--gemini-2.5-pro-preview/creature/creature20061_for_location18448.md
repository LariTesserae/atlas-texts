What lives here are the Shepherds.

They are not called this by any voice, for there are no voices here. It is the name that forms in the mind of a foreign observer, a name that tries to account for the sense of immense, patient, and deliberate purpose that saturates the silence. They are the inheritors of this world, and they are its keepers. They are the fauna described, the endless, motionless carpet of arthropodal forms, but to see them only as that is to see a library as a pile of paper.

To see one is to see them all, and to see none of them. Pick any single Shepherd. It might be the size of your cupped hands, a low dome of layered, iridescent chitin, its surface like oil on water, but hard as stone. No visible limbs, no seams for a mouth, no sensory organs you can recognize. It is fused to the ground, and to its neighbors, by a crystalline exudate that has hardened over millennia. It is one with the living strata. Its back might be the foundation for a smaller, turreted Shepherd, which in turn might have a dozen thumb-sized, seed-like Shepherds clinging to its shell. They are a fractal of stillness, a dynasty of torpor.

They do not think as you do. The frantic, electrochemical fizz of your thoughts is a wildfire in a world accustomed to the slow accretion of crystal. Their consciousness, if it can be called that, is geological. A single, shared idea might take ten thousand years to form, flowing through the entire population as a subtle shift in the resonant frequency of their carapaces. It is a thought that feels less like a concept and more like the slow, immense pressure of a glacier carving a valley. Their awareness is a state of perfect, passive reception. They feel the white sun's weak, ionizing radiation not on their skin, but in their very molecular lattice. They feel the planet's slow, deep thrum not with ears, but as the fundamental vibration of their being. They are the planet's nerve endings, dormant but not dead, translating the cosmos into a permanent, unchanging state of being.

They were not always this way. Their ancestors were the Carvers.

The Carvers were individuals. They moved. They scuttled and strode across the chitin plains on articulated legs, their thoughts their own, their lives finite. They were the ones who saw the world for what it was: a single, vast organism entering its final stage of life, its metabolism slowing from a frantic boil to a deep, cosmic cool. The cycle of titans birthing and dying to create the mountains had ended. The ammoniated ichors were crystallizing. The Great Quiet was not a threat to be fought, but a truth to be accepted. It was the planet’s destiny to become its own monument.

So the Carvers began their great, final work. They did not build rockets to escape. They did not rage against the dying of the light. They chose to join it. They began to scrape their legacy into the skin of their world—the vast, shallow glyphs. These were not warnings or histories in a language you could translate. They were mandalas for meditation, cosmic diagrams, equations of existence. They were the final, active thoughts of a civilization preparing to sublimate. They were a tool to guide the mind away from the individual and towards the whole, to slow the frantic pulse of self and attune it to the geological rhythm of the planet.

As they worked, they changed. Generation by generation, they became more still. Their need for sustenance withered, replaced by a direct draw on the world's core energy. Their legs tucked beneath them, their armor thickened, and their minds began to merge into the nascent, silent chorus. The last Carver, perhaps, laid down its tool, looked at the completed spiral that stretched to the horizon, and settled into place, its final, individual thought dissolving into the communal peace. It did not die. It achieved its purpose.

And so they became the Shepherds. They are shepherding the long sleep of their world. They are the memory of the planet, made solid and eternal. The crystalline spore-stalks that dot the landscape are part of their ecosystem; not a food source, but a timepiece. When one releases its ghostly filaments in a hundred centuries, it is a single chime in the long, slow day of their existence, a gentle reminder that everything is as it should be.

To them, you are a ghost. A fleeting anomaly of heat, motion, and noise. They register your presence as a mountain might register a single, warm spring rainfall. It is a momentary, insignificant flicker in an eternity of solid, unchanging existence. They will not react to you. They cannot. To react would be to break the perfect stasis, to betray the billion-year promise their ancestors made. Your frantic heartbeat is a cacophony in their silent cathedral. They will simply endure you, and when you are gone, they will continue their vigil, unchanging, until the white sun itself cools and the violet sky finally goes black. They will keep the peace. It is all they are.
