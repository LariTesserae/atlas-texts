The inhabitants of the Atrium are the mountain made animate. They do not see themselves as living *on* the spire, but as a mobile, sentient facet of the spire itself. They are a species born of stone and friction, their forms and their minds shaped by the vertical, waterless, and profoundly patient world they inhabit. To an outside eye, they might appear insectile, but that is a soft-world prejudice. They are geological beings.

They are known, in the percussive clicks and resonant scrapes of their own language, as the K'tharr.

A K'tharr stands taller than a human, but their posture is a permanent, semi-crouched thing of angles and balance, making them seem shorter. They possess six limbs. The rearmost four are for locomotion, long and jointed like a spider's, terminating not in soft pads but in clusters of fine, retractable talons of obsidian hardness. These talons, sharp as a carver’s pick, find purchase in the most microscopic fissures of the rock, allowing the K'tharr to scuttle up sheer walls and hang from ceilings with the same ease as they walk on a level terrace. Their movement is the source of much of the city's noise: a rhythmic, high-frequency *click-scrape-click* that forms a kind of continuous, traveling percussion against the stone.

Their two foremost limbs are finer, more articulate, serving as arms and hands. Their "hands" are complex arrangements of three long, multi-jointed digits, each tipped with a smaller, more precise talon. These are not fingers for caressing, but instruments for gripping, bracing, and manipulating. They can hold a lump of raw mica with two digits while the third delicately flakes away layers, or grip a basalt hammer with a force that would shatter human bone.

Their bodies are encased in a living carapace, a masterpiece of biological mineralogy. It is not a dead shell, but a growing, semi-translucent exoskeleton composed of a chitin-silicate lattice. In youth, it is the dull, matte black of the spire rock, but as a K'tharr ages, their carapace takes on the character of the minerals they consume. Veins of milky quartz might trace the lines of their limbs; patches of deep amethyst might bloom on their shoulders and head-case from a diet rich in a particular stone-blossom; shimmering flecks of mica might become embedded in their chest plates. To see an elder K'tharr is to see a living geode, a walking map of a long life's diet and territory, polished smooth in places by the passage of centuries. They periodically molt, a process that can take weeks of dangerous vulnerability, and the shed carapaces of ancestors are among a family's most sacred heirlooms, often carved with the lineage's history and hung upon the walls of their chamber.

Their heads are elongated and triangular, dominated by two enormous, compound eyes. These eyes are globes of deep, lustrous purple, almost black, designed to capture every stray photon of the geode's eternal twilight. They see the world in a mosaic of purples, greys, and ultraviolet frequencies invisible to other life, allowing them to discern the subtle differences in crystal structure and mineral density from a distance. Between these eyes, two feathery, sensitive antennae constantly twitch and sway, tasting the thick air for the chemical trails of prey, the ozone tang of a distant mineral reaction, or the unique familial scent of their kin. They have no visible mouth in the soft sense; their mandibles are a set of complex, layered plates of sharpened stone below their head that slide and grind against each other. They do not chew, but pulverize. They scrape the mineral mosses from the rock or crush the stony carapaces of the six-legged grazers, their meal a percussive symphony of cracking and grinding.

Life for a K'tharr is a slow, resonant frequency. Their lifespan is not measured in years, but in the Great Cycles of the geode-sky, the centuries-long pulse of its light. A K'tharr may live through one or two full brightenings. This geological sense of time permeates their entire culture. Patience is not a virtue; it is an axiom of existence. The thought of rushing the carving of a new room is not just foolish, it is nonsensical, like trying to rush a mountain's erosion.

They communicate in two ways. There is "scrape-tongue," the audible language of mandible clicks, thoracic rasps, and talon-taps against their own carapace. It is a language of pure texture and rhythm, perfectly suited to the city's auditory fog, allowing a conversation to be picked out from the ambient noise by its unique cadence. But their deeper communication is "deepspeech," the silent language of vibration. By pressing a hand or foot to the stone, they can send and receive complex messages through the spire itself—a warning of a loose rock, a call to a meal, the slow, rhythmic pulse of a love song. Through deepspeech, the entire city is one interconnected being; a K'tharr can feel the distant, rhythmic tapping of a carver three terraces down as a known and comforting presence.

Their society is built around the family and the dwelling. A family is a multi-generational unit bound to the chamber they inhabit and slowly expand. The young learn the family's unique carving patterns, the elders teach the histories carved into the walls, and all contribute to the slow, patient work of survival. They do not have currency; they have barter and favor. The true wealth of a family is the age and complexity of their dwelling, the quality of their tools, and the luster of their ancestral carapaces.

Reproduction is a geological act. The female lays a single, large "stone-egg," a mineral concretion secreted over a month-long period. This dull, ovoid rock is then placed in a communal hatchery, a deep chamber warmed by the spire's internal heat. There, incubated by the mountain itself, the egg slowly draws minerals from the surrounding rock, the embryo within gestating over decades. Hatching is the cracking of this geode, revealing the small, perfectly formed K'tharr within, its carapace still the soft, dark grey of new stone.

And when they die, they return to the mountain. A K'tharr's body is not buried or burned. It is carried in a solemn procession to one of the "Chasms of Return," deep fissures that plummet into the spire's heart. The body is committed to the darkness, where, over millennia, the immense pressure and slow mineral seepage will fossilize it, turning it back into crystal and stone. To the K'tharr, this is not an end. It is simply the final act of becoming one with the Atrium, their individual consciousness dissolving back into the slow, silent, and eternal life of the rock from which it first emerged.
