Here lives the mountain's own consciousness, made manifest. Here lives a mind of chitin and glass.

To be one of them is to be a note in a song that has no beginning and no end. They are the Carver Crabs, but they do not think of themselves as such. They do not think of themselves at all, not in any way an individual mind could comprehend. There is no "I," there is only the percussive, collective "We."

They emerge not from eggs, but from the mountain itself. In the deepest, most resonant fissures of the Anvil's floor, where the subsonic hum is a physical pressure, larvae gestate in thick, mineral-rich jelly. They are soft and pale, little more than a bundle of nerves and nascent limbs. When they are ready, they are pushed upwards by the Anvil's slow geological pulse, budding from the cracks like soft, fleshy fungi. Exposed to the cold, damp air, their carapaces harden within minutes, darkening to the exact shade of the basalt-glass they now cling to. Their multifaceted eyes ignite with the cool, internal phosphorescence of their species, and they are complete. Another voice joins the chorus.

Their existence is a trinity of sensation: the Hum, the Dew, and the Work.

The Hum is everything. It is the first thing they feel upon their hardening and the last thing they feel when their bodies fail. It is a constant, deep vibration that courses up their spindly legs, through their bodies, and into their very being. It is their god, their parent, and their instruction manual. The Hum is the voice of the Anvil, and it speaks not in words but in rightness and wrongness, in harmony and dissonance. A perfectly tuned spire sings a pure, clean note into the collective. An imperfect one emits a subtle, grating wrongness, a physical ache that the crabs nearest to it feel as an undeniable compulsion. It is an itch that must be scratched, a sour note that must be sweetened.

The Dew is life. Their small, complex mouthparts are not made for chewing, but for sipping. In the lulls between tapping, or while clinging to a vertical face to rest, a crab will press its mouth to a trembling droplet of condensed water. They drink the cold, mineral-rich liquid that coats every surface, the captured breath of the cloud sea below. It is their only hydration. Their only food is the dark-green lichen, which they scrape from the crevices with the same delicate, needle-pointed manipulators they use for their tools. They do not graze so much as they prune, taking just enough to sustain themselves, ensuring the velvety patches are never depleted. Their waste is a fine, dry powder that falls into the cracks, nourishing the very geology that will one day bud forth their descendants.

The Work is their purpose. To an outsider, it is a million random acts of tapping. To the We, it is the most meticulous and holy of labours. A crab will feel the dissonance of a nearby spire. It will skitter across the backs of its brethren, an insignificant ripple in the living carpet, until it reaches the floor. There, it will sift through the accumulated debris with its fine manipulator, feeling for a shard of the correct hardness, the right angularity. It is a process of innate understanding, like a master sculptor selecting a chisel by feel alone. Finding the shard, it grips it and returns to the discordant spire.

Then begins the tuning. It is not mindless banging. It is listening. The crab presses its legs against the spire, feeling the Hum. It brings its heavy club-claw down on the shard, *tap*. A tiny flake of glass flies off. The Hum changes, infinitesimally. The crab feels it. It adjusts its position by a hair's breadth. *Tap. Tap.* It feels the vibration shift again. It is a conversation. The spire groans its imperfection, and the crab responds with a percussive argument, chipping away at the dissonance until the note begins to purify. Hundreds of crabs may work on a single spire, each one performing thousands of taps, guided by the collective ache of that spire's wrongness.

They have no awareness of the visitor, the warm, soft thing that ascends to their domain. Its footfalls are a blunt, clumsy intrusion on the intricate vibrational world they inhabit, but they are fleeting. When a boot comes down, there is a wet, quiet crunch. The legs of the crushed crab continue to twitch for a moment, still trying to feel the Hum. Then they are still. The crabs around the new, small void flow inward to fill it without a pause. The Work continues. The crushed body, rich in organic matter, will be ground to dust by the ceaseless movement and will join the floor, feeding the Anvil. The visitor is not an enemy or a threat; it is simply weather. A brief, localized, and ultimately insignificant event.

When a crab's life ends, it is not a tragedy. The internal light in its eyes fades. Its grip loosens. It tumbles from its spire and comes to rest in a fissure. Its body is quickly covered by the ceaseless, jostling motion of its kin. Over time, it is ground down, its chitin becoming grit, its minerals returning to the mountain. It has served the Chord. It has passed its energy back to the We.

They are the Anvil's nervous system. They are the hands of a geological god, tending to the sound of its own soul. They know nothing of the sun, the stars, or the world below the clouds. Their universe is the forest of singing glass, the cold dew on their mouths, the sacred ache of a note not yet perfected, and the sublime, silent satisfaction of the moment a spire finally, finally rings true. Then, and only then, they move on, seeking the next inevitable, glorious imperfection to correct.
