What lives here are the tenders.

They are not fauna, not in any sense that a biologist would recognize. They are not separate from the Grove; they are the Grove's own ambulant, specialized organs. They are its hands, its senses, its immune system, given a form that can walk the living decks. Seeing one is a rare and unsettling thing, because they are not often seen in motion. More often, you see only the aftermath of their presence, or mistake them for a part of the architecture.

To see one emerge is to witness a slow miracle. A section of a pillar-wall, one where the bark-skin is patterned in a particularly complex whorl, might begin to deepen in color. The lines of the pattern will shift, flowing like ink on wet paper. A form will thicken, gathering substance from the woody mass. It resolves itself, delaminating from the surface not like a creature stepping out of a doorway, but like a reflection gaining weight and peeling itself off the mirror.

The tender stands, for a moment, an outline against its own absence in the wall. It is tall and impossibly slender, a figure of elegant length, as if stretched by the gravity of centuries. Its form is humanoid only in its bipedal stance and its possession of two long, multi-jointed arms. There is no true skeleton within; it is a hydrostatic structure, its shape maintained by the turgor pressure of the same clear, coppery fluid that fills the floor-vessels. Its skin is a seamless mosaic of the Grove's own textures: the smooth, warm calloused wood of the pillars forms its limbs, while its torso is covered in the iridescent, chitinous plating that jackets the great pistons. Patches of its body are soft and vascular, like the floor, and you can see the slow, rhythmic pulse of fluid moving just beneath the surface.

It has no face. Where a head should be is a smooth, ovoid shape of dark, polished wood, like a river stone. But embedded in this faceless prow is a constellation of the same bioluminescent moss that grows on the dead gauges. These points of cool, blue-green light wax and wane in a slow, hypnotic rhythm, a silent language of pressure, temperature, and chemical states. This is how it sees—not with light, but by tasting the saturated air, by feeling the resonant thrum of the Grove through the soles of its root-like feet, by sensing the subtle shifts in the moss-light of its brethren.

They move with the same unhurried pace as the Grove itself. A step is a slow, deliberate placement of a foot, which momentarily seems to fuse with the floor before lifting again. There is no sound to their passage but a faint, wet, frictional whisper, like thick leaves sliding against each other. They are functionally immortal and ageless, their existence a continuous cycle of service. They do not eat, or sleep, or speak. They are sustained by the Grove itself, drawing energy directly through their contact with it, and their communication is a silent communion of shared sensation.

Their work is maintenance. One might approach a groaning flywheel and press its long, tapering fingers against the axle. The fingertips will soften and flow, becoming a viscous lubricant that seeps into the mechanism, quieting its complaint. Another might stand before a junction of arterial pipes where the flow has become sluggish; it will place its wooden "head" against the conduit, its lights pulsing as it diagnoses the blockage. Then, it might strike the pipe with a hand that has momentarily hardened into dense, mallet-like wood, the resulting *thump* a calibrated shockwave to clear the obstruction. They are the living tools the builders left behind, or perhaps, the tools the builders became.

They tend to the final product. When a nodule of the amber-sap hardens on the floor, a tender will eventually arrive. It will gently, patiently work the nodule free, its fingers prying with immense, slow strength. It carries the glowing prize not to a storage bin, but to a specific, seemingly random alcove in the wall, placing it just so. These arranged nodules form patterns on a scale too vast to comprehend from the floor, a circuit board of solidified energy whose purpose is known only to the Grove.

There is no birth, and there is no death. When a new tender is needed, a node on a pillar swells and buds, gestating a new form which will eventually detach and join the silent work. When a tender is damaged, or its cycle of purpose is complete, it will find a quiet place. It will press itself against a wall or lie down upon the floor. Its form will soften, losing its distinct outline, its substance flowing back into the whole. The iridescent chitin, the polished wood, the glowing moss—all are reabsorbed, melting back into the substrate of the Peristaltic Grove, their experience and knowledge becoming another layer in the deep, silent memory of the living machine. They are waves on the surface of an ocean, distinct for a time, but always, inevitably, returning to the sea.
