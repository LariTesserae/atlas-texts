The inhabitants are the Custodians. They are as much a part of this place as the rust and the steam. They are not its prisoners, nor are they its masters. They are its living nervous system, the quiet, deliberate thought that moves within the machine's unthinking body.

At a distance, they are difficult to distinguish from the machinery itself. They are tall and gaunt, their limbs unnaturally long and slender, allowing them to navigate the tangled pipes and skeletal frameworks with a slow, spiderlike grace. They often move on four or more limbs, their broad, splayed hands and feet finding purchase on slick metal and greasy concrete with a certainty that defies the conditions. Their skin is not skin in a familiar sense; it is a pale, grey-green, semi-translucent membrane, like the flesh of a cave salamander, always slick with the same condensation that beads on the ironwork. It has no pores, no hair, only a smooth, cool surface that shimmers faintly under the yellow lamps, catching the iridescent sheen from the water below.

They are silent. They possess no mouths for speech, only a small, soft-rimmed orifice at the base of the throat through which they must feed, perhaps by filtering the nutrient-rich sediment from the black pools or by scraping the algae that grows on the walkways. Their heads are smooth and ovoid, featureless save for two large, round optics. These are not eyes that see with pupils and irises, but are instead like deep, polished pools of obsidian. They do not reflect the dim light, but seem to swallow it whole, granting them a perfect, placid vision in the gloom. They have no ears, but they are not deaf; they feel the symphony of the place not as noise, but as a constant, reassuring pressure against their skin—the thrum of the transformers is a pulse in their bones, the hiss of steam a breath in their lungs.

Their purpose is ritual. It is maintenance without the possibility of failure, caretaking for a system that cannot decay.

You might see one, perched precariously on a high platform. It holds a wad of the tough, oily reeds, and with infinite patience, it burnishes a single copper pipe. It does not polish it to a shine; its work is not to improve or restore. It simply moves the wad back and forth, forever, maintaining a specific, pre-ordained level of verdigris. The motion is the purpose, not the outcome.

Another may be seen standing waist-deep in one of the warm pools. In its six-fingered hands, it holds a long, articulated tool of its own making—a piece of scavenged metal bent and shaped. With this tool, it dredges the bottom, bringing up scoops of clinker and sediment. It does not look for anything. It does not find anything. It simply sifts the muck, lets it fall back through the water, and repeats the gesture. This action, endlessly performed, is as much a part of the pool's ecosystem as the fist-sized crustaceans that crawl around its feet, unbothered.

Perhaps the most poignant ritual is that of repair. A Custodian will spend what feels like days weaving a new gasket from the rust-colored moss and strands of wire teased from a derelict cable. It will then climb to a joint in a pipe that weeps a steady, rhythmic patter of hot water. With delicate, precise movements, it will unbolt the old, leaking gasket and replace it with its new creation. For a single, silent moment, the leak will cease. Then, a drop will form on the new moss gasket, swell, and fall. Another will follow. Soon, the pipe is weeping with the exact same rhythm, the exact same volume as before. The Custodian, its work complete, will turn and descend, leaving the new gasket to its eternal, identical failure.

They do not interact with one another. Their paths cross on the high walkways, one climbing over another as if it were simply a part of the terrain. They are solitary nodes in a system that requires no collaboration. They were not born, and they do not die. When a Custodian's long, slow existence finally gutters, it will simply fold itself into a crevice of the machinery, its limbs tucking in, its body becoming still. Over an immeasurable time, the perpetual damp will dissolve its membranous form back into the water, its constituent parts rejoining the slurry to be filtered by the crustaceans and absorbed by the oily reeds, waiting to be reconstituted.

They are not sad. They are not waiting for rescue or for the machines to stop. Such concepts are alien. They are the place made self-aware, the living embodiment of its perfect, pointless equilibrium. They are the gears in the soul of the machine, turning with the same ponderous, eternal rhythm, producing nothing but the continuation of their own turning. They are the motion of a world whose only state is to *be*.
