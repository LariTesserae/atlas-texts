They are the Quiet People. Not because they are without sound, but because they have learned to exist within a world that is only sound, and in that roar, they have found a different kind of silence. To an outsider, they would seem preternaturally still, their motions islands of deliberate calm in the chaotic, squirming sea of life.

They are slight of build, with frames made of whipcord muscle and tough, flexible bone. Generations of walking the springy, uneven mat have given them a low center of gravity and an uncanny sense of balance. Their legs are powerful, their feet are wide and splayed, with long, prehensile toes that can grip the woven surface, finding purchase on a knotted vine or a ridge of hardened fungus as easily as a hand. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and ranges in colour from a deep, mossy green to the ruddy brown of drying leaves, patterned with dark, shifting mottles that mimic the dappled light filtering through the canopy above. It is a living camouflage, but more than that, it is tough and waterproof, perpetually slick with a thin, non-greasy secretion that repels the constant damp and resists the ubiquitous spores and mildews.

Their faces are broad, with wide-set, dark eyes. These eyes are not their primary sense. In the hazy, pollen-choked air and the stupefying scale of the landscape, sight is often unreliable. Their vision is sharpest at middle distances, adept at picking out the subtle tremor of a predatory vine or the change in texture that signals a weak spot in the mat. Their true mastery of perception lies elsewhere.

Their ears are small and simple, almost vestigial. They are functionally deaf to the cacophony. The Great Hum, the shriek of the Sail-wings, the grinding of the grazers—this is not noise to them; it is the texture of the air, the pressure of the world. They are born into it, live their entire lives within it, and die in it. It is the defining constant of their existence, as fundamental and ignorable as gravity. Their hearing has evolved past it. Instead, they listen with their whole bodies. Standing perfectly still, feet planted firmly on the mat, a Weaver can feel the world. They can feel the distant, rhythmic footfalls of a migrating herd as a low-frequency tremor in their bones. They can discern the frantic, high-frequency skittering that signals a nest of Skitter-mites beneath the surface, a place to be avoided. They can feel the deep, groaning stress in the Canopy Sea that precedes a mat-quake, when a chasm might open up. This is their primary language of survival: a tactile, vibrational sense that maps the world for them in a way sight never could.

Because of this, their own language is almost entirely silent. They communicate through a complex and beautiful flowing gesture of hands, body, and face. It is a language of posture and balance, where a shifting of weight can change the meaning of a sentence, and the speed of a hand-sign indicates urgency or emotion. To see two Weavers converse is to watch a silent dance, their movements economical and precise, made stark against the riot of motion that is the rest of the world. For long-distance communication, they have other means. They can strike the mat with the hardened, resonating heel of a staff in specific patterns, sending messages that travel for miles through the root system to be felt by another Weaver. These are simple signals: *Danger*. *Gather*. *Sail-wing fall*.

They live in small, nomadic kin-groups, which they call Strands. A Strand might be only a handful of individuals or several dozen, and they flow across the Canopy Sea like water, following the seasonal flushes of edible fungi, the fruiting of the great spire-trees, or the migration patterns of the grazers they sometimes hunt. They have no concept of land ownership. The world is a single being, and they are simply moving across its skin. Their homes are woven for a night or a season and then abandoned to be reabsorbed by the mat.

Their identity is woven, not written. They carry their history in "story-knots," long cords of vine-silk dyed with pigments from pollen and berries. Each knot, twist, and braid represents an event, a person, a journey, a piece of knowledge. A child learns to "read" the knots with their fingers, feeling the story of their Strand, the great hunts, the ancient bridges, the names of their ancestors. When a Weaver dies, their personal story-knot, a small, intricate braid they have worked on their entire life, is carefully unwound and its threads woven into the larger knot of the Strand. Death is not an end; it is being re-integrated into the collective story.

The Weavers' relationship with their world is one of profound intimacy and respect. They are not its masters; they are its participants, its nerve-endings. The young, called Mat-crawlers, spend their early years on all fours, learning the feel of the world through the palms of their hands before they ever learn to stand. They learn which fungi are food, which are poison, and which contain the hallucinogenic spores used by their shamans, the Hum-listeners, to commune more deeply with the living world. They learn to harvest gut-silk from a freshly dead Sail-wing before scavengers arrive, to carve a hook from petrified wood that will hold against the strain of a chasm-spanning bridge, to read the colour of the sky for the coming of a Pollen Storm.

They are masters of a single, all-encompassing art: the art of finding the pattern in the chaos. They see the entire world as a great, ongoing process of weaving and unweaving. A predatory vine catching an insect is a thread being pulled. A grazer herd chewing a field of fungus is the pattern being altered. A Weaver's life is to maintain their own thread, to contribute to the pattern of their Strand, and to move in harmony with the great, roaring, breathing, deafening tapestry of the world. They are a quiet thought in a screaming mind, a moment of intention in an ocean of instinct.
