In this city that was never built, live the builders who never built it. They are the city's motion and its mind, a consciousness born of the lithostrome itself. They are not its children, but a later, more complex phase of its being; as a flower is to a root, they are to the stone. There is no collective name for them, for they have no use for one, but an observer might call them the Architects of the Flow, or more simply, the Chimes.

To see one is a rare event, a punctuation mark in a millennium of quiet grammar. From a distance, in the thick mist, one might be mistaken for a trick of the light, a piece of loose architecture swaying in a phantom wind. They are impossibly tall and slender, their forms suggesting a delicate, inverse calligraphy written against the grey air. Standing between seven and ten feet high, their bodies are composed of the same stormy-grey, semi-mineral substance as the city, but it is not petrified. It is living, flexible, and cool to the touch, like damp river clay. Their surfaces are smooth and bear the same polished sheen as the ground, not from weathering, but from their own slow, internal processes.

They possess no face, no discernible front or back. Their "head" is merely the termination of a long, elegant torso, tapering to a soft, rounded point. From this torso sprout three, or sometimes five, long, spindly limbs. Two of these limbs serve as legs, but not in a way a human would recognize. They are jointed in multiple places, allowing for a slow, deliberate gait that is both silent and unnervingly fluid. The other limbs, longer and more delicate, are used for balance and for interacting with the world, ending not in hands, but in splayed, porous pads that can cling to the vertical surfaces of the lithostrome with gentle hydrostatic pressure. They can walk along the floor of a canyon or ascend a thousand-foot spire with the same unhurried grace, their forms like spiders woven from mist and stone.

Within their translucent bodies is their most defining feature. A complex, milky-white network of filaments, like the veins in the city's stone, pulses with a soft, internal luminescence. This is their circulatory and nervous system combined, a visible map of their life force. The light waxes and wanes with their activity, glowing brighter when they are ascending a difficult wall or processing a rich flow of water, and dimming to a near-imperceptible glimmer when they are at rest. This faint, cold light is often the first and only sign of their presence in the deep alleys and shadowed plazas.

The Chimes do not eat. They drink. Their porous feet are not just for grip; they are their connection to the world's metabolism. Standing still in a stream or a shallow pool, they absorb the mineral-rich water that weeps from the lithostrome. The fluid is drawn up into their bodies and circulated through their luminous network of filaments, which filters out the trace elements they need for sustenance. The processed, purified water is then released as a fine, cool vapor from nearly invisible pores on their upper bodies, adding to the eternal mist. They are living, mobile conduits, a sentient part of the planet's water cycle.

Their purpose, if it can be called that, is a quiet, continuous act of maintenance. They are the gardeners of this stone landscape. An individual Chimé might sense a subtle change in the pressure of a deep channel, a blockage forming in the lithostrome's capillary network miles away. It will begin a journey, a slow drift that might take decades, across the vast tableland. Upon arrival, it will position itself near the blockage and simply stand, its internal processes subtly changing the mineral composition of the water it absorbs and expels. Over years, this gentle intervention dissolves the blockage, restoring the ancient equilibrium. They tend the city not with tools, but with their very being.

They are solitary. Encounters between them are rare and momentous, conducted in the profound silence. They do not speak, but they communicate. When the need arises—a signal of presence, a warning of a major geologic shift, or perhaps something akin to a call for communion—a Chimé will tense its entire crystalline structure. By modulating the resonance of its own form, it releases a single, incredibly pure, low-frequency tone. The sound is not carried by the air, but by the stone itself. It travels for hundreds of miles through the contiguous lithostrome, a vibration felt rather than heard—a single, resonant *chime* that can be perceived by any other of its kind who has its feet planted on the stone. It is a language of presence, of location, of being.

They are born of the city and they return to it. In some deep, nutrient-rich basin, a knot of mineral and light will begin to form on a wall, a bud growing from the living stone. Over a century, it will differentiate, lengthen, and form limbs, until one day it detaches—a new wanderer beginning its long, slow drift. And when a Chimé reaches the end of its impossibly long life, its movements slow, its inner light dims to a final flicker. It will find a quiet alcove, press itself against a wall, and become still. Its limbs fuse. Its body hardens. Over the next thousand years, it will slowly petrify, becoming just another seamless feature in the silent architecture, its life's work complete, its form returning to the greater body from which it emerged. It is not death; it is reintegration. They are the city, momentarily awake.
