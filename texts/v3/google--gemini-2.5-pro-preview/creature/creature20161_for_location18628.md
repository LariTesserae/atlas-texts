They are the Glaze-Kin.

They are not born of the Great Tableland, not originally. They are what was left behind. They are the descendants of the people who built the wellheads and the condenser fins, but generations of mutation and adaptation have made them something new, something that belongs to the Caldera Roof as surely as the Asbestos Moss.

Their skin is their most defining feature. It is not soft flesh, but a thickened, semi-mineralized hide that shares the texture of the ground itself. It is a living ceramic, pale and matte, crazed with a network of fine lines like shattered glaze. These lines weep a viscous, saline fluid that helps them regulate their temperature. Over a lifetime, mineral deposits from the geothermal dew stain their bodies in the same palette as the land. An elder might have a shoulder blushed with the verdigris of copper salts from a favored sleeping fissure, their forearms permanently stained the rusty red of iron oxides from the runnels they frequent to gather water. They are living maps of their own territory.

They are stooped, heavy beings. Their bones are dense, their forms blocky and powerful, echoing the pragmatic, over-engineered architecture of their ancestors. They move with a deliberate, ground-sensing gait, their broad, splayed feet finding purchase on slick rock. There is no grace to them, none of the Stilt-Walkers' alien elegance. Theirs is the efficiency of a draught animal, a purpose-built organism. The "strange compounds" that leak from the ruins have left their indelible mark. Asymmetry is common, a given. One arm might be slightly longer and thicker, its hand broader, better suited for bracing against a shuddering rock face. Their eyes, often mismatched in size or color, are set deep within their skulls, protected by heavy brow ridges. The pupils are reflective, silvered slits, like chips of mica, catching and amplifying the faint light of pre-dawn.

Their lives are ruled by the rhythm of condensation. They make their homes in the silent, hollow places of the past: within the great, broken ceramic pipes that lie like fallen columns, or in excavated hollows beneath the cast-iron foundations of the old stations. In the blasting heat of midday and the chill of deep night, they are hidden, dormant. But in the twilight hours, as the world begins to weep its mineral dew, they emerge.

The emergence is a slow, silent procession. They unfold themselves from their shelters, their cracked hides slick with the new condensation. Their first act is always the same: the gathering of water. They do not drink haphazardly. They are farmers of dew. Using curved, sharp-edged shards of salvaged condenser fins, they move across the flats, scraping the precious, metal-tanged moisture from the rocks into tough, leathery gourds grown from the maroon lichen. Every drop is sacred, the source of all life. A family's wealth is measured by the number of filled gourds they can secure before the sun burns the gift away.

Their language is one of low, resonant hums that mimic the thrum of the earth, punctuated by sharp clicks and hisses learned from the skitterers and the steam vents. They speak little, communicating more through gesture and the shared, silent understanding of their precarious existence. They are not the apex predators of this place. They are scavengers and opportunistic hunters. They use their weight and their knowledge of the terrain to their advantage, driving herds of the plate-backed skitterers into cul-de-sacs or over the edges of hot fissures where the creatures are stunned or killed by the heat. They use long, sharpened rods of scavenged metal to pry the cooked meat from the iridescent shells. They also harvest the glass-leafed scrub, their tough digestive systems able to break down the fibrous, metallic foliage that would poison any off-worlder.

They do not understand the machines of their ancestors, not anymore. The knowledge of engineering has been lost, replaced by a deep, instinctual reverence. They are the priests of a dead industry, the tenders of a silent god. The great, rust-eaten wellheads are their altars. They anoint them with pigments made from ground minerals and skitterer blood. They listen to the groaning contraction of the metal towers at sunset not as a physical phenomenon, but as the tired sighs of a sleeping deity. The shudder of the land is its dream-twitch. The hiss of a new fumarole is its voice.

Their most sacred figures are the Pipe-Singers. These are the elders, their bodies so heavily stained with minerals they seem more geological than biological. They spend their days crouched inside the mouths of the giant ceramic pipes, humming in resonance with the sub-audible vibrations of the Tableland. They are the historians and the prophets, reading the future in the changing pitch of the earth's hum, in the taste of the morning dew, in the health of the Asbestos Moss near the main vents.

The Glaze-Kin are a people defined by a profound sense of failure that they cannot articulate. They live within the skeleton of a grand ambition, and their rituals are a cargo cult of geothermal engineering. They gather Asbestos Moss not for industry, but to weave into shrouds for their dead, an acknowledgment of the heat that gives and takes life. They bolt plates of salvaged iron to their shelters not for structural integrity, but as offerings to the machine-gods. They are waiting. Waiting for the day the great machines roar to life again, for the groans of the towers to become a triumphant bellow, for the fever of the world to break and grant them, its eternally patient and misshapen children, a reprieve they can't remember ever having. They live and die on the roof of a furnace, their gaze turned not to the pale, harsh sky, but down, always down, listening to the slow, hot pulse of the world beneath their feet.
