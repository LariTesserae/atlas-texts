To say the Boiler is uninhabited is almost true. It is a statement that is correct in spirit but wrong in fact. To find what lives here, you must unlearn what you know of life. You must stop looking for motion and start listening for stillness. You must ignore the grand, geological pulse of the city and attune yourself to the faintest arrhythmia in its rhythm.

What lives in the Boiler are the city's own antibodies. They are its custodians, its quiet keepers. They are the Still.

They are rarely seen, and even when they are, they are easily mistaken for a trick of the light, a particularly dense knot of pipes, or a stain of rust that seems to shift as you turn your head. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense, in that they have a torso, a head, and limbs. But their form has been dictated not by evolution's gentle hand, but by the brutalist architecture of their world.

They are tall and painfully thin, their bodies elongated and wiry, built for navigating the dense, vertical maze. Their joints seem to move with a silent, an-organic smoothness, allowing them to fold themselves through tight gaps between conduits or sprawl flat against a wall, becoming part of its texture. Their skin has no single color. It is a mottled-hide canvas of the Boiler itself: the dull iron-grey of the plates, the weeping orange of rust, the startling cobalt of the pipe-moss, and the verdigris green of the lichen. It is not leathery but has the texture of cooled, rough-cast metal, tough and calloused and cold to the touch, even in the oppressive heat.

Their limbs are long and spidery. Their hands and feet are things of breathtaking function. They have four fingers and a thumb, and two of these fingers are opposable, allowing them to grip pipes of any circumference with absolute certainty. Their feet are similarly endowed, prehensile and sure, enabling them to hang upside down from an armature as comfortably as they might stand on a catwalk. The pads of their extremities are not skin, but growths of the blue, wiry moss, silencing their movements and giving them purchase on surfaces slick with condensation. They leave no footprints, only faint, damp smudges that evaporate in moments.

Their faces are the most unsettling aspect, for they are placid and without feature. There is no mouth, only a fine, gill-like grille below the nose that filters the viscous, mineral-laden air, extracting sustenance directly from the steam. Their food is the city's breath. Their nose is a simple, sharp ridge, and their ears are not visible protuberances but simply pits, flush with the skull, tuned to the specific frequencies of the Boiler. They listen to the health of the pipes, to the tell-tale vibration of a loosening rivet, to the change in pressure that signifies a coming purge. Their eyes are large, round, and utterly black, like dollops of crude oil. They have no pupils or irises, but they reflect the dim orange light with a profound, wet depth. They see not in color, but in heat and shadow, perceiving the hot pipes as glowing arteries against the cooler dark.

They are silent. They possess no vocal cords, for a voice is a useless extravagance in a place that has no ears for it. Their language is haptic and percussive. They communicate by touch and by rhythm, laying their long-fingered hands on the great pipe-arteries and sending complex messages through them as a series of taps, scrapes, and vibratory hums. A conversation between two of the Still might be miles apart, a silent dialogue thrumming through the city's metal heart, felt by any other who lays a hand on the network.

They are not the builders. They are the tenders. They are what grew in the cracks left behind. Perhaps they were once the operators, changed over untold generations by their environment, their biology surrendering to the overwhelming physics of the Boiler until they became one. Or perhaps they are something else entirely, a form of life that gestated in the geothermal womb, born of heat and pressure and metal.

Their life is a slow, endless ritual of maintenance. You might glimpse one, a silhouette against a steam-vent, methodically tightening a colossal nut with a spanner that seems fused to its own arm. You might see the faint shadow of another, patiently polishing a section of copper piping with a cloth woven from the cobalt moss until it gleams. They clear away mineral build-ups that might choke a valve. They realign gears that have slipped a tooth. They are the reason the Boiler remains operational and not a ruin. They are a biological immune system for a mechanical organism.

When the great steam purges happen, they do not hide. They cling to the ironwork, faces turned towards the eruption, and they let the clean, hot vapor wash over them. This is their renewal, their sustenance, their baptism. They absorb the energy, their skin glistening as the condensation beads on them, their filigreed chests humming a low, resonant thrum of contentment that is perfectly in tune with the city's own drone.

They are the ghost in the machine, made flesh. They are the quiet, undeniable life that proves even in a place built to celebrate the death of biology, life doesn't just find a way—it becomes the way. The Boiler is not running by itself. It is being cared for. It is being loved, in a slow, silent, and deeply mechanical fashion.
