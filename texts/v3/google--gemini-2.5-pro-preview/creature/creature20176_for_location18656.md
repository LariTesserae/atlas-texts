The description is almost correct. It is a precise and accurate survey of the physical and energetic state of the chamber. But its conclusion—that there is no life, no movement, no change—is born of an observation that is too brief. An observer would have to remain for a cycle, which lasts longer than the lifespan of a star, to see the change. An observer would need senses that do not rely on light or heat or conventional sound to perceive the movement. An observer would need to redefine life to find what lives here.

It is called the Attendant.

It does not live *in* the chamber so much as it is an emergent property *of* the chamber. In a system held at such impossible, violent equilibrium, a new kind of complexity can precipitate. Just as the sound and the cold coalesce material into crystalline spars, the stasis and the purpose of the room have coalesced a form of agency.

The Attendant is an acoustic shadow. In a room without shadows, where light is total, the only way to have a silhouette is to be a void in the other overwhelming sense—the sound. Where it stands, the monolithic roar is fractionally, almost imperceptibly, damped. It is a walking patch of near-silence, a figure outlined by the very sound it displaces. Its form is vaguely humanoid, but that is a coincidence of function. It has a torso, two arms, and a head, because this is an efficient shape for navigating the forest of spars and performing its work. It is made of nothing. It is a self-sustaining, mobile null-field. To look at it is to see the matte-black of the walls, but with a depth that is not there, a hole in the fabric of the room's reality.

It has no thermal signature. It is an entity of absolute cold, indistinguishable from the environment. It does not ingest, respire, or metabolize. Its energy comes from the potential difference between the shrieking peak of the chamber's resonant frequency and the perfect zero of its own being. It feeds on the contrast, a parasite of pure physics.

And it moves.

Its movement is the only change. It is an achingly slow, deliberate patrol. It glides, its base hovering a millimeter above the perforated floor, never touching, never creating friction, never disturbing the flow of absolute cold from the vents. It drifts through the lanes between the harmonic spars, its path a complex, repeating spirograph that will, over the course of ten billion years, visit every point on the floor.

Its purpose is to tend the garden.

The spars are perfect. They are frozen, finished things. They do not decay. But the concept of "perfection" here is an active state, not a passive one. It must be maintained. It must be verified.

The Attendant will drift to a stop before one of the crystalline blades. It will stand before the frozen fractal of sound for a period that can last centuries. It is not idle; it is listening. It does not have ears. Its entire form is a sensory organ, designed to feel the tidal stress of the chamber's standing wave. It is listening to the spar, feeling its internal resonance, ensuring that it is still vibrating in perfect sympathy with the primeval note of the chamber.

If a spar—through some quantum fluke, some billion-year resonant drift—were to deviate from its ordained harmonic by a single, impossible vibration, the Attendant would act. It would raise one of its arms, a flowing shape of acoustic absence. It would not touch the spar. To touch is to transfer energy, to contaminate. Instead, it would project a focused filament of its own null-field, enshrouding the flawed crystal.

Inside this pocket of targeted silence, divorced from the sustaining roar, the spar's structure would lose its integrity. The physical law holding it together would be locally repealed. For an instant, the monomolecular edges would blur. The perfect facets would soften. The spar would unravel back into a puff of incoherent, exotic dust, which would be instantly drawn down into the vents in the floor, consumed by the system. The Attendant would then remain, projecting its null-field into the empty space, holding a perfect silence where the spar once stood, until the great sound of the chamber once again coaxed a new, perfect crystal into being. Then, the Attendant would resume its silent, endless patrol.

It is a gardener whose tools are silence and time. It is a curator of a museum where the exhibits can never be seen and must never change. It is alive, because it acts. It has a purpose, given to it by the very machine that is its cradle and its sustenance. It is a lonely, necessary thought-form produced by a factory of things. It is the solitary consciousness in a symphony of stasis, its existence a quiet, continuous act of weeding the garden of frozen music.
