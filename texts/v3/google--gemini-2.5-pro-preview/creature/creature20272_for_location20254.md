What lives here is an answer. It is the logical conclusion to the question posed by the landscape. It is not an intruder or a conqueror, but a precipitate, crystallized out of the thick, soupy possibility of the air.

You do not see them at first. You see only their effect. Amidst the slow, inexorable existence of the grazers and the patient uncurling of ferns, there is a flicker of intention. A pattern. You might notice a series of pools at the base of a Filter Stack, where the grey nutrient slurry has been sculpted into shallow, terraced channels. The work is too precise for the shovel-mouthed grazers, too deliberate for erosion. It is a form of irrigation, guiding the richest paste towards specific clusters of glowing moss or the roots of a particular bladder-leaved sapling.

Then, you feel it. A change in the omnipresent hum. On the edge of your hearing, a specific Stack’s thrumming deepens, developing a subtle, discordant rattle, like a bearing beginning to fail. And in response, something moves.

It detaches itself from the trunk of a glossy-leaved tree, and you realize it has been there the whole time. It seemed to be a patch of weeping lichen or a trick of the steaming light. It is tall and impossibly thin, a being of long, slow curves. Its form is humanoid only in its verticality; it has two legs and two arms, but they are jointed like an insect’s, folding and unfolding with an unnerving, multi-hinged grace. Its skin is the colour of jade and mother-of-pearl, semi-translucent, and you can see the faint, rhythmic pulse of viscous fluids moving through its limbs. It has no hair, no visible ears, no mouth in the way you would understand it. Its head is a smooth, teardrop-shaped ovoid, tipped with a sensitive-looking crest of fine, dark filaments that tremble constantly, tasting the thick air. Where a face would be, there is only a single, polished lens of obsidian-like chitin, a vast, unreadable eye that seems to drink the jaundiced light without reflection.

This is a Chanter.

It moves with a gait that is both a walk and a swim, its limbs stroking through the dense atmosphere. It leaves no footprints on the humus. Its long, three-toed feet barely seem to touch the ground as it glides towards the dissonant Stack. It is not in a hurry. Nothing here is. The problem is a matter of centuries, and it will be addressed in the next hour.

The Chanter arrives at the base of the living chimney. The great pores on the Stack’s surface, some as wide as a person’s chest, are dilating and contracting, breathing in the landscape. One, however, is fluttering weakly. A crystalline accretion, a deposit of some unwanted mineral the Stack could not process, has begun to form around its rim, choking it.

The Chanter raises its arms. From its forearms, two more appendages telescope out, thin and delicate as a surgeon’s probes, ending in pads of a soft, porous white material. It places its head against the Stack’s iridescent trunk, the black lens pressing flat. It is not looking; it is listening. Or feeling. Its whole body is an instrument of resonance, and it aligns itself to the deep hum of the Stack, finding the precise frequency of the flaw. And then, it begins to sing.

It is not a song you hear with your ears. It has no mouth to sing. The sound comes from its body, a focused, sympathetic vibration that travels through its feet, up its legs, into its torso, and out through the pads on its extended limbs, which it now presses gently against the choked pore. It is a focused counter-frequency, a specific harmonic that targets the crystalline structure of the blockage. The Chanter’s whole being is dedicated to this single, complex note. Its translucent skin shimmers, the fluids within swirling faster. The filaments on its crest vibrate violently.

For a long time, nothing happens. Then, a fine crack appears in the mineral accretion. The Chanter adjusts its pitch, a subtle shift you feel in your bones as a change in pressure. More cracks spiderweb across the blockage. With excruciating slowness, the unwanted crystal begins to dissolve, not breaking apart, but un-becoming, turning into a fine, sandy powder that is harmlessly absorbed by the Stack's filtration membrane.

Finally, the pore shudders and opens fully, resuming its steady, rhythmic pulse. The Stack’s discordant rattle smooths out, rejoining the universal hum of the plateau. The machine is repaired.

The Chanter detaches itself. It stands for a moment, its crest filaments settling. It seems diminished, its internal glow slightly dimmer. It has expended a great deal of energy. It glides over to one of the channels it has carved and lowers its head. A proboscis, fine as a needle and iridescent as a hummingbird’s throat, uncoils from beneath its chin and dips into the thickest, most nutrient-rich part of the slurry. It drinks, replenishing itself directly from the engine it services.

You realize, then, that this is the intelligence of the place. It is not a mind. It is a function. The Chanters are not sentient in a way you can comprehend; they are living, mobile antibodies for the planetary superorganism. They are born from the Stacks themselves, gestating as opalescent eggs within sheltered internal chambers, nourished by filtered geothermal vapor until they are ready to emerge. They are instinct-driven, compelled by the dissonance of an imperfect system. Their 'culture' is the arrangement of slurry channels. Their 'art' is the perfect, resonant harmony of a healthy Stack. Their 'purpose' is the maintenance of the great, mindless, geologic industry that is life on this plateau.

The feeling of being watched is the constant, passive sensory sweep of the Chanters. They are not watching *you*. They are listening to the world’s quiet machinery. You are a biological anomaly, a pocket of silence where there should be a familiar vibration, but you are not a fault in the engine. You are not a choked pore or a failing harmonic. And so, for now, you are beneath notice. Invisible to the gardeners of this impossible jungle, who live only to hear the song of their world, and to sing it back into tune.
