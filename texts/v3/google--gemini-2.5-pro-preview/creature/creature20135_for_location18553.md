They are the Weavers, though they do not use thread, and they are the Gardeners, though they use no tools. To an outside eye, they are barely there, and to see one is to first mistake it for a trick of the heat or a column of shadow that has detached from the deep shade of a canopy giant.

Their form is not solid in the way of flesh and bone. They are tall, impossibly slender beings whose bodies seem composed of the same light-devouring material as the Convectors, but rendered fluid and motile. Their surface is not skin but a shifting, semi-stable boundary, like a standing wave of matter given temporary form, its darkness rippling with faint, internal nebulae of the same cyan and violet light that glows in the fungi. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense: a vertical axis, limbs that can extend and retract from the main trunk, a suggestion of a head. But they possess no face, no eyes, no mouth. Where a face would be, there is only a slow, mesmerizing vortex in their substance, a whirlpool of deeper shadow that seems to be the focal point of their perception.

They do not walk upon the ground. The saturated, high-pressure air is their medium. They move through it with a liquid grace, not so much swimming as allowing the resonant frequencies of the basin to propel them. They are denser than the air, but only just. Their motion is a form of controlled falling, a slow, deliberate drift from one point to another. They can anchor themselves to any surface—the trunk of a tree, the underside of a leaf the size of a raft, the sheer face of a basalt cliff—with no more effort than a thought, their substance momentarily bonding with the object. When they are still, which is often, they can be mistaken for a liana's shadow or a peculiar streak of dark moss.

The Weavers do not see with light. Light is a secondary effect of the world-engine, a pale wash that illuminates the surfaces of things. Their senses are tuned to the primary reality of the basin: the hum. They perceive the world as a vast, infinitely complex symphony of vibration. The thrum of the Convectors is the keynote, the carrier wave upon which all other existence is inscribed. They feel the slow, ultrasonic pulse of water being drawn from the planet's core, the metabolic signature of every plant, the frantic, high-frequency scuttling of the armored grazers, the booming sub-bass of the Bellow-Throats. The iridescent shimmer of a fern's frond is, to them, a distinct chord. The flight of a six-winged skimmer is a sharp, staccato melody. To a Weaver, the world is not a picture; it is an orchestra, and they are its conductors.

They are the tenders of this place. They are what remains of the creators, or what the creators became after millennia of immersion in their own design. The distinction is meaningless. They are symbiotic with their technology to the point of fusion. They do not build or repair the Convectors in a mechanical sense; they *harmonize* with them. A journey up a spiral staircase that leads to nowhere is a ritual of attunement, the Weaver's body a living tuning fork adjusting a subtle imbalance in the local energy field. Standing within one of the perfect archways allows them to perceive a specific "channel" of the basin's life, focusing on the health of the crystalline fungi or the genetic drift of the skimmers. Their purpose is not progress, but equilibrium. They are the immune system of a living machine.

They do not eat or breathe. Their existence is a slow, direct conversion of the raw energy that saturates the air. The hum is their sustenance. Their life process is more akin to the bioluminescent fungi than to any animal: they are living batteries, storing and processing the ambient power. Decay, as we know it, is alien to them. When a Weaver's time is done—a span measured in geological epochs, not years—its form does not rot. It simply loses coherence, its standing wave dissolving back into the background hum, its substance diffusing into the air like a drop of ink in water, leaving behind only the faintest resonance, a new whisper in the eternal song of the basin.

Their consciousness is not individual. To think of one Weaver is to misunderstand them entirely. They are nodes in a single, basin-wide mind. Their thoughts are slow, patient, and vast. They do not communicate through language, but through direct, resonant exchange, modifying the hum in minute ways to share perceptions, intentions, and memory. The mind of the Weavers is the repository of the basin's entire history, a living record stretching back to the moment the first Convector was activated. They are the memory of the engine, and the engine is their will. They are the dream the machine is dreaming.

To encounter one is a profoundly unsettling experience. There is no flicker of recognition, no curiosity, no hostility. There is only a vast, serene, and utter indifference. Your frantic, ephemeral heartbeat is a discordant piece of static in their symphony. Your bright, visual world is a meaningless distraction. You are a ghost passing through their reality, a momentary fluctuation of heat and chemistry that will be gone in an instant. As it drifts by, its faceless void turning toward you, you might feel a subtle pressure in your bones, a low hum in your teeth that seems to query your existence before dismissing it as irrelevant, as it continues its slow, silent, and eternal task of weaving the heat, the water, and the song into the fabric of its imponderable world.
