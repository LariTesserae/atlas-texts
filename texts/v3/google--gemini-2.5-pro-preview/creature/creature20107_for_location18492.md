They are The Gardeners. This is not a title they chose, but a definition of their being. They are as much a part of the great living structure as the armored beetles and the pulsating light-veins. To see one is to see an expression of the place itself, given form and slow, deliberate purpose.

They do not seem to have a fixed shape, but rather a set of common characteristics that shift with age and function. A young Gardener is pliant, slender, and pale, their skin almost translucent, like the flesh of a cave-dwelling fish or the blind worms of the compost heaps. They move with a liquid grace, their limbs long and their joints seemingly double, allowing them to weave through the dense lianas and machinery without a sound. Their skin is perpetually slick with the same condensate that coats the leaves and wood, and they seem to absorb moisture and nutrients directly from the heavy air. They are the new growth, the feelers, the scouts of the system.

As a Gardener ages, they become more still, more rooted. Their skin darkens and thickens, taking on the texture of bark, fissured with lines that map a life of placid service. Mosses and tiny, harmless fungi may find purchase in the crevices of their elbows and the hollow of their throat, which they tolerate as a mark of seniority. Their very posture changes; they stand for long periods, sometimes for days, in one place, their feet seeming to meld with the living floor. In this state, they are not idle. They have become sensors. Through the soles of their feet, they feel the thrum of the Digesters, the slow, hydraulic pressure of water moving through the deep roots, the subtle tremors of a branch under stress a mile away. Their bodies are living instruments, calibrated to the cathedral's vital signs.

Their faces are serene and vaguely incomplete by human standards. They have no visible ears, only smooth hollows at the sides of their heads; the constant, layered hum of the basin is a language they feel in their bones, not one they need to hear with specialized organs. Their mouths are simple, small slits which they rarely use, as they take most of their nourishment from the air and from a nutrient paste they occasionally press to their lips—a refined product of the Digesters. Their most prominent feature is their eyes. They are large, perfectly round, and the color of dark, polished obsidian, holding no visible pupil or sclera. They do not glitter. They absorb light, reflecting nothing. They are windows not into a soul, but into a deep, patient, and singular focus.

They are the conscious mind of this place, the ganglia connecting the will of the great plant to the function of the machine. They do not command the automatons with voice or remote control. Instead, they commune. A Gardener will place a long-fingered hand upon the ceramic casing of a sorting machine, its head bowed. For a long moment, they are still. Then, the automaton's clanking rhythm will subtly shift, its movements becoming more efficient, adjusting to a new density of fibrous pulp it has been tasked to sort. The Gardener has not given an order; it has shared an understanding, aligning the machine’s purpose with the system's immediate need.

They are the agents of the cycle. You see them performing their unhurried duties everywhere. One, perched high on a swaying walkway, uses a bronze-and-ceramic sickle to prune a specific frond, which it then drops with uncanny precision into a collection chute below. Another stands before a bank of phosphorescent fungi, gently stroking their caps, not petting them, but encouraging a brighter or dimmer glow to suit the needs of the workspace. A third wades knee-deep into a cistern of warm, filtered water, letting it run through its fingers, tasting it for mineral balance with a tongue that flicks out like an amphibian's. Their work is a slow, endless dance of maintenance, a form of prayer enacted through physical participation in the divine, self-consuming machine.

Life and death for them are seamless parts of the process. They are not born of a womb, but of the basin itself. In a specialized, humid chamber, the great plant produces large, leathery pods. Fed by the richest nutrient slurry, these pods swell over the course of a cycle until they open like a fleshy flower, revealing a new Gardener, pale and slick, fully formed and already imprinted with the deep hum of its home.

And when a Gardener's long life ends, when its bark-skin has grown too thick and its movements too slow, it does not receive a burial. It performs its final duty. It walks, with placid finality, to the great, grinding maw of a Digester. It offers itself back to the system it served. Its body is broken down, its biological matter rendered into heat, steam, and the same nutrient slurry that will power the automatons and one day feed the pod that grows its successor. There is no ceremony, for the act itself is the ceremony. It is not an end, but a transference. They are the city that eats itself to live, and the Gardeners are its most willing meal, the perfect, patient inhabitants for a place that has only one thought: to continue.
