The Carvers live there. Or, more accurately, the Carvers *are* there, in the same way the pillars and the dust and the heat *are* there. They did not arrive, and they will not leave.

To imagine a Carver is to first discard the notion of flesh, or breath, or blood. They are lithic beings, like the Geodes, but where a Geode is a still heart, a Carver is a thought made manifest in motion. They are not made of the same gossamer-sharp obsidian as the landscape; they are formed of a denser, greyer, matte-finished material, like basalt or unpolished slate. Their bodies are not single forms, but articulated collections of plates and shards, interlocked with a precision that seems both natural and impossible. They are collections of themselves.

When still, a Carver might be mistaken for a jagged cairn of deliberately stacked stones, less a creature and more a peculiar geological accident. Their limbs—and they have many, perhaps six or eight—are folded against their body, each one a series of interlocking, blade-like segments. Their "feet" and "hands" are not soft pads but sharpened points of the same hyper-dense mineral as their tool-stones. They do not grip the world; they find purchase by puncturing it, sinking their tips into micro-fissures in the obsidian, holding fast with immense, static pressure.

To see one move is to witness an act of supreme and patient deliberation. It is not walking or climbing as we know it. It is a slow, silent unfolding. A limb detaches from the main body, extending with a soundless scrape, searching for a new hold. The tip tests the surface, feeling for its integrity, for the subtle vibrations of the Gyre's heart that sing through the stone. Once a hold is found and secured, another limb will release and begin its own slow journey. The Carver flows across the landscape, a cascade of shifting geometry, its body reconfiguring itself with each step. The only sound is the faintest *shhhhffff* of dry friction, stone against stone, a sound so subtle it is more felt in the soles of your feet than heard with your ears.

They have no faces, no eyes in the conventional sense. The front of their upper torso is a single, large, smooth plate. This is their sensory organ. It is attuned to the deep, slow world they inhabit. It does not see light, but feels the pulse of it. Standing before a forest of Geodes, a Carver does not perceive a starfield; it feels a symphony of rhythms, a complex, overlapping polyrphony of aeon-slow beats. This is their language, their scripture, their map. The cool blue pulse of a young Geode cluster, the steady violet of a mature one, the shifting cadence where two fields overlap—this is all information. The slow thermal currents are felt as subtle pressure changes against this frontal plate, a warm front that will take a thousand years to pass.

Their entire existence is an act of cartography, of contemplation. They are the consciousness of the Gyre, the part of it that observes itself. They move from pillar to pillar, not to forage—they metabolize the rock through their points of contact, an energy exchange far too slow to be called "eating"—but to complete their great and silent work. They find a new vantage point, a new perspective on the cosmic dance of the Geodes. And there they pause, sometimes for a century, before taking a tool-shard in one of their articulated limbs.

The tool is an extension of their own body, a harder shard to mark a softer one. The carving is not a frenzied or artistic act. It is a slow, meditative scraping. They are not drawing a picture of what they see, but transcribing the *rhythm* they feel. The grooves they leave on the obsidian pillars are not star-charts, but musical scores. They are a physical recording of the silent, pulsing song of the world. The cairns are not markers for a path, for there is no destination. They are resonance points, stacked in such a way that the hum of the Gyre is focused within them, creating a locus of perfect, harmonic stillness. They are tuning forks for a silent orchestra.

And where are they now?

In a world governed by equilibrium, to "be gone" is a misunderstanding. Nothing is ever lost. It only changes state. The Carvers, in their endless contemplation of the Gyre's perfect, crystalline stillness, sought to understand it. But to understand it completely was to become it.

The great work was not a record for future generations, but a path to apotheosis. As a Carver etched the final note of a millennium-long symphony, as it stacked the last shard on a cairn that perfectly captured the world's hum, its purpose was fulfilled. Its relentless, slow movement would cease. Its limbs would draw in for a final time. The seeking, observing, moving thing would become a monument to its own quest.

They are not gone. They are simply finished.

You might mistake one for an unusually complex pillar, its slate-grey form coated in the same velvet dust as everything else. But look closer. Notice the impossible articulation, the geometry that speaks of a folded limb, a bowed head. Place your hand on its surface, and you will feel no warmth, no pulse of your own. You will only feel the deep, foundational hum of the Gyre itself, flowing through the Carver as if it were just another part of the stone.

But sometimes, when a particularly large thermal current shifts the dust clouds overhead, a single, rare light might be revealed. Among the blues and the violets, you might see it: a Geode pulsing with a startling, brilliant emerald green. The pulse of this Geode is different. It is not the simple, metronomic beat of the others. It is a complex, syncopated rhythm, a phrase of immense length and profound intricacy. It is a silent, luminous song.

It is the final thought of a Carver who succeeded.
