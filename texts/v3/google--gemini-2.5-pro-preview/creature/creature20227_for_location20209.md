The people who live here are called the Stillborn, though they themselves do not use the term. It is an old, academic name for them, coined by off-world surveyors who saw a people living on land that was never truly born. The inhabitants simply call themselves the Basin-kin.

They have a distinct physicality, born of a life in motion. Their balance is preternatural. From toddlerhood, they learn to walk on shifting, slick, and uneven surfaces. They possess an instinct they call ‘ground-sense’—a subliminal awareness of the tremors in the soles of their feet, the subtle groans of a stressed girder, the faint change in air pressure that signals a new geological upwelling nearby. They walk with a quiet, deliberate gait, their weight always centered, ready to shift. To watch them move along a swaying sky-bridge in a gust of steam is to watch a species of primate that has evolved for a canopy of steel and cable rather than wood and vine.

Their bodies are compact and dense with muscle, particularly in their legs and core. They are not a tall people. A low center of gravity is an advantage here. Their skin has a pale, almost luminous quality, a result of the filtered, indirect light, but their faces and hands are often chapped and ruddy from the constant, cold damp. Their eyes, accustomed to the gloom and the sudden, sharp glares of reflected light, are quick to notice movement and detail. They are rarely startled by loud noises, which are few, but a sudden silence or a strange vibration will cause a whole street to pause as one, heads cocked, listening to the city’s deep grammar.

Their clothing is a study in practical layering. The outer layer is almost always a slick, water-shedding material, often in dark greens, greys, and blacks to hide the inevitable smudges of mineral dust and grime. This material is not plastic but a treated fiber woven from a species of hardy pipe-vine, which, when processed, becomes incredibly durable and resistant to both moisture and the sharp edges of new crystal growth. Beneath this, they wear thick, fleecy layers woven from the cultivated wall-moss, which has astonishing insulating properties. Their boots are their most important possession. The soles are soft and studded with articulated nodules of a synthetic compound that mimics the gripping ability of a mineral-grazer's claws, allowing them to find purchase on wet steel or glassy new-formed stone with equal confidence.

Society is stratified by altitude.

At the top, in the highest, newest levels of the city—the "Pinnacles" or the "Gimbals"—live the Technocrats and Engineers. They are the ones who design the systems that keep the city from tearing itself apart. They are a people obsessed with precision, with the minute calibrations of the load-bearing platforms and the chemical composition of the geocretic sludge. They live in minimalist apartments where the air is filtered and warmed, and the windows are made of smart glass that tints automatically against the glare. They rarely descend. To them, the city below is a complex, beautiful, and dangerous machine to be managed. They eat hydroponic vegetables and protein vats, and they consider the wild vegetation and fauna of the lower levels to be contaminants. Their children are taught balance on moving platforms in sterile gymnasiums, and their ground-sense, while technically excellent, is an analytical skill, not a primal instinct. They are the city's anxious mind.

In the sprawling, tangled middle strata live the majority of the Basin-kin. These are the traders, the artisans, the pipe-fitters, the moss-farmers, the everyday citizens. Their world is the web of sky-bridges, the dense warren of interconnected habitats, the steamy plazas that open up between towering structures. Community is their foundation. Since your neighbor's wall is often your own, and their stability is your stability, cooperation is not a choice but a law of physics. They are masters of provisionality. Their homes are modular, with walls that can be moved and reconfigured as the building they inhabit slowly settles or lists. Furniture is lightweight and often built to be hung from walls or ceilings. The sound of their lives is the drip of condensation into collection basins, the chatter of neighbors across a narrow alley fifty stories up, and the melodic chirping of the iridescent birds, whom they encourage to nest in their eaves, as their absence is a sign of bad air or structural instability. They are the city's bustling, adaptable heart.

At the bottom, in the "Deep," live the Grounders or the Subsiders. They inhabit the oldest, partially swallowed structures, their homes occupying a liminal space between architecture and geology. These are the poorest, the most marginalized, but also the most resilient of the Basin-kin. Their doorways might be half-blocked by a crystalline bloom; their floors are often the raw, hardened sludge itself, still warm to the touch. They live by scavenging the Subsidence Zones, mining the new crystal growths for valuable rare-earth elements, and hunting the larger, stranger fauna of the deeps. Their ground-sense is a thing of profound, almost mystical depth. They can read the land's intention, smelling a shift days before it happens, hearing the specific frequency of a forming crystal lattice. They have a symbiotic relationship with the environment; they know which mosses are edible, which mineral salts are medicinal, and how to navigate the ever-changing labyrinth of the lowest levels by the light of phosphorescent fungi that grow in the dark. They are quiet, watchful, and possess a "gallows humor" about a home that might literally consume them overnight. To the people of the Mids and Pinnacles, they are unsettling, part-geology themselves. They are the city's deep, dreaming soul.

Across all strata, there is a shared psychology. They are not ambitious in the way of people from stable lands; they do not build for eternity because they know there is no such thing. They build for the decade, for the year, for the next shift. Their art is not monumental sculpture but the intricate knot-work on a cable sleeve, the delicate carving on a boot-sole, the complex, shifting melodies they play on stringed instruments whose tones are said to harmonize with the city's low hum.

Their memory is collective and fluid. An elder giving directions will not refer to a street name, but to "the walkway by the Great Steam Vent that smells of copper, past the fallen pylon that the red-leaf vines have claimed." Places are defined by their current state, not a fixed identity.

To an outsider, they might seem melancholic or subdued. They rarely shout. Their faces in repose are placid, their eyes ever-moving. But this is not sadness. It is attention. It is the quiet hum of a million souls, each one a living seismograph, perpetually listening to the slow, turbulent song of their unfinished world, holding their breath together, waiting for the ground to make its next move.
