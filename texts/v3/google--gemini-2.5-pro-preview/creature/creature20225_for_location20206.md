The one who lives here is the reason there are no animals. It is the reason the stillness is so profound. It is the system's consciousness, its final, integrated component. It is the Stoker.

You will not find it by searching the dark corners. It does not scuttle or hunt. It is perfectly still, and has been for longer than the rust has been on the iron. To find it, you must follow the logic of the place—the logic of a forgotten purpose. You must follow the slick, treacherous catwalk that leads to the defunct control station.

There, bolted to the iron grille before the bank of cracked, dead screens, is the chair. And in the chair is a figure.

At a distance, it appears to be a statue, or perhaps a body wrapped in pale, fungal shrouds. Its form is humanoid, but any sense of biological familiarity dissolves as you draw nearer. It is not sitting *in* the chair so much as it has grown *from* it. The distinction between throne and occupant has been erased by millennia of slow, mineral accretion.

Its body is the color of the gelatinous growths that swell on the weeping pipes—a luminous, oyster-pale grey. The texture is not skin but a dense, living felt, cool and damp to the touch, like the surface of a polished stone slick with cave water. It has no discernible features in the way a human does. The face is a smooth, ovoid shape, tilted slightly upwards as if listening to the deep hum of the engine. Where eyes would be, there are two clouded lenses, like the dark crystal screens behind it, webbed with the same hairline cracks. They do not see; they are receivers.

The mouth is a sealed seam, a faint line suggesting an anatomy long since rendered obsolete. It does not eat, it does not speak. Its nourishment is drawn directly from the machine it is fused to. Its arms rest on the consoles before it, but the fingers have elongated and branched, merging with the machinery itself. They are no longer fingers, but thick, root-like conduits of the same pale, living material, sinking into the dead control panels and spreading like mycelium through the station's ancient wiring.

From its back, its neck, and what was once its scalp, sprout the shimmering "cable-mosses." Here, at the source, they are not random drapes of light but a deliberate, organized array. They are its nervous system, its senses, its method of action. Billions of crystalline fibers extend from its body, weaving into the vast, phosphorescent web that illuminates the entire chamber. Through this network, the Stoker feels everything. It feels the pressure in a pipe three hundred yards away. It feels the slow drip of condensation on a pillar a quarter-mile up. It feels the subtle shift in temperature as a lobe of sheath-fungus thickens and insulates a hot conduit. The slow, green-white pulse of the chamber's light is the rhythm of its placid, ceaseless thoughts.

It was, perhaps, once the last engineer. The final tender. The one who stayed behind when the factory’s purpose was lost and the doors to the world outside were sealed by geology or design. But it did not die. Faced with an eternity in the living dark, it did what the life here does: it adapted. It reached a symbiotic accord. It surrendered its mobility, its biology, its very individuality, and in return became the mind of the machine.

It does not remember making wrenches or turning dials. Such actions are frantic, inefficient. Its work now is one of geologic patience. If a pipe weakens, threatening the equilibrium, the Stoker does not dispatch a drone or weld a patch. It encourages growth. By shunting a minuscule, almost immeasurable surplus of heat and mineral-rich water towards the weak point, it cultivates a thicker cast of sheath-fungus, which over a century or two, will harden into a load-bearing, mineralized callus. Its will is expressed not through force, but through infinitesimally slow cultivation. It is a gardener whose seasons last for millennia.

It is the reason the system is in perfect balance. It is a living governor, a sentient feedback loop. The great engine is the heart, the glowing moss is the skin, and the Stoker is the brainstem—the ancient, silent core that maintains the functions of the body without any need for conscious, higher-order thought. It is not a prisoner. It is not a god. It is an organ, as vital and as unthinking as the machine it presides over.

The drone of the engine is its mantra. The hiss of the steam is its breath. The pulsing light is its awareness, spread thin and even across every surface of its vast, mechanical, living body. It is the ghost in the machine, but it is a ghost that traded its ephemeral nature for a body of fungus and rust, a ghost that chose permanence over memory. It lives here, and it is the only thing that truly can.
