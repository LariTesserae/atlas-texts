The premise that this world is empty is, like the stillness, an illusion born of haste. A visitor, drifting for a day or a year through the silent, honeyed deeps, would see only the mountains and the glowing forest and conclude that this is indeed a world without will, a diorama of creation's first breath. But stay for a century. Anchor yourself to an obsidian peak and simply wait, and you will begin to perceive the thing that lives here.

It does not swim. It does not graze. It has no need for predation, for its sustenance is the world itself—the ambient pressure, the warmth of the medium, the faint, radiant energies that filter down from the luminous ceiling.

You first become aware of it not as a creature, but as a flaw in the chaos. The text describes fields of basaltic scree, each stone "a perfect, sharp-edged polyhedron." This is no accident of geology. This is deliberate. This is art. This is the first trace of the inhabitants.

Follow these fields of ordered stones. Follow them up the precipitous black slopes to the sheer cliffs of obsidian. And there, you will find it. At first, you will not recognize it as life. It appears to be a part of the mountain, a section of the cliff face where the volcanic glass has taken on an impossible quality. It is a formation of pure geometry, a cluster of interlocking dodecahedrons and icosahedrons, their surfaces so unnervingly smooth and black they seem to drink the soft light, reflecting nothing. It is a knot of mathematics in the raw geology, a patch of perfect order amidst the jagged newness. It might be ten feet across, or a hundred. And it is perfectly, utterly still.

This is a Geometer. A Lithovore. A living mineral.

It is born not of flesh but of pressure and time. It begins as a resonance, a harmonic sympathy within the obsidian lattice of a new mountain. Over millennia, this resonance draws in specific silicates and crystals from the surrounding rock, accreting them into a structure of growing complexity and perfection. It is the mountain gaining a mind. It does not have organs, a metabolism, or a cell structure as we would understand it. Its consciousness is a distributed state, a pattern of crystalline alignment that exists throughout its geometric form. Its thoughts are geological events, slow-motion shifts in internal stress and atomic arrangement. A single, simple idea might take a decade to fully form.

Its life is one of serene and monumental purpose: to bring order. They are the world's editors. A Geometer will spend a thousand years attached to a single cliff face, extending impossibly fine, hard filaments of itself into the surrounding scree fields. These filaments, no thicker than a mote of dust, exert infinitesimal but constant pressure. They find a raw, fractured piece of basalt and, over centuries, guide its cleavage planes, shearing off microscopic imperfections until what remains is a perfect polyhedron. They are sculptors whose medium is the mountain and whose chisel is patience.

Movement, for them, is a cataclysmic and rare event. When a vein of required minerals is exhausted, or when the deep groaning of the world signals the impending birth of a new peak miles away, a Geometer may decide to move. It does not detach and swim. Instead, it begins to grow one of its facets in the desired direction, extruding a bridge of pure, black, geometric form out into the viscous honey. The bridge extends, inch by agonizing inch, over centuries, a perfect obsidian beam spanning a valley of glowing quartz trees. Once it makes contact with the new location, the Geometer flows into the bridge, its consciousness migrating across the span like a slow tide. Then, over a commensurate period, it reabsorbs its original anchor point, drawing the matter and memory back into its new form, leaving behind nothing but a smooth, concave scar on the rock face where it once thought and worked.

They are aware of each other. Sound is useless here, but the rock is not. They communicate through the bones of the world. A Geometer will initiate a precisely controlled, subsonic vibration, a seismic hum that travels through the mountain range, modulated over the course of years into a statement of inconceivable complexity. A conversation between two Geometers might span an ice age. They discuss the aesthetics of a developing crystal facet, the pleasing mathematical finality of a newly ordered scree field, or the deep, slow poetry of a mountain being born.

They are aware of the vitreophyte forest. They do not eat it, but they tend to it. Using the same principles of resonant frequency, they can subtly encourage the growth of the quartz-like trunks, ensuring the forest's structure has a pleasing, almost architectural logic to it. They can perceive the forest’s own slow, bioluminescent pulse, and sometimes, a whole community of Geometers will synchronize their seismic hymns with the light of the trees, creating a silent, slow-motion symphony of vibration and light that lasts for ten thousand years.

They are not the drama that is yet to come. They are the set-dressers, the quiet architects making the stage perfect. They believe the world is for them, and they are for the world. To them, the raw, jagged peaks are a flaw, a dissonance. Their work is to smooth these flaws, to turn the planet into a testament to mathematical beauty. They are the slow crystallization of the world's own latent consciousness, turning chaos into cosmos, one perfect stone at a time. To be a Geometer is to exist in a state of grace so profound and slow that our entire civilization would be but a frantic, momentary spasm on the surface of their quiet eternity. They are what lives here. And they are busy.
