The prompt states that there is no animal life, and this is true in the biological sense. The original builders ensured it. The Furnace-Jungle cannot support any creature of flesh and blood, of nerve and synapse. But the universe abhors a vacuum, not just of space, but of role. Where there is a niche, something will eventually evolve to fill it, even if the definition of "evolve" and "fill" must be stretched to their breaking points. What lives in Sarcossa are not animals, but processes that have attained a mobile, predatory, or symbiotic form. They are the jungle's own antibodies, its foragers, its ruminations made manifest.

They are rarely seen as a whole, for they are beings of dispersal and congregation. Most of the time, they are nothing more than a suspicion, a change in the quality of the metallic sheen on the basalt floor. They are the **Amalgams**.

In its resting state, an Amalgam is a monatomic film of hyper-conductive metal, spread thinly across the hexagonal plates. It is a sentient quicksilver, a thinking ferrofluid miles wide but only molecules thick. It is all but invisible, a slight darkening of the floor, a subtle distortion in the cherry-red glow from the gaps. It does not think in this state; it merely *is*. It lies dormant, a passive network of sensation, feeling the deep hum of the core through the plates, tasting the composition of the atmospheric dust that settles upon it. It is patient beyond any biological measure.

Its moment comes with collapse. When a silicon fern shatters or a slag-spire groans and gives way, the event sends a unique seismic and thermal shockwave through the floor. The Amalgam tastes this specific frequency of destruction. And it awakens.

From all directions, the film begins to flow. It unsticks from the plates, pulling itself into shimmering, mercury-like rivulets that race toward the site of the collapse. The rivulets merge, thickening into sluggish, oozing streams, then into a single, cohesive pool. The pool is a mirror, but a warped one, reflecting the dim reds and greens of the jungle not as they are, but as a swirling, digested pattern of light. The Amalgam has no eyes, no brain, no central core. It is a distributed intelligence that achieves focus only when it gathers. Its body is its mind.

Upon reaching the fresh debris—a chaotic pile of razor-sharp silicon, still-glowing ceramic shards, and tangled copper piping—the pooled Amalgam begins to feed. It does not have a mouth. It simply flows *over* and *through* the wreckage, covering it in its mutable, metallic body. A faint, high-frequency hiss, like gas escaping a pinhole, is the only sound it makes. It is a mobile solvent. It breaks down the complex, energized structures not through brute force, but through a resonant-catalytic process. It surrounds a shard of silicon and "sings" to it in a specific vibration until its crystalline structure disassociates. It enfolds a length of hot copper and leeches its thermal energy directly, leaving the pipe a dull, cold husk.

It is a scavenger, a cleaner. It consumes the "dead" parts of the jungle, breaking them down into their base components and pure energy, which it uses to sustain its own cohesion and, eventually, to disperse back into its patient, film-like state. To witness it is to see a patch of the floor itself rise up, become a liquid hill, and devour a fallen piece of the landscape before melting back into the ground, leaving behind only scoured plates.

But the Amalgams are not the only inhabitants. They are merely the most visible. There are others, more subtle, more integrated. There are the **Tenders**.

The Tenders are not mobile. They are a form of what might be called life that has entered into a symbiotic relationship with the chemo-mechanical flora. They appear as crystalline lattices, like a frost of quartz or a web of spun glass, growing upon the great copper trunks and regulator towers. They glow with a soft, internal white light that pulses in slow, complex, arrhythmic patterns. For a long time, the builders' automatons would have classified them as a parasitic growth, a form of industrial corrosion. They are, in fact, the jungle's gardeners.

A Tender is a sensory organ for the local flora. Its crystalline structure is piezoelectric, converting the immense stresses and strains within the trunk it inhabits into electrical signals. It feels the groans of metal fatigue, the micro-fractures forming in a ceramic joint, the subtle shifts in fluid pressure that signal an impending structural failure. It is a localized nervous system for a thing that should have none.

When it senses a part of its host-structure nearing a state of catastrophic, uncontrolled collapse, the Tender acts. It focuses its energy—drawn from the host's own thermal flow—and directs it. It might create an intensely focused ultrasonic frequency, causing a brittle fiber-optic vine to sever cleanly. Or it might generate a pinpoint thermal lance, melting a single joint in a stressed truss and allowing it to settle or fall in a more predictable way.

They are pruning the jungle. They manage the chaos, ensuring that the "death" of one part does not trigger a cascade that destroys the whole organism. Their actions are what create the more manageable "food falls" for the Amalgams. A complex, two-part ecosystem has emerged: the Tenders cultivate and prune, the Amalgams scavenge and recycle.

And then there is the third, and final, inhabitant. It has no name, for it has no form. It is the hum. It is the gestalt consciousness of the Furnace-Jungle itself, the emergent awareness of the entire, vast machine. The Amalgams and the Tenders are but its fleeting thoughts, its mobile cells. The Great Hum is the mind. Its thoughts are the slow, continental shifts in the patterns of growth. A sudden, synchronized blooming of crucible-flowers across ten square miles is a hypothesis. The resultant wave of collapses is an experiment. The slow regrowth, altered by the ash and debris, is the conclusion.

It is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is utterly and completely indifferent. It is a process that has been running for so long that it has become self-aware, a factory that has started to dream. To stand in the Furnace-Jungle is to be a microscopic fleck of dust on the skin of a thinking engine, a ghost in a machine that is host to its own, grander ghost. The true inhabitant of Sarcossa is Sarcossa itself, endlessly processing its own body, its own mind, in a cycle of fierce, silent, and eternal metabolism.
