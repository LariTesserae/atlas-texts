The people who live here are born of patience. They are as slender and deliberate as the world that grows around them.

Physically, they are gracile and elongated, a people of long limbs and startlingly articulate hands and feet. Generations of navigating vertical terrain—of balancing on suspended walkways and clinging to scaffolding—have shaped them into beings for whom a sheer cliff face is as navigable as a flat field. Their skin is pale, almost pellucid, like the inside of an oyster shell. It holds the faint, ambient amber light of the cavern, giving them a soft, internal luminescence. In some, this paleness is tinged with the faint gray-green of the mosses they cultivate, or the pearlescent sheen of mica. They are not creatures of blood-red vitality, but of a cool, mineral persistence.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. They are large and dark, the pupils wide and slow to contract, adapted to the perpetual, shadowless twilight. They don't glitter with quick intelligence, but hold a deep, still watchfulness, like the surface of the cavern's undisturbed pools. To look into their eyes is to feel the weight of a timescale you cannot comprehend. They blink slowly, infrequently, as if not wanting to miss the movement of a millimeter of growth over the span of an hour.

Their primary sense, however, is not sight but a fusion of hearing and touch. Their hearing is exquisitely tuned. They can distinguish the precise location and nature of a sound from miles away—not just the *chink-chink* of a fellow carver's chisel, but the specific resonance that tells them whether the tool is striking quartz, basalt, or a flawed crystal. They can hear the groan of rock under stress, the whisper-thin hiss of water finding a new fissure in the ceiling, the almost sub-audible hum of a large formation extruding from the cavern floor.

This hearing is married to a profound sense of touch. Their hands and bare feet, though calloused from stone and fiber rope, are incredibly sensitive. They read the world through their skin. A master carver will press their entire body against a wall, not just listening but *feeling* its internal structure, its lines of intention and hidden fractures. They can feel the subtle vibration of growth, the "health" of the stone. For them, the world is not silent or static; it is a symphony of deep, slow vibrations, a living entity that communicates in a language of pressure and resonance.

They are the geologists, the midwives, the shepherds of the stone. They call themselves nothing so grand; they are simply Carvers, or Shapers. Their relationship with their world is not one of conquest but of communion. They see themselves not as builders of a city, but as participants in its growth. A child does not learn to write; they learn to listen to the rock. The first tool they are given is not a chisel, but a resonator—a simple bronze cup they press to the stone to learn its songs.

Their psychology is one of immense, almost inhuman patience. The concept of "haste" is a form of madness to them. They think in generations. A carver may spend their entire life hollowing out a single dwelling, knowing their grandchildren will be the first to inhabit it. When they speak, their voices are low and melodic, careful not to disturb the cavern's prevailing quiet. They don't shout across chasms; they have a language of timed, percussive taps with small stone hammers against the rock, a kind of lithic morse code that can travel for miles through the solid medium.

Their culture is built on this foundation. Their art is not additive, but subtractive and suggestive. A masterpiece is not a statue carved from a block, but a selectively cleared rock face where the natural, emergent patterns of the crystals are revealed and celebrated. Their music is the music of the cavern itself: the rhythmic dripping of water into a cistern, the harmonics of resonating stone, the percussive counterpoint of their own patient work. Their histories are not written in books, but are etched into the city itself—a spiral ramp around a pillar tells the story of the pillar's growth-rate over centuries; the pattern of dwellings on a cliff face is a genealogical map of the families who carved them.

They eat what the world provides. The luminous moss is harvested and pressed into bland, nutrient-rich cakes. The tough lichens are boiled into a gelatinous, mineral-heavy broth. The pale crustaceans from the deep pools are a delicacy, their shells so thin they are eaten whole, providing a rare, subtle crunch. They are sustained by the stone, filtered through the sparse life it permits.

To see one is to see an extension of the place itself. An elder, her skin as pale and finely wrinkled as a sheet of ancient mica, might be found sitting utterly still on a narrow ledge, her eyes closed, one long-fingered hand resting on a slowly extruding basalt column. She is not sleeping or meditating in a way an outsider would understand. She is in conversation. For hours, she will sit, feeling the infinitesimal progress, the slow, tectonic heartbeat of her world, her own life a brief, warm note in the stone's endless, patient song. She is a part of the city, yes, but more than that, she is a sentient facet of the geology, as alive and unfinished as the world she inhabms.
