What lives in Ghyre is what does not need to be warm. It is what has forgotten the sun, or what was born from a lineage that never knew it. They are the city’s own children, its mobile thoughts, its nervous system made flesh.

They are called by no name, for they have no spoken language to name themselves. To an outsider, they might be the Lacrimals, for their relationship with the city's weeping sap. To a poet, they might be the Still-Folk. To themselves, they simply *are*, as the trunk is, as the drip is.

To see one is to almost miss it. Their form is a study in verticality, an echo of the pillar-trunks they inhabit. They are impossibly slender, with limbs so long and fine they seem incapable of bearing weight, yet they move with a slow, terrifying grace. Their joints are many; a leg might have three knees, an arm three elbows, allowing them to brace themselves within the tightest of architectural confines and contort their bodies to any angle. Their skin is not skin at all, but a smooth, seamless integument the color of wet obsidian, often filmed with the same perpetual condensation as the world around them. In the faint fungal glow, this surface shimmers with a faint, oily iridescence, shifting from deep indigo to the black-green of the canopy leaves. They are perfectly camouflaged.

They move with a patience that borders on geological. A single step can be a process of minutes: a long, four-fingered hand, tipped with small, needle-sharp points of black chitin, reaches out and tests the surface. It does not grasp, but listens. Then, a foot, identical to the hand, lifts with excruciating slowness and is placed with absolute silence. They are climbers born, equally at home on the rhizome floors, the curved buttresses, or clinging upside down to the great vaulted ceilings. Their movements are a fluid, silent ballet, a living extension of the city's own slow groans and shifts. A sudden, quick motion is so alien to their nature that it is tantamount to a scream.

They have faces, of a sort. A long, narrow head atop a supple neck. Where a mouth would be, there is only a smooth, lipless seam. They do not eat or speak. Where a nose would be, there is nothing. Their skin breathes for them, absorbing the thin, oxygen-rich moisture of the air directly into their sluggish circulatory system. Their blood is not blood; it is a viscous, clear fluid, a cryo-protectant gel that remains liquid just above freezing. They are as cold as the stone-like wood they touch.

Their eyes are their most striking and misleading feature. They are large, perfectly round, and completely black, like pools of ink. They possess no pupil, no iris, no sclera. They are not for seeing in the way a predator sees. They are for perceiving polarity and intensity, for reading the slow, tidal pulse of the bioluminescent fungi—the city’s ‘day’—and for navigating by the ghostly patterns of light that shift across the canopy far above.

Their primary sense is not sight, but touch, taken to an almost telepathic level. Through their delicate, chitin-tipped fingers and toes, they feel the city. They read its vibrations like a blind man reads braille. They can feel the distant, ponderous shifting of a pillar-trunk on the other side of the district. They can feel the vibration of a single drop of water as it strikes a liana causeway a hundred yards away. They feel the slow, methodical rasping of a Rime Crawler on a patch of frost-moss. This is their landscape: a symphony of resonant frequencies, a constant stream of information from the living heart of Ghyre.

This is also their language. They do not speak with vocal cords, but with the city itself. By tapping their hard fingertips in complex, rhythmic sequences on a trunk or a buttress, they send messages shuddering through the structure. A conversation between two of the Still-Folk might be a silent, percussive duet, felt by any other of their kind within a mile's radius. For more intimate communication, they use their own bodies. Patches of their skin, particularly along their limbs and narrow torsos, can flush with the same cold, blue-white bioluminescence as the fungi. By pulsing this light in subtle, shifting patterns—a silent, ghostly semaphore—they convey emotion, intent, and complex abstractions that their tremor-language cannot.

They are inextricably bound to the city’s metabolism. They do not hunt the Rime Crawlers; they do not cultivate the moss. They drink. The slow, viscous sap that weeps from the great trunks, freezing into amber tears near the base, is their only sustenance. They will press their smooth, mouthless faces to these wounds in the city’s flesh and, through osmotic pressure, draw the nutrient-rich fluid into their bodies. They are parasites, yes, but symbiotic ones.

In their oldest and wisest members, a final, profound connection is made. These elders find the newest parts of the city—the pale, slick, still-hardening shoots—and press the palms of their hands flat against them. They close their great black eyes and become utterly still, sometimes for what amounts to a week. Here, in direct contact with the most vital, information-dense part of the structure, they do not speak or listen. They merge. They receive the slow, biological imperatives of the city-organism itself: the coming pulse of growth that will reshape a district, the subtle shift in the condensation cycle, the location of a structural weakness that must be resorbed. They are not the city's architects; they are its sensory neurons, its consciousness made manifest. They are the custodians of a purpose they do not create, only serve. They are the quiet, cold, and patient soul of the Under-Canopy, and their 'life' is nothing more than a slow, waking dream dreamed by the city of Ghyre.
