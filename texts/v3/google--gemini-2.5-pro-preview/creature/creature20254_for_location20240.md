To believe anything *lives* here, in the conventional sense, is to make a category error. There are no inhabitants *of* this place. There are only emergent properties of its boundless, grinding process. They are the place itself, made ambulatory. They are the Geode-Kin.

At first, you do not see them. They are as much a part of the landscape as the sweating pipes and the shuddering iron walls. A shape that seems to be a junction of conduits and rusted plating detaches itself from a wall, unfolds, and begins to move. It is tall and impossibly thin, a being of articulated limbs that seem too long, too skeletal to support a body. But there is little body to support. Its form is a framework, a scaffolding of bone-like material that is not quite bone, interwoven with metallic sinew and lattices of dark, fibrous crystal. Condensation rivulets down its surfaces as it moves, tracing paths through patches of dull mineral patina and weeping rust-stains that are not accretions from the environment, but integral parts of its skin.

They have no faces. The head is a smooth, teardrop-shaped node, featureless save for a single, deep groove that bisects it vertically. This is not a mouth. It is a resonance chamber. They possess no eyes, for light is a fickle and untrustworthy informant here. Their entire being is an ear, an organ of profound and subtle vibration. Their feet, broad and padded with a tough, leathery substance, never truly leave the ground, but slide and sense, reading the thrum of the floor plates, the deep pulse of the Precipitators, the high-frequency chatter of the crystal fields. Their long, six-jointed fingers are tipped not with nails, but with resonant forks of differing lengths and materials—some of iron, some of a glassy obsidian-like substance, some of bone.

They are the tenders, the homeostatic regulators of the great machine. They do not command it; they listen to its voice. One will stand for days, perhaps weeks, pressed against the casing of a shuddering silo, its entire body gone still, its resonance chamber trembling faintly. It is not idle. It is engaged in a deep diagnostic, feeling the rhythms of the unseen internal hammers, the viscosity of the slurry in the pipes, the harmonic signature of the bearings. It can feel a change in frequency smaller than a thousandth of a hertz, the precursor to a failure that might not occur for another century. When it detects such an anomaly, it moves with a slow, geologic purpose.

Their work is not one of tools and repair manuals. It is a kind of industrial husbandry. They are gardeners in a metal forest. Another Geode-Kin might be seen scraping a poultice of specific, shimmering mud from a particular estuary and applying it to a weeping joint on a pump-regulator. The poultice is not a sealant; it is a culture, a carefully selected biome of lithophilic bacteria that will grow into the fissure, consuming the corrosion and excreting a mineral compound that reintegrates the failing metal, healing it over decades. They anoint the machinery. They feed it.

Communication between them is a facet of the overwhelming noise. An observer would never detect it. It is a language of percussion and resonance. One Geode-Kin will tap a sequence of intricate rhythms onto a vast pipe with its tuning-fork fingers, sending a message that travels for miles through the metal skeleton of the world. The reply comes not as a sound, but as a sympathetic vibration in the floor, a subtle shift in the hum of a distant engine, felt through the soles of the feet. Their conversations are slow, deliberate, and woven into the very fabric of the cacophony. A single sentence might take an hour to transmit, a complex thought an entire day.

They are born from the place. In the most quiescent, stable sections of the crystal fields, where the vibrations are purest, certain crystals grow not outward, but inward. They form hollow, uterine geodes, some the size of a barrel. Within these, fed by a specific chemical and resonant signature in the mineral slush, a Geode-Kin gestates. It is a slow, crystalline embryology. Over centuries, a scaffold of bone and metal precipitates from the solution, tissues of mineral fiber knit themselves together, and a nascent consciousness begins to resonate with the world-song outside its shell. When it is complete, the geode cracks open with a sound lost in the general din, and the new being unfolds its long limbs, slick with amniotic sludge, and takes its first sliding, sensing step into the thunderous world that is its parent, its purpose, and its substance.

They do not seem to fear the other fauna, nor interact with it. The great, scraping armadillos are part of the process, tilling the crystal fields. The vibration-hunting stalkers are another, culling the weak or the errant. The Geode-Kin are simply on another plane of existence, their timescale too vast, their sensory world too alien. They are the geological nervous system of this place, the slow, lingering will of the mountain-sized engine. They are not alive *in* the factory; they are the factory, dreaming it is alive.
