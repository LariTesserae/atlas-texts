They who live here are the Carvers, though they have no name for themselves. A name is a sound, and they are a people without voice. Their throats can swallow and draw breath, but the organs for speech have long since withered into silence. They are born into it, live in it, and return to it, and the concept of a spoken word is as alien to them as that of a sun.

Their physical form is a direct answer to the pressures of their world. They are tall and gaunt, with an economy of flesh that speaks of a life without excess. Their skin is pale, not the pinkish-white of a cave-dweller but the slick, grayish-white of a mushroom’s belly, a color that absorbs the dim light rather than reflecting it. Their bodies are maps of wiry muscle, corded and dense from a lifetime of climbing. Their joints are large and knobby, their limbs long and spidery, allowing them to brace themselves within the carved galleries or move with unnerving grace across the sheer rock faces.

But it is their senses that truly define them. Their ears are small, almost vestigial, simple folds of cartilage pressed close to the skull. They may register the low, sub-audible vibration of the Breathing Mat’s rhythm, but that is all. Their true ears are their eyes. The Carvers' eyes are enormous, filling a large portion of their narrow faces. They are liquid, depthless pools of black, with no discernible sclera or iris, like drops of obsidian. These eyes are built for the perpetual twilight, able to perceive the most subtle gradations of gray and shadow. More than that, they are tuned to the silent languages of the caldera. They can read the frantic, geometric flashes of the armored trilobites as a detailed report on the Mat’s moisture content. They can interpret the slow, pulsing glow of a slug as a sign of a particular mineral deposit in the rock it's consuming. The ballet of the moths is not random; to the Carvers, it is a living map of the air currents and pheromonal weather.

Their hands are their other voice. Long, with an extra joint in each finger, their hands are tools of both creation and perception. The palms and fingertips are covered in tough, leathery calluses from chipping away at basalt, but beneath this armor lies a sensitivity that is almost a form of sight. By pressing a hand to the weeping wall, a Carver can feel the subtle tremors that precede a rockfall days in advance. They can read the texture of a chisel-mark to know who made it, when, and in what state of mind—haste, grief, or meditative focus. Their "language" is not solely gestural in the way of a signed conversation; it is far slower, a language of held postures, of deliberate turns of the head, of the minute angle of a hand. It is a language meant to be seen from a hundred meters away across a chasm of empty air.

They augment this physical language with light. The phosphorescent fluid harvested from the slugs is their ink. A Carver might anoint their face with it. A glowing streak drawn from the temple to the jaw is not decoration; it is a sentence. It might signify ‘I am seeking a mate,’ or ‘My chamber has a surplus of water,’ or ‘I am in mourning.’ These glowing statements fade within a few hours, a transient and deeply personal form of communication in a world of permanence and stone.

Their society is one of quiet, interwoven solitude. They are not communal in the way of a tribe that gathers around a fire. They gather in purpose—a team of Carvers hollowing out a new chamber, their obsidian chisels rising and falling in a silent, shared rhythm—but their inner lives are vast and isolated. Their thoughts are not structured by sounds, but by patterns of motion, by the memory of light-sequences, by the slow, tidal pull of the Mat underfoot. A child learns not by being told, but by watching, by imitating the impossibly patient movements of its elders for years until the scraping of stone becomes as natural as breathing.

Their life is a constant conversation with the caldera, a give and take. They are born on beds of woven Mat-fiber inside the rock-wombs of the city. They harvest the Mat for sustenance and textiles, they drink the water it gathers, they use the creatures that live upon it for their light. But when they die, they are given back. An elder, feeling their end near, will descend from the city for the last time. They will be taken to a sacred depression in the Breathing Mat, wrapped not in their pale shroud but only in the mist. There, they lie down. The Mat, in its slow, digestive way, accepts them. Over weeks, the body softens, dissolves, and is drawn down into the living floor, becoming part of the great, quiet respiration of their world. The other Carvers watch this from the galleries above, their faces unreadable, their glowing markings perhaps shifting from statements of identity to eulogies of light.

They are not masters of this place, nor are they its prisoners. They are its consciousness. They are the part of the caldera that has learned to hold a tool, to look back at the walls that contain it, and to carve its own fleeting existence into the stone. In the suffocating, overwhelming silence, surrounded by the ceaseless, silent motion of a million other lives, they exist as the quiet, patient thought of a world that has never learned to speak.
