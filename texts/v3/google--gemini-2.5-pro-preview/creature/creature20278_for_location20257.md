The being that lives here is the Custodian. It is both the first and only inhabitant, and the living mechanism that ensures the place remains as it is.

It is rarely seen, for its motion is so slow as to be mistaken for geology. Its form is of the same material as the ground, a body of fired, unglazed clay, the matte, porous texture of a new pot. Its shape is humanoid only in the most abstract sense: a torso, two arms, two legs, a head. There are no features, no muscles, no hints of a skeleton beneath. Its surface is the colour of dried blood and old terracotta, warm and earthy, but utterly sterile.

For years, or centuries, it might sit, submerged to its waist in one of the motionless, glass-like pools. It does not displace the water; the water yields to its form, clinging to its edges with perfect surface tension. Unlike the slick tiles and jade leaves upon which water beads and drips, the Custodian’s porous clay skin drinks the thick, wet air. It is perpetually saturated, dark with moisture, a shade deeper than the tiles around it. To watch it is to see the condensation of the air made manifest, soaking into a form that never becomes full.

Its head is smooth and ovoid, without a mouth, nose, or ears. Where eyes would be, there are two shallow, concave impressions, like thumbprints pressed into the wet clay before firing. They do not see in the way we do. They perceive stress, change, the slightest deviation from the world's perfect, established thought. They feel the hum of the white sky, and they notice when that hum falters, even minutely.

The Custodian is not alive in any biological sense. It is a maintenance drone, a function given form. Its purpose is singular: to correct imperfections. The world is a static thought, but even a perfect thought held for eternity can fray at the edges.

When an imperfection occurs—a microscopic fracture in a hexagonal tile from the sheer, unending pressure of stasis; a slight dimming in the lustre of a verdigris frond; a single mica wing on a wire dragonfly tilting a fraction of a degree out of formation—the Custodian perceives it. The low hum of the world changes its frequency in a way only it can detect, and it begins to move.

Its rising from a pool is an event of profound slowness. The water does not ripple; it stretches and shears away from its form like a sheet of cooling syrup, reforming its perfect surface behind the Custodian without a sound. As its feet, shaped like solid, smoothed stumps, meet the tiled floor, they make no clicking sound. Instead, there is a soft, grating whisper, the sound of dry earth on dry earth, a sound that is instantly dampened by the film of water on the tiles.

It walks. One leg lifts, taking a decade. It travels forward, taking another. The passage of the Custodian across one of the empty plazas between the sculptural groves is a process that can span millennia. It does not perceive this time. It perceives only the origin point and the destination: the flaw. The serpentine skinks adjust their patrol routes to give it a wide berth, their movements the only thing in this world that seems to acknowledge its passage. The turquoise frogs remain fixed, their glazed eyes reflecting its slow, inexorable progress.

Upon reaching the imperfection, the Custodian will stop. If the flaw is a crack in a tile, it will kneel, a posture it might take a century to fully adopt. It extends a hand, its fingers long, tapered, and without joints. It does not bring tools. Its tools are intention and memory. It lays its palm over the flaw.

The low hum of the air concentrates around the point of contact, rising in pitch until it is almost a discernible note. No heat is felt, no light is emitted, but the Custodian is feeding the world's original blueprint back into the flawed component. It is reminding the tile of its unbroken state. Under its palm, the hairline crack does not fill, but vanishes. It does not heal; it is *undone*. The matter is reverted to the moment of its creation, perfect and whole.

Once the repair is complete, the hum returns to its subliminal equilibrium. The Custodian will remain motionless for a period of years, its palm flat against the tile, ensuring the correction holds. Then, with the same glacial slowness, it will retract its hand, rise, and begin the long journey back to a pool, where it will settle into the still water and resume its vigil of thought, a silent, earthen mind holding its beautiful, terrifying, and unchanging world together, one eternal moment at a time. It is the gardener in a garden where nothing grows, the janitor of a hall that never tarnishes, the thought that thinks itself.
