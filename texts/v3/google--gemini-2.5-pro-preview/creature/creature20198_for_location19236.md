They are the Weavers, though they do not weave. They call themselves the Listeners, or perhaps a word that translates to something like *Those-Who-Feel-the-Hum*. They are the Gardeners of whom you speak.

To see one is to almost miss them. Their skin is the color of the wet Weaving, a shifting mosaic of moss-green, lichen-grey, and the deep, dark shade of shadow. It is not camouflage as a coat of paint is, but a living, breathing chromatism that subtly alters to match the light and background. Their skin is cool, smooth, and largely hairless, always retaining a slick sheen of moisture, as if they are a part of the Slow Breath, exuding it as much as living within it.

They are impossibly tall and slender, their limbs elongated beyond human proportion, giving them the appearance of figures stretched by a dream. Their joints are hyper-flexible; an ankle can rotate nearly 360 degrees, a spine can curve to follow the arc of a buttress. They move with a profound economy of motion, an unhurried, liquid grace. There is no wasted energy, no sudden start or stop. They flow over the lattice of their world, their long, prehensile fingers and toes finding purchase on surfaces a human would find impossibly slick. The pads of their feet and hands are broad, soft, and covered in microscopic ridges that grant them gecko-like adhesion, allowing them to walk up vertical trunks or hang from the undersides of causeways with unnerving ease. They often use all four limbs for locomotion, unfurling to their full bipedal height only when at rest or performing delicate work.

Their heads are narrow, their faces dominated by enormous, dark eyes. They are eyes built for twilight, pools of liquid black with no discernible sclera or iris, designed to drink in the faint, pulsing light of the moss-fields. A secondary, translucent nictitating membrane blinks sideways across these onyx orbs, a slow, hypnotic sweep that clears the constant condensation. They have no visible ears, but rather a series of gill-like slits just behind the jawline, covered by a thin membrane that vibrates in sympathy with the world. They hear the drip of water, yes, but their true sense of hearing is tuned to a lower frequency—the subsonic, metabolic hum of the Great Weaving itself.

This is their primary sense, more vital than sight. They can feel the health of the Weaving through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. They can sense a stressed fiber a mile away as a dissonant note in the deep chord of the world. They can feel the slow, tectonic shift of an arch as a change in pressure, a gravitational waver that a human might mistake for a moment of dizziness. This sense is why distances seem plastic to you, but not to them; they navigate not by sight but by the feeling of the Weaving's living geometry.

They do not speak in a way you would recognize. Language is conveyed through quiet, melodic hums that harmonize with the background vibration, and through a complex grammar of touch. A touch to the arm can convey direction, a brush of fingers can signal the location of a diseased node, and a prolonged embrace is a deep communion of shared experience, a silent telling of stories. When they work together, scraping a filament thick as a fallen redwood, they move in a silent, perfectly synchronized ballet, their actions coordinated by the shared rhythm they feel from the structure beneath them.

Their lives are as cyclical and patient as the Weaving's growth. They are born in specialized chambers, womb-like hollows within the largest nodes of the structure, where the water pools and is enriched with the Weaving's own nutrient-sap. They emerge not screaming, but silent and watching. They learn by listening to the hum, their elders guiding their hands to feel the difference between a healthy, resonant fiber and a decaying, silent one.

They eat sparingly. They harvest the glowing moss with bone scrapers, consuming it not just for light but for sustenance. They hunt the six-limbed climbers, not with weapons of projection, but by patiently waiting and catching them with their long-fingered hands. They drink directly from the rivulets on the Weaving, their bodies perfectly adapted to its metallic tang.

They have no concept of history as a series of events, but as a continuity of being. The Builders are not a lost race to be mourned; they are simply the part of the Hum that has gone silent. Their only religion is this maintenance, this sacred duty of care. Their greatest art is not painting or sculpture, but a perfectly pruned arch, a guided growth that will ensure stability for another ten thousand years. Their tools—sharpened obsidian from rare, vitrified nodes, bone needles, mallets of petrified heartwood—are passed down for generations, each carrying the silent memory of the hands that held it.

When a Listener dies, their body is not buried. They are taken to a "recycling grove," a place where the smallest filaments of the Weaving grow most actively. There, they are laid down, and the Weaving slowly, gently, reclaims them. Their bodies dissolve over the course of a year, their substance becoming part of the structure they tended their entire lives. To them, this is not an end, but a final act of listening, of dissolving their individual hum back into the great, cosmic chord of the Weaving. They become the Garden.
