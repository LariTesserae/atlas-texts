They are the Un-Hasted. The name, if translated, would mean something closer to "Those Who Have Become the Waiting."

Physically, they are built for the cold and the thinness of the air. They are broad of shoulder and hip, with deep chests that house lungs evolved for this oxygen-starved altitude. Their movements are ponderous, deliberate, economical. Haste is a foreign concept, a waste of precious energy and a hazard in a landscape where a misplaced foot can mean a gash from a rock fin or a long fall down a gravel slide. A child is taught stillness before they are taught to walk, learning to sit for hours looking out over the rubble-fields, their gaze patient and empty. Their bodies have a low, slow metabolism, a quiet internal fire that burns with miserly efficiency. Wrapped in layers of shaggy white fur and pale, bone-white hides, they are difficult to distinguish from a rime-covered boulder at any distance. From their wrappings, only their faces are visible, and sometimes not even that.

Their skin is not tanned by the sun, but cured by the wind and the cold. It has a pale, waxy, almost translucent quality, like old parchment, stretched tight over broad cheekbones. Around their eyes and mouths are dense networks of fine lines, the geographies of a lifetime spent squinting against the glare off the rime ice and the relentless wind. Their eyes are the most striking feature. Often a pale, washed-out grey or blue, the colour of the sky at the horizon, they are set deep in their sockets. They are not the eyes of a predator, full of fire and focus, but the eyes of a geologist or an astronomer. They are built for immense distance and infinitesimal change. They can discern the subtle shift in a great-beast’s gait from a mile away, a tell-tale slump that marks the beginning of the end. They can read the texture of the ever-present ground-mist, knowing where it will pool and where it will vanish. Their world is one of texture, not colour, and their eyes have learned this language.

To be near one is to be aware of their slowness. They breathe deeply, a slow and even cadence that does not quicken with exertion. Their hands, when ungloved, are thick and calloused, the knuckles like river stones, the skin crisscrossed with a lifetime of small cuts from sharp rock and bone slivers. They hold a bone awl or a stone scraper with the same unhurried, immovable patience with which their city holds to the mountain.

The Un-Hasted do not speak often, and their language reflects their world. It is a quiet, guttural tongue, full of soft clicks and long, resonant vowels that seem to mimic the whispering wind and the hum of the great-beasts. They have dozens of words for ice, for different qualities of stone, for the stages of a beast's decline. They have no word for "hurry." Their conversations are sparse, conveying vast meaning in few sounds. A shared glance, a subtle cant of the head, a single uttered word is often enough. Living in such close and constant proximity, packed into the warrens of their city-reef, they have developed a form of communication that is almost communal, a shared understanding that transcends speech.

Their inner world is a reflection of the outer. It is not empty, but it is uncluttered. Their thoughts move with the same deliberate pace as their bodies. Their minds are occupied by the long, slow rhythms of the world: the integrity of the wall, the thickness of a hide, the direction of the wind, the slow progression of the herd. They are not prone to flights of fancy or passionate outbursts. Emotion is a deep, slow current, not a crashing wave. Joy is a full larder of dried meat against the coming dark. Sadness is the sight of a calf falling, too young. Fear is the sound of a deep structural groan in the city's ancient foundations.

In the bone-and-hide warrens, the scent of the inorganic world is banished. It smells of smoked meat, of the lanolin-rich grease they use to treat the hides and waterproof their skin, of the low, smoky fire fueled by the dried dung of the great-beasts, and of close-packed, living bodies. This cloying, animal warmth is the smell of safety, of community, of life. The silence outside is replaced by a domestic soundscape: the soft scrape of a tool on bone, the creak of hide partitions, the endless, gentle clatter of the vertebrae chimes hanging in doorways, stirred by the drafts that snake through the city's labyrinthine passages.

They believe life is a closed and complete circle, and they are but one point on its circumference. The great-beast lives by eating the lichen that grows on the stone. The Un-Hasted live by consuming the beast when its time is done. And when one of the Un-Hasted dies, they are given back to the structure. Their flesh is rendered for its grease, and their bones are cleaned and incorporated into the walls of their own home, becoming part of the city-reef. They live, quite literally, surrounded by their ancestors. A child might learn to count by tracing the finger-bones of a great-great-grandmother set into the wall of their sleeping chamber. There is no horror in this, only a profound sense of continuity. They are a knot of life, woven from beast-flesh and their own kin, clinging to a world of stone that does not care. They are the Un-Hasted, and their patience is as deep as the rock and as long as the sky.
