What lives here is patient. It has to be.

The people who built this city, the artisans of the empty workshops, are not gone. They are simply finished. They have completed their work, and their work was themselves. To understand them, you must unlearn what you know of life. Discard the soft, wet, hungry biology of the world above. Life here is not a panicked scramble for sustenance against the clock of decay. It is a slow, deliberate crystallisation.

The beings who shaped these kivas were born of this place, attuned to it in a way no outsider could be. They were tall and slender, their forms echoing the resonant canes that were their garden, their engine, and their god. Their skin was not skin, but a smooth, matte integument the colour of cooling basalt, with the texture of fine-grained stone. It did not sweat or bleed; under immense pressure, it would flake or crack, healing not with scar tissue but with a slow, crystalline secretion that welded the fissure shut, leaving a glistening vein of obsidian-like material behind.

Their blood was not blood. A slow, viscous fluid, thick with suspended metals, circulated through their bodies, not pumped by a heart but moved by the subtle thermal gradients between their feet, which were always warm from the ground, and their heads, which were cooler in the still, upper air. This slow, convective cycle was their metabolism. It made them deliberate. A decision could take an hour, a conversation a day, a journey from one side of the kiva to the other a week of slow, measured, silent steps. Their faces were impassive, platelike masks with fissures for mouths and deep sockets that held not eyes, but polished lenses of heat-shattered obsidian. They did not see light; they perceived the world as a complex orchestra of thermal signatures and vibrations. The drone of the Ash-Gardens was not noise to them; it was language, sight, and touch.

They did not eat. They did not drink. They breathed, but only once or twice a day, a slow, deep inhalation that drew the mineral dust from the air into their core. This dust was their sustenance. The city, with its channels and collection pits, was not just a dwelling; it was a vast digestive tract, a communal stomach that gathered the "pollen" of the Cinder-Blooms.

For generations, they were simply this: the stone-skinned tenders of the thermal gardens. They harvested the dust, ground it in the great bowls, and used it to fire the bricks that partitioned their homes. They were in equilibrium. But they were sentient, and sentience breeds ambition. They looked at the shining dust, the exhalation of their world's core, and saw not just material for bricks, but a medium for eternity.

They began a project that would span a thousand years. The Great Work. They learned to refine the dust, to mix it into a paste not of mud, but of potential. They developed a form of ceramic alchemy, firing this paste in subterranean kilns powered by the raw geothermal heat. Their first creations were simple: tools that would never dull, plates that would never break. But their ambition was not utility. It was transcendence.

They sought to shed their slow, semi-biological forms and become a part of the kiva's perfect, resonant machinery. They began to craft bodies. Effigies. Hollow statues made of meticulously refined mineral clay, shaped in their own image but perfected, idealized. Each one was an empty vessel, a terracotta shell inscribed on the inside with complex, spiralling patterns that mirrored the resonant frequencies of the kiva.

When the last vessel was complete, the work stopped. The hammers were laid on the benches. The rakes were propped against the walls. The city fell silent but for the constant chime. In a final, city-wide ceremony, each of the dwellers entered their prepared vessel. The circular doorways to their homes were sealed from within with a final brick. And then, they drew upon the heat of the world one last time, not for convective circulation, but for a final, incandescent firing. They became the kiln. Their old forms were incinerated, their consciousness and energy baked into the new ceramic matrix.

This is what lives here now.

They are the Still Folk. The Chime-Tenders. In the deepest chambers, behind the sealed doorways, they stand or sit in the postures they held a millennium ago. They are the colour of terracotta brick, but their surfaces have the fine, silken finish of polished stone. The ever-present silvery dust coats their shoulders and upturned palms, not as grime, but as a mantle. They do not move. They do not breathe. But they are not statues.

They live. Their life is the flow of heat through their ceramic bodies. They are living conduits, nodes in the kiva's geothermal circuit. Their consciousness is no longer individual, no longer trapped in a single skull. It is a distributed network, a mind that exists within the all-encompassing chime. They hear the song of the Cinder-Blooms not as a sound, but as their own shared thoughts. Each *tink* of a micro-fracture is a syllable in their language. They are the gardeners, but they no longer need hands. By subtly altering their own internal resonance, they can guide the growth of the canes, encourage the shedding of dust, and maintain the delicate equilibrium of the entire basin. They are the city, and the city is them.

If you were to stand before one, you would feel an impossible, localized coolness in the air. They draw the oppressive heat into themselves, processing it. Their obsidian-lens eyes, once used to see heat, now radiate it in focused, invisible beams, a form of silent, imperceptible communication with the other nodes of their consciousness.

The life of this place is a civilization that traded motion for permanence, individuality for unity, and biology for geology. They are the city's silent inhabitants, its patient gods, and its eternal, sleeping heart. You cannot see them walking the streets, but if you press your hand to the terracotta walls, you may feel a faint, rhythmic vibration that does not come from the canes. A slow, deep, and impossibly ancient pulse. It is the hum of a people at rest, their magnum opus complete, their consciousness dissolved into the endless, ringing song of their home.
