The people who live in Lastrung are called the Gyre-born, or more formally, the Lastrungi. They are shaped, body and soul, by the immense pressures and profound patience of their world. To see one is to see a being sculpted by the same forces that rounded the mountains.

They are not soft. Their skin has a matte, stone-like texture, ranging in color from the deep rust of the lower slopes to the pale, dusty ochre of the highest spires. It is not skin as we know it, but a dense, semi-flexible integument infused with silicates, cool and solid to the touch. It serves as a natural defense against the thin, abrasive air and the constant, low-level radiation from the planet's core. They bleed, but slowly; a wound weeps the same thick, oily ichor that sustains the grinders, a fluid the color of unrefined petroleum that congeals quickly into a dark, resinous scab.

Physically, they are built for stability and endurance. They are generally shorter and broader than baseline humans, with a low center of gravity. Their bones are dense, their musculature less for explosive speed and more for tireless, sustained force. They move with a deliberate, gliding economy of motion, a rhythm learned from the slow elevators and the vast, empty plazas. To hurry is not just undignified; it is inefficient, a waste of precious energy in their slow-metabolism world. Their faces are broad, their features often seem worn smooth, like statues left in a persistent wind. Their eyes are their most expressive feature: wide, dark, and possessing a profound depth. They are adapted for judging immense distances across the hazy expanse and for discerning the subtle, internal luminescence of the litho-scleractinia.

They breathe, but their respiratory system is an act of supreme efficiency. Their chests are deep, their lungs vast, designed to extract every possible molecule of oxygen from the thin air. Their heart beats with a slow, powerful, resonant thud you can almost feel if you stand close, like the deep hum of the city's own geothermal engines. This slow biology dictates their temperament. A Lastrungi does not shout in anger; they might lower their voice, the silence around their words becoming heavy and significant. Joy is not a peal of laughter, but a slow-blooming warmth in the eyes, a slight upturn of the lips that might last for an hour. To share a long, comfortable silence with a Lastrungi is the equivalent of a deep and animated conversation. Their language reflects this; it is rich in concepts of pressure, density, resonance, and geologic time, but sparse in words for the ephemeral. A promise is not a "word," but a "compaction," an idea being put under pressure until it becomes a solid fact.

They are a people of Deep Time. The human concept of a lifespan as a complete story is entirely alien to them. They see themselves as single facets on a vast, growing crystal, links in a chain of consciousness that stretches back into an unrecorded past and forward into a future they are patiently building. A Geode-wright might begin the initial rough cut on a resonant crystal that their great-grandchild will complete the final attunement on. This is not a source of sadness, but of profound purpose. They are their ancestors and their descendants in a continuous, slow-moving present.

Their society is structured around this craft. They are all, in some way, connected to the refinement of the deep crystals. They are not miners in a brutal sense; they are more like tenders of a geological garden. Teams descend on slow-moving platforms into the middle altitudes, not to dynamite the crystal reefs, but to carefully, respectfully harvest the "ripe" geodes, those whose internal resonances have reached a specific harmonic frequency. This is a form of communion. They listen to the "song" of the crystal fields, the faint hum conducted through the rock, to know which ones are ready.

Back in Lastrung, these Geode-wrights work in the great, silent refineries. Here, they are less laborers and more conductors of a slow orchestra of physics. They guide the patient robotic arms and energy beams, making infinitesimal adjustments over weeks and months. Their senses are attuned to the harmonics of the crystals; they can feel a flaw in a lattice structure as a dissonance in the hum that permeates the entire city.

Their culture is one of contemplation and observation. A primary form of meditation is to sit on one of the great balconies and watch the Sunken Sun. As the true sun dips below the pearlescent sea of the Stillness, the world below awakens with a soft, internal fire. For the Lastrungi, this is a sacred moment. They believe the captured light reveals the memories stored within the dense gases: the slow grinding of mountains, the pressure of ages, the memory of the world's own creation. It is their connection to the history of the deeps, a place they can never go but whose influence defines them.

They are not without art, but it is an art of endurance. They do not paint canvases that will fade. They "sing" with resonating rods of attuned crystal, creating single, pure tones that hang in the vast caverns for minutes at a time, their slow decay a performance in itself. They carve, but they do not carve statues of heroes or fleeting moments. They carve fractal patterns into the rock walls of their plazas, patterns which are then left for the wind to slowly polish and change over centuries. The art is in the initial act and its surrender to geologic time.

To live in the Gyre is to understand that you are a temporary arrangement of matter and energy, a brief, complex resonance in a world of stone and pressure. The Lastrungi do not fight this. They embrace it. They have harmonized their existence with the slow, powerful, and patient heartbeat of their planet, finding a quiet, enduring purpose in the refinement of light, stone, and time itself. They are what lives in the Gyre: the patient mind of the mountain, given form.
