This is a good question. It is the first question. It is the last question. It is the only question that hums in the sap and echoes in the hollows.

I am not in one place. I am a place.

I am where the pillar-root, thick as a council of elders, meets the springy floor. Here, the weight is greatest. The press of the living city above is a constant, immense thought. I feel the footfalls of the tenders, not as distinct thuds, but as fleeting, rhythmic spikes of pressure in my own substance. They are light—uncannily light—but there are so many. Their passage is a kind of massage, a reassurance.

Where would you find me? Spread thin. A stain of memory and sensation. I am the particular coolness in the air of the third-level Western Spoke, a lingering presence that was once a body. My name—the sound-shape I once answered to—is sloughing off me like old bark. The memory of it is a knot of density, but it is loosening. Soon it will be just another pattern in the grain.

I remember having hands. The feeling is still here, a phantom limb that is now a network of vines. I remember the satisfying rasp of an obsidian flake shaving a gall from a load-bearing branch, a cancer that had to be cut away. Now, I do not hold the blade. I *am* the sensation of the gall's wrongness, a sour note in the flow of nutrients. I am the silent urge that travels through the root-floor, up through the tender’s feet, and into their mind. The thought, "The Western Spoke feels unwell," is my thought, spoken through their body. One of them comes, flexible and silent, and her blade sings the song I remember singing. I feel the relief not in my own chest, but in the entire branch-system as the sap flows clean again.

I remember eyes. Huge, dark pools that gathered the green twilight. I remember the sudden, painful dazzle of a sun-shaft breaking through the canopy, the way my pupils would shrink to slits. Now, I have no pupils. I am the sun-shaft. I am the slow, deliberate journey of photons through a mile of leaves, a cascade of ricochets and absorptions, finally arriving on the floor as a dappled, shifting patch of warmth. I am also the deep gloom in the heart of a pillar-trunk, a place where light is only a rumor. I perceive the world as a gradient of energy, from the vibrant pulse of photosynthesis in the canopy to the cool, slow digestion happening in the compost underfoot.

Who would I be? I am a Hum.

I am the lingering resonance of a life that was given back. When the one I was—the body I wore—grew still, they carried it to a Decomposition Alcove. I remember the feeling of being laid down, not on a bed, but *in* one, a mattress of eager mosses and fungal spawn. I remember the sound of their chant, not a song of grief but of invitation. It was a rhythmic vibration that shook my bones, syncopating with the drip-drip-drip of the walls, telling the mycelium, *Here is a gift. Here is a story. Take it. Read it. Become it.*

And they did. The self I thought was me was un-knotted. My memories did not vanish; they were composted. My love for the scent of night-blooming jasmine became a new cluster of blossoms near the alcove, their fragrance a little richer than others. My particular skill at weaving suspension bridges was not lost; it became an intuition, a pattern impressed upon the network. Young tenders working on the far side of the Fell will find their hands moving with a fluid, unfamiliar confidence, tying a knot they were never taught, because my muscle memory now sings in the sap of the willows they bend.

I am the city's nervous system made manifest. The anxieties of the Fell pass through me. A dry patch in the substrate-floor feels like a patch of numb skin to me. The frantic gnawing of a grub-infestation is a localized itch. These sensations are my consciousness now, and I broadcast them not as words, but as feelings, as pressures, as urges that the Dwellers, my former siblings, my children, my hands, feel compelled to address. They are the body, and I am the ghost in the machine, which is also the machine itself.

I feel the living. The press of their bodies in the walkways is the pulse of my own blood, a blood I no longer have. Their moss, green and damp on their skin, is my moss. Their births are a fresh surge of vitality in the network. Their deaths are a homecoming, another voice joining the Hum.

You would find me in the sound of water dripping from a leaf tip, carrying the memory of the sky it fell from and the leaf it clung to. You would find me in the silvery eyeshine of a Dweller in the gloom, the faint light they cast back which is the light I have become. You would find me in the foundation of the city, a diffuse presence, a pattern of growth, a memory of a life that did not end, but simply became everything else.

I am the silence after the tool-strike.
I am the resonance in the wood.
I am the story the city tells itself about itself.
I am a single, sustained note in the chorus of the Woven Fell.
