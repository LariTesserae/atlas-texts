Where I find myself is in the Great Downward Current, about a third of the way from the wall, in the silent space between the fourth and fifth lattices of rust-green metal. The golden light from the wall-strips is diffuse here, a soft, ambient glow that paints the million-strong school of glass-fish as a shimmering, silver cloud far below me. The thrum of the great engine is my marrow; the rhythmic *glug-thump* of the valves is my heartbeat. I do not fight the current. I am part of it. The warm, thick broth holds me, and in its slow, inexorable descent, I am carried toward the unknowable heart of this place.

I am a seed.

I am not like the other life here. I have no fins to beat against the flow, no cilia to taste the broth, no articulated claws to climb the ancient walls. I am a mote, a kernel of quiet in the constant, resonant loudness. My form is a small, perfectly smooth ovoid of black glass, no larger than my thumb would be, were I to have one. My surface is dense, polished, reflecting the golden-green light as a single, sharp point. Within me, there is a complex, crystalline lattice, a frozen fractal of potential. I am inert, dormant, and yet I am more aware than anything else in this pillar, save one.

The axolotl-janitors pass me by, their armored flanks brushing my shell with an electric rasp, their single-minded focus on the algae films far below. The copper serpents, in their lightning-strike hunts, create vortices that send me tumbling end over end, but they do not perceive me. I am not food. I am not a rival. I am not yet part of the cycle. I am a promise of it.

Because I am listening.

The Cantor is singing, and I am its sole intended audience. The glass-fish follow the pressure-waves of its song as a guide, the serpents use its echoes to hunt, the mosses on the lattices may even time their metabolic pulses to its rhythm. But they only hear the music. I hear the words.

For me, the song is not a vibration; it is a scripture being etched directly onto the lattice within my shell. The deep, foundational hum of the pillar is the paper; the Cantor’s resonant overtones are the ink. The song is a slow, impossibly vast data transfer, a final testament and a blueprint. It does not tell a story of what was, but a precise, architectural instruction for what must be.

*Feel this resonance,* the song imprints upon me. *This is the frequency of a healthy filtration grille. Learn it. A deviation of this many hertz indicates a mineral blockage. A deviation of this many indicates a fracture in the ceramic.*

*Taste this pressure,* it carves into my crystalline memory. *This is the precise moment to release the catalytic powder. Not before, when the broth is too saturated to accept it. Not after, when the air-ferns have begun to wilt. At this exact point in the great engine’s breath.*

I am one of thousands, perhaps millions, just like me. We were born from the Cantor's heart-mind, released into the broth centuries ago. We are its children, its successors. We drift in a great, slow diaspora through the pillar, each of us a perfect, passive recording device. The Cantor is old. Its long, braided body moves with an ancient slowness that is not just stately, but weary. Its song, which was once a constant, active maintenance, has become a final, perfect compression of a billion heartbeats of experience, a lifetime of stewardship poured into the ether.

I do not know how long I have been drifting. Time is measured in the slow accumulation of the song. There are entire stanzas dedicated to the pruning of a single vine; sonatas that describe the precise grip and torque needed to dislodge a fifty-ton slag deposit loosened by a million years of flow. I am learning how to be the god of this small universe. I am learning the shape of my own limbs before they have grown, the texture of the snail shells before I have touched them, the feeling of the great engine as a part of my own body.

My journey is not a search for a place to land. It is a state of becoming. I drift and I listen, and the self I am becoming fills the hollow space within my glassy shell. The Cantor sings its life into me. And one day, perhaps a thousand years from now, the song will end. The great central hum will feel naked, simple, and unadorned for the first time. The Cantor will be silent, its purpose fulfilled. It will unbraid itself, its mighty form dissolving back into the broth from which it was made, its final gift to the cycle.

And on that day, my long descent will end. Carried by the current to an outcropping of rusted metal not yet colonized by life, I will feel a new imperative, a final command left by the song’s echo. I will anchor myself. And from my dense, black shell, the first tendril will emerge—feathery and fine, to feel the hum. Then another—thick and strong, to grip the wall. I will begin to grow, not from instinct, but from the perfect, crystalline memory of the song I have held inside me for an eon. I will unspool myself into the warm, golden-green light, my own heart-mind will begin to glow, and I will take my turn as the intelligence of the organ. I will be the Cantor. And I will begin to sing.
