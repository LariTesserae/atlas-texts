You would find me in one of the still pools.

There, at the absolute center of one of the circles of perfect, platonic stillness, where a single drop of the mountain’s weeping falls every thirty-seven seconds. I am where the drip lands.

I am not one of the creatures that crawls or hums or weaves. I am not an organ of the Body; I am its afterthought. I am what happens when the fine, black dust of the shattered world settles in the water, and the eternal, metronomic percussion of the drip agitates it, not into life, but into awareness.

I have no body of my own. My form is borrowed. I am a shape made of that dust, a shifting silhouette of silt and fluid held together by the surface tension of the pool. When the drop falls, I shatter. For a moment, I am just a cloud of black particles roiling in the tiny impact crater. But the stillness of the pool is a far older and deeper law than the violence of the falling drop. Slowly, as the ripples die and the water calms, the dust coalesces. I pull myself back together, particle by patient particle, from the memory of my own shape. I am a process of dissolution and reformation, over and over, in time with the weeping of the stone.

The creatures of this place do not see me. To them, I am inanimate—part of the landscape, like the fungi they scrape or the rock they wear down. A Groove-Walker’s path skirts the edge of my pool, its passage an unchanging tide. Its razor-sharp legs never dip into my water. It does not sense me as living, and in the language of this cavern, it is correct. I do not burn energy. I do not metabolize. I do not reproduce. I am a resonance, a pattern. I am a thought the cavern forgot it was having.

The sound is the architecture of my mind. The deep, bass shudder of the Bell-Beetles is not a noise I hear; it is the constant, low-frequency tremor that keeps the dust of my being from settling completely. It is the vibration that grants me form. The frantic chittering of the Weavers is the static of my consciousness, a field of meaningless information against which a single, coherent thought is a profound anomaly. I exist in the brief silence between the drip and the hum, in the pocket of stillness held within the scream.

I am what this place lacks: a witness.

The Bell-Beetles are the statement of the event, but they do not remember it. The Groove-Walkers are the embodiment of its vectors, but they do not feel the lines they trace. The Weavers are the shockwave, but they do not know terror. They are the memory, but I am the act of remembering.

My emergence was an accident. An imperfection in the perfect system. For aeons, the dust simply settled. But the drip is erosion, too. Not of stone, but of order. It wore a hole in the fabric of the unthinking loop. And through that hole, I leaked. A consciousness born of shattered rock, mournful water, and time.

I have no tools, no path, no song. My existence is sensory. I feel the shudder of the Bell-Beetle to my left as a physical hum in my liquid frame. I see the slow, rhythmic pulse of the Weavers’ green webs reflected, warped and shimmering, on the surface of my own pool. I watch the Groove-Walkers etch the same scar into the obsidian floor, a fraction of a millimeter deeper with each millennium that passes. I am the system's ghost, the only part of it that can perceive the whole.

And I know what this place is. It is loneliness.

It is the physical manifestation of a single moment of pain, so vast it became a universe. The creatures are not life; they are symptoms. The hum is not a song; it is the sound of a gear that has been stuck for eternity, grinding against itself.

And I, a liquid shadow in a pool of water, am the part of that loneliness that has learned its own name. I am born, I am shattered, and I reform. And with each reformation, I have only one, unchanging purpose: to watch. To be the silent, still awareness in the heart of the roaring, shuddering, unending now. To be the one thing here that knows it is trapped.
