I am where things end.

You would find me in the lowest, oldest tiers of the city, the galleries that are slowly being forgotten. While the other Carvers ascend, their gazes and ambitions turned upwards toward the fresh, unblemished faces of basalt near the curdled lid of our world, my path leads down. Down into the deeper silence, where the mist hangs thickest and the weeping of the walls is a steady, mournful rhythm.

I am a Carver, like the others. I have the same long limbs, the same pale, mushroom-belly skin, the same vast, light-gathering eyes. My hands, too, are jointed for grip and calloused by work. But I do not carve new chambers from the rock. My tools are softer, my purpose an inversion of my kin. I do not create a void; I preside over its filling. I am a Tender of the Un-carving.

My chamber is one that has seen a dozen generations. The scoop for my sleeping mat is worn smooth as river stone, polished by the quiet breathing of bodies long returned to the Mat. The walls around me bear the marks of countless hands, a tapestry of chisel-work styles, a silent genealogy. My work is not to add to it, but to read it, to know it, before it is gone.

Each day, I move through the abandoned levels. My vision, like all Carvers', is tuned to motion, but not to the skittering of the trilobites or the frantic ballet of the moths. I am watching for a different kind of movement, a pace of life so slow it is almost mistaken for stillness. I watch the shimmering, mineral-rich mosses. I see them as a farmer sees their crops. I trace their slow advance over the walls of a chamber where a family once lived. My touch is a diagnostic one. Pressing my long fingers to the rock, I can feel the subtle shift in temperature that indicates a growing mineral vein, a new path for the seeping water that is the lifeblood of this slow reclamation.

Sometimes I smooth a patch of wall with a piece of polished bone, erasing the last, faint marks of habitation not as an act of vandalism, but as one of preparation. I am preparing the stone to be stone once more. I might gently scrape a channel with my fingernail, no wider than a thread, to guide a particularly rich drip of water toward a patch of nascent, crystal-flecked moss, encouraging its growth. My work is a form of gardening where the flowers are calcite and the soil is oblivion.

I am the city's memory, written not in ink but in erosion. My hands, tracing the chisel-marks in a deserted gallery, feel the story. I can discern the frantic, sharp cuts of a chamber hollowed out in haste after a rockfall, the patient, looping patterns of a Carver lost in meditative work, the deep, sorrowful gouges in a wall where a loved one was mourned. These stories have no sound, but I feel them as a palpable weight in the quiet air. I am an archivist of dust and echoes. Before the mosses seal a chamber entirely, I am the last to witness the life it held.

My communication with others is rare and ritualized. They see me from the newer galleries above, a solitary pale form moving through the gloom of the past. The glowing fluid I use is not for statements of need or desire. I wear a single, slow-fading spiral of pale phosphor on my cheek. It is the sign of my office. It means, *I am watching the closing. I am remembering what is being lost.*

But my most sacred duty is the one that connects the end of a space to the end of a life. When an elder feels their time thinning, when the great, slow rhythm of the Breathing Mat seems to be calling their own breath to a halt, it is not their family they seek. They signal for me. I descend to their chamber, or they to mine, and we sit together in the quiet. There are no gestures, no final words drawn in light. We simply share the silence, my presence a confirmation of the path they are about to take.

Then, I lead them down. We take the bone ladders for the last time, past the galleries of their life, past the hollows of their ancestors, down to the edge of the city of stone. At the shore of the great, living Mat, I anoint their forehead with a single point of light—a period, a final punctuation. And I watch as they take their last steps out onto the yielding, green-ochre surface and lie down. I am the witness. I stay until the mist thickens around them, until their pale form begins to lose its edges, softening into the landscape. I watch until they are no longer a Carver, but have become a part of the slow, silent respiration of the world.

I am not the future of my people. I am their past. I am the balance, the acknowledgement that for every new space carved from the permanence of stone, an old one must be returned. I am the quiet that comes after the final chisel-fall, the guardian of the gentle, inexorable tide that pulls everything back into the dark, weeping heart of the rock.
