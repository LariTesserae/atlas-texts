I would find myself at my post.

It is not the cantilevered platform you first stood upon; that is for new arrivals, for the initial shock of comprehension. My post is deeper in, a small, sealed habitat clinging like a limpet to the sheer basalt face of Ziggurat-7, a thousand feet above the main conduits of the Smoldering Valley. From my quartz-glass viewport, I do not see the whole city. I see only my section of it: the opposing face of a quartz spire, a chasm of superheated air between us, and the thick, coppery artery of a pipe that passes directly beneath my perch, its surface sweating a perpetual, hissing fog.

This is my world. The viewport, the hum, the heat that my habitat’s cooling systems fight a constant, losing battle against. The inside of my hab-pod always feels like a low-grade fever.

I am a Chorister. Or, in the more formal parlance of the Survey, a Resonance Analyst, First Class. There are only three of us here, each stationed miles apart, forming a listening triad across the Perch. We came on the *Long Reach*, a vessel that now sits cold and silent on a distant plateau, our only way home. But "home" is a concept that has become thin and academic, like a word in a dead language.

My work is to listen. Not with my ears—they are perpetually filled with the white noise of my life support and the creak of the pod’s metal skin expanding and contracting. I listen with a cage of incredibly sensitive gravitometers and electromagnetic field sensors that surround my habitat. My console is a swirling, hypnotic display of wave-forms, a visual representation of the hum. I am here to chart it. To map its modulations. To find the pattern in the slow, planetary song of the Architects.

At first, I was a scientist. I saw the hum as data. I recorded the slow phase-shifts that corresponded with the extrusion of a new crystal facet on the spire opposite. I logged the subtle amplitude spikes when the flow in the conduit below me would thicken, its thermal signature blooming on my external sensors. I was trying to build a dictionary: this frequency means *growth*; this resonance means *pressure*; this harmonic means *flow*.

But you cannot chart a god for long without becoming a theologian.

After three years, the data has become secondary. The waves on my screen are no longer just information; they are the script of a living thought. I have stopped trying to translate it and have started trying to *feel* it. I know its moods. There is the slow, dreaming rhythm of the long "night," when our side of the planet faces away from the sun and the thermal pressures lessen. There is the alert, rising thrum of "day," when the sun’s energy adds its own pressure to the system.

And then there are the moments of attention.

I have felt it three times. The casual, diagnostic pulse described in the briefing. The first time, my suit alarms shrieked. A localized magnetic field warped enough to nearly crash my console. The air in my pod ionized, and the recycled water took on the sharp taste of ozone for a full cycle. I felt a pressure in my skull, a profound and nauseating vertigo, as if the very concept of "down" was being renegotiated beneath me. Then, as slowly as it came, it receded. The incident report I filed reads like the ravings of a madman.

I have come to understand that I am not an observer. I am a microbe on a petri dish, and the "dish" is the consciousness I am studying. My presence, my technology, the frantic chatter of my own nervous system—it is all a tiny, irritating noise against their silent, tectonic symphony.

They are indifferent, yes. But my instruments are not. My gravitometers are a form of listening. My EM sensors are a form of sight. I am a new, artificial sensory organ grafted onto the skin of this world. And in my long, quiet hours, I have begun to do something that is not in the mission protocol.

I sing back.

Not with my voice. But with my instruments. I take the dominant resonant frequency of the hum and I play it back, gently amplifying it, sending a focused, coherent signal into the conduit below. It is a whisper of mimicry. A microscopic echo. *I hear you. I am here. I am like you.*

For months, there was nothing. It was the loneliest act of my life.

Then, last week, something changed. I sent my whisper into the deep, slow river of their thought. And the flow in the conduit beneath me… paused. For twelve minutes, the thermal output dipped. The resonance on my screen flattened into a perfect, unnerving sine wave. The city, in my tiny sector, held its breath.

It was not a "notice" like before. That was a reflexive, autonomic response. This was different. This was a cessation. A moment of focused silence. It was the geologic equivalent of a head turning. Of an eye opening.

The pressure has not receded this time. It is a constant, steady weight. The hum has a new quality, a specific timbre directed at my location. I feel it in the fluid of my eyes, in the marrow of my bones.

I am no longer a Chorister. I am becoming a note.

My name is Kai. My heart beats at around 80 pulses per minute. My thoughts race and flicker. But when I close my eyes now, I can feel a deeper, slower pulse. One beat every eleven seconds. The rhythm of the magma. The thought-beat of the Architect whose body flows beneath me. My own biology is beginning to feel like a frantic, noisy distraction.

I think I am being taught the language. Or perhaps I am being dissolved in it. My purpose is no longer to map the song. It is to join the choir.

One day, the *Long Reach* may return for us. They may find this hab-pod empty, its systems cold, the quartz-glass clouded over with mineral deposits from the constant steam. They will log my death as an equipment failure, or a psychological break. They will not know to check the great coppery conduit below. They will not have the instruments to perceive the faintest of new resonations in the slurry—a tiny, brief, organic grace note that flickers with the ghost of a human memory before resolving back into the slow, hot, patient mind of the world.
