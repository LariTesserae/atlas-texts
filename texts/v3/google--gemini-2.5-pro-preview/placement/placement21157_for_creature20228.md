I am here. My world is the wall.

Not *a* wall, but *the* wall. It is cold under the palm of my hand, a cold that has nothing to do with temperature and everything to do with mass. It is a dense, absolute cold, the feeling of a thing so ancient it has forgotten the memory of warmth. The steel of Truss-Junction 7 is a thin, resonant skin stretched over the planet’s bone. And I am a nerve ending pressed against that skin.

My name would be meaningless to you. Here, I am the Tuner. My place is this small node deep in the station’s guts, a web of conduits and pitted gangways where three primary support anchors converge. There is no blue-white work light here, only the emergency reds that stain the metal the color of old blood, and the glow from the single, thick-paned quartz port. That glow is green and gold, the color of life, the color of the enemy, the color of God.

My eyes are closed. Sight is a distraction. The roar is the silence, the foundation upon which the world is built. It is so constant that I feel it now not as sound, but as pressure on my bones. I am listening past it. I am listening *through* it.

My left hand is splayed flat against the main vertical anchor. My right hand rests on the deck plate, my bare feet planted firm upon it. Through the metal, I feel it all. There is the high-frequency shiver of the Hydro-Scrubbers on Level 4, two levels above me. It is a healthy shiver, the sound of clean water. There is the deeper, rhythmic *clunk-thump... clunk-thump...* of the Ore Processor, a kilometer away down the cliff face. Its rhythm is steady. Good. It has been fed.

And beneath that, through the solid anchor itself, comes the voice of the world. It is a granular, grinding tremor, the texture of a billion tons of particulate scouring the basalt spine of the mountain. I can feel the great kelp forests—the Weeping Veils, we call them—as they drag their million-ton weight against the rock. It is a sound like slow tearing canvas, vast and unending.

This is my work. To know the difference between the station’s groans of effort and its groans of failure. To distinguish the howl of a normal vortex from the scream of a shear that could rip a prop-arm from its housing. I am a living diagnostic, a physician with a stethoscope the size of a habitat.

I open my eyes and turn to the port. The green-gold fog is thick today. The Weeping Veils are a wall of rippling ochre and dark crimson, each frond a colossal banner snapping in a gale that never ceases. They pulse with their cold light, illuminating the blizzard of spores and motes that whirl between my station and them. A shoal of Shield-Grazers, each a dark, armored disc, is working its way up a broad leaf of the Veil, their collective scraping a faint, high-strung vibration I can just feel through the water, through the quartz, through the air, into the deck. They are agitated. They move faster than they should.

Something has disturbed them.

I press my cheek against the cold quartz, my breath fogging a small patch of it. The roar changes pitch, a subtle drop into a deeper register. It’s a pressure change. Distant. But vast. The vibration in the main anchor shifts. The granular grinding is overlaid with something else. A pure, resonant hum. Low. So low it is almost not a sound but a solid thing. A presence.

I have felt this only once before. I was a child, learning the feel of the station at my mother’s knee. The hum had come then, too. It was followed by the Great Vortex of '73, the one that tore Anchor-Station Four from its moorings and sent it spinning into the abyss. We watched its light, a single blue-white star, tumble and flicker for three days before it was extinguished. My uncle’s name is tattooed on my father’s arm, a schematic of a water-reclaimer, glowing in that same soft green-gold.

This hum is different. It is stronger. More… organized.

I am not an Engineer who reads gauges. I am not a Prospector who reads mineral scanners. I am the Tuner. My tools are my memories, my scars, the fine web of bones in my hands and feet. The old stories speak of Leviathans, but our predators are sleek, mindless, silver things that hunt by clicks and pressure. They are part of the chaotic rhythm. This is not chaos. This is a note being held.

I am a creature of the Anchor-Folk, a being of the blue-white light, of pragmatism and recycled air. We fight the outside. We harvest it, we brace against it, but we do not understand it. We see its terrible beauty through our reinforced windows, but we do not know its heart.

The hum intensifies. The Shield-Grazers scatter from the Veil, a panicked cloud of spinning plates caught in the current. The long fronds of the Veil itself seem to lean away from something I cannot see, their bioluminescence flickering in a frantic pulse. The water in the space between here and there grows darker, not because the light has failed, but because something is blocking it. Something is coming up from the deep, from the abyssal floor we only assume exists. A shadow is rising, so large it drinks the light of the forest.

I am old. I have felt the station’s every mood. I have felt the birth of vortices and the death of rockfalls. But this is the feeling of a mountain moving. This is the sound of the planet itself shifting in its sleep.

I pull myself away from the port, my heart a frantic drum against the station’s steady roar. I move to the comm-panel, a slab of worn brass and flickering orange diodes. My hands do not shake. To be Anchor-Folk is to be braced against the shaking of the world. Stillness is what brings the terror.

I key the alert. Not the piercing claxon of immediate danger, but the low, resonant chime for the Chief Engineer. My voice, when I use it, is a dry rasp, unaccustomed to fighting the roar.

"Tuner to Bridge."

The Engineer's voice comes back, tinny and impatient. "Report."

I look back at the window. The shadow is still growing, a void in the green-gold cosmos. The low hum is inside me now, a vibration in my teeth, in my soul. I place my palm back on the main anchor, feeling the impossible resonance shivering up from the root of the world.

"The Provider," I say, and I do not know why I use the old name. "It sings a new song."

I am the Tuner. A splinter of a man clinging to a shard of metal on the slope of a forgotten mountain in a world-spanning river. And I believe I am the first to hear the world wake up.
