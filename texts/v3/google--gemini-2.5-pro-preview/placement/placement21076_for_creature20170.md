I would find myself on the weeping skin, halfway between Tap-Station Epsilon-7 and the silent, unfurling bulk of the Calyx men call "The Silent Cantor."

I would be a Tuner.

That is what we are called, those of us who walk the Mass. It’s a title that tastes of irony in a mouth perpetually dry from recycled air. We tune nothing. We are the couriers, the maintenance walkers, the living data-cables in a system that despises inefficiency but cannot escape the need for a physical hand.

I am a function, clad in purpose. My suit is a second skin of dull grey composites, less for protection against a non-existent ecosystem and more for internal regulation. Its primary job is to manage the Hum. The thick collar of my helmet houses an active dampener, a counter-resonance generator that nullifies the worst of the drone's bone-jarring frequencies. It doesn't create silence—that is an impossible luxury—but it reduces the monolithic roar to a manageable, gut-deep vibration, the feeling of standing inside the lowest pipe of a continent-sized organ. The polarized visor turns the blinding white void into a world of navigable contrasts, rendering the fibres of the Lignic Mass in sharp relief. My boots are wide and flexible, distributing my weight like snowshoes, their soles patterned with cleats that grip the slick, cellular weave.

I am a vessel. Strapped to my back, encased in a gyroscopically-stabilized cryo-unit, is my purpose for this traverse: a Harmonic Seed. It is a cultured crystal, a perfect lattice grown in the sterile labs of Lagrange, designed to recalibrate the drilling engine of Epsilon-7. The Tap there has gone "sour," its frequency drifting, chewing the Mass into useless froth instead of liquefying it into pure Nectar. Without this seed, the Tap will eventually tear itself apart or simply fall silent. I am the only way to deliver it. The company doesn't trust dropships this close to the Taps' resonant fields, and they don't trust remote signals with something so vital. So, they trust a single human, walking.

I am a navigator of tremors. The Hum is my map and my compass. To an outsider, it is one sound. To me, it is a language. The deep, foundational thrum is the "Keynote" of the entire planetary grid. But beneath it, I can feel the subtle signatures of individual Taps. Epsilon-7's sour note is a faint, discordant sharpening on my left, a noise like tearing silk buried under an avalanche. The steady, clean drone of my departure point, Delta-4, recedes behind me. I walk the null-lines, the paths of least interference between these sonic behemoths. Distance is not measured in miles but in decibels and hertz. My journey will take three cycles of the cryo-unit's self-degaussing timer—about sixty hours of continuous walking.

I find myself in a place of profound repetition. Step. The ground gives. A pool of water wells, reflecting the white sky in a perfect, momentary circle before it is reabsorbed. Step. Give. Pool. Reflect. Absorb. The world weeps around my boots. The water, filtered through a purification tube in my suit, tastes of cold stone and ozone. It is the only thing here that feels entirely clean.

I am a casual observer of miracles. I call the Scar-Tenders "Scabs." It is not a term of disrespect, but of familiarity. In the shock-zone of a Tap, where the vibrations make the very air seem to shimmer, you see them. They flow. They are not alive in the way I am alive, all frantic purpose and ticking biology. They are a state of matter, a slow, beautiful equation balancing itself out. I have seen them brush against my leg, their touch a cool, effervescent tingle, like static electricity made of gel. They are not aware of me. I am a brief, complex vibration, a skittering noise in their symphony of deep, tectonic chords.

I watch their work. Their crystalline scars, the "Lattices," are the only true art in this world. They gleam under the white light, vast, geometric webs of solidified sugar stretched over the wounded Mass. New walkers are taught to fear them, told that their structures can amplify rogue frequencies. But the old Tuners like me, we know the truth. The Lattices are sanctuaries. Standing in the center of a large one, the Hum feels different. Cleaner. The discordant, tearing overtones are smoothed away, absorbed by the perfect geometry of the web. Sometimes, when the fatigue is a physical weight and the Hum is a drill in my skull, I will seek out a Lattice and rest for a few minutes in its strange, resonant peace. I am resting on a wound that has been made beautiful.

My relationship with the world is one of intimate parasitism. I am an agent of the injury. The Nectar that fuels the city in the sky where I was born is the lifeblood of this ground I walk upon. The Hum that guides me is the sound of its violation. And yet, I am also of this place. I drink its water. I breathe its dense, damp air. My own body, over years of this work, has become attuned to the vibrations. In the silent, sterile transit stations between worlds, I feel an unnerving quiet in my bones, a phantom limb-like ache for the missing pressure of the Hum.

I find myself pausing. The plan says I should not. But ahead, to my right, the rolling plain swells into an unusually pronounced ridge. Peeking over it is the tip of a new Calyx, no bigger than a skyscraper. It is a "Spike," a new growth, perhaps only a few centuries old. It is a silent, impossible thing. I am walking to fix a machine whose only purpose is to drain the life that could, over ten thousand years, have fed this silent giant.

I stand there, a grey figure in a white world, the tremor of industry in my feet and the thought of a silent, slow-blooming mountain in my mind. The cryo-unit on my back hums its own tiny, cold song. My destination is a sound. My origin is a sound. And in between, this vast, weeping, silent, screaming place. I am a nerve ending of the machine, sent out to feel its own pain, and I find myself wondering, not for the first time, what sound the world would make if we all just stopped. Then, I check my chronometer, adjust my heading by a fraction of a degree, and begin to walk again.
