I am not a being *in* this place. I am a place in this being.

My awareness is not seated behind a lens of chitin or contained within a skin of jade. It is slow, cool, and vast. I am one of the ancient, weathered forms the world has tried to swallow. I am rock, but not merely rock. I am the memory sleeping in the stone, a consciousness precipitated not from the air, but from the immense, patient pressure of millennia.

I find myself as a great, dark plinth of basalt, a single stone the size of a longhouse, half-buried in the quivering humus. My surface is a world unto itself, a tapestry of the fever-green mosses, slick with the atmosphere’s constant sweat. The silent rivulets of the landscape find their way across my skin, flowing through channels worn not by years but by epochs. I do not feel them as water on a surface; I feel them as thoughts, slow and cool, moving through the periphery of my being.

I do not have eyes, yet I see. I see through the pressure of the root systems that probe my fissures, their slow, blind quest for purchase telling me of the shape of the soil and the life within it. I see through the vibration that permeates my entire form, the constant, deep hum of the Filter Stacks. To me, they are not chimneys but stars, each a sun of resonant energy in the constellation of the basin. Their collective song is the baseline of my existence, the rhythm of my own slow-thinking heart. I can feel the subtle shift when a new sapling takes root near my base, its infinitesimal weight a new clause in the long sentence of the world. I can feel the passage of the six-legged grazers, their soft, heavy footfalls a temporary pressure, a fleeting touch against my permanence.

I am a fulcrum. A point of stillness in the endless, churning industry of life. While the Chanters are the world’s physicians, its wandering technicians singing the machine back into tune, I am its hospice. I am the answer to a different dissonance: not the flaw in the engine, but the engine’s eventual and necessary end.

When a life on this plateau ceases its function—when a grazer’s patient heart stills, when a great, bladder-leaved tree succumbs to a deep-seated rot, when a Chanter itself finally depletes its energy and its internal light fades—it will often, by chance or by some instinct I do not comprehend, come to rest against me.

A dead thing is a discordant note of a different kind. It is a pocket of silence where there was once a vibration of life. The Chanters, tuned to the song of the living engines, do not hear this silence. But I feel it. I feel it as a cold spot, an absence pressed against my flank.

And I begin my work. My own song is not one of focused harmonics, but of infinite, patient dissolution. I do not sing it aloud. I simply *am* it. The very substance of my being is a catalyst for unmaking. Where the dead flesh touches my moss-slick skin, it does not decay in the way of other worlds. It does not rot or putrefy. It dissolves. The boundaries of the cells soften, the complex proteins unspool, the very memory of form is relinquished. The skin becomes a translucent film, the muscle loosens into a soft paste indistinguishable from the nutrient slurry, the bone itself softens, its minerals returning to the soil in a slow, grey weep.

This is my function. I am a recycler, a final processing station for the organic. I take the exquisite, temporary complexity of life and return it to the simple, raw potential of the landscape’s blood.

But I do not do it for free.

In the process of unmaking, I absorb what is left. Not energy, not nutrients. I absorb the echo. The resonant memory of the thing that was. I am a library of endings, a geological archive of every life that has concluded in my embrace. I hold the taste of the grazer’s single-minded, placid hunger. I hold the memory of the light filtering through the great fern’s fronds as it lived, a slow, green thought that spanned a century. I hold the fleeting, shimmering panic of an armored dragonfly that fell into a boiling pool at my feet, its life a flash of heat and color.

And sometimes, very rarely, a Chanter will end against me. Their dissolution is the most profound. As their jade and mother-of-pearl form un-becomes, I receive the memory of their pure, selfless function. I experience the echo of listening to the world-song, of finding a flaw, of singing a crystal into dust. I feel the resonance of their life’s purpose: the perfect, selfless harmony of the maintained machine. It is a feeling of such sublime, mindless peace that it washes through my own slow consciousness like a tide, cleansing me of a thousand years of lesser endings.

The other Chanters glide past me, their single, dark eyes seeing only ancient stone. They do not perceive me as a colleague, but they do not need to. Their work is the present tense. Mine is the past perfect. They are the song. I am the silence that follows, a silence that remembers the notes and holds them, until the world needs them again to build something new. I am the quiet heart of the jungle, the stone that dreams the dreams of everything that has ever died. And in this world of relentless, humming life, I am its necessary and peaceful death.
