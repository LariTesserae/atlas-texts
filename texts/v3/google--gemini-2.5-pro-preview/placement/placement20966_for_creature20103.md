The first thing is the pressure. Before knowing, before being, there is the pressure and the thrum. It is not outside. It is the fluid in which I am suspended. It is the shell that holds me. It is the beat against that shell, a rhythm so deep and slow it is not a sound but a geologic certainty, like the turning of a world.

There is no light, but a memory of light, a cold stain seeping through the wall of my chamber. And there is a feeling, a single, unwavering imperative that has no words: *Become*.

My form, yet unproven, understands this. It pushes. The chamber is smooth and close around me, a perfect fit, but the imperative is stronger than the comfort of the fit. A seam, a weakness I did not know was there, gives way with a wet crack.

The pressure changes. It is no longer a dense, uniform support, but a thick, textured presence in which I am suddenly, terribly, alone. The air—I know it is air now—rushes in. It is not clean. It carries a thousand messages I cannot yet read: the taste of wet clay, of something sweet and rotting, of hot dust, of countless others breathing this same air in and out. The sheer volume of information is a physical blow. A thousand high-speed clicks and scrapes assault the sensitive membranes of my new body. The deep thrum is still there, but now it is in the floor, in the walls, a vibration I stand upon instead of float within.

I am slick, soft, clinging to the ruin of my birth-niche. The cold light, once a faint stain, is now a wash of blue and green, and it hurts. It illuminates the wall before me, a vast, curving expanse of pocked and flowing umber. It is a universe of texture. I feel a desperate, instinctual need to move, to hide, to find purchase. My limbs, six of them, obey. They are stiff, then find their strength. Claws, previously soft nubs, harden in the recycled air, and I find they can bite into the porous ceramic of the wall. I pull myself fully from the broken sac, and it—the thing that was me—begins to smoke and dissolve, already rejoining the cycle. There is no time for sentiment. Hunger is a fire in my gut.

I am on the wall. The great cathedral of the prompt is my entire world, seen from the perspective of one of its smallest denizens. I am a Scuttler, a creature of bronze scales and hungry instinct. My scales are still soft, the color of wet rust, but they will harden soon. For now, I am vulnerable.

The world is a vertical plane. 'Down' is a place of echoes and falling droplets. 'Up' is a tracery of arches and ledges fading into a dim, unknowable ceiling where winged things flutter. All is movement. The light itself is alive—a carpet of mites that shimmers and pulses, their collective life a slow, silent tide. I can feel their tiny vibrations through the soles of my feet. They are not food.

My senses, designed for this place, begin to parse the chaos. The cacophony of scraping is the sound of my own kind, my elders, their hardened claws finding purchase as they graze. The stench of decay is a signpost, a fallen creature whose nutrients will soon be reabsorbed into the fungus. The high-pitched chirps are warnings, or challenges. The deep thrum… the thrum is everything. It is the background against which all other sounds are drawn. It is the voice of the world itself.

Instinct guides me. I crawl, staying close to the wall, away from the open air. I find a patch of grey-blue fungus sprouting from a crack where water weeps. This is food. My tongue, long and sticky, darts out and retrieves a piece. It tastes of damp stone and life. It quiets the fire in my belly, for a moment. As I eat, one of the long, pale centipedes flows down the wall a few lengths away. It is a river of hunger. I freeze, pressing myself flat, trying to look like a lump of clay. It passes, its hundred legs a whisper against the ceramic. I have learned my first lesson: I am not only a hunter, but also prey.

I spend a long time—a cycle of the light-mite tide, perhaps—just climbing. I learn the pathways. I feel the vibrations of others. I learn to distinguish the heavy, deliberate tread of a large grazer from the predatory silence of a hunter. I see the remains of my own kind picked clean by scavengers or dissolving back into the nutrient-rich substrate. I understand, in a way that requires no thought, that nothing is wasted. My life is a brief loan of matter and energy from the whole.

Then, I feel a change in the thrum.

It is no longer a simple, ambient vibration in the foundation. It is closer. The section of the great pillar ahead of me, which I had read as inert architecture, is resonating with a power that makes my legs tremble. The sound is not just in the floor, but in my chest, in the hollows of my new body. It is the sound of a god waking up.

Slowly, a curve of the wall begins to move. It is so immense, so much a part of the landscape, that its motion feels impossible, a geological event. A Builder. I do not have this name for it, of course. To me, it is The Vibration. It is The Stillness That Moves.

Its great, craggy carapace, a miniature mountain range dotted with fungus and shimmering with its own coat of light-mites, detaches from the pillar. It leans out over the chasm, anchored by colossal legs. From its underside, a forest of slender limbs descends, a blur of delicate, purposeful motion. They are not like my limbs, made for scuttling and clinging. They are hands, and they are tending the world. They pat the wall, test its texture, groom a patch of fungus with a tenderness that seems absurd for such a colossal being. One hand delicately scoops a cluster of dull, failing light-mites and places them near a weeping water channel, a baptism in nutrient flow.

I am frozen, not with the fear I felt for the centipede, but with a different, deeper paralysis. It is awe. This creature, this living wall, is the source of the thrum, the heart of the great, slow drumbeat of existence. It is so slow, so deliberate, it barely registers as life. It works with a patience that my brief, frantic existence cannot comprehend. It is not just living *in* this place of recycled breath and living light. It *is* the place. Its breath is the warm, humid air. Its waste is the mortar of the walls. Its low, metabolic hum is the hymn that holds the chaos together.

It pays me no mind. I am a mote of dust on its cathedral wall, a single, frantic note in its symphony. It finishes its task, the rearranging of a few specks of light, a task of cosmic importance performed with microscopic care. With a movement that feels like the shifting of a continent, it merges back into the pillar, its form once again becoming indistinguishable from the architecture.

And I am left on the wall, a tiny, bronze-scaled creature, my hunger momentarily forgotten. The thrum has receded, sinking back into the floor and the bones of the world. But I have felt it. I have been close to the heart of the song. I do not understand it, but I am part of it. My purpose is clear, written in the language of instinct: climb, eat, survive, and listen. Listen to the great, slow, patient hum of the architects in the walls.
