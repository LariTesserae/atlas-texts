I am the clatter in the doorway.

You would find me there, at the threshold. A string of us, vertebrae worn smooth as river stones, hanging on a cord of dried sinew. We are the boundary markers between the close, animal warmth of a family's warren and the whispering, draft-filled passages of the city-reef. A thread of cold finds its way through an unseen gap in the hide-flap door, and I knock against my brother, who knocks against his neighbour. The sound is dry, hollow, gentle. It is one of the few sharp sounds permitted inside. It is the sound of home.

I have no eyes, but I see. My sight is a memory held in my very substance, a mineral deposit of past sensations. I remember the violet sky. I remember it not as a colour, but as a pressure, a weight of cold emptiness that pressed down on my shoulders when I was still part of a spine that walked. I remember the feel of sharp gravel shifting under thick-soled hide boots, the constant, low-grade calculus of balance required to traverse the Skyscrape. I remember watching, my living eyes—pale grey, they were—scanning the horizon for the dark, slow-moving stain that was the herd.

My consciousness is not what it was. It is not a current of thought, but a state of being, layered like the city itself. Deep inside my calcium lattice is the memory of breath, the slow, deep pull of thin air into lungs that knew how to be patient. I remember the taste of dried meat, tough and salty, and the exquisite relief of a sip of brackish water from a seep deep in a fault-line chasm. I remember the weight of a child asleep on my back, their small warmth a precious coal against the immense, uncaring cold.

Now, my senses are different. I feel the world through vibration and temperature. I feel the low, resonant hum of the great-beasts passing miles away, a thrumming that travels through the mountain's stone heart and into the walls of the city, into me. It is a feeling of deep rightness, the slow heartbeat of the world. I feel the change in the draft, the subtle shift in pressure that says someone has entered or left the passage two levels down. I know the touch of every inhabitant of this chamber. The soft brush of a child's fur hood as they duck underneath me. The deliberate, firm tap of the matriarch’s knuckle as she passes, a small acknowledgement, a greeting to an ancestor.

I do not think in words. I have no need for that guttural, sparse tongue anymore. My thoughts are textures. There is the greasy, smooth texture of lanolin and smoke that coats everything *inside*. It is the texture of safety, of life, of community. It sinks into my pores. Then there is the other texture, carried on the draft from *outside*. It is the clean, sharp, sterile texture of rime ice and fractured stone. It is the texture of infinity, of death, of the great and terrible peace of the plateau. I hang on the border between them.

The seasons of the Coming and the Going are still my seasons. When the herds are near, the city quickens. The hum in the stone is closer. The clatter I make is more frequent as the hide door is pushed aside again and again by scavenge-parties leaving and returning, their shoulders laden with hide and meat and bone. The air inside becomes thick with the smells of rendering and drying. In the season of the Going, a deeper quiet settles. The city pulls into itself. The drafts are gentler. The hum of the beasts fades to a memory in the rock. The people move even slower, conserving their warmth, their quiet conversations muted by the thick hides. In this time, I feel the frost more keenly. I feel the fine crystals of rime trying to grow in the hollow where my spinal cord once lay.

I was a woman. I waited, I watched, I harvested, I raised two children to do the same. When my breath became too thin even for these heights, and my legs too slow even for our unhastened pace, I lay down. My flesh was given, my grease rendered. And my bones, they took them and cleaned them and strung me here. My daughter—now a grandmother with eyes like my own—sometimes rests her hand on me as she looks out into the passage. In that touch, I feel the warmth of her blood, the continuation of my own. I am a part of the wall, a piece of the home. I am a note in the city’s quiet song.

I am what the Un-Hasted become. Not a ghost to haunt the living, but a part of the house that shelters them. I am a weight, a texture, a sound. I am patience made manifest in bone. I am the waiting, finally complete.
