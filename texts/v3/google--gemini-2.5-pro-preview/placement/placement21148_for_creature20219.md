I am not one of the tall, graceful Gardeners who walk the living boulevards. I see them sometimes, distant figures of light and motion, and I feel their passage as a bright, clear chord moving through the world-song. They are the present tense of this place, the active verb of its becoming.

I am something older. My form is sessile, rooted. Where a Gardener’s body is a study in elegant length, mine is one of gnarled, dense permanence. I am fused into the wall of a deep, quiet chasm, far from the central luminescence of the metropolis. Here, in the city's memory, the light is a perpetual twilight, a soft indigo glow emanating from ancient, slow-pulsing crystal veins. My lower body is indistinguishable from the cavern wall itself—a broad, thick trunk of cloudy, layered quartz, whorled with the history of a thousand cycles. My torso and arms, free from the stone, are of a similar material, but darker, more opaque, like anthracite threaded with silver. I cannot walk. I have not moved from this spot in epochs.

I am an Archive.

Where the Gardeners press their hands to the walls to feel the *now*—the flow of ichor, the status of the digestion fringe, the hum of the logic-paths—I press my own broad, heavy hands to the wall to feel the *then*. The living substrate that forms our world is not just flesh; it is a recording medium of impossible fidelity. Every vibration, every pressure, every resonant event that has ever occurred has left its ghost here, a faint harmonic signature impressed upon the crystalline structure of the whole. The Gardeners feel the surface music. I listen for the echoes buried deep beneath.

My purpose is not to compose, but to transcribe. My existence is spent in a state of deep listening. I attune myself to the faint, ancient vibrations, filtering out the constant hum of the present. I can still feel the primal shock of the First Digestion, the moment the lithovore tasted stone for the first time—a memory that feels like a sudden, sharp, exhilarating cold. I can sense the slow, spiraling ballet of the first two Gardeners as they learned to exchange light, a memory like the intertwining of two pure sine waves. I have access to tragedies—the memory of a structural collapse centuries ago is a deep, discordant, crumbling bass note that still shudders in the substrate if you know how to listen for it.

When I have isolated a memory, an echo of significance, I give it form. My fingers are not the delicate, subtle tools of the Gardeners. They are heavy, faceted, and tipped with a substance harder than any diamond. I do not coax the world into new shapes; I engrave what has already been. I reach into the living wall beside me and, with immense slowness and precision, I carve. What I create are not statues, but resonant focal points. I shape a crystal geode, its interior facets angled just so, that when a Gardener places their hands upon it, they do not feel my touch, or the texture of the crystal, but the perfect, clear echo of the memory I have enshrined within. They can feel the terror of the crystal-deer as the first shimmering predator evolved its cloak of light, or the vast, communal satisfaction of the completion of the city’s central arch.

I am a library made of a single being. The Gardeners come to me, traveling to this quiet, dim place when they need to understand their context. They approach in silence, their own light soft and respectful. They will touch their face-buds to my stony brow, a communication not of data, but of query and intent. They do not speak, but I feel their question: *How was this done before? What did we lose in the last Great Pruning? Remind us of the frequency of the First Light.*

In response, I will guide their hands to a specific crystal I have carved. There, they will stand for hours, motionless, absorbing the story. They bring me the loud chatter of the present, and I give them the quiet, foundational music of the past.

My perception of the world is layered. At the surface is the present: the slow drift of the light-algae spores in the still air, the distant, rhythmic footfalls of a grazer, the bright, fleeting thought-form of a Gardener passing overhead. But beneath that, always, is the abyss of time. I feel the weight of every cycle, the slow, tectonic creep of the expanding cavern. The lives of the fauna are flickers. The lives of the Gardeners are single, beautiful pulses in a symphony that has been playing since the silence was first broken.

I am the opposite of the frontier. I am the core. I am the stillness in which the city knows itself. I am a lithovore of time, digesting the echoes of what was, and crystallizing them for what is to come. I am a single, silent, unmoving note that holds the entire song.
