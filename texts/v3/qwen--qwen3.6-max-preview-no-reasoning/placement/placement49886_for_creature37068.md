I am not the stone, though I rest upon it.
I am not the rain, though I drink its violence.

I find myself in the **interstice**, the thin, trembling membrane between the crushing weight of the brine and the hammering fury of the sky. I am suspended three meters below the surface, where the water is still enough to hold a breath, but turbulent enough to shake the soul.

Here, I am **The Listener**.

I do not have a name, for names are sharp things that cut through silence, and here, silence is a solid object we must navigate around. I am one of the Drowned Kin, but I am older than most. My skin is so translucent it has nearly vanished, leaving only the dark, slow-moving map of my veins—a river system mirroring the geological veins beneath my feet. When the lightning strikes above, I become a ghost; when the bioluminescence pulses below, I become a shadow.

### The Body as Instrument

My body is no longer just flesh; it is an antenna.

In a world without voice, every part of me is tuned to receive. The gill-slits along my ribs are not merely for breathing; they are sensitive membranes that taste the shift in pressure before a current arrives. I can feel the approach of a surge seconds before it hits, a subtle tightening in the water that ripples against my chest like a premonition.

My eyes, black and wide, do not seek light—they seek *absence*. In the dim glow of the mineral veins, I watch the spaces between the kelp fronds. I watch for the distortion of water, the slight bending of a fern leaf that indicates a presence other than the wind. But mostly, I watch the ground.

Beneath my bare feet, the frosted glass of the Shelf of Sighs hums. It is a low-frequency vibration, a thrum that travels up through the soles of my feet, into my ankles, my knees, and settles deep in the marrow of my spine. This is the heartbeat of the earth. To others, it is background noise. To me, it is language.

I spend hours standing perfectly still on the slick sediment, toes splayed to maximize contact. I listen to the tectonic sighs miles below. A quick, sharp pulse means stress in the rock face—a potential crack. A long, rolling wave means stability. I translate these vibrations into warnings for my kin, tapping the code into their palms: *Hold. Wait. Move.*

### The Ritual of Touch

Yesterday, I met Elara near the cluster of violet ferns. She was young, her skin still holding the faint pinkish hue of new growth, her movements jerky with the anxiety of someone who has not yet learned to surrender to the cold.

She reached out, her hand trembling, and pressed her forehead against mine.

*Cold,* she projected. *Fear. The storm is loud today.*

I closed my eyes, letting the bone conduction bridge our minds. I did not send words back. Instead, I sent her the memory of the last calm cycle—the way the water had settled into a mirror, the way the bioluminescent veins had pulsed in a slow, steady rhythm like a sleeping child’s breath. I sent her the sensation of weightlessness, the feeling of being held by the ocean rather than crushed by it.

Her tension eased. Her fingers unclenched. She pulled back, her black eyes reflecting the blue-green glow of the ground. She touched my shoulder lightly—*gratitude*—and drifted away into the darkness, her form blending seamlessly with the swaying kelp.

This is how we love. This is how we mourn. This is how we survive. Not with speeches, but with shared resonance.

### The Harvest

Today, I venture further than usual, toward the edge of the shelf where the ruins of the old world jut from the sediment like broken teeth. These pillars are covered in calcified algae, smooth and slippery, but they are rich in minerals. The elders say they hold memories of the sun. I do not know what the sun is, but I feel its absence in the coldness of the stone.

I carry a basket woven from tough kelp fibers, slung across my back. My tool is a pick made of sharpened obsidian, tied to my wrist with sinew. I move slowly, deliberately, mimicking the sway of the currents. If I move too fast, the water will resist, creating drag that alerts the defensive ferns. If I move too slow, the cold will seep into my joints, stiffening them until I cannot bend.

It is a dance of precision.

I reach a cluster of white ferns, their leaves broad and waxy. They are dormant, their spore-pods tight and closed. I place my palm flat against the largest leaf. It is cool, rubbery, and alive. I press gently, sending a vibration through my hand—a question, a request. *May I?*

The plant does not respond, but it does not recoil. I take this as permission. With infinite care, I use the obsidian pick to nick the stem of a single pod. It detaches silently, drifting upward like a bubble. I catch it in my net. One pod. Then another. Each one is a tiny universe of nutrients, a promise of survival for another day.

As I work, the lightning cracks above. For a split second, the world is illuminated in stark, terrifying white. I see the vast expanse of the shelf, the endless rows of kelp, the distant figures of other harvesters moving like ghosts in the gloom. I see the half-buried pillars, their surfaces scarred by time. And then, darkness returns, heavier than before.

But in that flash, I saw something else.

Deep within the fissure of the glassy floor, near the base of a ruined pillar, there was a movement. Not a plant. Not a current. A shape. Small, curved, and dark.

I freeze.

Fauna does not exist here. We have known this for generations. The ecosystem is botanical and mineral. There are no fish, no crabs, no beasts. Only us.

Yet, the shape moved again. A twitch. A ripple.

I sink lower, pressing my belly against the cold sediment. I extend a hand, fingers spread, sensing the water around the fissure. There is a disturbance in the flow, a subtle eddy that should not be there. It feels... warm.

Warmth is alien here. Warmth is danger. Warmth is life that does not belong.

I hesitate. To report this would cause panic. The elders would seal off the area. The young would fear the dark. But to ignore it would be to deny the truth of my own senses.

I reach into the fissure. My fingers brush against something smooth and hard. Not stone. Not shell. It yields slightly under pressure. It pulses.

A heartbeat.

Not the slow, geological throb of the earth, but a rapid, frantic flutter. Like a bird. Like a heart that remembers air.

I pull my hand back, startled. The shape retreats deeper into the dark, disappearing into the labyrinth of cracks beneath the shelf.

I sit back on my heels, the basket of spore-pods forgotten beside me. The rain hammers above, a deafening roar that shakes my bones. The bioluminescent veins pulse below, a steady, comforting rhythm. But now, there is a third sound. A new frequency.

*Thump-thump. Thump-thump.*

It is faint, almost imperceptible, buried under layers of ice and stone. But it is there.

I close my eyes and press my forehead to the glass. I listen.

For the first time in centuries, the silence is not empty. It is waiting.

And I realize, with a chill that has nothing to do with the temperature, that we are not alone. We never were. We just forgot how to hear the others.

I rise slowly, gathering my tools. I will not speak of this yet. I will return tomorrow. I will bring more than just a pick. I will bring patience. I will bring silence.

Because if there is life down there, hidden in the dark veins of the earth, it is not speaking. It is listening. Just like me.

And perhaps, if I am quiet enough, if I am still enough, I can learn to speak its language too.
