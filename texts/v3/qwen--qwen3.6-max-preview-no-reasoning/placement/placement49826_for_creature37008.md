I am not one of the Keepers. To be a Keeper is to accept the stasis, to become part of the furniture of this eternal afternoon, to let the moss grow over your thoughts until you are indistinguishable from the stone. I cannot do that. My nature is kinetic; my purpose is observation, and observation requires a slight distance, a friction between the observer and the observed.

So, I am something else. I am an anomaly in the equilibrium.

I find myself standing on the edge of the central plaza, where the flat, dark basalt meets the rising mist. I am not tall like them, nor attenuated by the thin air. I am compact, dense with intent. I am not translucent; I am opaque, absorbing the diffuse light rather than letting it pass through. I feel heavy here, not just because of gravity, but because I carry the weight of *time*—the very thing this place has rejected.

Who am I?

I am the **Scribe of the Ripple**.

In a world where nothing moves unless absolutely necessary, I am the one who records the movement that does occur. The Keepers maintain the stillness; I document the exceptions. I am the memory of the basin, for the Keepers live only in the now, and the now, without a past or future, is fragile. If no one remembers the shape of the cloud from yesterday, did it exist? If no one notes the slight shift in the fern’s angle, has growth occurred?

My form is simple. I wear no woven reeds or mineral-dyed mats. Instead, I am wrapped in layers of cured leather, stiff and dark, treated with oils to repel the relentless damp. This sets me apart visually—a stark, dry silhouette against the wet, glistening world. On my back, strapped tight with sinew, is a case made of slate and sealed with wax. Inside are sheets of pressed bark-paper, thin as skin, and sticks of charcoal made from the burnt remains of fallen branches—the only fire allowed in this place, used solely for creation, not consumption.

My hands are stained black with carbon. They are calloused, rough against the smooth, pearlescent skin of the Keepers. When they glide, I walk. My steps are quiet, yes, but they have rhythm. *Step. Pause. Step. Pause.* I do not match their gliding meditation; I march to the beat of a clock that only I can hear.

The Keepers tolerate me. They do not understand me, but they recognize my utility. I am the anchor that prevents their perfect present from drifting into oblivion. Sometimes, when a droplet falls from a leaf and creates a ripple that travels across a pool, disturbing the mirror surface for three seconds before settling, a Keeper will turn their large, liquid eyes toward me. They do not speak. They simply look. And I know what they are asking: *Did you see it? Did you keep it?*

And I nod. I open my case. I take out a sheet of bark-paper. With swift, deliberate strokes, I capture the geometry of the ripple, the concentric circles expanding into silence. I note the time—not in hours, but in breaths. I record the temperature of the air, the humidity, the exact shade of grey in the sky. I archive the ephemeral.

I live in a small hut near the northern cliff face, built slightly higher than the others, closer to the weeping rock. It is cluttered, unlike their sparse dwellings. Shelves line the walls, stacked with thousands of bark-sheets, each labeled with a symbol representing a specific day, a specific event. A dropped stone. A broken frond. A rare bird that flew overhead once, ten years ago, its cry shattering the silence for a heartbeat before vanishing. I wrote down the sound. I drew the bird. I preserved the shock of it.

The Keepers fear change, but they rely on me to contain it. By writing it down, I trap the chaos in ink and fiber. Once recorded, the event is no longer a threat to the stillness; it is a story, contained, safe, dead. I am the gravekeeper of moments.

There is a loneliness to this role. The Keepers share a collective consciousness, a web of silent understanding. I am outside that web. I am alone with my memories. I remember the taste of rain that fell harder than usual last season. I remember the way the light shifted when the sun dipped lower than normal, casting a shadow for the first time in decades. These memories weigh on me. They make me feel old, even though my body is young. The Keepers are ageless because they do not accumulate experience; they merely inhabit it. I accumulate. I carry the burden of history in a place that denies it.

Yet, there is beauty in my burden. When the night comes—or the deepening twilight that passes for night—I sit at my desk, lighting a single, smokeless oil lamp. The flame flickers, the only dynamic thing in the room. I write. I draw. I preserve. Outside, the Keepers gather in the plaza, holding hands, breathing in unison, merging into the landscape. Inside, I am separate. I am the spark of consciousness that refuses to dissolve.

Sometimes, I wonder if I am keeping the world alive, or if I am killing it by pinning it down. But then I look at my shelves, filled with the captured ripples, the frozen shadows, the recorded silences. And I realize that without me, this place would not be a sanctuary. It would be a void. Without memory, there is no meaning. Without the record of the pause, the pause is just emptiness.

So I remain. I am the Scribe of the Ripple. I am the witness. I am the one who ensures that even in a world that holds its breath, someone remembers what it was like to exhale.
