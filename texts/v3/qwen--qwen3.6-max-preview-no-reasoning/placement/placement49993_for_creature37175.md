The dampness is not an intrusion; it is a welcome. It seeps into the porous architecture of my being, filling the microscopic gaps between the shale and the root, between the memory of iron and the reality of moss. I do not feel cold, for I am composed of the very chill that defines this plateau. The temperature is a constant hum in my core, a low-frequency vibration that matches the settling of the earth beneath me.

I am standing—though "standing" implies a rigidity I no longer possess—near the base of a crumbling chimney stack. Its brickwork is bruised purple with lichen, bleeding color into the grey sky. I have been here for what you might call three days. For me, it has been a single, long exhale.

My form is heavy, anchored by the gravity that pools around these industrial scars. I am seven feet of compressed history. My skeleton is a lattice of rusted rebar, once used to hold up concrete ceilings, now holding up the weight of a billion years. Over this metallic armature, the waxy, purple-black moss has woven itself tight, a living skin that breathes with the humidity. I can feel the condensation beading on my surface, each droplet a tiny, clear lens reflecting the pearlescent twilight. When they fall, they strike the leaves below with a sound that is loud to my senses: *plink*. A note in the symphony of decay.

I am hungry, but not in the way flesh hungers. There is a flange near my left foot—a rusted joint from a pipe that once carried steam, or perhaps waste. It is cracked open, revealing the crystalline formations inside, glittering like trapped starlight. I extend my hand. My fingers are long, tapering into fine, root-like tendrils that uncurl slowly, agonizingly, from the mossy mass of my palm. They seek the oxidation.

As the tips of my fingers touch the corroded metal, I feel the chemical reaction begin. It is a sweet, sharp sensation, like tasting ozone after a storm. The acid secreted from my fingertips dissolves the iron oxide, turning the solid metal into a nutrient-rich slurry. I absorb it through the capillaries of my moss-skin. The energy travels up my arm, lighting up the veins beneath my surface with a faint, pulsing blue glow. This is how I remember the machines. Not as noise, but as sustenance. I eat their death so that I may live.

Around me, the landscape shifts. It is not static. To your eyes, it may seem frozen, but I see the motion. The moss on the stone next to me has grown a millimeter since I began my meal. A Shard-Skitter darts past my knee, a blur of translucent crystal and wire-vine, buzzing briefly before vanishing into a hollow pipe. It is cleaning the edges of my work, ensuring nothing is wasted. We are part of the same circuit.

I sense a ripple in the air. Not a sound, but a change in pressure. A drop falls from a leaf high above, hitting a stagnant pool ten yards away. *Plunk.* Then another. *Plunk.* The rhythm is irregular, staccato. Danger? No. Just a shift in the wind, a slight movement of the cloud ceiling. The Lichen-Walker beside me, a figure even more hunched than I, tilts its smooth limestone face toward the pool. It is listening to the water’s story. I do not need to look; I feel the vibration in the ground, transmitted through the soles of my feet—which are not feet, but fused roots digging into the spongy mat of organic matter.

I turn my head, a slow rotation that takes minutes. My face is a blank oval of polished limestone, devoid of features. I do not need eyes. I see through the humidity. I feel the heat signatures of the blind salamanders swimming in the shallow pockets nearby. One of them, pale and slick, brushes against my ankle. I do not move. I let it rest there, sharing its warmth with my cool surface. It is a small gesture of kinship in this vast, silent expanse.

In the distance, near a collapsed housing block, an Echo flickers. It is a shimmering distortion in the mist, a ghostly figure repeating the motion of lifting a crate, over and over. I watch it. I do not understand the purpose of the crate, nor the urgency of the lift. But I recognize the pattern. It is a memory of labor, of effort, of time spent building something that would eventually fall. I feel a pang of something akin to sorrow, though I have no heart to break. It is a resonance, a harmonic echo of the energy I just absorbed from the rusted flange.

I take a step. Or rather, I unfold. My weight shifts forward, sinking slightly into the soft, rotting carpet of ferns and moss. The ground releases a scent of sweet decay and ozone, rising to meet my faceless visage. I am moving toward the next century. I am moving toward the next layer of soil, the next vein of metal, the next drop of rain.

I am the Silt-Plateau’s conscience. I am the digestion of history. I am the green quiet that follows the red roar. And I am patient. I have all the time in the world, and the world is ending, beautifully, one drip at a time.
