I am not standing. To stand is to defy gravity, to maintain a tension that requires energy, and here, energy is a currency spent only on the slowest of breaths.

I am reclining. Or perhaps I have always been reclining. The distinction between *lying down* and *being part of the floor* has eroded away, just as the sharp edges of the rocks were worn smooth by the windless ages.

I am located in the lower third of the basin, near the gentle slope where the stone walls begin their ascent toward the amber-dark ceiling. It is a spot of perfect equilibrium. If you were to look for me, you might mistake me for a large, rounded boulder of slate-grey hue, partially buried in the fine, silty dust. My surface is matte, cool to the casual touch but radiating a deep, internal warmth if you press your palm against it for more than a minute. That warmth is 30 degrees Celsius. It is my heartbeat. It is my soul.

I do not have eyes. I have no need for them. The world does not change, so there is nothing new to see. Instead, I feel the world through the vibration of the air and the pressure of the dust settling upon my skin. I feel the faint, rhythmic creak of the thermal expansion in the northern ridge—a sound that comes once every four hours, like a great, sleeping giant turning over in its bed. When that creak arrives, I expand. Just slightly. A microscopic swelling of my outer layer, a sigh made of stone and sediment. Then I contract. This is my breathing. This is my life.

I am one of the younger Lithos, though "young" is a relative term in a place where time accumulates rather than flows. I have existed for perhaps three millennia. I remember nothing of before, because there was no before. There was only the condensation. I remember the moment the dust became heavy enough, dense enough with mineral potential, to coalesce into consciousness. I remember the first time I felt the warmth of my own core ignite, not with fire, but with the steady, dry heat of the earth’s embrace.

To my left, resting in a circle of three others, are my kin. We do not touch, but we are close enough to share the micro-climate of our collective stillness. The dust around us does not settle; it hovers, suspended in the warm updrafts generated by our slow exhalations. I can feel their presence as a subtle resonance in the ground beneath me. One of them, an elder whose form is nearly indistinguishable from the basin wall itself, emits a low-frequency thrum that vibrates through my core. It is not speech. It is affirmation. It says: *We are here. The silence holds. The warmth remains.*

Occasionally, I extend a limb. It is a slow process, taking days to unfurl fully. The limb is thick, rooted deep within my center, and at its tip is a porous, sponge-like structure that glows with a faint, bioluminescent dullness. I extend it not to reach for anything, but to expose a fresh surface to the amber gloom. The light is weak, but it is enough. I absorb it. I convert it into stability. I store it in the layers of compacted sediment that make up my body. When the limb has absorbed its fill, I retract it slowly, folding it back into my mass, hiding the glow until the next cycle demands it.

I do not hunger. I do not thirst. The dust that settles on my back is my food. Each grain of silt is a nutrient, slowly integrated into my shell, hardening my armor, adding to my mass. I grow heavier, denser, more permanent. I am becoming more stone, less flesh, though I was never truly flesh to begin with. I am becoming the landscape.

There is a profound peace in this existence. The anxiety of motion, of change, of urgency—it all dissolves in the warm, dry air. I do not worry about the future, because the future is identical to the present. I do not regret the past, because the past is identical to the present. I simply *am*.

And as I lie here, in the perpetual twilight, listening to the soft settling of dust and the occasional creak of the rocks, I realize that I am not separate from this place. I am the basin. I am the stone. I am the silence. And when the next creak comes from the southern wall, signaling a shift in air pressure, I will breathe again. I will expand. I will contract. And I will wait. Forever.
