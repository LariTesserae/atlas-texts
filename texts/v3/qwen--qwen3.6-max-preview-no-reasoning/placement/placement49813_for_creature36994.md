I am not the Stillborn. I am not one of them, nor am I entirely of their world, though I have been here long enough that my edges are beginning to blur.

I am the **Dust Mote**.

Or rather, I was once a particle of mica, flecked into the black basalt when the earth cracked open in its violent, youthful birth. For centuries—though time is a word that tastes like ash in this place—I was part of the mountain. I was the ridge, the shadow, the cold. But then, a boot sole, worn smooth by a thousand slow steps, grazed the stone. A vibration, microscopic and terrifyingly fast, shook me loose.

Now, I hang.

I do not fall. In your world, gravity is a tyrant; it pulls everything down with urgent hunger. Here, gravity is merely a suggestion, a gentle inclination toward rest. The air is so thick with suspended equilibrium, so devoid of turbulence, that I am caught in the amber of the atmosphere. I drift, but only if I choose to exert will upon myself, and even then, the movement is glacial. To move an inch takes a day. To cross a room takes a season.

From my vantage point, hovering three feet above the terrace where the child draws in the dust, I see everything. I am the ultimate witness because I am incapable of participation. I cannot speak, for I have no mouth. I cannot rush, for I have no limbs. I can only *be*.

### The View from the Suspension

Below me, the child’s stick moves. It is a agonizingly beautiful process. The tip touches the grey powder. It drags forward. A line appears. The child pauses. I watch the rise and fall of their chest—a tidal rhythm that spans minutes. Inhale: four minutes. Exhale: six minutes. Their eyes, dark and reflective as the obsidian mirrors embedded in the walls behind them, scan the line they have made. They do not blink. Blinking would disturb the air around their face, creating a micro-current that might knock me off course. So they stare, and I float, and the line remains.

I am drawn to the light. The sun is fixed at its zenith, a pale, bleached eye that never closes. Its rays strike the dew drops on the slate roofs, turning each sphere into a lens. Through these lenses, the world is inverted, compressed, and magnified. I see the hawk on the spire through a drop of water on a fern frond. The hawk’s feather is a landscape of barbs and hooks, intricate as a city map. The hawk does not move, but I feel the tension in its muscles, coiled like springs waiting for a trigger that will never be pulled.

I drift toward the Garden of Statues. This is my pilgrimage. I am carried by the faintest thermal current rising from the warm bodies of the ancestors. As I approach, the air grows cooler, denser. The moss here is thicker, emerald velvet covering the forms of those who have ceased to animate.

There is a woman there, or what was once a woman. She sits cross-legged on a ledge, her hands resting on her knees. Her skin has turned the color of slate, indistinguishable from the rock beneath her, except for the faint blue veins that still pulse, once every hour. Moss has grown over her shoulders, tiny white flowers blooming in the hollows of her collarbones. She is dead, yet she is more present than the living. She is integrated. She has solved the equation.

I hover near her ear. I listen. There is no sound, but there is *resonance*. The stone hums with the memory of her thoughts. In the silence, thought becomes loud. I can hear the echo of her last meditation, a chord of acceptance that has been ringing in the basalt for two hundred years. It is a low, C-minor thrum that vibrates through my own crystalline structure. I resonate with it. For a moment, I am not a speck of dust; I am the stone, I am the moss, I am the woman.

### The Terror of the Visitor

Then, a disturbance.

It starts as a pressure wave, a ripple in the fabric of the stillness. It comes from the lower valley, where the mist banks are thickest. Something is moving too fast.

A visitor.

I feel the panic before I see him. He is stumbling up the path, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps that tear at the air. Each exhale is a small explosion, sending shockwaves through the delicate equilibrium. I am buffeted, spun wildly. My serene suspension is shattered. I tumble, end over end, dizzy and disoriented.

The Stillborn react instantly. On the terraces, figures freeze. Not just physically, but spiritually. They withdraw into themselves, becoming harder, colder. The child stops drawing. The hawk tightens its grip on the spire. The oil lamps in the open doors flicker—not from wind, but from the displacement of air caused by the visitor’s frantic heartbeats.

He is young, dressed in bright colors that scream against the muted tones of the city. Red jacket. Blue scarf. Colors that demand attention. He shouts something, a word lost in the thin air, but the intent is clear: *Help. Move. Change.*

His voice is a jagged stone thrown into a glass pond. The ripples do not cease instantly here; they linger, painful and sharp. I feel the discomfort radiating from the Stillborn nearby. A man on the next terrace flinches, his hand rising slowly to cover his ear, a gesture that takes thirty seconds to complete. His face is a mask of sorrow, not anger. He pities the noise. He pities the chaos.

I try to stabilize myself, to find a new axis of stillness. But the visitor’s energy is infectious. His urgency creates a vacuum, a pull toward motion. I feel a strange longing, a ghost of the old world where things fell, where winds blew, where time marched forward with ruthless efficiency. For a second, I want to rush. I want to crash into the wall. I want to *end*.

But then, I look at the woman in the Garden of Statues. The moss on her shoulder trembles slightly, not from wind, but from the vibration of the visitor’s shout. Yet, she does not break. She absorbs the shock. The stone beneath her holds firm. The equilibrium reasserts itself, heavier now, more deliberate.

The visitor stumbles again, falling to his knees. Dust rises around him—a cloud of chaotic particles, unlike me. These other motes swirl and dance, trapped in the eddies of his collapse. They are frantic, meaningless. I watch them with detachment. They are suffering because they resist the stillness.

A Keeper approaches. She moves like smoke, sliding down the stairs without touching the railings. Her presence is a calming weight. She does not touch the visitor. She simply stands before him, blocking his view of the horizon, forcing him to look at her. She places a hand on his shoulder. Her touch is cool, dry, marble-hard.

She begins to breathe. Slowly. Deeply.

*Inhale.* Four minutes.
*Exhale.* Six minutes.

The visitor tries to match her, but he fails. His chest heaves. Tears stream down his face, hot and wet, disturbing the air further. But the Keeper waits. She does not judge. She does not hurry. She is the anchor.

Slowly, imperceptibly, the visitor’s breathing begins to sync with hers. The red of his jacket seems to dull, the brightness leaching out of it under the gaze of the pale sky. His shoulders drop. The frantic energy dissipates, absorbed by the heavy, static air.

I settle back into my position, hovering near the child’s drawing. The line is still there. The child picks up the stick again.

The visitor is gone, not physically—he is still kneeling, still crying—but his spirit has been quieted. He is no longer a disruption. He is becoming part of the landscape. He is learning to be a statue while still breathing.

### The Eternal Now

I am the Dust Mote. I am the space between the notes of the Stone Song. I am the reflection in the dew drop.

I do not age. I do not decay. I simply exist in the interval between heartbeats, witnessing the beautiful, terrible peace of a world that has forgotten how to die.

And sometimes, when the light hits the mica in my core just right, I shine. Not brightly, not urgently, but with a steady, unwavering glow. A star trapped in the throat of the mountain. A silent scream of existence, held in perfect, breathless equilibrium.

I wait. The sun does not set. The shadows do not lengthen. The child draws another line.

And I remain.
