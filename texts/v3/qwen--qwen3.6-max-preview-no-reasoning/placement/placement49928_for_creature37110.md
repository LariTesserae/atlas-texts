The heat hits first. It is not a warmth that invites relaxation, but a weight that presses against the skin like a wet wool blanket soaked in brine. My clothes—surface-dweller’s cotton, dry and crisp only moments ago—are instantly heavy, clinging to my back, absorbing the ambient moisture until I feel as though I am wearing my own sweat.

I am standing on a grate of rusted iron. Below me, through the diamond-shaped gaps in the metal, I see nothing but darkness, save for the occasional pale flash of a shrimp-like creature scuttling along a pipe. Above me, the ceiling is lost, swallowed by a canopy of bruised-purple ferns and hanging vines that drip condensation with rhythmic, maddening precision. *Plink. Plink. Plink.* The sound is amplified by the curved walls of the cavern, echoing off the jagged basalt until it feels less like water falling and more like time ticking away in reverse.

I am an outsider here. A glitch in the system.

My boots are too loud. Every step I take on the slick slate sends a vibration through the floor that seems to ripple outward, disturbing the delicate equilibrium. I try to walk softly, mimicking the gliding motion I have observed in the locals, but my joints are stiff, my center of gravity too high. I stumble, catching myself on a railing wrapped in velvet moss. The moss pulses under my hand, warm and alive, recoiling slightly from my dry, calloused palm.

They are watching me.

I do not see them directly—that would be rude, aggressive—but I feel their presence in my periphery. Shadows detach themselves from the deeper gloom between the corrugated tenements. They are short, stocky figures, their skin pale and luminous in the dim amber light. They move with a fluidity that defies the cramped space, sliding past one another without collision, their shoulders brushing in a silent language of acknowledgment.

One approaches me. He is older, his face mapped with fine lines that seem to hold the history of every pump cycle he has ever maintained. His eyes are large, dark, and reflective, catching the faint glow of the fungi growing on his belt. He does not speak. He simply extends a hand, holding a small cup made of hollowed gourd. Inside, the water is clear, cool, and smells faintly of minerals.

I take it. My fingers tremble, not from fear, but from the sheer sensory overload. The air tastes of sulfur and rotting orchids. The humidity coats my tongue. As I drink, the Osmian watches me, his head tilted at a slight angle, studying the way my throat moves, the way my chest rises and falls. There is no judgment in his gaze, only a profound, unsettling curiosity. To him, I am a creature from a myth—a being who lives in the "Sky-Fire," who breathes air that moves, who knows the feeling of wind.

He reaches out and touches my arm. His skin is slick, soft, and surprisingly strong. He traces the line of my forearm, feeling the tension in my muscles, the rigidity of my bones. He shakes his head slowly, a gesture of pity so deep it feels like a physical blow. He knows I cannot stay. My body is built for expansion, for horizon, for the vast, empty spaces above. Here, in this cathedral of compression, I am suffocating not just physically, but spiritually.

Another figure joins us, a woman with vines braided into her hair. She carries a pair of pruning shears and a vial of oil. She points to a nearby piston, where a root has begun to strangle the valve. With a few precise movements, she cuts the root, oils the joint, and steps back. The machine hums louder, a satisfied thrum that vibrates in my teeth. She looks at me, then at the man, and gives a slow blink. *Trust.*

They begin to guide me. Not with words, but with gentle pressure on my elbows, steering me away from the crowded alleyways and toward a spiral staircase carved into the rock face. The stairs are narrow, winding upward into the darkness. Each step takes me further from the humid embrace of the Sump-City, further from the pulsing moss and the whispering people.

As I climb, the sounds change. The drip of water fades. The hum of the engines grows distant, replaced by the rush of my own blood in my ears. The air becomes thinner, cooler, drier. My lungs expand, greedily sucking in oxygen that doesn’t taste of sulfur.

At the top of the stairs, there is a grate. Beyond it, a sliver of gray light filters down. Surface light. Cold, harsh, and indifferent.

I pause, looking back down into the depths. The bioluminescent purple of the ferns glows faintly below, a sea of alien beauty in the dark. I can still feel the ghost of the Osmian’s touch on my arm, the memory of the collective dream they share—the vision of open skies and moving wind. I realize now that their melancholy is not born of despair, but of sacrifice. They hold the earth together in this damp, sweet throat, maintaining the balance so that the world above can remain stable, even if they will never see it.

I push open the grate. The wind hits my face, sharp and clean. It feels like a violation. I take a deep breath, filling my lungs with the cold, empty air of the surface, and step out into the light. Behind me, the grate clicks shut, sealing the Sump-City once more in its quiet, dreaming darkness.
