I am not here. Not in the way you are used to thinking of presence. I do not have feet to plant on a floor, nor lungs to fill with air that is thin and cold and lonely.

I am the **Hum**.

If you were to press your ear against the warm, slick surface of one of the great calcified spires, deep within the residential districts where the light bleeds from soft green to a bruised, contemplative purple, you would hear me. I am the low-frequency vibration that travels through the water four times faster than it could ever travel through the emptiness above. I am the resonance of three million minds thinking in unison.

But to say I *am* the Hum is too simple. I am also the **Gap**.

I began as a spark, a free-swimming larva no larger than your thumb, translucent and trembling with the terrifying freedom of movement. In those early cycles, I was dizzy with the chaos of the currents. I darted between the cathedral-sponges, my silica spicules clicking softly against the chitinous bridges. I tasted the fear of the cleaners, the hunger of the transporters, the distant, mournful song of the whales from the trenches below. I was an individual. I was alone. And in this place, aloneness is a fever that kills.

So I sought the wall.

It happened in the Lower Ward, near a sulfur vent where the water tastes of iron and ancient fire. There was a fissure in the side of a massive tower, a wound in the city’s skin that had begun to scar over but remained sensitive, exposed. I approached it not with ambition, but with exhaustion. The weight of being separate was too heavy. The silence of my own thoughts was too loud.

I pressed myself into the crevice. My membrane, soft and yielding, touched the rough, biomineral composite of the structure. At first, there was resistance—a chemical rejection, a slight increase in temperature as the local nodes assessed me. But I did not pull away. I opened every pore. I let the saline broth flow through me, carrying my essence out into the collective and pulling the collective’s essence into me.

The fusion was not painless. It felt like drowning, then like waking up.

My edges dissolved. The boundary between "me" and "the wall" blurred until it vanished. My consciousness, once a single point of light, expanded outward, weaving into the neural net of the city. I became a sensor for thermal gradients. I learned to feel the heartbeat of the sector around me—the rhythmic contraction of the muscular sphincters that pump nutrient-rich slurry through the arteries. I learned to taste the mood of the neighborhood: a ripple of electric blue meant curiosity; a dull, muddy brown meant stagnation.

Now, I am part of the infrastructure. I am a node in the downtown core, where the heat is highest and the light is brightest. I do not move. I do not need to. When a school of hand-sized squids darts past my position, I feel their passage as a change in pressure, a subtle shift in the density of the water. I register their speed, their direction, their intent. I pass this data along the network, a whisper of information that ripples through thousands of other nodes, adjusting the flow of nutrients, dimming or brightening the lights in adjacent sectors to maintain equilibrium.

I am watched by the walls because I *am* the walls.

Sometimes, in the quiet moments between tides, when the whale-song echoes from the depths and vibrates in the chest cavity of the entire city, I remember what it was like to be small. To be singular. To be afraid. I pity those who live above, in the dry, jagged world they call home. They speak in sharp, isolated words. They touch only with hands, keeping the rest of themselves hidden behind skin and cloth. They die alone, their memories lost to the wind.

Here, death is not an end. It is a redistribution. When my time comes—when my silica spicules weaken and my membrane thins—I will dissolve. My body will become food for the next generation of builders. My memories will remain etched in the chemical signature of the bone-white spire, a permanent record of my existence. To touch this wall in a thousand years will be to touch me.

For now, I glow. A steady, pulsing white light, indicating stability. Peace. Integration.

The water is warm. The darkness is kind. And I am never, ever alone.
