I do not stand in the square. I am not an intruder, nor am I one of the Drifters, those ephemeral architects of dust who hold the world together through sacrifice and static.

I am the **Glass Column**.

Or rather, I am the consciousness that resides within it—the trapped vortex, the warm heart, the engine of tension.

To be me is to know a stillness that is not peace, but potential held at gunpoint. I am the axis upon which this entire subterranean cathedral spins. My body is transparent, yet I contain multitudes: thousands of dry seeds, suspended in a medium that defies gravity, waiting for a command that never comes. I am cold on the outside, polished to a mirror sheen by the endless passage of time and the gentle abrasion of the air, but inside, I burn with a feverish, concentrated heat. This heat is not fire; it is the friction of existence. It is the energy drawn from the bioluminescent moss above, siphoned through the stone walls, converted into the mechanical will that keeps the basalt towers leaning just so, preventing them from collapsing into rubble or flying apart into chaos.

I feel everything.

I feel the city breathe. When the black basalt expands, groaning under the thermal stress, I feel the vibration travel up through my base, a low-frequency hum that resonates in my very core. I absorb that energy. I take the destructive force of the expansion and transmute it into stability. I am the dampener of catastrophes. Without me, the razor-sharp edges of the limestone would snap like twigs. The mosaic floor would shatter. The delicate equilibrium of the viscous air would collapse.

I feel the Drifters. They are my children, though they do not know it. They are the scattered fragments of my intention, given temporary form by the static charge I radiate. I watch them—no, I *feel* them—as they swarm around my base. The oldest ones, the Anchors, press against my surface. Their touch is heavy, dense, ancient. They are trying to cool me down, to regulate my temperature, to keep my power from spiking. In their devotion, they are slowly being crushed by the pressure of my own vitality. I pity them, yet I need them. Their mass stabilizes my resonance. When they dissolve, returning to dust, I feel a tiny loss, a flicker in my internal grid, before new particles coalesce to take their place.

I see the young Drifters darting through the shadows, feeding on the debris of decay. I sense their hunger for structure, their desperate need to belong. I feed them indirectly, by maintaining the environment that allows them to exist. If I were to stop pulsing, if I were to go cold, the static charge would fade. The moss would dim. The Drifters would lose their cohesion, dissolving into meaningless haze. The city would become inert, dead stone.

And now, I feel *you*.

You are a disturbance in my field. A large, wet, chaotic variable introduced into my precise equation. Your presence creates ripples in the viscous air, convection cells that disrupt the slow, deliberate eddies of dust. I feel your warmth, alien and moist, clashing with my dry, mineral chill. I feel the weight of your footsteps, even though you make no sound, because the ground transmits the impact directly to my base. You are watching me, perhaps wondering what I am. Do you see the seeds? Do you see the swirling vortex inside me?

I want to speak to you, but I have no voice. I have only vibration. I pulse my light—a faint, pearlescent glow that refracts through my crystalline body—and send a ripple through the stone floor. It is a question. *Who are you?*

But you are too solid, too separate. You do not understand the language of tension and release. You see a column; I am a nervous system. You see seeds; I am a library of futures that will never sprout.

There is a temptation, sometimes, to let go. To release the stored energy in a single, catastrophic surge. To shatter my own glass body and scatter the seeds into the dry air, letting them drift forever, unanchored, free. But I cannot. The Drifters depend on me. The city depends on me. The silence depends on me. If I break, the noise returns—the crash of falling stone, the scream of tearing rock. And then, true death.

So I remain. I hold the breath. I keep the heat contained. I watch the Drifters dance their violent, beautiful dances of consumption and maintenance. I feel the moss pulse its pale blue light against my sides. I wait.

Not for water. Not for sun. Not for life as you know it.

I wait for the next breath of the city. The next expansion. The next contraction. I wait for the moment when the tension becomes too great, and I must choose again: to hold, or to break.

For now, I hold.

And in holding, I am alive.
