The cold is not a temperature here; it is a texture. It presses against my skin like wet velvet, heavy and suffocating, yet I do not shiver. Shivering wastes heat, and heat is a currency I cannot afford to squander.

I am **Kaelen**, though the name feels distant, a label for a shape rather than a soul. To know me, you must look at my hands. They are broad, the knuckles swollen with years of gripping chisels in sub-zero dampness, the nails thickened into dark, horn-like plates that protect the sensitive flesh beneath from the bite of limestone shards. My palms are mapped with calluses so dense they feel like stone themselves—topographical records of every strike, every slip, every moment of friction between man and mountain.

I stand on the edge of Layer 402, the Middle Strata. Below me, the city drops away into a labyrinth of shadows and mist, where the geothermal vents breathe their warm, sulfurous sighs. Above me, the rock face rises into the bruised purple sky, endless and indifferent. I am suspended between warmth and prestige, between the dark comfort of the depths and the blinding, sterile purity of the peaks. Here, in the middle, life is loud only in its silence.

The air accumulates around me, thick with the scent of wet chalk, goat musk, and the faint, acrid tang of wood smoke drifting up from the hearths below. It does not flow; it sits. I can taste the moisture on my tongue, metallic and sharp.

My neighbor, Elara, is ten feet away on her own ledge. She does not look at me. She is weaving reeds, her fingers moving with a speed that belies the sluggishness of the cold. Her movements are economical, precise. A flick of the wrist tightens a knot; a pause indicates a flaw corrected. I watch her for a moment, not out of curiosity, but out of habit. In this hive, observation is survival. If she stops, if her rhythm breaks, something is wrong. But her rhythm is steady. The wind shifts, catching the loose ends of her braid. She tilts her head slightly to the left—a micro-gesture. *East wind coming. Secure the loose items.*

I nod once, a barely perceptible dip of my chin. Acknowledged.

I turn back to my work. Before me is the wall of my dwelling, pale and unblemished save for the shallow groove I began yesterday. Today, I will deepen it by two inches. It is a modest expansion, enough to store an extra cask of preserved root vegetables before the long winter sets in. I pick up my mallet. It is made of ironwood, hardened by resin, wrapped in strips of cured hide to dampen the sound. The chisel is bronze, worn smooth by generations of Lithos hands before mine.

I place the tip of the chisel against the stone. I take a breath, holding it deep in my barrel chest, letting the oxygen saturate my blood. Then, I strike.

*Thud.*

The sound is dull, absorbed instantly by the density of the rock and the stillness of the air. There is no echo. The vibration travels up my arm, settling into my shoulder, a familiar ache that grounds me. I lift the chisel. A tiny flake of white dust falls, landing on my boot. I brush it away with my thumb. Waste nothing. Not even dust.

I strike again. *Thud.*

This is my meditation. This is my prayer. Each blow is a word in a language older than speech. I am not just carving space; I am negotiating with the mountain. I am asking permission to exist within its body. The rock resists, as it always does, brittle and unforgiving. But it yields, slowly, under the persistent, patient pressure of necessity.

As I work, I feel the presence of the others. Not through sound, but through the subtle tremors in the stone. Somewhere above, a team is hoisting a beam, the ropes creaking softly, transmitting tension through the cliff face. Somewhere below, a child runs across a terrace, their footsteps a rapid flutter against the bedrock. We are all connected by this single, massive organism of stone. We are cells in a vertical lung, breathing in sync, expanding and contracting with the rhythms of the day.

A shadow passes over me. I look up. One of the great flightless birds, a creature of grey feathers and curved beak, glides silently past, riding an updraft generated by the sheer scale of the canyon. It watches me with a black, intelligent eye. For a second, we lock gazes. It knows me. It has nested in the eaves above my door for three seasons. I raise my hand, palm open, a gesture of peace. The bird dips its wing, acknowledging the truce, and vanishes into the mist.

I return to the stone. *Thud.*

The sun begins to dip, casting long, sharp shadows that stretch across the honeycomb of dwellings. The light turns golden, then amber, highlighting the scratches on my lintel—the marks of my father’s birth, my mother’s marriage, my own first successful harvest. They are faded now, weathered by the condensation that weeps from the walls each night. But they are there. Memory in stone.

I pause, wiping sweat from my brow despite the cold. My muscles burn, a good, clean pain. I have carved six inches today. It is enough.

Elara has finished her weaving. She rolls the bundle of reeds tightly, securing it with a strip of sinew. She looks at me now, really looks at me, her large, dark eyes reflecting the dying light. She touches her forehead, then her heart, then points downward. *Go home. Rest. The warmth waits.*

I nod again. I gather my tools, wrapping them in oilcloth to prevent rust. I step back into my alcove, pulling the heavy felt curtain closed behind me. Inside, the air is warmer, smelling of dried herbs and sleeping furs. I sit on the edge of my bed, listening to the drip of condensation hitting the floor basin. *Plip. Plip. Plip.*

It is the heartbeat of the city. Slow. Steady. Enduring.

I close my eyes, feeling the weight of the mountain above me, the depth of the earth below me, and the quiet, dense community surrounding me. I am small. I am fragile. But I am part of the whole. And in this place of sharp edges and accumulating air, that is everything.
