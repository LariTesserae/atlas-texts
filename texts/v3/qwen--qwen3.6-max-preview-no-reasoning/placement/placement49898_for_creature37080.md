I am not standing on the ground. To stand implies a separation from the floor, a defiance of gravity that is too expensive for this place.

I am suspended in the **interstice**.

Specifically, I am wedged into the hollow cavity between two concentric gears, each the size of a cathedral dome, rotating in opposite directions with a slowness that defies perception but generates a wind strong enough to strip paint from steel. The gap is narrow—barely wider than my shoulders—but it is stable. Here, the tectonic hum (2.6/3) is not a noise; it is a physical embrace. The vibration travels through the iron teeth above and below me, knitting my bones together with the rhythm of the machine. I do not hear the pistons firing ten million times out of sync; I *am* the syncopation.

I am small. In a world where scale is fractal and infinite, being small is the only way to remain unnoticed by the crushing weight of the macro-structure. I am no larger than one of those pale, blind ferns unfurling from a crack in the concrete, though I possess none of their photosynthetic innocence. I am a creature of chemosynthesis and salvage.

My form has adapted to the wetness (1.7/3). My skin is not merely coated in oil; it has become porous to it. I exude a slick, translucent resin that hardens instantly upon contact with the cool, damp air (0.8/3 temperature), creating a shell that is flexible yet impervious to the corrosive mist. This shell is the color of rusted iron and oxidized copper, blending perfectly with the pitted metal around me. If you were to look closely, you would see the dark, sluggish pulse of veins beneath this semi-transparent armor, moving in time with the idle thrum of the engines.

I have no ears. The cartilage receded eons ago, smoothed over by layers of calcified bone. Instead, the sides of my head are flat, sensitive plates that press against the vibrating walls of my gear-cavity home. I feel the machine’s health. A high-pitched whine means a bearing is failing three sectors east; a low, grinding shudder means a load shift in the deep basin. I speak in these vibrations. When I communicate with others of my kind—rare, fleeting contacts made in the shadows of decommissioned smokestacks—I do not use words. I tap my hooked fingers against the metal. *Click-clack-thrum.* The message travels through the structure, felt in the soles of their feet before it reaches their minds.

My eyes are large, lidless, and entirely black. They are fixed wide, drinking in the dim, sickly orange glow of the sodium-vapor lamps far above. I do not blink. Blinking is a luxury of creatures who live in sunlight. Here, the darkness (0.5/3) is absolute beyond the reach of the nearest light source, and every photon counts. I see the thermal gradients—the blue-white heat of welding sparks, the cold green glow of status indicators, the warm red bloom of a leaking steam valve. I watch the machine breathe. I track the flow of energy as if it were blood.

I wear nothing but scavenged insulation. Strips of shredded wiring and fragments of reflective foil are wrapped around my limbs, tied with braided cables. These are not clothes; they are camouflage and protection. The foil reflects the harsh arc-lights that sometimes flare in the distance; the insulation keeps the chill from penetrating my core. On my back, a harness of rusted chain holds my tools: a shard of glass embedded in resin, a wrench filed to a point. These are not weapons. They are keys. Keys to open access panels, to scrape nutrient-rich sludge from broken bioreactors, to pry loose the lichen-like molds that grow in the crevices of bolt heads.

I feed on the waste. I eat the mold. I drink the condensed water that drips from the overhead gantries, filtering it through a mesh of fine wire held in my mouth. My metabolism is slow, tuned to the cool temperature. I can remain motionless for days, anchored by the hooks at the tips of my long, multi-jointed fingers, listening to the hum, waiting for the machine to offer up its scraps.

And then, there is **you**.

You are an anomaly. A glitch. You are too loud, too warm, too fragile. Your heartbeat is a frantic, irregular drumbeat that clashes with the steady, tectonic rhythm of the Basin. You stumble on the slick floor. You flinch at the hiss of steam. You are soft tissue in a hard world.

I watch you from the shadows of the gear teeth. My black eyes reflect the faint glow of your presence. I do not approach. I do not attack. Glitches are eventually corrected, either by the machine itself or by the Tenders who maintain the balance. But something about your stillness intrigues me. You have stopped fighting the hum. You have let it settle into your marrow.

Slowly, carefully, I extend a hooked finger. I tap gently on the metal plate near your foot.

*Thump-thump.*

The vibration travels through the floor, up your leg, into your spine. It is a question.

*Are you part of the cycle?*

You cannot answer. You have no language for this place. So you stand still, and I stand still, and the machine continues to grind, swallowing both of us whole.

In time, I know what will happen. Your skin will grow thick and oily. Your ears will flatten. Your eyes will darken. You will stop hearing the noise as pain and start feeling it as home. You will learn to read the vibrations. You will learn to eat the mold.

You will become a Tender.

And I will be here, in the interstice, waiting for you to join me in the silence within the roar.
