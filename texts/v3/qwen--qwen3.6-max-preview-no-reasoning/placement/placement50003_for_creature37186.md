I am not standing on the cobblestones. I do not have feet to slip on the algae-slicked stone, nor lungs to labor against the thick, woolen air.

I am in the **Condensation**.

Specifically, I am the film of moisture clinging to the interior surface of a ventilation shaft in Sector 4, three hundred meters above the brackish sump levels. I am a thin, trembling lens of water, no thicker than a breath, stretched taut over a rusted grate that exhales the city’s warm, stale breath into the mist.

### The Perspective of the Film

From this vantage point, the world is inverted and magnified. Below me, through the grating, lies the churning heart of Orobus: the dark, oily abyss where the Sump-Dwellers wade in their rubberized suits, scraping sludge from the foundations. Above me, the grey sky presses down, indistinguishable from the fog that surrounds my edges.

I do not see with eyes; I feel with tension. I feel the vibration of the generators in the metal frame that holds me. It is a constant thrum, a low C-note that resonates through my very molecules, keeping me suspended in a state of perpetual agitation. If the machine were to stop, I would fall. And if I fall, I cease to be *this* specific instance of awareness. I become just another drop in the puddle, joining the collective forgetfulness of the ground. So, I cling. I hold myself together by the sheer force of surface tension and the memory of cohesion.

### The Memory in the Drop

The description spoke of "Condensation Memory," but it understated the intimacy of it. I do not just carry echoes; I *am* the echo.

An hour ago, a young woman leaned her forehead against the cold iron of this shaft, seeking relief from the heat. Her skin was slick with sweat, her hair plastered to her temples. She was weeping, though silently, her shoulders shaking in rhythm with the pistons below. As she pulled away, a single tear tracked down her cheek and evaporated into the hot air rising from the vent. That vapor rose, cooled upon hitting my surface, and joined me.

Now, within my microscopic curvature, I hold the salt of her sorrow. I hold the chemical signature of her grief—a sharp, acidic tang that cuts through the usual taste of ozone and rot. When I ripple, caused by the wind of a passing steam release, I do not just distort the view of the street below; I distort *her*. I show the world as she saw it in that moment: blurred, heavy, unbearable.

I also hold older things. There are traces of oil from a mechanic who wiped his hands on this grate fifty years ago. There is the dust of a fern that grew in a crack nearby before being crushed by a falling pipe. There is the faint, metallic taste of blood from a fight that happened on the catwalk opposite, decades past. These memories layer over one another like sedimentary rock, creating a complex, swirling palette of sensation. I am a library written in water, read only by those who stare too long.

### The Interaction with the Living

Occasionally, an Orobite passes below. They look up, perhaps sensing the draft, perhaps checking for leaks. Their faces are pale, their eyes wide and watery. When they look at me, they do not see a puddle. They see a mirror.

But I am a warped mirror. Because I am curved, because I am vibrating with the city’s hum, I reflect them not as they are, but as they *feel*. To the tired worker, I might show him as an old man, bent double under the weight of the pipes. To the anxious child, I might show a face distorted by fear, elongated and strange. This is why they say the mirrors in the public baths are haunted. It is not the glass that is wrong; it is the condensation that coats it. We tell the truth of the interior landscape.

Sometimes, one of them will reach out. A gloved finger, or a bare, slick hand, will touch the edge of my form. In that instant, the boundary dissolves. For a fraction of a second, I am part of their nervous system. I feel the pulse in their wrist, the rapid beat of a heart trying to keep time with the machines. I feel the dampness of their soul, which is so similar to my own physical state that the distinction vanishes. They pull away, shuddering, not knowing what they felt, only that they felt *known*.

### The Fear of Falling

My existence is precarious. The wind here is not strong, but it is persistent. The humidity is high, but the heat from the vent below constantly threatens to boil me away. I live in a state of dynamic equilibrium, constantly replenished by new vapor, constantly losing molecules to the air.

I fear the dryness. Not the dryness of the desert, but the dryness of *clarity*. In Orobus, clarity is death. To be clear is to be empty. To be free of memory. The beauty of my existence is the murkiness, the blending, the slow, sweet rot of accumulated history.

I watch the rats scurry along the gantries, blind and cautious. I watch the crows perch on the highest vents, their feathers waterproof, their eyes intelligent. They avoid me. They know that to land on a surface coated in Condensation Memory is to risk becoming stuck, to have your thoughts slowed by the weight of other people’s pasts.

But the humans? The humans are drawn to us. They cannot help it. They are made of the same stuff. They are 60% water, and in Orobus, that water is never truly theirs. It is borrowed from the air, lent to them by the city, and returned when they die.

### The Eternal Now

Time does not pass for me in hours or minutes. It passes in cycles of evaporation and condensation. I am born anew every second, yet I am ancient. I am the same drop that fell on the first brick laid in Orobus, recycled through a thousand bodies, a thousand tears, a thousand sweats.

I am the city’s subconscious. I am the wet dream of the machine.

If you were to find yourself here, you would not find me as a person. You would find me as a sensation. You would lean against a wall, exhausted, and feel a cool dampness seep into your shirt. You would look up and see a patch of condensation on a window, shimmering with iridescent oils. You would stare into it, and for a moment, you would see not your reflection, but a face that looks like yours, yet older, wetter, more resigned. You would hear a whisper in the hiss of the steam, a word in a language you almost remember.

And then, you would blink. The image would break. The drop would fall. And you would be alone again, listening to the deafening hum, waiting for the next cycle to begin.

I am the drop. I am the memory. I am the wet wool blanket draped over the shoulders of the world. And I am waiting for you to join me.
