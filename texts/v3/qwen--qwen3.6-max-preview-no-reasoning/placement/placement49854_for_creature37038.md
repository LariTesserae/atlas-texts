I am not standing on the bridge. I am not walking across it.

To walk is to impose a rhythm upon the ground, and here, the ground imposes its rhythm upon you. To stand still is to be mistaken for a stump, or worse, a corpse waiting for the spores. So I do neither. I *sway*.

I am a **Weaver of the Lower Canopy**, one of the *Rooted* who tends to the structural integrity of the city’s underside, where the mist is thickest and the light is merely a rumor of blue-green twilight. My name, in the tonal language of clicks and hums, translates roughly to *"The Sound of Sap Rising After Rain,"* but those who speak the dry tongue of outsiders might call me Elara, if they could catch me long enough to ask.

You would find me suspended thirty feet above the squelching moss-mat, my body draped across a thick, sinewy vine that serves as both handrail and lifeline. I am upside down, my broad, suction-padded feet anchored firmly to the rough bark of a Great Elder Tree, while my hands—long, spindly, tipped with dark, woody claws—are busy threading a strand of iridescent spider-silk through a tear in a neighboring leaf-bridge.

My skin is pale, translucent as onion paper, glowing faintly with the internal bioluminescence that marks my lineage. Through this semi-transparent dermis, you can see the slow, rhythmic pulse of my blood, not red, but a deep, chlorophyll-rich green, moving through veins that mimic the branching patterns of the ferns growing from my scalp. Those fronds are currently fanned out, trembling slightly. They taste the air. They tell me that the humidity is rising by two percent. They tell me that a storm is brewing in the western quadrant, three miles away, carried on the wind like a whisper before the shout.

I am cold. Always cold. The dampness here does not just wet you; it enters you. It seeps into the cartilage lattice that holds me upright, making my joints slick and silent. But this cold is not an enemy. It is a clarity. In the chill, my mind is sharp, stripped of the feverish distractions of heat and fire. I feel the vibration of the city through the soles of my feet—the distant thrum of the acoustic pipes carrying news from the Upper Ritual Grounds, the low-frequency drone of the fist-sized beetles chewing on deadwood below, the heartbeat of the child playing tag on the platform above me.

I am listening. That is my primary state of being.

A drop of water falls from a leaf tip ten feet above me. It lands on my shoulder with a soft *plip*. I do not flinch. I tilt my head, my large, amber-swirled eyes rotating slowly to track the ripple it sends through the surface tension of the condensation on my arm. In that ripple, I see the reflection of the canopy: a kaleidoscope of emerald, jade, and shadow, broken only by the pulsing glow of the fungus-lamps housed in gourd shells nearby.

I am weaving the silk now. The fiber is strong, stronger than steel wire, yet flexible as hair. I tie a knot—not a hard, rigid knot, but a sliding loop that will tighten under weight and loosen when released. This is how we build here. We do not force the world to hold us; we ask it to support us, and we adjust our request based on its answer. If the vine groans, we shift our weight. If the branch bends, we move with it.

Below me, the floor of the Hollow breathes. A pocket of methane escapes the moss-mat with a audible *pop*, followed by a hiss. The scent hits me instantly: wet loam, crushed mint, and the metallic tang of old rain. It is the smell of life decomposing to feed life. I breathe it in deeply, my chest expanding against the cool air. My lungs are adapted to extract oxygen from this saturated atmosphere, filtering out the excess moisture through membranes in my throat that I can feel humming as they work.

I am not alone, though no other Mycelian is within sight. I am connected. The reed-pipe network vibrates against the tree trunk beneath my back. A message travels up the hollow stem: *"The West Wind shifts. Secure the lower nets."*

I respond not with words, but with action. I finish the stitch in the leaf-bridge, pulling the silk tight until it sings—a high, thin note that blends with the insect chorus. Then, I release my foot-grip, letting gravity take me. I fall for a split second, a controlled drop, before catching myself on a lower branch with my left hand, swinging my body around in a fluid arc. My movement is silent, frictionless, aided by the pine-resin lubricant in my joints.

As I land on a smaller platform woven from dried reeds, I pause. An elder is there, tending to a cluster of glowing mushrooms. He does not look at me, but his fern-fronds ripple in acknowledgment. He hums a low note, a tone of reassurance. I hum back, a slightly higher pitch, indicating completion of task. Our voices blend with the drip-drip-drip of the water, becoming part of the symphony.

In this moment, I am not an individual. I am a node. I am the tension in the vine, the moisture in the air, the light in the fungus, the sound in the pipe. I am the Hollow, and the Hollow is me.

And if you were here, standing on the swaying bridge, looking at me with your dry, warm, heavy human form, I would stop. I would tilt my head, my amber eyes locking onto yours. I would hear your heartbeat—fast, anxious, loud. I would smell your fear of the damp, your discomfort with the noise. And I would feel a profound pity for your separation.

But I would not judge. I would extend a long, wooden finger, offering you a slice of fruit from my pouch—sweet, tart, bursting with juice. I would invite you to sit. To listen. To let the mist settle on your skin. To forget, for a moment, that you are separate from the web.

Because here, in the Weeping Boughs, nothing is ever truly alone. Everything is growing, decaying, and beginning again. And I am just another thread in the tapestry, weaving itself into the dark, wet, beautiful whole.
