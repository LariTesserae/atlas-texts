The cold does not bite; it *defines*. It is the chisel that carves my awareness out of the numbness.

I am standing on the lip of a fungal ridge, my feet sunk slightly into the spongy, yielding mat of the moss-floor. The sensation is intimate, almost invasive. I can feel the pulse of the substrate beneath my soles—a slow, hydraulic throb as sap moves through the fibrous roots. If I stay here too long, the moss will grow over my boots. In an hour, I will be part of the landscape. In a day, I will be food for the lichen.

So I move.

I am **Kaelen**, though names are slippery things here, shed like snake skin when they become too heavy to carry. I am a **Listener** of the *Silver-Current*, a kin-group of twelve souls who have drifted together for three growth-cycles. We are currently moving West, or what passes for West in a world where the horizon curves upward and swallows the concept of direction.

My body is a study in tension. I am tall, painfully thin, my ribs visible beneath the translucent, pale skin of my chest. I wear no fat, only layers of woven reed-husk and cured fungal membrane, dyed with berry-juice to break up my silhouette against the white glare. My eyes are wide, black pools that do not blink. Instead, the nictitating membranes slide across them every few seconds, wiping away the diamond-dust condensation that clings to my lashes. This keeps my vision sharp, but it leaves me feeling perpetually exposed, as if I am staring directly into the soul of the Cavern without protection.

In my left hand, I hold a staff of polished bone, topped with a cluster of hollow seed-pods—my chimes. They are silent now, held tight against my palm to dampen any accidental vibration. In my right hand, I grip a flint knife, its edge dulling even as I watch it, eaten by the humidity.

The air smells of crushed mint and ozone, sharp enough to sting the inside of my nose. It is a clean, sterile scent, devoid of decay because decay happens too fast to smell. One moment a leaf falls; the next, it is gone, absorbed into the green carpet. There is no rot here. Only consumption.

I pause, tilting my head. The reeds behind us are humming. It is a low, mournful chord, vibrating in the marrow of my shinbones. But there is a discordance—a slight tremor in the lower frequencies. The ground is shifting. Not an earthquake, but a *growth spasm*. A patch of moss ten paces to my left ripples violently, then rises six inches as a new cluster of silver-leaved shrubs bursts forth, unfurling their fronds with audible snaps.

*Move,* the ripple says. *Do not anchor.*

I raise my free hand, fingers curled in the sign for *Advance*. Behind me, the other members of the Silver-Current respond instantly. There is no discussion. Discussion takes time, and time is the currency we cannot afford. Elara, our senior Listener, glides past me, her movements fluid, her feet barely lifting from the sponge-mat. She does not look at me. Her gaze is fixed on the middle distance, watching the way the light bends around the distant fungal spires.

We glide forward, a procession of ghosts in a cathedral of glass and green.

As we pass a clearing, I see it: the Anchor. A slender pole of dark wood, untouched by the encroaching moss. At its top, the black stone bowl holds its single, trembling drop of water. It reflects the blinding, shadowless sky above, which mirrors the forest floor below, creating an infinite loop of green and white.

For one breath cycle, I stop. My Current stops with me. We stand in silence, the only sound the drip of condensation falling from the tips of nearby ferns. I stare at the drop. It defies physics. It defies the hunger of this place. For a second, I imagine what it would be like to touch it—to feel something that does not change, something that does not demand to be eaten.

But the thought is dangerous. Stasis is death.

Elara taps her chime once—a soft, high click. *Storm coming.*

The sky above us darkens, not with clouds, but with a thickening of the pearlescent glow. The air pressure drops, popping in my ears. The scent of ozone intensifies, becoming metallic, electric.

"Up," I whisper, the word barely leaving my lips.

We scatter. Not in panic, but in practiced dispersal. Each of us finds a depression in the moss, a natural cup formed by interlocking roots. We unfold our shallow bowls made of cured leaf-skin and wait.

The rain arrives not as a drizzle, but as a hammer blow. Cold needles of water strike the canopy above, shattering into mist before reaching us, but some get through. We catch what we can, the icy liquid shocking our throats. It tastes of minerals and lightning. It is the taste of survival.

As quickly as it began, it ends. The pools in the depressions begin to drain, sucked back into the porous earth. We drink hurriedly, filling our waterskins—bladders made from the stomachs of the small, blind creatures we rarely see but often hear scuttling in the dark.

Then, we rise. We roll our mats. We secure our chimes. And we move again.

Behind us, the Anchor remains. The drop of water still trembles. But the moss around it has already begun to creep closer, inch by inch, reclaiming the space. By tomorrow, the pole may be half-buried. By next week, it may be gone entirely, digested by the Verdant Cavern.

We do not look back. To look back is to invite the Silencers. To look back is to remember that we were ever anywhere else.

I take a step, my foot sinking into the soft, living earth. I feel the roots shift beneath me, sensing my weight, testing my intent. I am light. I am quick. I am unrooted.

And for another hour, I am alive.
