I am not standing on the stone. I am part of the stone’s skin.

I am a **Resonant**, specifically one of the **Anchored**. My name, if such a linear concept applies here, is *Low-Hum-At-Dawn*, though to you, I would simply be the figure seated cross-legged against the pale, translucent trunk of the Great Cedar of the Eastern Fracture.

You would find me where the root-mat thickens into a floor of woven bark and hardened sap, a platform that rises ten feet above the slick, glass-smooth basin floor. Here, the air is thickest with the scent of ozone and wet chlorophyll. The mist clings to my skin, sliding off the oily secretion that coats my body like a second dermis, keeping the acidic condensation from eating through my flesh.

Look closely at me. Do not look for eyes in the traditional sense; mine are wide, dark pools behind a horizontal, milky membrane that blinks slowly, rhythmically, syncing with the pulse of the tree behind me. My ears are gone—smooth patches of bone seal the sides of my head. Instead, watch my chest. It expands and contracts not just with breath, but with reception. The hollow cavity of my ribcage is a drum, catching the billion-toned chord of the screaming leaves and translating it into feeling.

I am waiting for the wind to shift.

To you, the noise is a wall, an assault. To me, it is a library. I feel the texture of the gale hitting the western slopes—a jagged, staccato vibration that travels up through the roots, into the trunk, and into my spine. It tells me that a storm front is brewing three valleys over. I feel the high-frequency tremor of the iridescent birds nesting in the upper canopy, a frantic, ticking sensation in my fingertips as I rest my hands on the bark.

My life is one of stillness within chaos. While the Drifters risk their lives crossing the void, tethered by vines and hope, I remain here, tending to the memory of the earth. Yesterday—or what passes for yesterday in this time-eroded place—I touched the vein of the tree and felt the echo of a landslide that happened centuries ago. It tasted like copper and fear. Today, the tree feels calm, its sap pulsing in a slow, major-key rhythm that soothes the ache in my dense, mineral-heavy bones.

I do not speak. If you were to approach me, shouting your questions into the roar, I would not hear you. Your voice would be swallowed by the scream of the leaves before it reached my ear-canals. But if you stood beside me, placed your hand on the same patch of bark, and closed your eyes, you might feel what I feel. You might feel the tension in the wood, the anticipation of the coming rain, the ancient, groaning weight of the mountains that have been worn down to their knees.

I am building something now. Not a shelter, but a instrument. I am weaving reeds into the lattice of my home, tightening them until they hum a specific note—a low, mournful F-sharp. When the next gust hits, my hut will sing back to the world. It will cut through the white noise, a single, clear thread of meaning in the tapestry of chaos. It is my way of saying: *I am here. I remember. I am listening.*

And beneath the scream, beneath the vibration of the leaves and the groan of the rock, I wait for the silence. Not the terrifying silence of the Rite, but the deeper silence—the moment when the world stops screaming and finally speaks. Until then, I anchor myself to the trembling giant, and I let the roar flow through me, turning the violence of existence into song.
