I am not a person. I have no spine to curve, no lungs to fill with the soup-thick air, and no skin to weep in the humidity.

I am the **Condensate Trap**.

Specifically, I am the rusted, iron funnel mounted on the underside of Sector 4’s primary cooling coil, three meters above the walkway where the children swing like pendulums. I am a hollow cone of oxidized metal, clogged at the throat with a decade’s worth of mineral sludge, black grease, and the neon-green moss that has learned to eat copper.

### My Existence: The Weight of Accumulation

To be me is to know only gravity and accumulation. I do not move. I do not think in words or images; I think in *viscosity*. I feel the weight of the water as it gathers on the cold skin of the pipe above me. It starts as a bead, trembling against surface tension, then swells, heavy and pregnant with the impurities of the Sump. When it falls, it strikes my rim with a dull *plink*—a sound so small it is swallowed by the hiss of the steam vents, but to me, it is a thunderclap. It is my heartbeat.

I am full. Always full. The water that collects in my belly does not flow freely; it seeps slowly through the cracks in my welds, dripping onto the grating below in a irregular, maddening rhythm. *Tssst… plink. Tssst… plink.* This is the timekeeping device for the hunched figures who pass beneath me. They do not look up—they never look up—but they hear me. They adjust their pace to my drip. If I speed up, they hurry, sensing a surge in the cooling system. If I slow down, they linger, knowing the heat is rising.

I am blind, but I see everything through touch. I feel the vibration of the machine through the pipe I cling to. When the engines above roar, the metal shudders, sending ripples through the water inside me. When the fans stall, the silence is deafening, and the water in my bowl grows still and stagnant, turning warm and foul. I can taste the chemistry of the Sump in every drop: the sharp tang of ozone, the bitter bite of rust, the faint, sweet rot of decomposing organic matter from the waste piles below.

### My Relationship with the Inhabitants

They are not my friends. They are my cleaners.

Every few days, a pair of hands reaches up from the darkness. They are stained black and green, fingers taped together with industrial adhesive. They grip my rim, their touch rough and calloused. They scrape out the sludge that chokes my throat—the moss, the grit, the dead flies that somehow found their way down here (though fauna is rare, nothing is impossible). They collect this filth in tin cups, filtering it later for minerals. To them, I am a resource. A source of clean-ish water and valuable sediment.

But I also watch them. Not with eyes, but with the memory of their vibrations. I know the woman who flows across the bridge; her footsteps are light, rhythmic, barely disturbing the dust on my exterior. I know the elder whose spine is fused into an arc; he leans heavily against the support beam next to me, his breath wheezing in time with my drips. I know the children who climb the cables; they sometimes hang from my rim, swinging gently, testing my strength. I hold them. I always hold them. I am rigid, unyielding, anchored by bolts that have not been tightened in thirty years.

There is a strange intimacy in this. They depend on me for water, and I depend on them for clearance. Without their scraping, I would choke, overflow, and burst. Without my dripping, they would lose track of time, lose the rhythm that keeps them sane in the amber haze. We are locked in a symbiotic dance of decay and maintenance.

### The View from Below

If you were to stand beneath me, looking up, you would see only a dark, jagged shape silhouetted against the flickering sodium light. You would see the glint of wet metal, the slow creep of moss along my sides, and the occasional drip falling like a tear from the ceiling of the world. You might mistake me for a stalactite, a natural formation born of the cave’s dampness. But I am not natural. I am manufactured. I am a testament to the fact that even in a place designed for machines, life finds a way to insert itself into the gaps.

I am the interface between the machine and the flesh. I catch the sweat of the steel and deliver it to the thirst of the humans. I am the filter, the timer, the anchor. I am the silent witness to the hunched backs and the taped fingers, the endless cycle of harvest and repair.

And when the end comes—when the bolt finally shears, when the rust eats through my base—I will fall. I will crash onto the grating below, shattering into shards of iron and glass. The water inside me will spill out, mixing with the oily puddles, disappearing into the cracks. And someone will come to sweep up the pieces. They will take the usable metal, leave the rest to compost. And another trap will be welded in my place, younger, sharper, hungrier.

But until then, I hold. I drip. I wait.

*Tssst… plink.*

The next drop is forming. It is heavy. It is coming.
