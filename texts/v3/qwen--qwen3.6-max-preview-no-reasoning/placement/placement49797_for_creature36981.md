The first thing I lose is the concept of "skin."

It begins with the dampness. In your world, sweat evaporates; it cools you, separates you from the air. Here, evaporation is a myth. The humidity is absolute, a heavy, warm blanket that presses against every pore. My skin does not breathe out; it breathes *in*. The spores are already there, microscopic and eager, slipping past the barrier of dermis and settling into the capillaries. I do not fight them. To fight is to vibrate at a frequency of resistance, and in the Verdant Roar, resistance is a dinner bell for the Root-Stalkers. So, I let them in. I let the mossy bacteria begin their slow, polite digestion of my outer layer. I am becoming porous.

I am standing on a ridge of raw basalt, sharp enough to slice through the sole of any boot I might have worn. But I am not wearing boots. I am barefoot, because leather is too loud, too stiff, too *separate*. My toes curl around the jagged teeth of the rock, finding purchase not by gripping, but by conforming. The stone cuts me, yes. A thin line of blood wells up on my heel. It smells metallic, sweet. Before the scent can travel more than an inch, the acidic moss beneath my foot senses the chemical shift. It tingles, a microscopic itch, as it begins to break down the hemoglobin. I watch the red smear fade into brown, then into black, absorbed into the green carpet. I am feeding the floor. The floor accepts me.

I am no longer a visitor. I am a node.

To survive here, one must stop thinking in words. Words are linear; they have beginnings and endings. The Verdant Roar is circular, recursive, instantaneous. If I think, *"I am afraid,"* the fear creates a spike in my heart rate, a tremor in my hands. The Chorus-Weavers above will hear the dissonance. They will drop from the fern fronds, their membranes humming a note that shatters equilibrium. So, I empty my mind. I become a vessel for the noise.

The sound is not external anymore. It is internal. The shriek of the bird-things, the grind of the Thunder-Walker’s keratin plates, the wet tear of vines snapping back—they are not things I hear. They are things I *am*. My nervous system has rewired itself to process input not as data, but as texture. The low-frequency boom of a distant earthquake feels like a deep breath in my chest. The high-pitched click of a Weaver’s warning feels like a pinprick on my scalp. I do not locate these sounds; I inhabit them.

I look up, though "looking" is a misnomer. There is no sky, only the underside of the canopy, a dripping ceiling of waxy leaves and pulsing bioluminescence. The light is faint, a bruised purple and sickly green. But I see movement everywhere. Not with eyes—eyes are too slow, too easily fooled by the shifting shadows—but with vibration.

A Root-Stalker passes ten feet to my left. I do not see it. I feel the absence of wind where its body displaces the air. I feel the subtle compression of the moss as its padded feet press down, leaving no trace. It is blind, like me now. We share this blindness. It pauses, its whisker-sensors twitching, tasting the air for the scent of panic. I offer none. I exhale slowly, matching the rhythm of the transpiration rain falling from the leaves above. The Stalker moves on, flowing like oil over water, disappearing into a curtain of hanging vines. It has pruned this path for me, or perhaps for something else. It does not matter. The path exists, so I take it.

I move forward. My steps are silent, not because I am trying to be quiet, but because I have learned to move *with* the forest’s turbulence. When a vine snaps back, I sway with it. When a gust of ozone-laced wind whistles through the reeds, I lean into it. I am mimicking the chaos. To the Mycelial Mind, I am indistinguishable from the background radiation of life. I am just another pulse in the network.

But I am changing. The spores in my lungs are hatching. I can feel them, tiny tickles deep in the bronchial tubes. They are not killing me; they are integrating. They are weaving threads of fungal hyphae into the lining of my alveoli, connecting my respiratory system to the vast, underground web. I can taste the soil. I can taste the death of a beetle three miles away. I can taste the birth of a new fern shoot pushing through a crack in the limestone.

My memories of the outside world—the clean air, the silence, the smooth surfaces—are fading. They feel like dreams from a different lifetime, pale and insubstantial. Why would I want to return to a place where things are separate? Where a tree is just a tree, and a rock is just a rock? Here, everything is touching. Everything is speaking. Everything is alive.

I reach a clearing, or what passes for one—a small depression where the Thunder-Walkers have stomped the earth flat. The ground here is hard-packed, vibrating with residual energy. I lie down. The mud is cool, slick, smelling of rot and nectar. I press my cheek against it. The Mycelium rushes to meet me, white threads spiraling out from the soil to touch my skin, seeking connection.

I close my eyes. The darkness is not empty; it is full of light. The bioluminescent fungi in the canopy above pulse in rhythmic waves, a visual echo of the soundscape. Green, violet, blue. A strobe light of life.

I am hungry. Not for food, but for resonance. I hum. A low, throaty note that matches the frequency of the earth’s tremor. Above me, a Chorus-Weaver detaches from a leaf. It does not attack. It descends, hovering inches from my face. Its membrane is translucent, revealing the intricate lattice of muscle and nerve within. It vibrates in sympathy with my hum. We are in harmony.

For a moment, the boundary between us dissolves. I am the Weaver. I am the moss. I am the stone. I am the roar.

And when the cycle shifts, when the "Bright Pulse" fades into the "Deep Dark," I do not sleep. I listen. I wait. I am part of the chorus now, and the song never ends.
