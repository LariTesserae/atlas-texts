It begins with the pressure. Not atmospheric, but spatial. You are not standing on a mountain; you are standing inside a mountain’s breath.

The scale is deceptive, microscopic in its confinement yet vast in its implication. You are wedged into a fissure no wider than your shoulders, a vertical throat of obsidian and basalt that pierces upward into a sky you cannot see. The elevation parameter suggests you are miles high, near the roof of the world, but the hidden nature of this place means you experience it as a closet-sized universe. The walls are close enough to touch, slick with a condensation that feels less like water and more like liquid mercury—cold to the thought, hot to the skin.

The temperature is 2.9/3. It is a dry, baking heat that radiates from the rock itself, as if the stone has recently remembered it was once magma. Yet, because the space is so small, the air does not circulate. It hangs heavy, saturated with moisture that refuses to evaporate, creating a sauna of pure mineral steam. You breathe in droplets that taste of iron and sulfur. There is no vegetation here—no moss, no lichen, no desperate alpine flower clinging to a crack. This is a pre-life or post-life zone, a realm where biology has been scrubbed away by heat and time, leaving only the raw geology.

But the silence is wrong.

The sound parameter reads 0.4/3, suggesting quiet, but in this dense, enclosed space, silence is a physical weight. It is the absence of wind, the absence of bird calls, the absence of the rustle of leaves. Instead, there is a low-frequency thrum, a vibration in the teeth, caused by the movement of the earth below. And then, the fauna arrives.

They do not walk; they pour.

The fauna parameter is 2.7/3—overwhelming. But these are not animals as you know them. They are translucent, gelatinous entities, no larger than a human thumb, composed of pressurized steam and silicate dust. They swarm the wet rocks, thousands upon thousands of them, layering over one another until the black stone disappears beneath a living, shifting carpet of pale blue and gray. They are blind, sensing only heat and vibration. They move with a fluid, synchronized grace, rippling across the vertical surfaces like oil on water.

There are so many of them that the density (1.5/3) becomes suffocating. They brush against your boots, your legs, your hands. They do not bite; they do not sting. They simply *are*. Their presence is so absolute that you feel your own individuality dissolving. To stand still is to become part of the colony. To move is to disturb a hive mind that has no queen, only a collective thermal instinct.

The weirdness (2.8/3) manifests in the light. Though the external light level is normal (1.5/3), within this fissure, the light behaves incorrectly. It does not cast shadows. Instead, it bends around the swarming entities, refracting through their semi-solid bodies into spectra that do not exist in the visible range. You see colors that hurt your eyes—not bright, but deep, impossible hues of violet-black and infrared-red. The reality breakdown is subtle: sometimes, when you look at the wall, the rock appears to be breathing. Sometimes, the swarm seems to form shapes—faces, maps, equations—before dissolving back into chaos. It is a hallucination born of heat exhaustion, or perhaps the landscape is trying to communicate in a language of geometry and heat.

The terrain is young (erosion 1.1/3). The edges of the fissure are sharp, jagged shards of volcanic glass that have not yet been worn smooth by rain or wind. Every step risks a cut, every shift in weight sends tiny avalanches of glass-dust cascading down into the dark below. The ground beneath you is not soil, but a compacted layer of cooled lava foam, porous and brittle. When you step, it crunches, releasing puffs of superheated gas that hiss softly—the only sound besides the thrumming vibration.

Water is everywhere (2.2/3), but it is not free-flowing. It weeps from the pores of the rock, forming beads that roll slowly downward, gathering mass until they detach and fall into the darkness. These drops catch the strange, bent light, acting as lenses that focus the heat into pinpoint lasers on your skin. The atmosphere is thick with humidity, so much so that your clothes cling to you, heavy and damp, yet the heat prevents any cooling relief. It is a paradox: you are drowning in air, burning in water.

There are no structures (built 0.0/3), no tools (tech 1.0/3 implies a primitive state, but here, even primitive tools would melt or shatter). You are naked before the elements, save for whatever minimal gear you brought. The ecosystem is active (dynamic 1.7/3); the swarm moves in cycles, rising during the hottest parts of the day to cool themselves against the upper vents, descending at night to huddle in the warmer depths. You are caught in their rhythm. If you stay too long, you will begin to sweat out your own minerals, joining the cycle. Your salt will feed the next generation of silica-swarmers.

The rules of this world are simple: Do not fight the heat. Do not resist the swarm. Move slowly, deliberately. Respect the sharpness of the young stone. Listen to the silence, for it is full of voices you cannot hear.

As you climb higher, the fissure narrows further. The swarm becomes denser, almost solid. You must push through them, feeling their cool, slimy bodies slide against your skin. They do not resist; they flow around you like water. For a moment, you are submerged in them, blind, deaf, surrounded by the hum of a million tiny lives. Then, you break through.

Above, the fissure opens slightly, revealing a sliver of sky. It is not blue. It is white, blindingly bright, filtered through layers of atmospheric ice and steam. The heat intensifies. The swarm thins out, unable to survive the direct exposure. You are alone again, perched on a ledge of black glass, looking out over a horizon of clouds that stretch endlessly below. You are miles up, hidden in a crack in the world, hot, wet, silent, and utterly alive.

And then, you notice something on the ledge beside you. A single, perfect sphere of obsidian, polished smooth by the swarm. Inside it, trapped in the glass, is a swirl of color that matches the impossible hue you saw earlier. It pulses, faintly, in time with your heartbeat.

The landscape has given you a gift. Or a warning.

You pick it up. It is warm.
