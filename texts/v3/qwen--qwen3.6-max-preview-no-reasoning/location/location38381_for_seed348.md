The place is called the Sump. It is not a city in the way you understand cities, nor is it a swamp in the way you remember swamps. It is a single, vast, enclosed basin, perhaps three kilometers across, sunk deep into the crust of a world that forgot how to breathe. The elevation is low, pressing down on your lungs, but the air is cool, carrying the chill of deep stone and old metal.

There are no plants here. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen, not even a mold. The ground is a mosaic of black basalt, polished smooth by millennia of friction, interspersed with patches of rusted iron grating and slick, oily concrete. It is a mineral world, dead and sterile, save for the life that has taken over everything else.

The fauna is overwhelming.

It begins with the sound. The Sump is loud. It is a constant, deafening roar that vibrates in the teeth and the marrow. It is not the sound of wind or water, but the sound of movement—millions of chitinous legs scraping against stone, the wet slap of gelatinous bodies hitting pavement, the high-pitched shriek of avian-like creatures with too many joints, and the low, thrumming bass of massive, slow-moving beasts whose footsteps shake the foundations of the structures around them.

The structures are industrial, layered, and dense. Towers of riveted steel rise from the basin floor, connected by catwalks, pipes, and gantries that form a chaotic web overhead. These were once factories, power stations, and refineries. Now, they are hives. The technology is evident: pistons still pump, though they pump nothing but air; conveyor belts run empty, worn smooth by the claws of things that ride them like rivers; turbines spin idly, their blades coated in a thick, iridescent slime. The infrastructure is organized, but the organization has been repurposed by biology. Pipes are used as nesting tubes; smokestacks are hollowed out into breeding chambers; control rooms are packed tight with writhing masses of insectile larvae.

The light is normal, filtered through a haze of condensation and steam. The air is damp, clinging to every surface. Water does not flow in rivers here; it exists as pockets, puddles, and seeps. Condensation drips constantly from the ceiling of the basin—a natural rock dome far above—and collects in the crevices of the machinery. These pools are dark and still, reflecting the chaotic sky of wings and limbs. Occasionally, rain falls, not from clouds, but from the sweating walls of the industrial complex, a cold drizzle that mixes with the oil and the excrement of the inhabitants.

The ecology is strange because it is entirely carnivorous, or at least, entirely dependent on the energy of the place itself. There are no herbivores, for there is nothing to eat. The smaller creatures—beetles the size of dogs, spiders with translucent abdomens filled with glowing fluid, birds with beaks like needle-nose pliers—feed on each other. They feed on the heat radiating from the still-warm engines. They feed on the static electricity crackling between broken wires. They feed on the moisture.

The larger beings are terrifying. They are the size of buses, armored in plates of chitin that mimic the rusted metal around them. They move slowly, deliberately, their multiple eyes scanning the environment with a cold, intelligent indifference. They do not seem to age. They do not seem to die. When one falls, its body is consumed within hours by the swarm, leaving only the hard shell, which becomes part of the architecture, fused into the landscape by the relentless pressure of time and weight.

The weirdness lies in the stability. Nothing changes. The noise is always the same pitch. The density of life is always at capacity. If a creature dies, another takes its place instantly. If a pipe bursts, the spray is absorbed by the surrounding biomass before it can hit the ground. The system is in perfect, horrifying equilibrium. Time does not pass here; it accumulates. The layers of construction are visible—the original brickwork, the added steel beams, the newer plastic conduits—all covered in the same patina of grease and shed skin.

You walk along a catwalk, the metal groaning under your weight. Below you, a sea of movement churns. A million eyes look up, not with hunger, but with recognition. You are an anomaly. You are soft. You are warm. You are dry. But you are also small. The rules of this world are simple: occupy space, consume energy, remain still. To move is to invite attention. To stop is to become part of the structure.

The atmosphere is thick with the smell of ozone, rot, and hot metal. It is a scent that sticks to the back of the throat. The humidity makes every breath feel heavy, like inhaling soup. Yet, despite the dampness, there is no sense of decay. Everything is preserved in the amber of the present moment. The rust is fresh. The slime is new. The bones are white.

In the center of the Sump stands the Great Piston, a relic of the old world, towering hundreds of feet into the air. It moves up and down, slowly, rhythmically, powered by some unseen geothermal heart. Around its base, the largest of the creatures gather, basking in the vibration, their bodies humming in resonance with the machine. They are the priests of this place, the keepers of the equilibrium. They do not attack. They simply watch.

To leave the Sump is impossible. The walls of the basin are sheer, slick with condensation and scaled with climbing creatures. The only way out is through the ventilation shafts high above, but those are choked with webs and nests. So you stay. You find a corner, a niche in the machinery where the noise is slightly less, where the light is dimmer. You sit. You wait. You listen to the roar. And slowly, inevitably, you begin to understand the language of the scrape and the scuttle. You begin to feel the vibration in your own bones. You begin to forget what it was like to be alone.

The Sump does not hate you. It does not love you. It simply includes you. You are another variable in the equation, another piece of matter in the closed loop. Your warmth is noted. Your moisture is measured. Your silence is appreciated. And when you finally stop moving, when your heart slows to match the rhythm of the Great Piston, you will not be eaten. You will be incorporated. Your clothes will become insulation. Your bones will become support struts. Your memory will become part of the hum.

This is the landscape: a cathedral of industry turned hive, a tomb of technology made alive, a place where life has won so completely that it has forgotten what death means. It is cool, damp, loud, and utterly, perfectly still.
