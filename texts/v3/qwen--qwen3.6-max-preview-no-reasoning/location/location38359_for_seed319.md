The air here does not move; it hangs, heavy and warm, like a wet wool blanket draped over the shoulders of the world. You are standing on the edge of **Orobus**, a city that is less a place people live in and more a machine that has forgotten how to stop.

It sits at an elevation where the clouds should be thin, but they are not. The atmosphere is thick with condensation, a perpetual, low-hanging mist that clings to the iron bones of the settlement. It is high up, yes—perhaps two thousand meters above the sea level of whatever ocean lies below the cloud floor—but it feels submerged. The water table is so close to the surface that the ground itself seems to weep. Pools of stagnant, oily rainwater collect in every depression, reflecting the dull, greyish-white light of the sky. This is not a fresh rain; it is the residue of centuries of atmospheric processing, a constant drizzle that never quite falls, only accumulates.

### The Terrain: Settled Erosion

The landscape is defined by **established erosion**. This is not the chaotic tearing of a young mountain range, nor the soft rolling hills of ancient earth. It is a terrain that has been worn down by its own weight and the relentless, slow grind of industry. The rock is sedimentary, layered like pages of a book left out in the damp, crumbling into fine, red-grey dust that mixes with the mud.

The ground is uneven, pocked with sinkholes that have long since stabilized, filled with brackish water. These pools are connected by subterranean channels, a hidden aquifer that bubbles up through cracks in the pavement and the foundations of buildings. The earth is soft underfoot, a sponge saturated with warmth. There is no sharpness here. Every edge has been rounded by time and moisture. Stone steps are worn smooth, not by feet, but by the sheer persistence of dripping water. Metal railings are coated in layers of verdigris and rust, blooming outward like slow-moving fungi.

### The Built Environment: Industrial Urbanism

Orobus is a dense, sprawling urban mass, yet it feels sparse because of the sheer scale of its emptiness between structures. The architecture is industrial brutalism taken to its logical, humid extreme. Buildings rise in jagged clusters of concrete and riveted steel, stacked atop one another in a desperate bid for vertical space, as if trying to escape the wet ground. They are not tall skyscrapers, but wide, squat fortresses of production, interconnected by a web of catwalks, pipes, and suspended cables.

The construction is **layered**. You can see the history of the city in its walls. The base levels are old stone, slick with algae and moss. Above them, brickwork from a century ago, now cracked and repointed with grey mortar. Higher still, corrugated iron and prefabricated concrete panels, bolted on in haste during some past expansion boom. The entire city is wrapped in infrastructure. Massive ventilation shafts pierce the skyline, exhaling warm, moist air into the already saturated atmosphere. Pipes run along the sides of buildings like veins, carrying steam, coolant, or waste water—no one remembers which anymore. They leak constantly, adding to the ambient dampness.

There are streets, but they are narrow canyons between these towering blocks. They are paved with cobblestones that have sunk into the mud, creating a treacherous, undulating surface. Bridges span the gaps between buildings, made of grating that allows the sound of the machinery below to rise up, unimpeded.

### The Atmosphere: Warmth and Wetness

The temperature is consistently warm, around 20–25°C (68–77°F), but it feels hotter due to the humidity. The air is near saturation. Sweat does not evaporate; it beads on the skin and stays there. Clothes cling. Hair becomes heavy and lank. The warmth is not tropical or vibrant; it is the warmth of a compost heap, of a running engine, of biological decay slowed by preservatives.

This warmth keeps the water from freezing, allowing the ecosystem of mold and mildew to thrive unchecked. Every surface that is not actively heated by machinery is covered in a thin film of slime. Lichens, pale yellow and green, crust over the metalwork. Fungi grow in the corners of windowsills, their caps glistening with condensed moisture. The air smells of ozone, wet rust, stale oil, and something sweetly rotting, like overripe fruit left in a cellar.

### Vegetation: Sparse and Struggling

Vegetation is rare, not because the conditions are harsh, but because they are *too* specific. Only the most resilient, opportunistic plants survive here. There are no trees. No shrubs. Instead, you find patches of hardy ferns growing in the crevices of broken concrete, their fronds dark green and waxy, designed to shed water. Moss carpets the north-facing walls, thick and spongy, drinking the constant condensation.

In the few open squares, where the buildings pull back to allow a glimpse of the sky, there are planter boxes filled with soil that has turned to black sludge. Here, stunted, pale weeds struggle upward, their leaves broad and thin to catch the diffuse light. They are sickly things, choked by the humidity, prone to fungal infections that turn their stems black. They do not flower. They simply exist, clinging to life in the shadow of the machines.

### Fauna: Rare and Adapted

Animals are scarce. The noise and the pollution have driven most wildlife away. What remains are scavengers and specialists. Small, blind insects crawl in the damp shadows of the pipework, feeding on the organic sludge that accumulates in the drains. Rats are common, but they are large, hairless, and cautious, moving silently along the upper gantries, avoiding the flooded lower levels.

Birds are almost nonexistent. The few that appear are crows, their feathers sleek and waterproof, perching on the highest vents, watching the city with intelligent, wary eyes. They do not sing. They watch. Occasionally, a bat might dart through the misty air, hunting the insects attracted to the streetlights, but even they seem hesitant, confused by the acoustics of the space.

### Sound: The Deafening Hum

The defining feature of Orobus is the **sound**. It is deafening, a constant, overwhelming roar that vibrates in the chest cavity. It is not a single noise, but a layered cacophony:

1.  **The Bass Note:** A deep, throbbing hum from the massive generators buried beneath the city. This is the heartbeat of Orobus, felt in the teeth and the bones. It never stops.
2.  **The Hiss:** The sound of steam escaping from countless valves and leaks. A continuous, white-noise hiss that overlays the bass, filling the spaces between the heavier sounds.
3.  **The Clatter:** The rhythmic pounding of pistons, the grinding of gears, the clang of metal on metal. These are irregular, punctuating the steady hum with sudden, jarring impacts.
4.  **The Drip:** Underneath it all, the sound of water. Millions of drops falling onto metal, stone, and water. *Plip. Plip. Splash.* It is a subtle counterpoint to the industrial roar, a reminder of the pervasive wetness.

Because the sound is so constant, the human ear adapts, tuning out the background noise until it becomes a silence of its own. But when the machinery cycles down for maintenance—a rare event—the sudden drop in volume is physically painful, leaving the inhabitants dizzy and disoriented, hearing the rush of blood in their own ears.

### Ecology and Rules of the World

The ecology of Orobus is artificial. It is a closed loop, or attempts to be. Water is collected from the air via condensers, filtered through layers of charcoal and sand, and reused. Waste heat is vented upward, contributing to the local microclimate. The city feeds on itself. Scrap metal is melted down and recast. Organic waste is composted into the sludge that feeds the few plants.

The rules of this world are simple: **Stay dry. Keep moving. Ignore the noise.**

Movement is slow. The combination of slippery surfaces, crowded walkways, and the psychological weight of the environment means that nothing happens quickly. People walk with deliberate care, heads down, hands in pockets. Conversations are shouted, or conducted via hand signals and written notes. Eye contact is brief. There is a sense of shared resignation, a collective understanding that this is just how things are. The city has always been here. It will always be here. It is eroding, yes, but slowly, gracefully, settling into its final form.

### The Weirdness: The Condensation Memory

There is a strangeness to Orobus, a subtle wrongness that gnaws at the edges of perception. The condensation on the windows doesn’t just drip; it sometimes forms patterns. Geometric shapes, faces, words in languages no one speaks. If you stare at a fogged-up pane too long, you might see your own reflection blink when you didn’t.

The mirrors in the public baths are always slightly warped, showing a version of yourself that looks older, wetter, more tired. Some say the city remembers everything. That the water, cycling through the pipes and the air and the bodies of the inhabitants, carries traces of memory. A drop of rain that fell on a roof fifty years ago might now be part of the sweat on your brow, carrying with it the echo of a shout, a laugh, a secret whispered in the dark.

This is why the fauna is rare. Animals feel it. They sense the weight of the accumulated past in the air, the pressure of all those lives lived in the damp and the noise. They flee, if they can. Humans stay, because they have nowhere else to go. Or perhaps because they, too, are becoming part of the machine, their thoughts slowing, their movements synchronizing with the hum, their souls dissolving into the warm, wet air.

### Visual Summary

Imagine a valley carved from soft, red rock, filled with a labyrinth of rusting iron and concrete towers. Mist curls around the spires like smoke. Green algae stains the walls. Steam rises from manholes, mixing with the fog. The light is flat, diffuse, casting no sharp shadows. Everything is wet. Everything is loud. And yet, there is a profound stillness in the motion, a patience in the decay. Orobus is not dying. It is enduring. It is a monument to the slow, inevitable victory of entropy, dressed in the guise of industry.
