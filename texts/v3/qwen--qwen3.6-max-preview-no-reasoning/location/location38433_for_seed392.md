The world is not grown; it is woven.

You stand in the Basin of the Still-Loom, a low-lying expanse that stretches to the horizon, bounded by no mountains, for there are no stones here. The ground beneath your feet is not soil, nor rock, nor sand. It is a vast, interlocking mosaic of cured leather, treated bark, and hardened resin, stitched together with fibers thick as your wrist. This is the floor of everything. Every inch of this place has been placed by hand, knotted, lashed, or pressed into existence by generations of builders who knew nothing of geology, only of craft.

It is hot. A heavy, wet heat hangs in the air, pressing against the skin like a warm, damp cloth. The light is blindingly bright, a white-gold glare that seems to come from everywhere at once, reflecting off the polished surfaces of the constructed terrain. There is no sun visible in the sky—only a uniform, luminous ceiling of stretched translucent hide, taut across the upper atmosphere, diffusing the brilliance into an omnipresent, shadowless noon.

Despite the brightness, it is quiet. Not the silence of emptiness, but the hushed, held-breath quiet of a library or a temple. The soundscape is muted by the density of the vegetation, which is lush but strangely sparse in its distribution. Giant ferns made of layered felt and green-dyed silk rise thirty feet high, their fronds unfurling in slow, deliberate spirals. They do not sway, for there is no wind here—the equilibrium is absolute. Between these colossal textile trees, there are wide gaps of open space, revealing the intricate patchwork of the ground.

Water is present, but it does not flow. It condenses. Because the temperature is high and the materials are organic, moisture weeps from the seams of the world. Beads of sweat gather on the undersides of the giant leaf-fronds, growing heavy until they detach with a soft *plip* that echoes too loudly in the stillness. These droplets fall onto the leather ground, where they are immediately absorbed into spongy mats of moss-cloth, keeping the surface perpetually damp but never flooded. Pools form in depressions carved into the resin, clear and mirror-still, reflecting the bright ceiling above.

The fauna is abundant, yet it moves with a dreamlike slowness. Creatures here are not born of flesh and bone, but of assembly. You see insects the size of dogs, crafted from jointed bamboo and iridescent beetle-shell, clicking softly as they traverse the leather plains. Birds, stitched from feathers and wire, hang suspended in the air, their wings frozen mid-beat, held aloft by some unseen tension in the fabric of reality. They do not fly so much as they occupy space, shifting position only when you look away. A herd of grazing beasts, resembling deer but covered in velvet upholstery, stands motionless in the distance, their heads lowered to nibble at the edges of the felt-ferns. They do not chew; they simply press their muzzles against the plant matter, absorbing sustenance through contact.

This is the weirdness: the breakdown of cause and effect. The landscape is young, sharp-edged, and freshly made, yet it feels ancient because it never changes. The stitches holding the ground together are pristine, unfrayed by time. There is no decay, no erosion, no rot. The primitive technology of the inhabitants—simple awls, needles of bone, looms of wood—is sufficient because the world requires no maintenance. It is a finished product.

If you walk toward one of the distant structures—a spire of woven wicker rising like a cathedral needle—you notice that the perspective is wrong. The spire does not recede into the distance as it should. It remains the same size, whether you are ten paces away or a mile. The scale of the valley is regional, town-sized, yet the spatial logic is fluid. You can reach out and touch the horizon if you stretch far enough, though your arm never seems to lengthen.

The materials tell the story of the rules. Everything is tactile. The air smells of tannin, fresh-cut grass, and ozone. The humidity clings to your clothes, making them heavy. There is no dirt under your fingernails because there is no earth. There is only the Made.

In the center of the basin lies a great pool, circular and perfect, lined with tiles of polished obsidian-glass (the only mineral allowed, imported and set by hand). In the water, shapes move. They are not fish, but shadows detached from objects. A shadow of a bird detaches from a hanging tapestry-bird and dives into the pool, swimming through the liquid darkness. When it emerges, it is a real bird, shaking off droplets that turn into pearls upon hitting the air.

The ecosystem is closed, self-contained, and perfectly balanced. The "plants" provide shade and moisture; the "animals" provide movement and sound; the "ground" provides stability. Nothing competes. Nothing dies. The abundance of life is static, a tableau of vitality frozen in eternal afternoon.

You realize, with a creeping unease, that you are also part of the construction. Your breath fogs in the air, but the fog does not dissipate. It hangs there, a small cloud of your own making, adding to the humidity. If you were to stay here long enough, would you become stiff? Would your joints lock into place, your skin harden into leather, your voice fade into the ambient quiet?

There is no answer. The light remains bright. The heat remains steady. The stitches hold. The world waits, not for change, but for observation. It exists to be seen, and in being seen, it confirms its own perfection. To disturb it would be rude. So you stand still, listening to the occasional *plip* of condensation, watching the velvet deer graze without moving, feeling the wet warmth of the manufactured air, and understanding that this is not a place you pass through. It is a place that holds you, gently, firmly, forever.
